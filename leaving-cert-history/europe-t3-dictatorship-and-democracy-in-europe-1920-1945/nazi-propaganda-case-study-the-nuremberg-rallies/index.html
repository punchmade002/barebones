Nazi propaganda: aims and state control of the media

Once in power the Nazis created a state propaganda ministry and brought the press, radio and public entertainment under central control.

  • The Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda was created on 13 March 1933 under Joseph Goebbels, and it took responsibility for the press, radio, film, theatre, music and the arts.
  • The Editors' Law of October 1933 required newspaper editors to be members of the Nazi-approved professional organisations, and Jewish journalists were excluded from the press.
  • Radio was brought under state control in 1933 when the ministry took over the broadcasting companies, and the Volksempfänger (People's Receiver), a cheap radio set costing 76 Reichsmarks, was launched at the Berlin radio exhibition in August 1933 to reach ordinary households; loudspeakers carried major speeches into factories and public squares.
  • The Reich Chamber of Culture, set up in September 1933 under Goebbels, controlled the members of the arts, and writers, painters, musicians and actors had to be admitted to it to work.
  • Propaganda used repetition of simple themes, such as national unity, the leader's authority, the threat from communism and the Jews, and the glory of the German nation.
  • Posters, parades, slogans and mass rallies were used to create a sense of community and to bind people to the leader and the party.

Film, art, books and the Olympics

The regime used film and public spectacle to present an image of strength and unity, and it condemned art and literature that did not fit its ideology.

  • On 10 May 1933 students and party members burned books by Jewish, Marxist and liberal authors in public ceremonies in Berlin and other university towns.
  • The Degenerate Art exhibition in Munich in July 1937 displayed works by modernist painters, including Expressionists, as examples of decadence, while the Great German Art Exhibition showed approved art at the same time.
  • The 1936 Summer Olympics in Berlin were used to present Germany as a peaceful and modern nation, and the regime temporarily removed some anti-Semitic signs before the games.
  • German cinema was controlled through the Reich Film Chamber, which approved scripts and banned films that the regime judged unsuitable, and the state funded films with propaganda messages.
  • Feature films such as Jud Süß (1940) were made as explicit anti-Semitic propaganda, and they were shown to audiences in Germany and occupied countries.

Key personality: Joseph Goebbels

Goebbels was the Gauleiter of Berlin and the head of Nazi propaganda from 1933 to 1945, and he was responsible for the regime's most important mass campaigns.

  • Born in 1897 in Rheydt, he studied literature and gained a doctorate before joining the Nazi party in 1924, when he worked for Gregor Strasser's wing of the party.
  • Hitler appointed him Gauleiter of Berlin in 1926, where he organised street campaigns, newspapers such as Der Angriff and mass meetings to build support in the capital.
  • He was a participant in the Reichstag election campaigns of 1930 and 1932, using modern techniques such as posters, loudspeaker vans and radio.
  • As Reich Minister of Propaganda from 1933, he shaped the regime's cultural policies and directed the Nazi response to the Jewish population through the media.
  • In his speech at the Berlin Sportpalast on 18 February 1943 he called for total war, and his speech was broadcast to the German public.
  • Historians differ in their view of his power: some see him as the regime's most effective propagandist and one of Hitler's most loyal followers, while others stress his rivalry with Himmler, Göring and Ribbentrop; his influence rose again from 1944 when he became Plenipotentiary for Total War, and he killed himself with his wife after poisoning their six children in Berlin on 1 May 1945.

Key personality: Leni Riefenstahl

Riefenstahl was a film-maker whose documentaries of the Nazi party's rallies and the 1936 Olympics gave the regime its most influential visual image.

  • Born in Berlin in 1902, she trained as a dancer and actress and made mountain films before Hitler commissioned her to film the party's rallies.
  • She was a participant in the Nazi film project, directing Triumph of the Will (Triumph des Willens) of 1935, which recorded the 1934 Nuremberg Rally.
  • Her documentary Olympia, released in 1938, covered the 1936 Berlin Olympics, and it used innovative camera techniques to present athletic achievement as a national triumph.
  • Contemporaries in Germany praised her technical achievement, Triumph of the Will won the German National Film Prize in 1935 and a gold medal at the Paris exposition of 1937, and foreign audiences saw her films as a powerful example of cinematic art, while critics abroad recognised their propaganda purpose.
  • After 1945 she claimed that her films were artistic documents rather than propaganda and denied knowing of the crimes of the regime; after several hearings she was classified as a fellow traveller (Mitläufer) in 1952, and she died in 2003 aged 101.
  • Historians have judged her films as among the most effective propaganda ever made, and they continue to debate the boundary between art and political propaganda in her work.

Case study background: why Nuremberg and the rally grounds

Nuremberg was chosen for the party's annual rally because of its historic association with German culture and its suitability for mass gatherings.

  • The Nazi party held its first large rally in Nuremberg in August 1927, and the city became the site of the annual party rally, known as the Reichsparteitag.
  • Nuremberg was a medieval imperial city with a strong sense of German history, and the Nazis used its historic buildings and traditions to link the regime with the German past.
  • The rally grounds were built on the Dutzendteich area to the south of the old city, and the largest planned building, the Congress Hall, was never completed.
  • The Zeppelin Field, with a grandstand designed by Speer, was used for mass parades and the appearance of the Führer before the party's formations.
  • The Luitpold Arena was used for the mass assemblies of the SA, SS and Hitler Youth, and the arena was designed to hold large numbers of marching men.
  • Speer's design for the rallies used massed banners, stone architecture and, from 1934, about 130 anti-aircraft searchlights pointed upwards at night, which became known as the cathedral of light.

The rallies, 1933-1938: staging and themes

Each year's rally had a theme that reflected the regime's stage of development, and the rallies expanded from party gatherings into national spectacles.

  • The 1933 rally, held in September, was the first held after Hitler became Chancellor, and it was called the Rally of Victory.
  • The 1934 rally of 4 to 10 September was filmed by Riefenstahl in Triumph of the Will, and it was the first after the Night of the Long Knives; it featured mass parades of the party, the SA and the SS, and army units, as a demonstration of unity.
  • At the 1935 rally, called the Rally of Freedom and held in September, the Reichstag met in Nuremberg, and the Nuremberg Laws were announced there on 15 September 1935.
  • The 1936 rally was called the Rally of Honour and the 1937 rally was called the Rally of Labour and included parades of workers and the Labour Service.
  • The 1938 rally, held in September just before the Munich Agreement, was called the Rally of Greater Germany, and it celebrated the Anschluss and the German expansion.
  • The 1939 rally, planned as the Rally of Peace, was cancelled at the outbreak of war, and after 1945 the rally grounds were used by the US Army and the city, with parts of the Zeppelin Field surviving today.

Participants, purpose and effect

The rallies aimed to create emotional bonds between the leader, the party and the German people, and they were designed for both those who attended and the wider public.

  • Hundreds of thousands of party members, workers, soldiers and youths travelled to Nuremberg, and many found the rallies a powerful experience of collective belonging.
  • The rallies used ritual, including the consecration of party banners, torchlight processions and the oath of loyalty, to create a sense of sacred purpose.
  • Newsreels, radio and the press reported the rallies to the German public, and Riefenstahl's films extended their reach to cinemas in Germany and abroad.
  • Participants were organised into units and marched in disciplined formations, which created a visual impression of unity and order.
  • Foreign visitors and journalists were impressed by the rallies, and some saw them as evidence of the order and vitality of the new Germany.
  • The rallies also served to publicise the regime's laws and policies, including the 1935 Nuremberg Laws, and to present anti-Semitic ideas as part of the national community.
  • Riefenstahl's earlier film of the 1933 rally, Victory of Faith, was withdrawn after the murder of Ernst Röhm in 1934, which is one reason Triumph of the Will shows Hitler and the party as the sole leadership.
  • Triumph of the Will opens with Hitler's aeroplane descending through clouds over Nuremberg and is built on contrasts of the individual leader and the massed ranks, with music by Herbert Windt.

Press, radio, cinema and other media in practice

Control worked through licensing, daily instructions and a mixture of entertainment and political messages.

  • The Reich Press Chamber and the ministry's daily press conferences told editors what to print, and the Nazi publisher Max Amann bought up or closed many independent papers; the party paper Völkischer Beobachter and Goebbels's own Der Angriff were prominent.
  • Radio mixed light music and entertainment with news and speeches by Nazi leaders, and from 1939 listening to foreign broadcasts such as the BBC was a crime punishable by prison or, in serious cases, death.
  • The cinema was the most popular medium: entertainment films outnumbered political ones, but every programme included a newsreel, and from 1940 the single Deutsche Wochenschau newsreel was shown across occupied Europe.
  • Posters and slogans such as 'Ein Volk, ein Reich, ein Führer' were widely displayed, and youth and women's organisations, schools and the Labour Front were used for indoctrination.
  • The regime promoted the cult of Hitler as the saviour and father of the nation through the 'Heil Hitler' greeting, his birthday celebrations, portraits in public buildings and books of photographs by his photographer Heinrich Hoffmann.

The rallies in detail: programme and ritual

A standard programme of set pieces over about a week made each rally a ritual of loyalty.

  • Each rally had days for particular groups: the party leadership, the Labour Service, the Hitler Youth, the SA and SS and, from 1935, the armed forces.
  • At the Luitpold Arena Hitler walked between ranks of tens of thousands of SA and SS men to lay a wreath at the war memorial, in 1934 with SA chief Viktor Lutze and Himmler, a staged image of the new order after the purge of Röhm.
  • In the consecration of the flags, Hitler touched new standards with the Blood Flag, said to have been carried in the Munich Putsch of 1923.
  • At the 1934 rally the Labour Service marched with spades, and the 'cathedral of light' made a night-time spectacle for the first time.
  • Hitler's speeches, usually delivered at night or in the evening when crowds were most receptive, expressed party doctrine and foreign policy, and at the 1936 rally the Four Year Plan was announced.
  • Rallies also had a popular side, with special trains, free food and accommodation in camps, and much of the cost was met by the party and by industry.

Assessment: the effectiveness and limits of Nazi propaganda

Judgements of Nazi propaganda weigh its success in creating consent and loyalty against the limits of persuasion and the role of terror in maintaining order.

  • Achievement: propaganda helped the Nazis build popular support in the 1930s, and the rallies and films gave the regime a powerful image of unity.
  • Limit: propaganda could not by itself sustain support, and the regime relied on the police, the courts and the camps to suppress opposition.
  • Contemporary view: many Germans responded to the rallies with enthusiasm, while exiled opponents and foreign critics saw them as an exhibition of dictatorship.
  • Later view: historians have debated how far the population believed the propaganda and how far it accepted the regime for other reasons, such as economic recovery and national pride.
  • Achievement: Riefenstahl's films remain among the most studied examples of film propaganda, and they have shaped the way later film-makers have used visual rhetoric.
  • Limit: after 1943 the propaganda effort focused increasingly on defence and national survival, and its credibility declined as the war turned against Germany.

Key terms

Propaganda
The organised spread of information and images to shape opinion and promote a regime, its leader and its policies.
Personality cult
The promotion of a leader as an infallible and heroic figure, shown through propaganda, rituals and his image in public life.
Totalitarianism
A system in which the state seeks to control every aspect of public and private life through a single party, secret police and propaganda.
Reich Chamber of Culture
The Nazi body set up in 1933 under Goebbels, through which artists, writers and journalists had to be admitted to work in Germany.
Volksempfänger
The cheap radio set produced in Germany from 1933 to bring Nazi broadcasts into ordinary households.
Reichsparteitag
The annual Nazi party rally held in Nuremberg, staged as a demonstration of party unity and national strength.
Degenerate art
The Nazi term for modernist art, which was condemned and removed from German public collections, as shown in the Munich exhibition of 1937.
Triumph of the Will
Leni Riefenstahl's 1935 film of the 1934 Nuremberg Rally, one of the most influential propaganda films ever made.
Gleichschaltung of culture
The bringing of art, press, radio and film under Nazi control through the Chamber of Culture and the propaganda ministry.
Cathedral of light
Albert Speer's effect of about 130 searchlights pointed into the night sky at the Nuremberg rallies, first used in 1934.
Blood Flag
The swastika banner said to have been carried in the 1923 Munich Putsch, used to consecrate new party flags at the rallies.
Goebbels's Sportpalast speech
The speech of 18 February 1943 calling on Germans to accept total war after Stalingrad.
