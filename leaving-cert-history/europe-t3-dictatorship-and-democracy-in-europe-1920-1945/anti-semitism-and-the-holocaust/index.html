Nazi racial ideology and its roots

Anti-Semitism had a long history in Europe, and the Nazi regime turned it into a racial doctrine that treated Jews as enemies of the German nation.

  • Anti-Semitism in Europe combined religious hostility, economic resentment and, from the late nineteenth century, racial theories that claimed Jews were a separate and inferior race (the notes on the period before 1914 cover its earlier growth).
  • Hitler's Mein Kampf of 1925 blamed Jews for Germany's defeat in 1918, for communism and for the Versailles settlement, and it set out the aim of removing Jews from German life.
  • The Nazi idea of a herrenvolk, a master race of Germanic people, required that Jews be defined as racially alien and excluded from the German community.
  • The Nazi party's propaganda, including the newspaper Der Stürmer, promoted anti-Semitic stereotypes and encouraged hostility towards Jewish neighbours.
  • Anti-Semitic thinking was not confined to Germany: in 1938 Italy introduced racial laws excluding Jews from schools, the professions and the armed forces.
  • Jews made up less than one per cent of Germany's population in 1933, and most had been fully integrated into German economic, cultural and civic life before the Nazi period.

Exclusion and the Nuremberg Laws, 1933-1935

From 1933 the regime excluded Jews from public life through boycotts, dismissals and legal definitions of race.

  • A national boycott of Jewish shops and businesses was ordered on 1 April 1933, although it was poorly supported and was called off after a day.
  • The Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service of April 1933 dismissed Jewish officials, and later laws excluded Jews from the legal profession, medicine and journalism.
  • The Nuremberg Laws of 15 September 1935 defined Jews by descent and removed German citizenship from Jews, who became subjects with no political rights.
  • The Nuremberg Laws also banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and people of German or related blood, and their wording was used to define who was a Jew in later persecution.
  • Jewish businesses were 'Aryanised' by forced sale or confiscation, transferring their property to non-Jewish Germans at low prices.
  • Many German Jews emigrated in these years, although the restrictions on emigrants' property and the difficulty of obtaining visas made flight hard for many.

Kristallnacht and the failure of emigration, 1938

Violence against Jews escalated in November 1938, and the international effort to find refuge for Jewish refugees failed to match the scale of the crisis.

  • Kristallnacht, the Night of Broken Glass, took place on 9 and 10 November 1938 after the shooting of the German diplomat Ernst vom Rath in Paris by Herschel Grynszpan, a young Jewish refugee whose family had been expelled to Poland; Goebbels used the news to launch the pogrom.
  • During the pogrom, Nazi supporters and the SA destroyed over 250 synagogues and damaged more than a thousand, wrecked thousands of Jewish shops and homes, and killed about 90 Jews; Jews were then fined a collective penalty of one billion marks.
  • About 30,000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to the concentration camps of Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen, and many were released only after agreeing to emigrate.
  • The Evian Conference of July 1938, called by the United States to discuss refugees, was attended by 32 countries, but few agreed to admit more refugees.
  • Many countries, including the United States and Britain, kept strict limits on immigration, and the quotas for Jewish refugees were not filled in many years.
  • The Kindertransport carried about 10,000 mostly Jewish children from Germany, Austria and Czechoslovakia to Britain between late 1938 and the outbreak of war.

Ghettos and war, 1939-1941

After the invasion of Poland, the Nazis confined Jews in ghettos and used forced labour and mass shootings in the occupied east.

  • Following the invasion of Poland in 1939, Jews in occupied Poland were forced to wear an identifying badge, and the yellow star was imposed in Germany from September 1941; property was seized throughout the occupied territories.
  • Ghettos were set up in Polish cities, the largest being the Warsaw ghetto, sealed in November 1940, holding about 400,000 people, and the Łódź ghetto, sealed in 1940.
  • Conditions in the ghettos were marked by starvation, disease and overcrowding, and the Nazi authorities used forced labour in factories and on construction projects.
  • The Einsatzgruppen, mobile killing units of the SS and police, followed the German army into the Soviet Union from June 1941 and carried out mass shootings of Jews, Roma and political commissars.
  • At Babi Yar near Kiev, about 33,000 Jews were shot on 29 and 30 September 1941, one of the largest single massacres of the war.
  • The Nazi regime extended the persecution to Roma, people with disabilities and Soviet prisoners of war, who were also murdered in large numbers.

The Wannsee Conference and the decision for genocide

The murder of Jews in the occupied east was systematised in 1941 and 1942, and the Wannsee Conference set out the plan for the deportation and murder of European Jewry.

  • Historians date the decision for the systematic murder of the Jews to 1941, though no written order from Hitler survives; it was carried out through the Einsatzgruppen, deportations and death camps.
  • The Wannsee Conference, held on 20 January 1942 near Berlin and chaired by Reinhard Heydrich, coordinated the deportation of Jews from across Europe to camps in the east.
  • The plan was called the Final Solution of the Jewish Question, and the minutes of the conference record the Jewish population of each European country, about eleven million in all, including countries outside German control.
  • Gas vans were first used to murder Jews at Chelmno in December 1941, and the Operation Reinhard camps of Belzec, Sobibor and Treblinka began operating in 1942.
  • Between 1942 and 1944 most of the Jews deported from occupied Europe were killed at Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Sobibor, Belzec, Chelmno and Majdanek.
  • Historians have debated the role of Hitler's orders and the initiative of local officials, but agree that the genocide was a deliberate policy carried out by the state and its agencies.

Jewish resistance, bystanders, rescuers and collaborators

Jews resisted the persecution in the ghettos and the camps, although the scale of armed resistance was limited by the German control of weapons and information.

  • The Warsaw ghetto uprising began on 19 April 1943 when Jewish fighters resisted the German attempt to deport the remaining inhabitants, and the fighting lasted until May 1943.
  • Armed resistance also took place at the Treblinka camp in August 1943 and at Sobibor in October 1943, where prisoners killed guards and escaped.
  • Jewish partisan groups fought in the forests of Eastern Europe, and the Jewish partisans of the Bielski group rescued families from the ghettos.
  • Many Jews resisted in quieter ways, including the smuggling of food and arms into the ghettos, the keeping of diaries and the maintaining of schools and cultural life.
  • Collaboration took the form of police units, local administrators and volunteers who helped round up and deport Jews in occupied countries, including the French police and the Dutch and Lithuanian authorities.
  • Many bystanders did nothing to stop the persecution, and in some countries neighbours profited from the seizure of Jewish property.
  • Rescuers hid Jews, forged documents and smuggled people across borders, and Yad Vashem in Jerusalem honours non-Jewish rescuers as the Righteous Among the Nations.
  • Oskar Schindler, a German industrialist in Krakow, employed Jewish workers in his factory and saved about 1,200 people from deportation to Auschwitz.
  • Denmark's rescue of most of its Jewish community in October 1943, when over 7,000 were ferried to Sweden, showed that large-scale protection was possible where the population and the local authorities were willing to act.

Liberation and the trials

Soviet and Allied troops liberated the camps in 1944 and 1945, and the Nuremberg trials began the legal prosecution of the leaders responsible.

  • Soviet troops liberated the Majdanek camp in July 1944 and Auschwitz-Birkenau on 27 January 1945, where they found the remaining prisoners and evidence of the murders.
  • British and American forces liberated Bergen-Belsen and Dachau in April 1945, and their survivors were among the first witnesses to the camps' conditions.
  • The International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg, which sat from November 1945 to October 1946, tried 22 leading German figures, and its judgments condemned the persecution of Jews as a crime against humanity.
  • Later trials at Nuremberg and in other countries prosecuted doctors, judges, industrialists and camp staff, and Adolf Eichmann was tried in Jerusalem in 1961.

Other victims of Nazi racial policy

The regime's racial ideology also led to the persecution and murder of other groups.

  • A law of July 1933 allowed compulsory sterilisation of people with hereditary conditions, and about 400,000 people were sterilised by 1945.
  • The Aktion T4 programme, begun in 1939, murdered about 70,000 disabled patients in gas chambers by August 1941, when public protests including Bishop Galen's sermon led to its formal end, although killing continued secretly.
  • Roma and Sinti, treated as racially inferior, were deported, imprisoned and murdered, with estimates of the dead ranging from about 200,000 to 500,000.
  • Millions of Soviet prisoners of war died in German captivity from starvation, disease and execution, about three million of the roughly 5.7 million captured.
  • Homosexual men were arrested under Paragraph 175, Jehovah's Witnesses were persecuted for refusing military service and loyalty oaths, and Slavs in Poland were treated as a labour reserve, with their intelligentsia targeted.

The death camps and the scale of the murders

The camps were centres of killing run by the SS, and the Jewish victims came from every country the Germans controlled.

  • Auschwitz-Birkenau in occupied Poland combined a forced-labour camp with a gas chamber complex using Zyklon B; about 1.1 million people were murdered there, around 1 million of them Jews, and arriving prisoners were selected on the ramp for labour or immediate death.
  • Treblinka, Belzec and Sobibor were pure killing centres in which the great majority of arrivals were gassed within hours; Treblinka alone accounted for over 800,000 deaths.
  • In 1944 about 430,000 Hungarian Jews were deported to Auschwitz in under two months, once Germany had occupied Hungary in March, and most were murdered on arrival.
  • Jews in the occupied cities were ruled by Jewish Councils (Judenräte) that the Germans forced to administer the ghettos and select people for deportation, a role about which survivors and historians still disagree.
  • As Soviet forces advanced in late 1944 and 1945, the SS forced prisoners on death marches into Germany, in which tens of thousands died of exhaustion, cold and shooting.
  • National rates of loss varied: about 90 per cent of Polish Jews and a similar share of the Jews of the Baltic states were killed, about three-quarters of Dutch Jews (among them the diarist Anne Frank), and about a quarter of French Jews.
  • Allied governments knew of the mass killings by late 1942; the Allies declared in December 1942 that they would punish those responsible, but did not bomb Auschwitz or the railways leading to it.

Assessment: the Holocaust and the wider European response

Judgements of the Holocaust focus on its scale and its planning, and on the failure of most states and societies to act to prevent or stop it.

  • Scale: about six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust, and the murders were carried out across occupied Europe, with about three million Jews from Poland among the victims.
  • Planning: the Holocaust was a deliberate, state-organised genocide that developed over several years, with decisions taken at each stage of the war.
  • Failure: Allied governments received reports of the murders from 1942 and were slow to act, and the Evian Conference and Allied immigration quotas limited the rescue of refugees before the war.
  • Achievement: rescuers and resistance groups saved many Jews, and the Nuremberg trials established the principle that crimes against humanity could be prosecuted.
  • Contemporary view: many Europeans in the 1930s and 1940s saw the persecution as a domestic German matter or ignored it, and few governments treated it as a priority.
  • Later view: historians have debated the reasons for the Allied response, the role of the bystanders and the balance between intentions and circumstances in the decision for genocide.

Key terms

Anti-Semitism
Hostility towards and discrimination against Jews, which the Nazi regime turned into a racial doctrine and state policy.
The Holocaust
The systematic murder of about six million European Jews by the Nazi regime and its collaborators between 1941 and 1945.
Herrenvolk
The Nazi idea of a master race of Germanic people, used to justify racial hierarchy and the persecution of Jews and others.
Nuremberg Laws
The laws of 15 September 1935 that defined Jews by descent, removed their citizenship and banned marriage with people of German blood.
Kristallnacht
The pogrom of 9 and 10 November 1938, when Nazi supporters destroyed synagogues and Jewish property across Germany and Austria.
Einsatzgruppen
Mobile SS and police units that followed the German army into the Soviet Union and carried out mass shootings of Jews and others from June 1941.
Wannsee Conference
The meeting of 20 January 1942 near Berlin that coordinated the deportation and murder of European Jews under the Final Solution.
Ghetto
A section of a city in which Jews were confined under Nazi rule, often sealed off from the outside world and used for forced labour.
Final Solution
The Nazi term for the plan to murder all the Jews of Europe, set out at Wannsee in January 1942.
Aryanisation
The forced transfer of Jewish businesses and property to non-Jewish owners at low prices.
Judenrat
A Jewish Council set up by the Germans in a ghetto to carry out their orders.
Righteous Among the Nations
The title given by Yad Vashem in Jerusalem to non-Jews who risked their lives to save Jews.
