Blitzkrieg and the war in Poland, 1939

Blitzkrieg, or lightning war, combined fast armoured thrusts with air power and was first used on a large scale against Poland in September 1939.

  • Blitzkrieg relied on concentrated armoured divisions, close air support from dive-bombers such as the Junkers Ju 87 Stuka, and rapid breakthroughs that encircled enemy forces.
  • Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and the Polish army was defeated within about five weeks, with Warsaw surrendering at the end of September.
  • The Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on 17 September 1939 under the terms of the Nazi-Soviet Pact, and the two powers divided the country.
  • Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939, but their armies made no major attack on the Western Front during the Phoney War.
  • The Phoney War lasted from September 1939 until the German offensive in the west in May 1940.
  • Germany invaded Denmark and Norway on 9 April 1940; Denmark fell within hours, and Norway was conquered by June despite a failed Anglo-French landing, giving Germany Atlantic naval bases and Swedish iron-ore supplies.

The fall of France and the Battle of Britain, 1940

In May and June 1940 German armoured forces defeated France in six weeks, and the Battle of Britain that followed was the first major campaign fought entirely in the air.

  • The German offensive in the west began on 10 May 1940, with an advance through the Netherlands, Belgium and the Ardennes, which bypassed the Maginot Line.
  • German armoured divisions reached the Channel coast at Abbeville by 20 May, cutting off the British and French forces in the north.
  • The Battle of Britain was fought from July to October 1940, as the Luftwaffe attacked RAF airfields and aircraft factories in an attempt to gain air superiority.
  • The RAF's Fighter Command, using Spitfire and Hurricane fighters and the radar early-warning Chain Home system, defeated the attempt to win air superiority.
  • Germany then switched to bombing London and other cities in the Blitz, which began on 7 September 1940 and continued into 1941.
  • Germany's invasion plan for Britain, Operation Sea Lion, was postponed in September 1940 and then abandoned.

Two fronts: Barbarossa, Pearl Harbor and the war in the Pacific, 1941-1945

The German invasion of the Soviet Union and the Japanese attack on the United States in December 1941 turned the war into a global conflict.

  • Operation Barbarossa, the German invasion of the Soviet Union, began on 22 June 1941 along a front of about 3,000 kilometres from the Baltic to the Black Sea.
  • German forces advanced rapidly in the first months and reached the outskirts of Moscow and Leningrad by late 1941, but were halted by the winter and Soviet counter-attacks in December.
  • The Japanese attack on the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 brought the United States into the war, and Germany and Italy declared war on the United States on 11 December.
  • The Lend-Lease Act of March 1941 allowed the United States to supply Britain, and later the Soviet Union, with weapons and equipment without immediate payment.
  • Japan's attacks also took the Philippines, Malaya and Singapore, forcing Britain to surrender Singapore in February 1942.
  • Japan had been at war with China since 1937 and occupied French Indochina in 1940 and 1941; the United States responded with an oil embargo, which pushed Japan towards the attack of December 1941.
  • Within months Japan captured Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore, the Dutch East Indies, the Philippines and Burma.
  • The American victory at Midway in June 1942, in which four Japanese carriers were sunk, ended Japan's advance, and the Guadalcanal campaign began the American counter-offensive.
  • The United States used an island-hopping strategy, backed by submarines and carrier air power, to approach Japan, and later bombed Japanese cities with B-29 bombers.
  • Japan surrendered after the atomic bombs and the Soviet declaration of war on 8 August 1945; the formal surrender was signed on 2 September 1945.

The Axis alliance

The Axis powers of Germany, Italy and Japan were linked by formal treaties, but their war aims were not coordinated and they often acted separately.

  • The Pact of Steel of 22 May 1939 bound Germany and Italy into a military alliance, although Italy was not ready for war when it entered in June 1940.
  • The Tripartite Pact of 27 September 1940 added Japan to the Axis, recognising the leadership of Germany and Italy in Europe and of Japan in East Asia.
  • Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Slovakia later joined the Axis, and Finland fought alongside Germany against the Soviet Union.
  • Italy's defeats in Greece and North Africa in 1940 and 1941 forced Germany to intervene in the Balkans and in North Africa, drawing forces away from Barbarossa.
  • The Axis powers did not share a joint strategy, and Germany and Japan did not coordinate their attacks on the Soviet Union and the United States.

The Grand Alliance and its tensions

Britain, the Soviet Union and the United States formed a wartime coalition in which cooperation over strategy and the future of Europe was often strained.

  • Churchill and President Franklin Roosevelt issued the Atlantic Charter in August 1941, setting out shared principles including self-determination and the restoration of sovereignty to those deprived of it.
  • The Soviet Union joined the Allied coalition after Barbarossa, and Stalin repeatedly called for a second front in Western Europe to relieve pressure on the Red Army.
  • The Tehran Conference of November and December 1943 brought Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin together for the first time, and it set the plan for the invasion of France.
  • The Yalta Conference of February 1945 agreed the occupation zones of Germany, the principle of free elections in liberated Eastern Europe and the entry of the Soviet Union into the war against Japan.
  • Disputes over the timing of the second front, the future of Poland and the Soviet occupation of Eastern Europe foreshadowed the tensions of the Cold War.

Turning points and the road to D-Day, 1942-1945

The Allied victories of 1942 and 1943 reversed the Axis advance, and the Normandy landings of June 1944 opened the way to the defeat of Germany.

  • The Second Battle of El Alamein, from October to November 1942, defeated the Axis armies in North Africa under Erwin Rommel, and the Allies landed in Morocco and Algeria the same month.
  • The Battle of Stalingrad ended in February 1943 with the surrender of the German Sixth Army, and the Battle of Kursk in July 1943 became the last major German offensive in the east.
  • The Allies invaded Sicily in July 1943 and Italy's government surrendered in September 1943, but Germany occupied most of northern and central Italy.
  • The Normandy landings (D-Day) began on 6 June 1944, and Allied forces broke out of the beachheads and liberated Paris on 25 August 1944.
  • The Soviet Red Army advanced into Poland and the Balkans in 1944, and the Soviet offensive on Berlin began in April 1945.
  • Hitler killed himself in Berlin on 30 April 1945, and German forces surrendered in May 1945, with the surrender at Reims on 7 May and in Berlin on 8 May.

The technology of warfare

Technological developments shaped the war on land, at sea and in the air, and the decisive use of the atomic bomb ended the war against Japan.

  • Tanks: the German Panzer divisions used radio communication and combined armour with motorised infantry, while the Soviet T-34 and the American Sherman were produced in large numbers.
  • Aircraft: strategic bombing by RAF Bomber Command and the US Army Air Forces attacked German cities, causing fires and civilian deaths, with the bombing of Hamburg in July 1943 and Dresden in February 1945 among the most devastating raids.
  • Radar: the British Chain Home system and later airborne and ship-borne radar gave early warning of attacks and helped locate U-boats and aircraft.
  • Codebreaking: at Bletchley Park the British decrypted German Enigma messages, and the resulting intelligence, known as Ultra, was used in the Battle of the Atlantic and the North African campaign.
  • Submarines and convoys: German U-boats sank large numbers of Allied merchant ships in the Atlantic, but the convoy system and escort groups reduced losses from 1943.
  • Rockets and jets: Germany's V-1 flying bombs were launched against London from June 1944 and its V-2 rockets from September 1944, and later against Antwerp, and the Messerschmitt Me 262 jet fighter entered service in 1944, too late to change the outcome.
  • Atomic weapons: the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, and Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945; the bombs had been developed in the Manhattan Project, and the first test took place in New Mexico on 16 July 1945.

Key personality: Winston Churchill

Churchill served as Prime Minister from May 1940 and led Britain through the war and the negotiations of the Grand Alliance.

  • Born in 1874 at Blenheim Palace into an aristocratic family, he entered Parliament in 1900 as a Conservative, joined the Liberals in 1904, was First Lord of the Admiralty from 1911 to 1915, and was Chancellor of the Exchequer from 1924 to 1929, then spent the 1930s out of office warning against Hitler.
  • He became First Lord of the Admiralty again in September 1939 and succeeded Neville Chamberlain as Prime Minister on 10 May 1940.
  • His speech of 4 June 1940 included the phrase 'we shall fight on the beaches', and his leadership in the Battle of Britain made him a symbol of national resistance.
  • He was a participant in the Allied conferences at Tehran and Yalta and negotiated closely with Roosevelt and Stalin, and he lost office in July 1945 after the general election.
  • Contemporaries, including some of his own party, criticised his part in the Gallipoli campaign in 1915 and his return to the gold standard in 1925, but his wartime leadership won wide admiration.
  • Historians have praised his resolve in 1940 and his strategic leadership, while criticising the bombing of German cities such as Dresden, the diversion of forces to the Mediterranean and Greece in 1941, and his attitude to colonial peoples, as in the Bengal famine of 1943.
  • He became Prime Minister at the head of a coalition including Labour leaders Clement Attlee and Ernest Bevin, and he also acted as Minister of Defence.
  • His speeches on the radio and in Parliament, including 'blood, toil, tears and sweat' (13 May 1940) and 'their finest hour' (18 June 1940), sustained morale after Dunkirk and during the Blitz.
  • Between 26 May and 4 June 1940, Operation Dynamo evacuated over 330,000 Allied troops from Dunkirk.
  • He built a close relationship with Roosevelt, meeting him at Placentia Bay, Washington, Casablanca (January 1943, where unconditional surrender was announced) and Quebec.
  • In October 1944 he met Stalin in Moscow and proposed the 'percentages agreement' on spheres of influence in the Balkans, which has been seen as a sign of wartime realism or of cynicism.
  • He attended the Potsdam Conference in July 1945 until the Labour election victory replaced him with Attlee.

The technology of warfare between the wars

The 1920s and 1930s produced the ideas and machines that were used in the Second World War.

  • Armoured warfare theory by British writers such as J.F.C. Fuller and Basil Liddell Hart, and by the German general Heinz Guderian in Achtung - Panzer! (1937), influenced the formation of German Panzer divisions from 1935.
  • Air power theorists such as Giulio Douhet argued that bombers would decide wars; the bombing of Guernica in April 1937 by the German Condor Legion in the Spanish Civil War, which tested aircraft and tactics, shocked world opinion.
  • Britain developed radar from 1935, when Robert Watson-Watt showed that radio waves could detect aircraft, and Chain Home stations were built along the east and south coasts from 1936.
  • Fighter design improved with the Hawker Hurricane (first flown 1935) and the Supermarine Spitfire (1936), and the Messerschmitt Bf 109 entered service in Germany.
  • Polish mathematicians broke the early Enigma machine in the early 1930s and passed their methods to Britain and France in July 1939.
  • Frank Whittle patented the jet engine in 1930, and the first German jet aircraft, the Heinkel He 178, flew in August 1939.
  • Rocket research by Wernher von Braun for the German army began in the early 1930s and led to the V-2 at Peenemünde.

Assessment: Churchill and the wartime alliances

Judgements of Churchill and the alliances weigh the British war effort and the coalition's success against its divisions and its costs.

  • Achievement: Britain's refusal to negotiate a peace in 1940 kept open a base from which the Allies could later invade Europe.
  • Limit: British strategy relied heavily on American resources after 1941, and the British Empire's position in Asia was lost in 1942.
  • Achievement: the Grand Alliance defeated the Axis powers and produced the United Nations as a post-war organisation.
  • Limit: the alliance concealed deep divisions over Eastern Europe, and these became the Cold War once Germany was defeated.
  • Contemporary view: many British people saw Churchill as the embodiment of national resistance, while critics on the left and in his own party questioned his judgement.
  • Later view: historians still debate how far Churchill's leadership was essential, with some stressing his role in 1940 and others emphasising the decisive contribution of Soviet and American forces.

Key terms

Blitzkrieg
Lightning war: a German method of combined armoured thrusts, air support and rapid encirclement that defeated Poland and France in 1939 and 1940.
Axis powers
The alliance of Germany, Italy and Japan, linked by the Pact of Steel of 1939 and the Tripartite Pact of 1940.
Grand Alliance
The coalition of Britain, the Soviet Union and the United States against the Axis powers, formed after 1941.
Lend-Lease
The American programme of 1941 that supplied Britain and later the Soviet Union with weapons and equipment without immediate payment.
Enigma
The German electro-mechanical cipher machine whose messages were decrypted by British codebreakers at Bletchley Park.
Ultra
The British name for intelligence obtained by decrypting German Enigma messages, used to guide Allied operations.
Strategic bombing
The bombing of an enemy's cities, industry and transport to undermine its economy and civilian morale, as practised by the Allies against Germany.
Operation Barbarossa
The German invasion of the Soviet Union begun on 22 June 1941.
Phoney War
The period from September 1939 to May 1940 in which Britain and France were at war with Germany but made no major attack in the west.
Atlantic Charter
The declaration of August 1941 by Churchill and Roosevelt of shared war aims including self-determination.
Radar
A system using radio waves to detect aircraft and ships, which gave Britain early warning during the Battle of Britain.
Manhattan Project
The American programme that developed the atomic bomb, first tested in New Mexico on 16 July 1945.
