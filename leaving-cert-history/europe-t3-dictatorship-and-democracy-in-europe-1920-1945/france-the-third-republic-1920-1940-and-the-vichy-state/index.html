Post-war France, the Bloc National and political instability, 1919-1932

France emerged from the First World War victorious but exhausted, and its governments were dominated by parties that demanded firm treatment of Germany.

  • The Third Republic, founded in 1870, was governed by an elected Chamber of Deputies and a President with limited powers.
  • The November 1919 election returned the Bloc National, a coalition of conservative, centre and Catholic parties, which governed until 1924.
  • France had lost about 1.4 million dead in the war, and many villages and farms in the north-east were devastated by fighting.
  • Reconstruction of the devastated areas was financed by German reparations, which the government expected Germany to pay in full.
  • Raymond Poincaré, Prime Minister in 1922 to 1924, pursued a hard line towards Germany and ordered the occupation of the Ruhr in January 1923.
  • The Ruhr occupation damaged French relations with Britain and the United States, which opposed it, and it ended in 1925 without securing full reparations.
  • The Cartel des Gauches, a coalition of Radicals and Socialists, won the May 1924 election and Édouard Herriot formed the first government of the Cartel.
  • The Cartel fell in April 1925 after a run on the franc and a financial crisis, and governments changed frequently in the following years.
  • Poincaré returned as Prime Minister in July 1926 with a national unity government and stabilised the franc in practice by the end of 1926 through tax increases and cuts in spending, and the Poincaré franc was fixed in law in 1928 at about a fifth of its pre-war value.
  • The Radical Party, a centre-left party with strong rural support, was the largest force in most governments of the 1920s and early 1930s.
  • The Chamber of Deputies held frequent votes of no confidence, and the government's ministers were often replaced after only a few months in office.
  • The constitution gave the President a largely ceremonial role, so policy was decided by the Prime Minister and the parliamentary majority.

The Depression in France and the Stavisky affair of 1934

France's economy was less affected by the Depression at first than Britain or Germany, but the slump arrived later and was prolonged by deflationary policy.

  • The French economy was built on small family farms and small businesses, which cushioned the country against the immediate effects of the Wall Street Crash.
  • Industrial production fell sharply from 1931, and unemployment rose more slowly than in Germany or Britain but remained a serious problem.
  • Governments kept the franc at its gold value until 1936, so deflation was used to defend the currency and lower prices, which hurt industry and farming.
  • The franc was devalued by the Popular Front government in October 1936, after which exports recovered slightly.
  • Heavy spending on the Maginot Line, a chain of fortifications along the German border built between 1930 and 1938, was one of the largest state investments of the period.
  • Alexandre Stavisky, a financier with political protection, was found shot dead in January 1934 after the collapse of his fraudulent bond sales; the police called it suicide but the left and right both suspected that he had been silenced.
  • The scandal forced the resignation of the Radical Prime Minister Camille Chautemps in January 1934, and right-wing leagues used it to attack the republic as corrupt.
  • On 6 February 1934 the leagues, including the Croix-de-Feu, Action française and the Solidarité française, marched on the Chamber of Deputies, and police opened fire; about fifteen people were killed and over a thousand injured.
  • The Daladier government resigned after the riots, and a national government under Gaston Doumergue was formed to restore order.
  • The left responded with a general strike and mass demonstration on 12 February 1934, and in July 1935 Socialists, Communists and Radicals united in the Popular Front against the threat of fascism, after the Comintern had reversed the Communists' hostility to other left parties.

The leagues and the Popular Front, 1934-1938

The Popular Front won the 1936 election and introduced major social reforms, but its economic policies failed and it split over Spain.

  • In the elections of April and May 1936 the Popular Front coalition won a majority, and Léon Blum, a Socialist and the first Jew to hold the post, became Prime Minister in June 1936.
  • The Matignon Agreements of June 1936, negotiated between employers and unions under Blum's chairmanship, gave wage rises of about 7 to 15 per cent, union recognition, collective bargaining and elected workers' delegates, following a wave of sit-down strikes; laws that month brought in the 40-hour week and two weeks of paid holiday.
  • Around two million workers took part in strikes and factory occupations in May and June 1936, and the agreement was designed to end them.
  • The Popular Front's programme of nationalising armaments and the railways, and of spending on public works, was limited by financial pressure and capital flight.
  • Blum's government adopted a policy of non-intervention in the Spanish Civil War in August 1936, which was opposed by many on the left but supported by Britain, and it dissolved the far-right leagues in June 1936.
  • Blum paused the reforms in February 1937 and resigned in June 1937 when the Senate refused him emergency financial powers; after a brief second ministry in March and April 1938, Édouard Daladier became Prime Minister and the coalition ended, and in November 1938 his decrees undid the 40-hour week.
  • The Croix-de-Feu, led by Colonel François de la Rocque, was a veterans' league that attracted hundreds of thousands of members, and it became the Parti Social Français after it was dissolved in 1936.
  • Action française, led by Charles Maurras, was a monarchist and anti-Semitic movement which opposed the republic from before 1914.
  • Historians disagree on how far the leagues threatened democracy: René Rémond saw them as part of France's traditional right, while Zeev Sternhell argued that fascist ideas had deep roots in France.
  • Economic hardship and fear of war also split French opinion into left and right camps, and Communists and Socialists gained from the unity forged after February 1934.

Defence policy and the fall of France, 1939-1940

France declared war on Germany in September 1939, but the defeat of its army in June 1940 ended the Third Republic and brought the Vichy state into being.

  • Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939 after the invasion of Poland, and the French army, commanded by General Maurice Gamelin, remained largely inactive during the Phoney War.
  • The German invasion of France and the Low Countries began on 10 May 1940, and German armoured divisions broke through the Ardennes to reach the Channel coast by late May.
  • The British Expeditionary Force and many French troops were evacuated from Dunkirk between 26 May and 4 June 1940.
  • Paris, declared an open city, was occupied on 14 June 1940; the government left for Bordeaux, Paul Reynaud resigned on 16 June, and Pétain became head of government and asked Germany for an armistice, before the government settled in the spa town of Vichy in July.
  • On 22 June 1940 an armistice was signed at Compiègne in the same railway carriage used for the 1918 armistice, dividing France into an occupied zone and an unoccupied zone.
  • On 10 July 1940 the National Assembly, the Chamber and Senate meeting together at Vichy, voted by 569 to 80, with about 20 abstentions, to give full powers to Marshal Philippe Pétain to draw up a new constitution, which ended the Third Republic.
  • The Maginot Line, named for the War Minister André Maginot, guarded the Franco-German border with deep forts, but it did not extend along the Belgian frontier or through the Ardennes.
  • French generals including Gamelin and Pétain believed that the Ardennes forest was difficult terrain for tanks, and the main French and British forces moved into Belgium in May 1940 on the Dyle plan.
  • France had as many tanks as Germany, but it spread them among infantry units instead of concentrating them in armoured divisions as Germany did.
  • German armoured units crossed the Meuse at Sedan on 13 and 14 May 1940 and reached the Channel coast by 20 May, cutting off the northern armies.
  • Contemporary explanations blamed political decadence under the Third Republic, but historians such as Ernest May stress poor intelligence, slow command and German skill rather than moral failure.
  • France lost about 90,000 killed in six weeks and well over a million men were taken prisoner.

The National Revolution, the Vichy state and the occupation

Pétain's regime rejected republican and secular values in favour of authoritarian conservatism, which it called the National Revolution.

  • The regime renamed the state the État français and replaced the republican motto of Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité with Travail, Famille, Patrie (Work, Family, Fatherland).
  • Pétain's National Revolution blamed the Third Republic and the left for the defeat, and promoted traditional values, the Catholic Church and rural life.
  • Pierre Laval, dismissed in December 1940, returned as head of government in April 1942 and pursued a policy of close collaboration with Germany.
  • Pétain met Hitler at Montoire in October 1940 and announced that he had entered a path of collaboration with Germany.
  • The Vichy regime was an authoritarian state that relied on a veterans' organisation, the Legion of French Combatants, rather than a mass party, and it ended elections, banned strikes and dissolved the trade unions in favour of a Labour Charter in 1941.
  • Vichy's own press and radio supported the National Revolution, and its youth organisations aimed to instil discipline and loyalty to Pétain.
  • Under the armistice Germany occupied the north and the Atlantic coast, while the south stayed 'free' under Vichy until German troops occupied it in November 1942, after the Allied landings in North Africa.
  • France had to pay the costs of the German occupation, set at 400 million francs a day, and its food and industrial output were directed to Germany.
  • The British attack on the French fleet at Mers-el-Kébir on 3 July 1940 turned many Vichy supporters against Britain.
  • Food rationing, shortages and a black market affected daily life, and about 1.5 million French prisoners of war remained in Germany for years.
  • Vichy's sense of 'neutral' independence ended in 1942, as Laval's return led to closer co-operation, and the Germans occupied the whole country.
  • Collaboration took forms from economic cooperation and propaganda to active fascist parties such as the Parti Populaire Français of Jacques Doriot and the Rassemblement National Populaire of Marcel Déat.

Collaboration, persecution and the Milice

The Vichy government took active steps against Jews, political opponents and resisters, and created a paramilitary force that fought against the Resistance.

  • Vichy passed the first anti-Jewish statute in October 1940, without German demand, excluding Jews from public office and many professions, and in 1942 it agreed to hand over foreign Jews in the unoccupied zone for deportation; about 76,000 Jews were deported from France and about 2,500 survived.
  • On 16 and 17 July 1942 French police arrested about 13,000 Jews in Paris, including about 4,000 children, and many were held at the Vélodrome d'Hiver cycling stadium before being sent via the Drancy camp to Auschwitz.
  • The Milice, a paramilitary force founded in January 1943, hunted members of the Resistance and carried out reprisals against civilians.
  • The Service du Travail Obligatoire (compulsory labour service), introduced in February 1943 after a voluntary scheme had failed, sent young French men to work in Germany, and many went into hiding in the maquis to avoid it.
  • Vichy's police and administration carried out many of the arrests and deportations, and collaboration extended to the economy, with supplies sent to Germany.
  • Historians since Robert Paxton's Vichy France of 1972 have argued that Vichy's collaboration was active and not merely a shield against harsher German policy.

The Resistance and de Gaulle

Resistance to Vichy and the German occupation grew from a small group in London to an organised movement inside France by 1943.

  • Charles de Gaulle, a brigadier-general who had been Under-Secretary for War in Reynaud's government in June 1940, broadcast an appeal from London on 18 June 1940 calling on the French to continue the fight.
  • De Gaulle formed the Free French movement in London and was recognised by Britain, though his relations with Britain and the United States were often strained.
  • Jean Moulin united the main resistance groups in 1943 and presided over the first meeting of the National Council of the Resistance in May 1943.
  • Resistance activity included the production of underground newspapers, sabotage of railways, intelligence gathering and armed attacks on German troops by the maquis, groups of guerrillas in rural areas.
  • The Resistance was divided among Gaullists, Communists and other political groups, and the Communist-led Francs-Tireurs et Partisans, formed in 1942, became one of the largest armed groups.
  • Resistance groups carried out executions of collaborators and informers after the liberation, and these reprisals were a source of political tension.
  • Early groups such as Combat, Libération and Franc-Tireur ran underground newspapers and helped Allied airmen to escape, and Communists joined the armed struggle after Germany invaded the USSR in June 1941.
  • The maquis grew as young men avoided compulsory labour service in 1943 and 1944, and Resistance groups helped the Allied invasion by cutting railways.
  • The Germans took hostages and shot them in reprisal for attacks, and on 10 June 1944 SS troops massacred more than 600 civilians at Oradour-sur-Glane.
  • The village of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, where Protestant villagers led by Pastor André Trocmé sheltered thousands of Jewish refugees, became a symbol of civilian rescue.
  • After the war the myth of a nation united in Resistance, promoted by de Gaulle, was challenged from the 1970s by historians and by the film The Sorrow and the Pity (1969-71), and in 1995 President Chirac acknowledged the state's responsibility for the Vél' d'Hiv round-up.

Liberation and the end of Vichy

Allied landings in Normandy and southern France in 1944 led to the liberation of France and the collapse of Vichy.

  • The Allied invasion of Normandy began on 6 June 1944, and the Allies landed in southern France on 15 August 1944.
  • Paris was liberated on 25 August 1944 after an uprising by the Resistance and the arrival of General Leclerc's Free French armoured division, and on 26 August de Gaulle led a procession down the Champs-Élysées as head of the Provisional Government.
  • Pétain was taken to Germany in 1944 and was later tried in France, where he was sentenced to death in 1945; the sentence was commuted to life imprisonment by de Gaulle.
  • Laval was tried and shot in October 1945 after being convicted of treason.
  • An ordinance of April 1944 gave women the vote, first used in 1945, and the Fourth Republic was established after a new constitution was approved in 1946.

Assessment: the Third Republic and Vichy

Judgements of the Third Republic weigh its stability and democratic survival against its inability to deal with the Depression or to resist invasion.

  • Achievement: the Third Republic survived the Depression and the leagues' challenge in 1934 without a coup, and its institutions held until 1940.
  • Limit: frequent government changes and the failure to modernise the army left France unprepared for the German attack of 1940.
  • Achievement: the Popular Front brought paid holidays and shorter working hours to French workers in 1936.
  • Limit: the Popular Front's economic policies failed to reduce unemployment, and it could not agree on how to respond to the Spanish Civil War.
  • Contemporary view: many French conservatives in 1940 saw the armistice and Pétain's rule as a necessary response to military defeat and the failures of the republic.
  • Later view: historians stress that Vichy was not forced into collaboration, and that its anti-Jewish policies went beyond German demands in some areas.

Key terms

Democracy
A system of government in which power rests with elected representatives or with the people, protected by free elections and the rule of law.
Collaboration
Cooperation with an occupying power, in France the policy of the Vichy regime of working with Germany after 1940.
Resistance
Organised opposition to an occupying power or a regime, including sabotage, intelligence gathering, propaganda and armed action.
Popular Front
The alliance of Socialists, Communists and Radicals in France, which won the 1936 election under Léon Blum.
Vichy regime
The authoritarian state set up by Pétain in July 1940 in the unoccupied south of France, which collaborated with Germany.
National Revolution
The name given by the Vichy regime to its programme of authoritarian and conservative reform.
Milice
The paramilitary force founded by Vichy in January 1943 to hunt members of the Resistance.
Maginot Line
The chain of fortifications built along the German border between 1929 and 1938 to protect France from attack.
Cartel des Gauches
The 1924 coalition of Radicals and Socialists under Édouard Herriot, which fell in 1925 after a financial crisis.
Leagues
Right-wing paramilitary movements of the 1920s and 1930s, such as the Croix-de-Feu and Action française, that attacked the parliamentary republic.
Matignon Agreements
The June 1936 settlement between employers and unions under Blum that ended the sit-down strikes and gave pay rises and collective bargaining.
Free French
The movement led by General de Gaulle from London from June 1940 that continued the war against Germany and Vichy.
