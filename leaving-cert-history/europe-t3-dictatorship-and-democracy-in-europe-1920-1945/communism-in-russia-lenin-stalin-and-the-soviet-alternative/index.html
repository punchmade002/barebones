Lenin's regime, the civil war and the New Economic Policy, 1917-1924

After the Bolsheviks seized power in 1917 (see the notes on the Russian Revolution), Lenin built a one-party state and fought a civil war that shaped the Soviet system.

  • The Cheka, the Bolshevik secret police set up in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky, used arrest and execution against opponents, including the Socialist Revolutionaries and Mensheviks, and after the attempt on Lenin it carried out the Red Terror from September 1918.
  • The Russian Civil War (1918-1921, with fighting continuing in the Far East until 1922) pitted the Red Army, led by Leon Trotsky as War Commissar, against the White armies and foreign intervention forces.
  • War Communism (1918-1921) nationalised industry and banks, forced peasants to hand over grain at fixed low prices, and rationed food to towns.
  • Peasant revolts and famine in 1920-1921, combined with the grain requisitioning, showed how unpopular the policy had become.
  • The Bolsheviks banned rival parties during the civil war, and the Communist Party became the only legal party in the Soviet state.
  • Lenin survived an assassination attempt in August 1918, after which the Cheka's use of terror was greatly expanded.
  • In March 1921 sailors and workers at the naval base of Kronstadt revolted against Bolshevik rule, demanding free elections and an end to the monopoly of power; the Red Army crushed the revolt.
  • At the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921 the Bolsheviks adopted the New Economic Policy (NEP), which allowed small private trade, peasant sales of surplus grain and some private small-scale industry.
  • The NEP restored food supplies and the economy recovered by the mid-1920s, while large industry and banking remained under state control.
  • The NEP was seen by many Communists as a retreat, and critics on the left argued that it produced a new class of traders and wealthy peasants called Nepmen and kulaks.
  • The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was formally created on 30 December 1922, bringing together Russia, Ukraine, Belarus and the Transcaucasian republics.
  • Lenin suffered strokes in 1922 and 1923, the first in May 1922, and died on 21 January 1924, leaving the party without a clear successor.

The struggle for succession, 1924-1929

Stalin's rise as General Secretary, and his defeat of rival factions, decided who would lead the Soviet Union in the years of the Five-Year Plans.

  • Stalin had become General Secretary of the Communist Party in April 1922, a post that gave him control of party appointments.
  • Lenin's Testament, dictated in late 1922 and early 1923, criticised Stalin's rudeness and called for his removal from the post of General Secretary; the Central Committee did not publish it at the time.
  • Zinoviev and Kamenev first allied with Stalin against Trotsky in 1923-25; Trotsky's Left Opposition argued for rapid industrialisation and world revolution, and when Zinoviev and Kamenev joined him in the United Opposition of 1926 it was defeated, with its leaders expelled from the party by December 1927.
  • Trotsky was expelled from the party in 1927, sent to internal exile in Kazakhstan in 1928 and exiled from the Soviet Union in 1929; Stalin's agent killed him in Mexico in 1940.
  • The Right Opposition led by Nikolai Bukharin, Alexei Rykov and Mikhail Tomsky favoured the NEP and gradual growth, and was defeated in 1928-29 after Stalin, who had used it to beat the Left, turned to the Left's policy of rapid industrialisation and forced grain collection.
  • Stalin's defeat of rivals in the party allowed him to launch the First Five-Year Plan, and the Trotsky and Bukharin factions were both removed from political influence by 1929.

The Five-Year Plans and industrialisation

The First Five-Year Plan (1928-1932) set targets for heavy industry, and later plans extended the drive for rapid industrial growth.

  • Gosplan, the state planning agency, drew up the First Five-Year Plan, which was launched in October 1928 and aimed to transform the Soviet Union into an industrial power.
  • Targets were set for coal, steel, electricity, oil and machinery, with heavy industry given priority over consumer goods.
  • The steel city of Magnitogorsk was built in the southern Urals, becoming a symbol of the drive for industrialisation and of the labour that went into it.
  • Alexei Stakhanov, a Donbas coal miner, was reported to have cut 102 tonnes of coal in a single shift in August 1935, and the Stakhanovite movement was used to promote higher output, though the feat was prepared with help and made norms harder for ordinary workers.
  • Steel output rose from about 4 million tonnes in 1928 to about 18 million in 1940, but official statistics were inflated and the costs were very high: wages fell, housing was overcrowded, quality was poor and forced labour was used on projects such as the White Sea-Baltic Canal (1931-33).
  • The Second Five-Year Plan (1933-1937) and the Third (1938-1941) continued the emphasis on heavy industry, and the Soviet economy was militarised by the late 1930s.

Collectivisation and the famine of 1932-1933

Collectivisation aimed to bring peasant agriculture under state control, and its enforcement caused famine, mass deaths and the destruction of the kulaks as a class.

  • Collectivisation was launched in late 1929, when Stalin announced the 'liquidation of the kulaks as a class', and peasants were forced to join collective farms (kolkhozy) in which land and livestock were pooled and run to state orders.
  • Kulaks, the richer peasants, were described as enemies of the state and were deported or killed in a campaign of dekulakisation.
  • Peasants resisted by slaughtering livestock, and grain requisitions were set at levels that left villages short of food.
  • The harvest failures and forced requisitions of 1932 and 1933 produced a famine that killed several million people, mainly in Ukraine, the North Caucasus and Kazakhstan.
  • The Soviet authorities denied the famine and restricted movement to prevent starving peasants from reaching the towns.
  • Collectivisation transformed agriculture into a system of state-controlled production, but it produced lower output of meat and grain for several years.

Soviet society and the Soviet alternative as seen from the West

The regime pursued a programme of social change that promoted literacy, changed family life and sought to replace religion with atheism and Marxist culture.

  • Literacy campaigns in the 1920s and 1930s raised reading levels greatly, and education was expanded to train workers and engineers.
  • The Bolsheviks legalised abortion in 1920 and promoted equality for women in work and politics, but abortion was restricted again in 1936.
  • The League of the Militant Godless, founded in 1925, campaigned against religion, and many churches and monasteries were closed or destroyed.
  • Socialist realism became the official style of art and literature in 1932, and the Union of Soviet Writers, formed in 1934, enforced its standards.
  • Cultural life was controlled by the party, and artists and writers who did not follow the official line faced censorship, expulsion or arrest.
  • The Soviet system promoted a new Soviet man and woman and a culture of collective achievement, expressed in parades, posters and public monuments.
  • Western visitors toured Soviet factories and collective farms in the early 1930s, and some returned with favourable reports about rapid industrial growth and the absence of unemployment.
  • The Webbs, Sidney and Beatrice, British socialists, published Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation? in 1935, which presented the Soviet system as a model for social planning.
  • The Depression made the planned economy look more attractive to some socialists, intellectuals and trade unionists in Britain, France and the United States.
  • Critics in the West, including many liberal and conservative commentators, pointed to the famine, the show trials and the absence of political freedom.
  • Contemporary opinion was therefore divided, and the Soviet alternative was a source of debate between supporters of planning and defenders of liberal democracy.
  • The Soviet Union's diplomatic recognition by the United States in 1933 and its membership of the League of Nations from 1934 gave the regime greater international standing.

The Stalinist state in war, 1941-1945

The German invasion of June 1941 tested the Soviet system, and Stalin's wartime leadership combined the mobilisation of resources with terror against soldiers and civilians.

  • Germany invaded the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941 in Operation Barbarossa, and the Red Army suffered heavy losses in the first months of the war.
  • Stalin chaired the State Defence Committee set up on 30 June 1941 to direct the war economy, and became Supreme Commander in August 1941; he stayed in Moscow during the German advance in the autumn of 1941.
  • Factories were moved east to the Urals and Siberia in 1941, and output of tanks, aircraft and munitions rose sharply by 1942.
  • The Battle of Stalingrad, from August 1942 to February 1943, marked the turning point on the Eastern Front, when the German Sixth Army was encircled and surrendered.
  • Stalin used the Red Army and the NKVD to enforce discipline, including executions of deserters and punishment of soldiers who had been captured.
  • The Soviet Union's victory in the war was used to strengthen Stalin's personal authority, and he was given the rank of Generalissimo in 1945.
  • Order 227 of July 1942, 'Not one step back', led to blocking detachments behind the front line and penal battalions for those who retreated.
  • Soviet industry relocated about 1,500 factories east of the Urals in 1941 and 1942, and Lend-Lease supplies from Britain and the United States, including trucks and food, supported the Red Army from 1941.
  • Soviet victories at Stalingrad (February 1943) and Kursk (July 1943) turned the war, and the Red Army reached Berlin in April-May 1945.
  • Soviet deaths in the war are usually estimated at about 27 million, military and civilian combined, far more than any other combatant.
  • Prisoners of war who returned from German captivity and whole nationalities such as the Chechens and Crimean Tatars, accused of collaboration, were punished by imprisonment or deportation.
  • In 1943 Stalin eased pressure on the Orthodox Church and appealed to Russian nationalism, calling the conflict the Great Patriotic War, to win popular support.
  • In 1945 Stalin joined Churchill and Roosevelt at Yalta and Potsdam, and the USSR emerged as a superpower with control over Eastern Europe.

Key personality: Vladimir Ilyich Lenin

Lenin led the Bolsheviks from 1903 to 1924, and he created the institutions that Stalin inherited.

  • Born Vladimir Ulyanov in 1870 at Simbirsk, he had an elder brother who was executed in 1887 for plotting against Tsar Alexander III.
  • He was a participant in the 1917 revolutions, returning to Russia in April 1917 and issuing the April Theses calling for the transfer of power to the soviets.
  • He led the Bolsheviks in seizing power in October 1917 and presided over the Council of People's Commissars, the first Soviet government.
  • As leader in the civil war, he authorised the use of terror, the grain requisitioning of War Communism and the suppression of the Kronstadt revolt.
  • Contemporaries in the party saw him as the authority on Marxist theory and tactics, and his death in 1924 was marked by an embalmed body displayed in a mausoleum in Moscow.
  • Historians judge him as the architect of the one-party state, though some stress that the NEP shows a pragmatic side, and others argue that his methods made the later terror possible.

Key personality: Josef Stalin

Stalin's leadership from the late 1920s to his death in 1953 transformed the Soviet economy and society, and he built a cult of personality around himself.

  • Born Ioseb Dzhugashvili in 1878 at Gori in Georgia, he was trained for the priesthood before joining the revolutionary movement and taking the name Stalin.
  • As General Secretary he built a network of loyal appointees, and by 1929 he had defeated his main rivals in the party.
  • He directed the Five-Year Plans and collectivisation, and his decisions set the timetable and targets for the whole economy.
  • Stalin was the central figure of Soviet propaganda, with his portraits, statues and sayings displayed across the country, and cities such as Stalingrad were named after him.
  • Western admirers in the 1930s, such as the Webbs, praised his industrial achievements, while the famine, the show trials and the Terror provoked criticism from liberal and socialist opponents.
  • After Stalin's death, Nikita Khrushchev denounced the cult of personality in a secret speech to the 20th Party Congress in February 1956, and historians have since debated how far his policies reflected ideology, paranoia or the pressures of industrialisation.

The cult of Stalin, the Stalinist state and Soviet foreign policy

A personality cult presented Stalin as the infallible successor of Lenin, and a system of terror and control gave the state its totalitarian character.

  • The cult began with the celebrations of Stalin's fiftieth birthday in December 1929, when Pravda hailed him as Lenin's heir and the 'leader' of the party.
  • Official history was rewritten: the History of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (Short Course) of 1938 gave Stalin the central role in the revolution, while Trotsky and other rivals were written out and removed from photographs.
  • Towns, factories and streets were named after Stalin, and poets, artists and film-makers portrayed him as the wise father of the people.
  • The 1936 Constitution, called the Stalin Constitution, promised universal suffrage, a secret ballot and civil rights, while the Communist Party's monopoly of power was left untouched.
  • The NKVD and the Gulag network of forced-labour camps held millions of prisoners, many of them peasants and political prisoners, who built canals, railways and mines.
  • Internal passports introduced in 1932 and 1933 and the registration of residence controlled movement, tying peasants to the countryside and keeping 'undesirables' out of the cities.
  • The Treaty of Rapallo with Germany (1922) restored relations between the two outcast states and allowed secret military co-operation on German rearmament on Soviet soil.
  • The Comintern, founded in 1919, directed foreign communist parties; its policy of denouncing Social Democrats as 'social fascists' split the German left in the early 1930s.
  • After Hitler took power, the USSR joined the League of Nations (1934), signed pacts with France and Czechoslovakia (1935) and backed the Popular Front policy of co-operation against fascism.
  • The USSR gave aid to the Spanish Republic in the Civil War from 1936, but was excluded from the Munich conference in 1938.
  • The Nazi-Soviet Pact of August 1939 gave Stalin time and a buffer: the Red Army entered eastern Poland on 17 September 1939, the USSR attacked Finland in the Winter War of November 1939, and the Baltic states were annexed in 1940.
  • The pact left the USSR unready when Germany attacked in June 1941, despite many intelligence warnings which Stalin discounted.

Assessment: the Soviet transformation under Lenin and Stalin

Judgements of the Soviet system balance its industrial and military achievements against the human costs of its methods.

  • Achievement: by 1941 the Soviet Union had built a large heavy industry base, and its war production helped defeat Germany.
  • Limit: collectivisation destroyed much of the peasant farming system and caused a famine that killed millions in 1932 and 1933.
  • Achievement: literacy and education expanded greatly, and women gained formal equality in work and the professions.
  • Limit: the cult of personality, the suppression of rival parties and the arrests of opponents removed any independent political life.
  • Contemporary view: many Western socialists and intellectuals saw the Soviet Union as a model for planning during the Depression.
  • Later view: historians after 1945 stressed the costs and the totalitarian features of the system, while more recent work has examined its social change and the views of ordinary people.

Key terms

Communism
A political and economic system based on Marxist ideas, in which the state owns the means of production and a single party leads the working class.
Collectivisation
The forced combination of individual peasant farms into large state-controlled collective farms, carried out in the Soviet Union from 1929.
Personality cult
The promotion of a leader as an infallible and heroic figure, shown through propaganda, rituals and his image in public life.
Totalitarianism
A system in which the state seeks to control every aspect of public and private life through a single party, secret police and propaganda.
War Communism
The Bolshevik economic policy of 1918 to 1921 that nationalised industry, requisitioned grain and rationed food during the civil war.
New Economic Policy
The partial return to private trade and small-scale enterprise adopted by the Bolsheviks in 1921 after the civil war.
Five-Year Plan
A state plan setting production targets for a five-year period, first used in the Soviet Union from 1928 to drive industrialisation.
Kulak
A term used by the Soviet regime for richer peasants, who were targeted for deportation or destruction during collectivisation.
Cheka
The Bolshevik secret police founded in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky, the forerunner of the NKVD and KGB.
Gulag
The Soviet network of forced-labour camps, run by the NKVD, that held millions of prisoners.
Socialist realism
The official Soviet style in art and literature from 1932, requiring works to glorify the socialist state and its workers.
Stakhanovite movement
The campaign launched in 1935, after the miner Alexei Stakhanov's reported record, to raise productivity by rewarding exceptional workers.
