The post-war boom and slump, 1919-1922

A brief post-war boom gave way to a sharp slump, and the government's cuts in public spending deepened unemployment in the early 1920s.

  • Demand for goods surged in 1919 and 1920 as wartime controls were removed, and prices rose sharply before collapsing in 1920 and 1921.
  • Unemployment rose above two million by mid-1921 as export markets were lost and industries shed workers.
  • The Geddes Axe of 1922, named after Sir Eric Geddes and his committee of economy, cut public spending on education, housing and the armed services.
  • Unemployment insurance, first introduced in 1911, was extended in 1920 to cover most workers, but benefits were limited and payments ended for many after a set period.
  • The coal industry was returned to private ownership in 1921, and the owners' attempt to cut miners' wages led to a lock-out; on 'Black Friday', 15 April 1921, the railwaymen and transport workers of the Triple Alliance refused to strike in support, leaving the miners to be defeated.

Return to gold and the General Strike, 1925-1926

The decision to return to the gold standard at the pre-war rate made British exports more expensive, and its effect on the coal industry led to the General Strike of 1926.

  • In April 1925 the Chancellor of the Exchequer, Winston Churchill, returned sterling to the gold standard at the pre-war parity of about 4.86 dollars to the pound.
  • John Maynard Keynes criticised the decision in his 1925 pamphlet The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill, arguing that the overvalued pound would make British exports uncompetitive and force down wages.
  • The coal owners announced wage cuts and longer hours, and the miners refused, leading to a lock-out in May 1926.
  • The Trades Union Congress called a General Strike from 4 to 12 May 1926 in support of the miners, and it was the only nationwide strike in British history.
  • The government mobilised volunteers and used the Organisation for the Maintenance of Supplies to keep essential services running, and the TUC called off the strike after nine days.
  • The miners stayed out for several months and were forced back to work on worse terms later in 1926.

Regional unemployment and the staple industries

Unemployment was concentrated in the older industrial regions of Wales, the north of England and central Scotland, while the south-east prospered.

  • The staple industries of coal, cotton, shipbuilding, iron and steel had been the basis of the Victorian economy, and they declined sharply in the inter-war years.
  • The coal industry lost jobs steadily from the mid-1920s, and South Wales, Durham and Northumberland were the most heavily affected coalfields.
  • The cotton industry of Lancashire lost export markets to Japan and India and suffered a long decline, with many mills closed by the 1930s.
  • Shipbuilding on the Tyne, Wear and Clyde was badly hit by the collapse of world trade and the lack of new orders, and many yards closed in the 1930s.
  • Unemployment in the distressed areas could remain above 30 per cent in many towns in the early 1930s, and higher still in places such as Jarrow and Merthyr Tydfil, while the south-east and the Midlands had relatively low unemployment.
  • Workers in the depressed regions often moved to the new industries of the south and Midlands, but many could not afford the cost of relocation or of housing.

The crisis of 1929-1931 and the National Government

The Wall Street Crash and the collapse of exports forced the Labour government to confront a budget crisis, leading to the formation of a National Government and the abandonment of gold.

  • Ramsay MacDonald led the second Labour government of 1929 to 1931, which lacked a majority and struggled with rising unemployment.
  • The May Committee on National Expenditure, reporting in July 1931, forecast a budget deficit of about 120 million pounds and recommended cuts of 20 per cent in unemployment benefit and public sector pay.
  • The Cabinet split over a 10 per cent cut in unemployment benefit, and MacDonald formed a National Government in August 1931 with Conservative and Liberal support; the Labour Party expelled him for doing so.
  • On 21 September 1931 Britain abandoned the gold standard, and sterling fell sharply against other currencies, which helped exports and allowed interest rates to fall.
  • The National Government won a landslide in the October 1931 general election, with Labour reduced to a small group of MPs.
  • The Invergordon mutiny of 15 to 16 September 1931, a few days before Britain left gold, in which sailors of the Atlantic Fleet protested against pay cuts, showed how the economy measures were resented in the armed forces.

Protectionism and devaluation, 1932-1934

The National Government moved away from free trade, introducing tariffs and imperial preference, and used cheap money to support recovery.

  • The Import Duties Act of February 1932 introduced a general tariff of 10 per cent on most imports, ending Britain's long commitment to free trade.
  • The Ottawa Agreements of 1932 gave preferential trade terms to the British Empire, and Britain kept a system of imperial preference until the 1970s.
  • Bank Rate was cut to 2 per cent in June 1932 and stayed there until 1939, a policy of cheap money that reduced the cost of borrowing and encouraged house building.
  • Protection helped industries such as steel and motor vehicles, but it raised the cost of food and materials for consumers.
  • The Ottawa system strengthened trade with the Dominions and colonies, but it reduced trade with the United States and with Europe.

Unemployment relief, hunger marches and the means test

The unemployed depended on insurance and then on means-tested assistance, and the most visible protest against the system came from hunger marches organised by local communities.

  • The National Unemployed Workers' Movement (NUWM), founded in 1921 by Communist-linked activists, organised protests, marches and resistance to evictions.
  • Hunger marches from the depressed regions to London, notably in 1922, 1932, 1934 and 1936, drew attention to the poverty of the unemployed; the National Hunger March of 1932 presented a petition with a million signatures to Parliament, which refused to receive it.
  • A household means test was introduced in November 1931, when benefit after 26 weeks passed to local Public Assistance Committees, and the Unemployment Act of 1934 created the national Unemployment Assistance Board to pay long-term unemployed people according to the same test of household income and savings.
  • The means test was unpopular because it looked at the income of all family members, and many families lost their assistance when a son or daughter earned money.
  • Local councils that paid relief in the depressed areas sometimes refused to apply the means test, and when the Board's new scales cut payments in early 1935, protests in South Wales and elsewhere forced the government to suspend them under the Standstill Act.
  • Before 1931 insured workers drew insurance benefit for limited periods and others relied on Poor Law relief, which the Local Government Act of 1929 transferred to local council Public Assistance Committees in 1930; both were blamed for stigma and hardship.

Special Areas, housing and new industries

The government's policies for the depressed regions were limited, but house building and new light industries in the south and Midlands created jobs and transformed the economy.

  • The Special Areas (Development and Improvement) Act of 1934 designated the worst-hit areas and offered grants for new industries and public works there.
  • The Special Areas included South Wales, Tyneside, West Cumberland and parts of Scotland such as Clydeside, and the Commissioners appointed under the Act had limited funds.
  • About four million houses were built in Britain between the wars, roughly two-thirds of them privately built for owner-occupiers, with about a million council houses built by local authorities under acts such as Addison (1919) and Wheatley (1924), and the largest growth of suburban housing was in the south and Midlands.
  • The Housing Act of 1930 gave local authorities money to clear slums and build council housing, and the Housing Act of 1935 extended subsidies for slum clearance.
  • New light industries such as motor vehicles, electrical goods and consumer goods grew rapidly in the south-east and Midlands, with factories built on the outskirts of London and Birmingham.
  • The regional policy of the 1930s was modest in scale, and the depressed regions did not recover fully until rearmament created new demand at the end of the decade.

Key personality: John Maynard Keynes

Keynes was an economist and government adviser whose ideas about public spending and demand became the basis of modern economic policy.

  • Born in Cambridge in 1883, he was a Fellow of King's College, Cambridge, and worked in the Treasury during the First World War.
  • He was a participant in the Versailles peace negotiations as a Treasury representative, resigned in protest at the reparations settlement, and wrote The Economic Consequences of the Peace in 1919, which condemned the settlement as unworkable.
  • In 1929 he co-wrote with Hubert Henderson the Liberal Party pamphlet We Can Conquer Unemployment, which proposed large public works programmes to reduce unemployment.
  • He argued in his General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money of 1936 that government spending could restore demand and employment when private investment was low.
  • Contemporaries disagreed about his ideas: the Treasury and the Bank of England were sceptical of deficit spending, while many Labour and Liberal politicians welcomed his arguments.
  • Historians judge his work as a turning point in economic thought, though they debate how far the 1930s recovery depended on his policies rather than on rearmament and cheap money.
  • In How to Pay for the War (1940) he proposed compulsory saving to prevent wartime inflation, and he returned to the Treasury as an adviser.
  • He led the British delegation to the Bretton Woods conference in 1944, where he argued for an international clearing union and a more flexible monetary system, and the agreement set up the International Monetary Fund and World Bank.
  • Created Baron Keynes in 1942, he died in April 1946 after negotiating the American loan to Britain.

Two Britains: living standards and social conditions

The interwar years brought rising living standards for those in work and persistent poverty in the depressed regions.

  • Real wages rose for those in work, prices fell in the early 1930s, and a growing number of families in the south-east and Midlands bought houses, cars, radios and electrical goods.
  • Trade unions were weakened after 1926, and the Trade Disputes Act of 1927 banned sympathetic strikes and made union members opt in to the political levy.
  • In the depressed areas, long unemployment brought malnutrition, overcrowded housing and high infant mortality, as shown by J.B. Priestley's English Journey (1934), George Orwell's The Road to Wigan Pier (1937) and the Pilgrim Trust's survey Men Without Work (1938).
  • Registered unemployment peaked at about three million, over 20 per cent of insured workers, in 1932 and 1933, and fell with recovery and rearmament after 1936 without ever dropping below a million before 1940.
  • Rearmament from 1936 increased demand for steel, shipbuilding and engineering and eased the problems of some depressed regions.
  • Mass leisure grew: cinema going, the BBC (founded 1922), dance halls and cheap holidays gave a shared culture, although families on the dole could afford few of these.

Assessment: how effectively the problems were handled

Judgements of the government's response weigh its success in restoring growth and stability against its failure to reduce unemployment in the depressed regions.

  • Achievement: the abandonment of gold and cheap money helped recovery in the south-east, and industrial production grew after 1932.
  • Limit: unemployment stayed above one million for most of the 1930s, and the depressed regions suffered long-term decline.
  • Achievement: house building and social provision expanded, and the National Government kept the parliamentary system working during a severe economic crisis.
  • Limit: the means test and the Special Areas policy were judged inadequate by critics on the left, who saw them as punishing the poor rather than creating jobs.
  • Contemporary view: the National Government's supporters argued that its policies had protected the currency and prevented a worse slump.
  • Later view: historians disagree about whether the 1930s recovery was caused mainly by monetary policy or by the expansion of consumer demand in the south-east.

Key terms

The Depression
The worldwide economic slump that followed the Wall Street Crash of 1929, marked by falling output, bank failures and mass unemployment.
Protectionism
The use of tariffs, quotas or preferential trade agreements to protect domestic industries from foreign competition.
Democracy
A system of government in which power rests with elected representatives or with the people, protected by free elections and the rule of law.
Staple industries
The traditional heavy industries of coal, cotton, shipbuilding, iron and steel on which Britain's Victorian economy was built.
Gold standard
A monetary system in which a currency's value is fixed to a quantity of gold, which Britain returned to in 1925 and abandoned in 1931.
Means test
A test of household income and savings used to decide whether an unemployed person's family qualified for state assistance, introduced in Britain in 1931.
Imperial preference
Trade arrangements that gave goods from the British Empire lower tariffs than goods from other countries, agreed at Ottawa in 1932.
Special Areas
The depressed regions of Britain designated in 1934 for government grants to attract new industries and public works.
Distressed areas
The regions of high unemployment around the declining staple industries, such as South Wales, Tyneside and Clydeside, later called the Special Areas.
Black Friday
15 April 1921, when railwaymen and transport workers of the Triple Alliance refused to strike in support of the miners.
Cheap money
The policy of keeping interest rates low, with Bank Rate at 2 per cent from June 1932, to encourage borrowing, investment and house building.
Hunger march
A march of unemployed people to London, often organised by the NUWM, to draw attention to unemployment and the means test.
