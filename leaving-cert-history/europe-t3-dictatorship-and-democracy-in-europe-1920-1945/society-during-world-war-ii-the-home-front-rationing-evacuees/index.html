Evacuation in Britain, 1939-1940

Britain's government planned the evacuation of children and vulnerable adults from cities in anticipation of air attack, and the first movement took place in the first days of September 1939.

  • Operation Pied Piper began on 1 September 1939, moving schoolchildren, mothers with young children, the disabled and teachers from London, Birmingham, Liverpool and other cities to the countryside.
  • About 1.5 million people, most of them children, were evacuated in the first few days of the operation, and the government organised billeting of evacuees in private homes.
  • Evacuees were received unevenly in the countryside: some host families welcomed them, while others were shocked by poverty, poor hygiene and unfamiliar behaviour.
  • Many evacuees returned to the cities in late 1939 and early 1940 during the Phoney War, when the expected bombing did not come.
  • The evacuation exposed the gap between urban poverty and rural comfort, and its social findings were used by reformers to argue for improved health and education services.
  • Evacuation was repeated in the summer of 1940, when more children were sent away after the Blitz began and the fall of France threatened invasion.

Rationing and the Ministry of Food

Rationing spread the burden of shortages evenly, and the government's food policy aimed to keep the population healthy on limited supplies.

  • Rationing of bacon, butter and sugar began in January 1940, and rationing of meat followed in March 1940, with a fixed weekly allowance for each person.
  • The Ministry of Food, led by Lord Woolton from 1940, controlled food imports, set prices and organised the supply of basic foods such as National Loaf and National Dried Milk.
  • Clothing was rationed from June 1941 and furniture from 1943, and Utility schemes set minimum standards of design and quality for goods sold at controlled prices.
  • The Dig for Victory campaign encouraged families to grow vegetables in gardens, allotments and parks, and the Land Army recruited women to work on farms.
  • Shortages and rationing were accepted by most of the population because they were seen as fair; the government published ration allowances in the press to support confidence.
  • Rationing continued after the war, and meat was not freed from control until 1954.

The Blitz, the Home Guard and daily life in wartime Britain

The German bombing of British cities from September 1940 tested civilian morale and produced new forms of civil defence and public information.

  • The Blitz began on 7 September 1940 with the bombing of London's docks, and London was bombed on 57 consecutive nights between September and November 1940.
  • Anderson shelters, issued free to households from early 1939, Morrison shelters (from 1941) and Underground stations were used as shelters during raids.
  • The Local Defence Volunteers, renamed the Home Guard in July 1940, were formed in May 1940 to guard against invasion and to support civil defence.
  • Air Raid Precautions wardens, fire watchers and ambulance crews were among the civilian volunteers who worked during the raids.
  • The Ministry of Information and the BBC encouraged resilience through broadcasts and posters, and the Mass Observation surveys recorded public opinion and morale.
  • The Blitz of 1940 and 1941 killed about 40,000 civilians in Britain, over half of them in London, and other cities such as Coventry (14 November 1940), Liverpool, Birmingham and Belfast were heavily bombed.
  • The blackout, enforced from September 1939, raised road deaths sharply in the first winter, and air raid wardens enforced it.
  • Children were also sent abroad, and the sinking of the liner City of Benares by a U-boat in September 1940 with the loss of many evacuee children ended the overseas scheme.
  • Ernest Bevin, as Minister of Labour from 1940, directed workers into war industries, and from December 1943 some conscripts were sent to the coal mines as Bevin Boys.
  • The Women's Land Army put women to work on farms, and Land Girls helped to raise home food production.
  • Radio was central to civilian life, with news bulletins, Churchill's broadcasts, and morale shows such as ITMA, and the cinema newsreels and Ministry of Information films provided official messages.
  • V-1 flying bombs and V-2 rockets killed thousands of civilians in southern England in 1944 and 1945.
  • The shared experience of danger and rationing encouraged support for government planning and the welfare reforms after 1945.

Women and the Home Front; the Beveridge Report

The war brought women into industry, the armed services and civil defence, and the Beveridge Report of 1942 proposed a welfare state that became a post-war programme.

  • Women took up war work in factories making aircraft and munitions, and by 1943 women made up a large share of the industrial workforce.
  • The Auxiliary Territorial Service (ATS), the Women's Auxiliary Air Force (WAAF) and the Women's Royal Naval Service (WRNS) recruited women into the armed services.
  • The National Service (No. 2) Act of December 1941 required single women aged 20 to 30 to register for war work, and some were conscripted into factories or the services.
  • The Women's Voluntary Service organised civil defence and welfare work, including the provision of meals and clothing for bombed-out families.
  • The Beveridge Report, published in December 1942, identified five giants to be defeated: want, disease, ignorance, squalor and idleness.
  • The report proposed a comprehensive system of national insurance, family allowances and a national health service; it sold hundreds of thousands of copies, and its recommendations were largely carried out by the National Insurance Act of 1946 and the National Health Service of 1948 under the Labour government, along with the Education Act of 1944 and family allowances from 1945.

Germany's Home Front and foreign labour

Germany's home front was shaped by the economic demands of war, the use of foreign workers and prisoners, and the strain of bombing from 1942.

  • Germany entered the war with a shortage of labour, and the regime was reluctant to ask German women to work in factories in large numbers before 1942.
  • After Goebbels's call for total war in February 1943, the regime extended the mobilisation of women and the workforce, though women's employment rose less than in Britain.
  • By 1944 about seven million foreign workers and prisoners of war from occupied Europe were used in German industry and agriculture, many living in camps under harsh conditions.
  • Forced labourers from the Soviet Union, known as Ostarbeiter, were treated more harshly than workers from western Europe, and many died from disease, overwork and mistreatment.
  • Rationing in Germany was tied to the status of the population: German civilians received higher rations than foreign workers and occupied populations.
  • Allied bombing of German cities caused heavy civilian deaths and the destruction of housing, as in the firestorm at Hamburg in July 1943, and children and families were evacuated from the cities to the countryside in a scheme called the Kinderlandverschickung.

Refugees and displaced people

War and occupation produced millions of refugees and displaced people, and their treatment reflected the policies of each government.

  • In May and June 1940 millions of French people fled from the advancing German army, crowding the roads of northern and central France.
  • Britain interned about 27,000 German, Austrian and Italian nationals as enemy aliens in 1940, including many refugees from Nazi persecution, and some were sent to the Isle of Man or overseas; over 800 died when the Arandora Star was sunk by a U-boat in July 1940.
  • The British government gave refuge to some Jewish refugees and to governments in exile, including the Polish, Norwegian and Dutch governments, which were based in London.
  • Displaced persons in Europe at the end of the war included forced labourers, prisoners of war, concentration camp survivors and people who had fled the Red Army.
  • Relief and repatriation of displaced people were organised after 1945 by the Allied military authorities and the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA), set up in 1943.

Collaboration in occupied Europe

Many occupied peoples cooperated with the German occupiers, from the work of administrations to the active participation in persecution and deportations.

  • Vidkun Quisling, leader of the Norwegian fascist party Nasjonal Samling, headed a collaborationist government in Norway from February 1942, and his name became a term for traitors.
  • In the Netherlands the National Socialist Movement (NSB) under Anton Mussert cooperated with German authorities, and Dutch police helped round up Jews for deportation.
  • In Belgium the Rexist movement led by Léon Degrelle supported the German occupation, and Degrelle later fought in the Waffen-SS on the Eastern Front.
  • Vichy France under Pétain and Laval collaborated actively in the deportation of Jews and the supply of labour to Germany, and the Milice carried out reprisals against resisters.
  • Collaboration took many forms, including economic cooperation, the supply of workers and the provision of police and volunteers for German units on the Eastern Front.

Resistance in occupied Europe

Resistance movements ranged from underground newspapers and sabotage to armed partisan warfare and the rescue of persecuted people.

  • The Polish Home Army (Armia Krajowa), formed in 1942, was the largest underground army in occupied Europe and fought in the Warsaw Uprising of August to October 1944.
  • The Yugoslav partisans led by Josip Broz Tito fought German, Italian and Croatian forces from 1941, and they gained control of much of the country by 1945.
  • The White Rose group of Munich students distributed leaflets against the Nazi regime, and Hans and Sophie Scholl were executed in February 1943.
  • In Denmark, after a warning in early October 1943 of a German round-up, ordinary Danes helped to ferry most of the Jewish community across the Øresund to Sweden, more than 7,000 people in total.
  • Italian partisans fought the German occupation and the Salò Republic from 1943 to 1945, and the Italian resistance became a major political force after liberation.
  • The French Resistance and the Polish Home Army were both linked to Allied intelligence and special operations, which provided weapons, radio equipment and training.

Civilians in occupied France

For French civilians, occupation meant shortage, fear of reprisal and divided loyalties.

  • Ration cards were issued by age and work category, and bread, meat, fat and fuel were short; many city dwellers made trips to the countryside to buy food on the black market.
  • German soldiers requisitioned buildings and food, and curfews, identity checks and the taking of hostages made daily life uneasy.
  • Allied bombing of French industry and railways before and after D-Day killed tens of thousands of French civilians.
  • Families coped with the absence of around one and a half million prisoners of war in Germany, and many women had to take over family farms and businesses.
  • Attitudes changed over time: most people accepted Vichy at first, but the compulsory labour service and the German occupation of the south in November 1942 turned many against Vichy and the Germans.
  • Many civilians were passive 'attentistes' waiting on events, and only a minority joined the Resistance or actively collaborated.

Assessment: civilian experience and the moral record

Judgements of wartime society balance the solidarity and sacrifice of civilians against the wide scale of collaboration, and the limits of resistance.

  • Achievement: British society sustained the war effort through rationing, evacuation and civil defence, and the Beveridge Report reshaped post-war social policy.
  • Limit: Germany's home front relied on foreign labour and forced labourers, and its civilians were protected by systematic exploitation of occupied peoples.
  • Achievement: resistance movements in occupied Europe kept political opposition alive and provided the bases for post-war governments in France, Italy and Yugoslavia.
  • Limit: most people in occupied Europe neither resisted nor collaborated actively, and resistance remained a small minority of the population until 1944.
  • Contemporary view: the British public generally accepted the sacrifices of the war and supported the welfare plans that followed.
  • Later view: historians have examined the extent of collaboration and the myth of a unified resistance, with research since the 1970s showing a more complex and divided record.

Key terms

Collaboration
Cooperation with an occupying power, including the supply of labour, police help and political support for the occupation.
Resistance
Organised opposition to an occupying power or a regime, including sabotage, intelligence gathering, propaganda and armed action.
Home Front
The civilian population of a country at war and the work, rationing, evacuation and bombing that shaped its daily life.
Rationing
The official allocation of limited supplies of food, fuel and clothing to each person or household during wartime.
Evacuation
The movement of children and vulnerable civilians from cities to safer areas, as organised in Britain under Operation Pied Piper in 1939.
Refugee
A person who has fled from their home country or region because of war, persecution or occupation.
Quisling
A term for a collaborator with an occupying power, taken from Vidkun Quisling, who headed a collaborationist government in Norway from 1942.
Operation Pied Piper
The government evacuation of schoolchildren and others from British cities that began on 1 September 1939.
Beveridge Report
The 1942 report that proposed a national insurance system and named five giants of want, disease, ignorance, squalor and idleness.
Blackout
The enforced darkening of lights at night to hinder enemy bombers.
Ostarbeiter
Forced labourers from the Soviet Union and eastern Europe who worked in Germany, treated more harshly than western workers.
Maquis
Rural bands of French resisters, swollen by young men avoiding forced labour in Germany in 1943 and 1944.
