Mussolini and the Church: the Lateran Treaties

The Lateran Treaties of 1929 ended the long dispute between the Italian state and the papacy and gave the Fascist regime Catholic support.

  • Since 1870 the popes had regarded themselves as prisoners in the Vatican, after the Italian state took Rome and ended the papal states.
  • The Lateran Treaties were signed on 11 February 1929 by Mussolini and Cardinal Pietro Gasparri, and they created the independent state of Vatican City.
  • The Italian state paid the Church compensation of 1.75 billion lire (750 million in cash and 1,000 million in government bonds) for the loss of the papal states, and the Church recognised the Kingdom of Italy with Rome as its capital.
  • Under the Concordat part of the treaties, Catholicism was recognised as the sole religion of the Italian state, and religious marriage was given civil effect.
  • Mussolini gained the support of the Catholic majority, and the Church's endorsement helped legitimise the regime in the eyes of many Italians.
  • The treaties were ratified in June 1929, and the Church gained religious teaching in state schools, state-paid priests and the recognition of Catholic marriage, in return for accepting the Italian state.

Disputes and the racial laws, 1931-1938

Relations between the regime and the Church worsened when the regime tried to control Catholic youth organisations, and the racial laws of 1938 caused further tension.

  • In 1931 the regime clashed with Catholic Action, the Church's lay organisation, over its youth and student groups, which the regime saw as rivals to the Fascist youth movement; Fascist squads attacked Catholic centres and the regime closed many of its branches, and in June Pius XI protested in the encyclical Non abbiamo bisogno.
  • An agreement of 1931 limited Catholic Action to religious, cultural and charitable activities, and it kept its youth groups out of politics and sport.
  • Apart from the 1931 encyclical, the Church's objections were mostly made in private diplomacy and in the Vatican's press, and Pope Pius XI rarely challenged Mussolini openly.
  • Mussolini's Manifesto of the Race, published in July 1938, defined the racial basis of Italian identity, and the racial laws of September to November 1938 excluded Jews from schools, the professions and the armed forces.
  • The Church opposed the racial laws in part, since they challenged the Catholic doctrine that all people shared a common humanity, but it did not break with the regime.
  • The Vatican's relationship with Italy was affected by the alliance with Germany, and the Church's independence was limited by the regime's pressure on the Catholic press and youth.

Hitler and the churches: the Concordat and the Reichskirche

Hitler signed a Concordat with the Vatican in 1933, but he also planned to bring the Protestant churches under Nazi control through a single Reich Church.

  • The Reich Concordat between Germany and the Holy See was signed on 20 July 1933 and was intended to protect Catholic institutions and schools in return for the Church's withdrawal from politics.
  • The Centre Party, the main Catholic party, dissolved itself on 5 July 1933 as the Concordat was being concluded, and Catholic organisations in Germany lost much of their political role.
  • The German Christians, a pro-Nazi movement within German Protestantism, won about two-thirds of the seats in the Protestant church elections of July 1933, and they called for a single Protestant Reich Church under Nazi control.
  • Ludwig Müller, a German Christian, was appointed Reich Bishop of the new Protestant Reich Church in September 1933, but his leadership was challenged within the churches.
  • The Reichskirche plan sought to remove the independence of the Protestant regional churches, to exclude Jewish Christians from church office, and to replace Christian doctrine with an ideology of race and national blood.
  • The Nazi regime violated the Concordat within a few years, closing Catholic schools and youth organisations, and arresting Catholic priests and religious.

The Confessing Church and Catholic protest

Opposition to the regime's control of the churches came from the Confessing Church among Protestants and from Catholic bishops and the papacy.

  • The Confessing Church was formed in opposition to the German Christians, and its founding Barmen Declaration of 31 May 1934 rejected the state's claim to control the Church's teaching.
  • Martin Niemöller, a Protestant pastor and former U-boat commander, led opposition to Nazi control, was arrested in 1937, and was held in concentration camps until 1945.
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, a Lutheran theologian, joined the Confessing Church's opposition and was later involved in the conspiracy against Hitler; he was executed at Flossenbürg in April 1945.
  • Pope Pius XI's encyclical Mit brennender Sorge (With Burning Anxiety) of 14 March 1937 criticised Nazi violations of the Concordat and the racial ideology, and it was read from pulpits in Catholic churches.
  • Clemens August von Galen, Bishop of Münster, preached sermons in July and August 1941 against the murder of people with disabilities under the euthanasia programme, and the programme was halted in August 1941.
  • The Church's protests were limited to defending its own institutions and to opposing the euthanasia programme, and the churches did not openly challenge the persecution of Jews on the same scale.

Radio in peace and war

Radio became a mass medium in the 1920s and 1930s, and governments used it to inform, entertain and persuade listeners.

  • The British Broadcasting Company was founded in 1922 and became the British Broadcasting Corporation in 1927 under John Reith as its director-general, with a public service remit.
  • The American networks NBC, founded in 1926, and CBS, founded in 1927, were commercially financed and competed for advertisers and audiences.
  • President Franklin Roosevelt began his fireside chats on the radio in March 1933, speaking directly to listeners about the New Deal and, later, the war.
  • In Britain the BBC Home Service carried news, music and government information during the war, and Churchill made radio broadcasts to the nation at key moments.
  • The American Forces Network broadcast entertainment and news to Allied troops in Britain and Europe from 1942, and it played American popular music to a wide audience.
  • The German government used radio to reach the population, and William Joyce, known as Lord Haw-Haw, broadcast propaganda from Germany to Britain from 1939.

Cinema in peace and war

Cinema was the most popular mass entertainment of the 1930s, and Hollywood dominated the world market, while British and European film industries sought to protect their own production.

  • The Warner Brothers film The Jazz Singer, released in 1927, introduced synchronised sound to mainstream cinema and led to the rapid spread of talking pictures.
  • The Hollywood studio system of the 1930s, dominated by major studios such as MGM, Paramount, Warner Brothers and Fox, produced and distributed most of the films shown in Britain and Europe.
  • The Cinematograph Films Act of 1927 required cinemas in Britain to show a quota of British films, which aimed to protect the domestic industry against American competition.
  • Newsreels such as Pathé and British Movietone News brought the events of the 1930s to cinema audiences in Britain, and Germany and Italy used newsreels for propaganda.
  • In Germany American films were restricted from the mid-1930s and in effect excluded by 1940, and German cinema was dominated by home-made films and newsreels.
  • During the war Hollywood and British studios made films to support the war effort, including morale films and documentaries, while the American government used films to explain the war to the public.

Key personality: Charlie Chaplin

Chaplin was a British-born comedian and film-maker whose Tramp character made him the most famous film star of the silent era, and whose later films criticised modern society and the dictators.

  • Born in London in 1889, he began on the stage as a child and was a member of a music hall troupe before he went to the United States in 1913 and joined the Keystone studio.
  • The Tramp character first appeared in 1914 and was developed in films such as The Kid (1921), The Gold Rush (1925) and City Lights (1931).
  • He was a participant in the Hollywood studio system, and he made Modern Times in 1936, a satire on industrial life and mechanisation, in which the Tramp is caught in the machinery of a factory.
  • In The Great Dictator, released in 1940 and his first full sound film, he played both a Jewish barber and Adenoid Hynkel, a parody of Adolf Hitler, ruler of Tomania, and it was a major commercial success in the United States; Chaplin, born four days before Hitler in April 1889, later said he would not have made it had he known the full horror of the camps.
  • Contemporaries admired his comic genius, but some American politicians and newspapers criticised his political views, and in the 1940s his leftist sympathies led to difficulties with the authorities.
  • Historians have judged Chaplin's work as a major achievement in silent and sound cinema, while debating the political content of his later films and the reasons for his departure from the United States in 1952.

Key personality: Bing Crosby

Crosby was an American singer and actor whose radio shows, records and films made him the most popular entertainer of his age, and whose songs were heard across the world during the war.

  • Born in Tacoma, Washington, in 1903, he sang with a jazz band before becoming a radio singer in the early 1930s, when he developed his relaxed crooning style with microphone technique.
  • He made hundreds of records and starred in films, including the Road series with Bob Hope from 1940, and he won the Academy Award for Best Actor for Going My Way (1944) at the 1945 ceremony.
  • His recording of White Christmas, written by Irving Berlin, was released in 1942 for the film Holiday Inn and became one of the best-selling songs of all time; its longing for home made it hugely popular with troops.
  • Crosby entertained Allied troops during the war through radio broadcasts and USO tours, including a tour of Britain and France in 1944, and his popularity with the forces was very high.
  • Contemporaries in the United States and Europe saw him as an embodiment of American easy-going popular culture, and his music spread the sounds of American radio into Europe.
  • Historians tend to present Crosby as a key figure in the development of the modern popular music industry, and his role in wartime morale is studied alongside that of other entertainers.

Popular culture in wartime and Americanisation in Europe

Governments used entertainment to sustain morale and to explain the war.

  • Britain's Ministry of Information backed films such as In Which We Serve (1942), and radio shows such as ITMA (It's That Man Again) kept up spirits.
  • Vera Lynn's song We'll Meet Again (1939) became a symbol of separation and hope for servicemen and families.
  • The BBC's news bulletins gained a reputation for trustworthy reporting, in contrast to German propaganda, and BBC broadcasts to occupied Europe included coded messages to resisters.
  • Hollywood made films such as Mrs Miniver (1942), which won the Best Picture Oscar, and Casablanca (1942), and Frank Capra's Why We Fight series explained the war to American troops.
  • German troops and civilians popularised the song Lili Marleen, broadcast from a German-run station in Belgrade, which Allied soldiers also listened to.
  • By the late 1930s Hollywood films filled most of the screens in Britain and much of Europe, and the cinema was the weekly habit of millions, including young women and the working class.
  • Nazi Germany condemned jazz and swing as 'degenerate', but swing youth in Hamburg and other cities defied the ban, and the regime responded with arrests.
  • Fascist Italy imposed quotas on American films and used a state film body, Cinecittà, built in 1937, to boost home production.
  • In Britain, critics worried about the effect of Hollywood and American radio on language, taste and morals, and the Cinematograph Films Act and the BBC's public service ethos were partly a response to this.
  • American troops arriving in Britain from 1942 brought with them swing, chewing gum and cheap goods and sparked both admiration and resentment, summed up in the phrase 'overpaid, oversexed and over here'.
  • Lasting consequences were the dominance of American popular music and film in postwar Europe.

Assessment: church-state relations and popular culture

Judgements of church-state relations weigh the concordats and the Church's survival against the regimes' control of religious life, and judgements of popular culture weigh its appeal against state control and cultural reaction.

  • Achievement for Mussolini: the Lateran Treaties ended the dispute with the papacy and brought the Church's support to the Fascist regime for a decade.
  • Limit for Mussolini: the Church resisted the regime's attempt to take over Catholic youth, and it withdrew its support over the racial laws of 1938, though not openly.
  • Achievement for Hitler: the Concordat secured the Catholic institutions in Germany, and the regime avoided a rupture with the Catholic population in the early 1930s.
  • Limit for Hitler: the Reichskirche failed to unify the Protestant churches, and the Confessing Church and Catholic protests exposed the limits of Nazi control over religion.
  • Contemporary view: the Lateran Treaties were welcomed by Catholics around the world, while critics on the left saw them as a bargain that made the Church complicit in Fascism.
  • Later view: historians stress that American popular culture spread widely in Europe during the 1930s and became a key influence on European youth, despite attempts by the dictatorships to restrict it.

Key terms

Reichskirche
The Nazi plan for a single Protestant Reich Church under state control, pursued through the German Christians and the appointment of a Reich Bishop in 1933.
Concordat
A formal agreement between the state and the papacy regulating the rights and status of the Catholic Church, as in the Italian Lateran Treaties of 1929 and the Reich Concordat of 1933.
Confessing Church
The German Protestant movement formed in 1934 to oppose the state's control of the churches and the German Christians.
Propaganda
The organised spread of information and images to shape opinion and promote a regime, its leader and its policies.
Fireside chat
The radio talks in which President Franklin Roosevelt addressed the American public directly from 1933.
Studio system
The Hollywood system of the 1930s in which major studios controlled the production, distribution and cinemas that showed their films.
Lateran Treaties
The agreements of 11 February 1929 that created Vatican City and settled the dispute between the Italian state and the papacy.
Non abbiamo bisogno
The 1931 encyclical of Pius XI protesting at the Fascist attack on Catholic Action.
Reich Bishop
The head of the planned Protestant Reich Church, a post given to Ludwig Müller in 1933.
German Christians
The pro-Nazi movement in German Protestantism that backed a Reich Church and the exclusion of Jewish Christians.
Talkies
Films with synchronised sound, which spread after The Jazz Singer in 1927.
