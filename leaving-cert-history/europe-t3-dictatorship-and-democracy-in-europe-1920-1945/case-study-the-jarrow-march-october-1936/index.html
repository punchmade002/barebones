Background: Jarrow and Palmer's shipyard

Jarrow was a one-industry town on the south bank of the Tyne in County Durham, and the loss of its shipyard in the early 1930s left most of its working men without work.

  • Palmers Shipbuilding and Iron Company was founded in Jarrow in 1851 and grew into a yard and steelworks that dominated the town; it launched its thousandth ship in July 1930.
  • Almost the whole local economy depended on the yard, so when orders collapsed in the Depression, shops, trades and families that depended on its wages collapsed with it.
  • World shipbuilding was in slump: orders fell with world trade, and British yards had far more capacity than orders, which was the thinking behind the National Shipbuilders Security scheme.
  • National Shipbuilders Security Ltd (NSS) was formed in 1930, backed by the shipbuilders with the support of the Bank of England, to buy and close redundant yards, financed by a levy on the shipbuilding industry.
  • Palmers launched its last ship, the destroyer HMS Duchess, on 19 July 1932; creditors appointed a receiver in June 1933.
  • NSS bought the yard in 1934 and began to dismantle it, and sites sold by NSS were barred from shipbuilding for at least forty years, which ruled out any revival of the yard.
  • In 1934 the American businessman T. Vosper Salt proposed a modern steelworks on the Palmers site, but the British Iron and Steel Federation, which feared competition and a precedent for other areas, diluted the plan until it was unworkable; Wilkinson later said that it had been strangled at birth.
  • Unemployment in the town was far higher than the national average, with well over half of insured workers on the dole in the mid-1930s and figures of around two-thirds often quoted, compared with about 17 per cent in the north-east in 1936 and about 33 per cent in shipbuilding.
  • Jarrow had one of the worst records for poor housing, hunger and ill health in Britain, and Wilkinson later called it the town that was murdered; the Special Areas Act of 1934 had given it little help.

Key figure: Ellen Wilkinson and the organisers

The march was run by the town council with cross-party support, and its public face was the town's Labour MP.

  • Ellen Wilkinson, known as Red Ellen, was a former Communist, a trade union organiser and Labour MP for Middlesbrough East from 1924 to 1931; she was elected for Jarrow in November 1935.
  • She backed the march as MP and did much of the publicity and parliamentary work, but she left it at Chester-le-Street to attend the Labour Party conference in Edinburgh and joined it again later, so she was not on foot for the whole way.
  • Councillor David Riley, the Labour leader on the council, was the leader and organiser of the marchers on the road.
  • Jarrow Borough Council backed the march, and Conservatives, Liberals and Labour on the council supported it, so it was presented as a cross-party appeal by the whole town, not a party or Communist protest.
  • Local clergy blessed the march at a departure service, and churches and Co-operative societies along the route provided food and beds.

The Crusade: aims, organisation and preparations

The organisers called the march a crusade and prepared it carefully to give it a respectable character.

  • Their aim was a petition asking the government to bring new industry to Jarrow, above all a steelworks, and to end the distress of the town.
  • The marchers were chosen from more than a thousand volunteers; about 200 men set out, although records name fewer, and each was examined by a doctor for fitness.
  • The men carried a petition of over 11,000 signatures in an oak box, and a banner reading Jarrow Crusade, and a mouth organ band set the pace.
  • The marchers were disciplined, wore good boots donated by supporters, and were organised in a way to show that the unemployed were responsible citizens who wanted work.
  • The Labour Party and the TUC did not back the march, in part because they feared that it would be taken over by Communists and in part because they preferred parliamentary pressure; Labour's conference in Edinburgh in October 1936 gave it little support.
  • The Communist-led National Unemployed Workers' Movement had its own tradition of hunger marches, and the Jarrow organisers deliberately kept apart from it.

The march, 5 to 31 October 1936

The men walked from the north-east to London, relying on the hospitality of towns along the way.

  • The marchers set out from Jarrow on 5 October 1936 after a service and a send-off by crowds.
  • They walked by way of Chester-le-Street, Darlington, Ripon, Harrogate, Leeds, Barnsley, Sheffield, Chesterfield, Nottingham, Leicester, Market Harborough, Northampton, Bedford, Luton and St Albans to London, a distance of about 290 miles.
  • They covered about 15 miles a day, slept in church halls, schools and workhouses, and ate at meals provided by supporters and local Co-operative societies.
  • In many towns, mayors, councils and Labour parties welcomed them, and the Liberal and Conservative leaders in some places joined in, although some local Conservatives distanced themselves.
  • They arrived at Marble Arch in London on 31 October 1936, after which the petition was handed to Parliament.
  • The press and newsreel coverage, much of it sympathetic, made the march a national story.

The government's response and the Commons petition

The government met the march with a refusal to act, but the petition itself was received.

  • Stanley Baldwin, the Prime Minister, declined to meet a deputation from Jarrow on 29 October 1936, and cited a fall in Jarrow's unemployment.
  • Wilkinson answered that the fall was due to combining the figures for Jarrow with those of South Shields, which hid the true numbers.
  • Wilkinson presented the petition in the House of Commons on 4 November 1936, but it was not debated and the government made no new commitment.
  • The Unemployment Assistance Board ruled that the marchers' benefit should be reduced on the grounds that they were not available for work while they marched.
  • The marchers went home by train on 5 November 1936, when crowds welcomed them in Jarrow.
  • The government's argument was that national recovery would restore jobs, and it questioned whether special help for one town was fair when many areas suffered.

Outcomes in the short and long term

The march brought no immediate results; recovery came with rearmament later in the decade.

  • Sir John Jarvis, a Conservative MP and businessman, helped to attract small firms to the Palmers site, and by 1939 about 100 men worked in a furniture factory and up to 500 in metal industries.
  • The Special Areas (Amendment) Act of 1937 provided tax reliefs for firms setting up in the depressed areas, but it was already being prepared and historians do not claim that the march caused it.
  • Rearmament and the Second World War brought real employment back to Tyneside, and Jarrow's recovery owed far more to war orders than to the march.
  • Wilkinson became Minister of Education in the Labour government of 1945 and died in 1947.
  • Many marchers felt they had failed; one called it a waste of time, and David Riley and some councillors briefly left the Labour Party after the march.

Significance and differing views

The march is remembered more than it achieved.

  • Contemporaries sympathetic to the march praised the discipline and dignity of the men, and Labour later adopted its banners as a symbol of the injustice of the 1930s.
  • Critics in the government and the press argued that the march was political theatre and could not solve structural problems.
  • The march helped to form the postwar belief that the 1930s were a time of government failure, which contributed to support for full employment policy and the welfare state after 1945.
  • The march was re-enacted in 1986 and 2011, and it remains one of the best-known symbols of the Depression in Britain.
  • As a documents-based topic, sources such as photographs of the marchers, newspaper reports, Wilkinson's speeches and government statements show how supporters and critics saw the same event.

The wider context of October 1936

The march took place in a month of major events, which affected how it was reported and received.

  • The National Government under Stanley Baldwin (Prime Minister since 1935) held a large majority, and ministers argued that general recovery, already well advanced in the south, would reach the distressed areas in time.
  • The march began in the same week as the Battle of Cable Street in London on 4 October 1936, when anti-fascists blocked a march by Oswald Mosley's British Union of Fascists, and it coincided with the Spanish Civil War, which dominated foreign news.
  • The abdication crisis over Edward VIII broke in December 1936, soon after the marchers had gone home, and pushed the Jarrow story out of the headlines.
  • Unemployment nationally was falling, to under two million by late 1936, which strengthened the government's argument that special help was unnecessary.
  • The Unemployment Assistance Board paid benefit by household means test, so the long-term unemployed of Jarrow depended on a rate set in London that took no account of local conditions.
  • Wilkinson was an experienced campaigner, and she used the march to dramatise the plight of her constituents in the Commons, where she argued that private industry could not be left to decide the fate of towns.

Placing a document: who said what and why

Sources on the march show differences of purpose and audience that are useful in a documents-based question.

  • Wilkinson, the council and the Daily Herald presented the march as a respectable appeal by a whole town, and photographs of the disciplined column with its banner and mouth organ band supported that image.
  • Government statements, including Baldwin's reference to falling unemployment, aimed to show that recovery was under way and that special action was not needed.
  • Labour and TUC leaders were cautious because they wished to avoid association with Communist-led marches and preferred political action through Parliament.
  • Conservative and some local newspapers questioned the march as a political stunt, while sympathetic papers described crowds and gifts along the route.
  • Wilkinson's book of 1939 was written after rearmament had begun to revive the town, so it combines a campaigner's anger with hindsight.
  • A cartoon or poster of the period usually shows the contrast between the needs of the distressed areas and the comfort of the south or of ministers, and the date helps to place whether it comes before or after the petition on 4 November.

Assessment: success or failure?

The judgement depends on whether the march is measured by its immediate aims or by its long-term effect on opinion.

  • Failure: the government refused to meet the marchers, no steelworks or shipyard was secured, and the men lost benefit for the weeks they marched.
  • Failure: the real cause of recovery in Jarrow was rearmament and war, not the march.
  • Success: the march made Jarrow a national symbol, kept the problem of the distressed areas before the public, and showed that the unemployed were disciplined and honest.
  • Success: it was one of the few protests that won wide sympathy across party lines, and its memory shaped postwar attitudes to unemployment.
  • Contemporary view: supporters saw a moral victory; Conservatives and officials saw an exercise in publicity that offered no solution.
  • Later view: most historians see it as a failure in its immediate aims but a powerful moment in the politics of memory of the 1930s.

Key terms

The Depression
The worldwide economic slump that followed the Wall Street Crash of 1929, marked by falling output, bank failures and mass unemployment.
Democracy
A system of government in which power rests with elected representatives or with the people, protected by free elections and the rule of law.
Distressed areas
The depressed industrial regions of Britain, such as South Wales and the north-east, where unemployment remained high throughout the inter-war years.
Jarrow Crusade
The 1936 march of about 200 unemployed men from Jarrow to London to press the government for new industry for the town.
National Shipbuilders Security
The company formed in 1930 to buy and close redundant shipyards, which bought the Palmers yard in 1934 and barred shipbuilding there for forty years.
Special Areas
The depressed regions of Britain designated in 1934 for government grants to attract new industries and public works.
Rearmament
The expansion of military forces and war industry in Britain from the mid-1930s, which brought new orders to Tyneside and reduced unemployment.
Petition
A formal written request signed by many people and presented to Parliament; Jarrow's carried over 11,000 signatures.
Unemployment Assistance Board
The national body set up under the Unemployment Act of 1934 to pay the long-term unemployed according to a household means test, which reduced the marchers' benefit.
Hunger march
A march of unemployed people to London to draw attention to unemployment, a tradition of the NUWM that Jarrow differed from by being cross-party.
