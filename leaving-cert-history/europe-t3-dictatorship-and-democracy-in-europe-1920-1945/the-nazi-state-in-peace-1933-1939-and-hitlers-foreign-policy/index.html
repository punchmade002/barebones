Consolidating power, 1933

Within months of his appointment as Chancellor, Hitler removed legal and political limits on his power, using emergency decrees and intimidation.

  • The Reichstag fire of 27 February 1933 was blamed on a Communist plot (the Dutch communist Marinus van der Lubbe was found in the building) and used to justify the Decree for the Protection of People and State of 28 February, which suspended civil liberties such as freedom of the press and assembly.
  • Communists were arrested in large numbers after the fire, and the Communist Party was effectively destroyed in early 1933.
  • The Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 allowed the cabinet to make laws without the Reichstag for four years; only the Social Democrats voted against it.
  • Trade unions were abolished on 2 May 1933 and replaced by the German Labour Front, led by Robert Ley.
  • Gleichschaltung, the coordination of institutions, brought state governments, the civil service, the courts, universities and cultural bodies under Nazi control.
  • A law of 14 July 1933 made the NSDAP the only legal party in Germany.

The Night of the Long Knives, the Führer state and police terror

The purge of the SA leadership in 1934 removed the last rival power bloc, and Hitler combined the offices of Chancellor and President after Hindenburg's death.

  • The SA under Ernst Röhm had grown to millions of members and wanted a second revolution and a larger role in the army.
  • Between 30 June and 2 July 1934, Hitler ordered the murder of Röhm and other SA leaders, and of political figures such as Gregor Strasser and former Chancellor Kurt von Schleicher.
  • The cabinet legalised the killings retrospectively on 3 July as acts of state self-defence, and the army leadership accepted the purge in return for the removal of the SA as a rival force.
  • President Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934, and Hitler combined the presidency and the chancellorship in the title of Führer and Reich Chancellor.
  • Soldiers swore a personal oath of loyalty to Hitler as Führer rather than to the constitution.
  • Hitler's authority rested on the Führer principle, in which decisions flowed down from the leader, and on the cult of his personal leadership in propaganda.
  • Heinrich Himmler, Reichsführer-SS from 1929, built up the SS and took control of the political police in most German states.
  • Reinhard Heydrich led the Security Service (SD) and, with Himmler, controlled the Gestapo, which had wide powers of arrest without legal challenge.
  • Dachau, opened near Munich in March 1933, was the first concentration camp, and others such as Sachsenhausen (1936) and Buchenwald (1937) followed.
  • Political opponents, trade unionists, Social Democrats and members of religious groups were the first people held in the camps.
  • Courts were restricted by the Special Courts and the people's court, which handed down political verdicts with little legal protection for defendants.
  • The Gestapo and the SS were given powers to detain people without trial, which the regime described as protective custody.

Economic recovery, rearmament and the economy under the Nazis

Nazi economic policy reduced unemployment through public works and rearmament, and prepared the economy for war.

  • Hjalmar Schacht, appointed president of the Reichsbank in 1933 and Economics Minister in 1934, financed rearmament through Mefo bills, which concealed military spending.
  • Public works, including autobahns (the first section opened in 1935), housing and military construction, reduced unemployment, which fell sharply between 1933 and 1936.
  • The Reich Labour Service became compulsory for young men in June 1935, and conscription was reintroduced in March 1935.
  • The New Plan of 1934 controlled foreign trade through bilateral agreements and exchange controls, which gave priority to imports needed for rearmament.
  • The Four Year Plan of September 1936, under Hermann Göring, aimed to make Germany ready for war within four years and to achieve economic self-sufficiency (autarky).
  • Schacht resigned as Economics Minister in 1937 after disagreements with Göring over the pace and cost of rearmament.

Hitler's early foreign policy, 1933-1935

Hitler's first foreign moves rejected the Versailles restrictions and gained international acceptance of rearmament.

  • On 14 October 1933 Germany left the League of Nations and the Geneva Disarmament Conference, citing unequal treatment.
  • The Saar territory, governed by the League since 1920, returned to Germany after a plebiscite in January 1935 in which over 90 per cent voted for reunion.
  • On 16 March 1935 Hitler announced the reintroduction of conscription and an air force, in breach of the Treaty of Versailles.
  • The Stresa Front of Britain, France and Italy condemned the move in April 1935, but took no effective action against Germany.
  • The Anglo-German Naval Agreement of June 1935 allowed Germany a navy about 35 per cent the size of the British navy, and it was accepted by Britain without consulting France.
  • The agreement gave Hitler the diplomatic recognition that he had sought, and it encouraged him to test the western powers further.

Expansion in Europe, 1936-1939, and the road to war

Between 1936 and 1939 Hitler took territory by threat and by diplomacy, and each success made the western powers more reluctant to resist him.

  • On 7 March 1936 German troops reoccupied the demilitarised Rhineland, and the Locarno Treaties were breached without military resistance from Britain or France.
  • The Anschluss, the annexation of Austria, took place on 12 March 1938 after German pressure forced Austrian Chancellor Kurt Schuschnigg to cancel a plebiscite on independence and resign, and German troops entered Austria.
  • Hitler's demands for the German-speaking Sudetenland in Czechoslovakia led to the Munich Agreement of 29 to 30 September 1938, which Britain and France signed with Germany and Italy.
  • Czechoslovakia was not consulted at Munich, and Britain's Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain described the agreement as securing "peace for our time".
  • German troops entered Prague on 15 March 1939, and Bohemia and Moravia became a German protectorate while Slovakia became a nominally independent client state.
  • Britain gave a guarantee to Poland on 31 March 1939, followed by France, but Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939, with a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe between them.
  • Konrad Henlein's Sudeten German Party, funded from Berlin, demanded autonomy for the three million Sudeten Germans, giving Hitler a pretext to threaten Czechoslovakia in 1938.
  • Chamberlain flew to meet Hitler at Berchtesgaden on 15 September and again at Bad Godesberg on 22 September, where Hitler raised his demands; the Munich conference followed on 29 September.
  • The Munich Agreement required Czechoslovakia to hand over the Sudetenland to Germany between 1 and 10 October 1938; the Soviet Union, which had a treaty with Czechoslovakia, was not invited.
  • Hitler's occupation of Prague in March 1939 broke the Munich promises and ended the belief that his claims were limited to Germans, which led Britain to abandon appeasement and guarantee Poland.
  • The Nazi-Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939 removed the threat of a war on two fronts; Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and Britain and France declared war on 3 September.
  • Earlier steps included the German-Polish non-aggression pact of January 1934, a failed Austrian Nazi coup in July 1934 in which Chancellor Dollfuss was murdered, and the German Condor Legion's intervention for Franco in the Spanish Civil War from 1936.

Ideology, Lebensraum and the road to war

Hitler's foreign policy was driven by racial ideology and the goal of Lebensraum in the east, which the intentionalist school of historians sees as a plan for war from the start.

  • In Mein Kampf Hitler argued that Germany needed Lebensraum (living space), to be won in Eastern Europe and settled by a racially pure population, with the Slavs to be subjugated.
  • The idea of a herrenvolk, a master race of Germanic people, underpinned both domestic policy and plans for the conquest and resettlement of Eastern Europe.
  • At a meeting of 5 November 1937, later recorded in the Hossbach Memorandum, Hitler told his military and foreign policy chiefs that Germany's space problem could be solved only by force, probably around 1943 to 1945.
  • Appeasement was British and French policy to satisfy Germany's grievances through concessions, based on the belief that Versailles had been unfair and that war must be avoided.
  • Historians disagree about whether Hitler planned a general war from 1933 (the intentionalist view) or responded to opportunities and his own followers' initiatives (the functionalist view).
  • A.J.P. Taylor's 1961 book argued that Hitler's aims were opportunist and similar to those of earlier German leaders, a view that was widely challenged by later historians.

Key personality: Adolf Hitler (the Nazi state and foreign policy, 1933-1939)

Hitler's rule from 1933 to 1939 combined a consolidated dictatorship at home with a foreign policy of rearmament, expansion and war.

  • As Chancellor and then Führer, he set the direction of policy, while rival agencies competed for influence, a structure historians call polycracy.
  • He was a participant in the foreign policy decisions of 1933 to 1939, making the key choices about withdrawal from the League, rearmament, the Anschluss and Czechoslovakia.
  • Public opinion in Germany responded enthusiastically to his foreign successes, especially the Saar, the Rhineland and the Anschluss, which made his popularity high by 1938.
  • Foreign contemporaries were divided: some British politicians saw him as a revisionist who could be satisfied, while others, such as Winston Churchill, warned from the 1930s that he was a threat.
  • Later historians discuss how far he controlled events and how far his policies were shaped by pressure from the military, the party and the economy.
  • His foreign policy and the deaths of Jews, opponents and others in the camps are judged together by historians as the central crimes of his regime, a matter taken up in the notes on the Holocaust.

The Volksgemeinschaft: workers, youth and women

The regime aimed to unite Germans of 'pure' race in a national community under party control, and it reshaped work, education and family life to do so.

  • The German Labour Front under Robert Ley replaced the free unions; its Strength Through Joy (Kraft durch Freude) section organised cheap holidays, cruises and theatre trips, and its Beauty of Labour section improved factory conditions.
  • The Volkswagen savings scheme, in which workers paid weekly instalments towards a Strength Through Joy car, never delivered cars to the savers, because the factory was turned over to military production.
  • The Hitler Youth, led by Baldur von Schirach, became the only legal youth organisation under the Hitler Youth Law of December 1936, with boys in the Jungvolk and Hitler Youth and girls in the League of German Girls (BDM).
  • Schools taught racial science and nationalist history, textbooks were rewritten, and teachers were pressed to join the National Socialist Teachers' League.
  • Nazi ideology assigned women the roles of wife and mother (often summed up as Kinder, Küche, Kirche); marriage loans from 1933 were reduced by a quarter for each child born, and the Mother's Cross was awarded from 1938 to women with large families.
  • Women were pushed out of the civil service and professions such as law and medicine in 1933, although rearmament and labour shortages brought many back into work by the late 1930s.
  • Opponents, 'asocials' and people judged racially or biologically inferior were excluded from the community: Jews by the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, and disabled people by forced sterilisation under the law of July 1933.
  • Unemployment, officially about six million in early 1933, fell below one million by 1937 and gave way to labour shortages, although the figures excluded Jews and many women, and wages were held down.

The Nazi state in war, 1939-1945 (outline)

The war gave the regime radical new powers; the course of the fighting is covered in the notes on the Second World War.

  • Albert Speer, Hitler's architect, became Minister for Armaments in February 1942 and raised output of weapons by rationalising production, even as Allied bombing increased.
  • After the defeat at Stalingrad, Goebbels called for total war in his Sportpalast speech in Berlin on 18 February 1943, leading to the closure of non-essential businesses and the conscription of women up to the age of 45 into war work.
  • Millions of forced labourers from occupied Europe, including prisoners of war and concentration camp inmates, worked in German factories and farms.
  • On 20 July 1944 Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg planted a bomb at Hitler's headquarters at Rastenburg (the Wolf's Lair), but Hitler survived and the plot, which involved army officers and conservative civilians, was crushed; thousands were arrested and several hundred executed.
  • Hitler remained in command to the end, committing suicide in Berlin on 30 April 1945.

Assessment: how far Hitler caused the Second World War

Judgements of Hitler's foreign policy divide between seeing him as the driving force of a deliberate war and seeing the war as the product of wider forces and of appeasement.

  • Achievement: by 1939 Hitler had reversed the main Versailles restrictions, rebuilt the army and expanded German territory without a major war.
  • Limit: the expansion depended on the restraint of the western powers, which many German generals feared would fight, and the economy was strained by rearmament.
  • Intentionalist view: Hitler's Mein Kampf aims, the Hossbach Memorandum and the invasion of Poland show a deliberate plan for war.
  • Functionalist view: the policies of the Nazi state, the competition between agencies and the choices of Britain and France shaped how and when the war began.
  • Contemporary view: appeasement was supported by many British people who wanted to avoid another war, and Munich was welcomed at first as a rescue of peace.
  • Later view: Munich is generally judged a failure, because it removed Czechoslovakia's defences and left Hitler convinced that the western powers would not fight.

Key terms

Totalitarianism
A system in which the state seeks to control every aspect of public and private life through a single party, secret police and propaganda.
Herrenvolk
The Nazi idea of a master race of Germanic people, used to justify racial hierarchy, persecution and the conquest of other peoples.
Lebensraum
The Nazi goal of acquiring living space, mainly in Eastern Europe, for the German population through conquest and resettlement.
Dictatorship
Rule by one leader or party that holds power without free elections, an independent parliament or a free press.
Gleichschaltung
The Nazi policy of bringing all institutions, states and organisations into line with the party and its leadership.
Enabling Act
The law of 23 March 1933 that allowed Hitler's cabinet to make laws without the Reichstag for four years.
Appeasement
British and French policy of making concessions to Germany in the 1930s to avoid war and satisfy grievances about Versailles.
Anschluss
The annexation of Austria by Germany on 12 March 1938.
Volksgemeinschaft
The Nazi ideal of a racially united people's community in which class divisions would disappear and individuals served the nation.
Night of the Long Knives
The purge of 30 June to 2 July 1934 in which Hitler had Ernst Röhm and other SA leaders, and several political opponents, killed.
Strength Through Joy
The Nazi leisure organisation, part of the German Labour Front, that offered cheap holidays and entertainment to workers.
Hossbach Memorandum
The record of a meeting of 5 November 1937 at which Hitler outlined to his chiefs his aim of seizing living space by force.
