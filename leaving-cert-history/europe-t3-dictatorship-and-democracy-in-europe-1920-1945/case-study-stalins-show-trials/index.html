Background: earlier show trials and why Stalin purged

The trials of 1936 to 1938 followed a pattern set by earlier public trials, and they served several purposes for Stalin at once.

  • The Shakhty trial of 1928 tried engineers from a Donbas coal town on charges of sabotage and foreign conspiracy, and the Industrial Party trial of 1930 repeated the charges against leading technical specialists.
  • In April 1933 six British engineers of the Metropolitan-Vickers company were tried in Moscow for espionage and wrecking, a case that showed the regime blaming foreigners for industrial failures.
  • The costs of the First Five-Year Plan and collectivisation, including shortages, accidents and the famine of 1932-33, created a need for scapegoats among managers, engineers and officials, who were accused of 'wrecking' and sabotage.
  • Stalin's rivals from the succession struggle, Trotsky in exile and former oppositionists such as Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin, still had prestige as old Bolsheviks and could be presented as a potential alternative leadership.
  • The 17th Party Congress of January and February 1934, known as the Congress of Victors, celebrated the success of the plans, yet most of its delegates did not survive the Terror.
  • Historians differ on motive: some stress Stalin's paranoia and desire for total personal power, while others point to real tensions over the pace of industrialisation, fear of foreign attack as Hitler rose, and the pressure on local officials to find enemies.
  • A party purge or chistka began in 1933, when members judged unreliable were expelled, and in 1935 a check on party documents led to further expulsions; these purges were not at first fatal.

The Kirov murder, 1 December 1934

The shooting of a leading Stalinist gave Stalin the pretext for the repression that followed.

  • Sergei Kirov, party leader in Leningrad and a member of the Politburo, was shot dead on 1 December 1934 at the Smolny Institute, the party headquarters in Leningrad, by Leonid Nikolaev, a young party member who had been dismissed from his job.
  • Nikolaev was tried in secret and shot in December 1934, and the NKVD at first blamed a plot by 'white guards' and then by former supporters of Zinoviev.
  • A decree of 1 December 1934, issued the day of the murder and apparently drafted by Stalin, ordered that terrorism cases be heard in days, with no defence lawyer, no appeal and immediate execution of death sentences.
  • Zinoviev and Kamenev were tried in secret in January 1935 and given prison terms of ten and five years; in 1936 they were brought back for a public trial on much graver charges.
  • Historians disagree about the murder: some, such as Robert Conquest, suspected that Stalin or the NKVD arranged it, while others, including J. Arch Getty and Matthew Lenoe, find evidence that Nikolaev acted alone; no document proving Stalin's involvement has been found.
  • Either way, Stalin exploited the killing: he claimed that a network of conspirators existed and that the opposition had been drawn into terrorism, and he used this claim to justify the show trials.

The first Moscow trial, August 1936: Zinoviev and Kamenev

The Trial of the Trotskyite-Zinovievite Terrorist Centre opened the public series.

  • The trial was held from 19 to 24 August 1936 in the Hall of Columns of the House of Unions in Moscow, with sixteen defendants led by Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev, two of Lenin's closest colleagues and former members of the Politburo.
  • They were charged with forming a terrorist centre under Trotsky's direction, murdering Kirov, and plotting to kill Stalin and other leaders.
  • Before the trial Zinoviev and Kamenev had held out for months; Stalin reportedly promised that their lives and their families would be spared if they confessed, and the NKVD interrogators told them that confession would serve the party.
  • All sixteen defendants confessed in open court and all were sentenced to death; they were shot on 25 August 1936, within hours of the verdict, despite the promise.
  • Trotsky, living in Norway, and his son Lev Sedov were named as the organisers of the plot and were condemned in their absence.
  • The trial opened a campaign against 'Trotskyite-Zinovievite' enemies and led to the arrest of thousands of former oppositionists, and Mikhail Tomsky, a Right Oppositionist named during the trial, shot himself on 22 August 1936 before he could be arrested.

The second Moscow trial, January 1937: Pyatakov and Radek

The trial of the so-called Anti-Soviet Trotskyite Centre moved the charges on to industrial sabotage.

  • It was held from 23 to 30 January 1937 and tried seventeen defendants, among them Yuri Pyatakov, a deputy commissar for heavy industry, and the journalist Karl Radek.
  • The defendants were accused of wrecking in factories and mines, causing railway accidents and plotting with Trotsky and with Germany and Japan to restore capitalism; the charges gave an explanation for the failures of the plans.
  • Pyatakov claimed to have flown to Oslo in December 1935 to meet Trotsky, but the Norwegian authorities reported that no foreign aircraft had landed there at the time, an inconsistency that foreign critics noticed.
  • Thirteen defendants, including Pyatakov, were shot; Radek and Grigory Sokolnikov received ten years in prison for co-operating with the prosecution, and two others received eight years.
  • Radek died in 1939 in prison; the circumstances are unclear, and the usual account is that he was killed by a fellow prisoner, probably on NKVD orders.
  • Sergo Ordzhonikidze, head of heavy industry and Pyatakov's superior, shot himself in February 1937, an event officially described as a heart attack.

The military purge, 1937-1938

The destruction of the army leadership took place largely in secret, unlike the public trials.

  • Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky, Deputy Defence Commissar and the leading advocate of modern mechanised warfare, was arrested in May 1937 with other senior officers.
  • They were tried by a closed military tribunal on 11 June 1937 on charges of treason and spying for Germany, and were shot the following day; the evidence was fabricated and relied on confessions.
  • Three of the five marshals of the Soviet Union, most army commanders and a large share of corps and divisional commanders were arrested, as were many naval and air force officers.
  • In total roughly 30,000 to 35,000 officers were dismissed or arrested between 1937 and 1939, although some were later released, and political commissars regained control of the army.
  • The purge left the Red Army poorly led at the time of the Winter War against Finland (1939-40) and the German invasion of June 1941, though historians debate how much the purge explains the defeats of 1941.
  • Tukhachevsky was rehabilitated in 1957.

The third Moscow trial, March 1938: Bukharin, Rykov and Yagoda

The Trial of the Right-Trotskyite Bloc was the last and the largest of the public trials.

  • It was held from 2 to 13 March 1938 and tried twenty-one defendants, including Nikolai Bukharin (former editor of Pravda and Comintern chairman), Alexei Rykov (former Prime Minister), the former NKVD chief Genrikh Yagoda, and Nikolai Krestinsky.
  • The defendants were charged with treason, spying for foreign powers, wrecking, planning to dismember the USSR, and conspiring to murder Lenin, Stalin, Kirov and the writer Maxim Gorky; Yagoda and a group of doctors were accused of causing deaths by medical murder.
  • On the first day Krestinsky retracted his confession, saying it was false, but he reversed himself the next day after a night in NKVD hands.
  • Bukharin accepted overall political responsibility for the 'bloc' but denied the specific charges of espionage and murder; this has been read as an attempt to signal his innocence to a careful reader.
  • Eighteen defendants, including Bukharin, Rykov and Yagoda, were shot on 15 March 1938; three others received prison terms.
  • Bukharin was rehabilitated by the Soviet government in 1988.

Stage management of the trials and reactions in the West

The trials were scripted from start to finish, and Stalin himself reviewed the charges and the testimony.

  • The prosecutor Andrei Vyshinsky, a former Menshevik and lawyer, prepared the cases with the NKVD and argued that a confession was sufficient proof of guilt.
  • The NKVD obtained confessions by sleep deprivation, long interrogations, threats against family members, false promises of leniency and, from 1937, with Stalin's approval, physical torture.
  • Defendants rehearsed their parts, and some seem to have been persuaded that confession served the party's interest, an argument used by interrogators on loyal communists.
  • The trials took place in public in the Hall of Columns or the October Hall of the House of Unions before an invited audience, with foreign journalists and diplomats present and the full transcripts published in several languages.
  • The presiding judge Vasily Ulrikh, a military judge, helped to keep the proceedings tightly controlled, and Soviet newspapers demanded the death penalty before the verdicts.
  • Weaknesses in the evidence, such as the Oslo flight, an alleged meeting in the Hotel Bristol in Copenhagen that had been demolished, and the near-identical wording of confessions, were noticed by foreign observers.
  • Joseph Davies, the American ambassador, who attended parts of the 1937 and 1938 trials, reported to Washington that the evidence appeared to establish guilt, and the German writer Lion Feuchtwanger, who visited Moscow in 1937, defended the trials in a book.
  • Some other diplomats and journalists were doubtful, and many liberals and socialists condemned the proceedings as a travesty of justice.
  • In 1937 an international commission chaired by the American philosopher John Dewey heard Trotsky's evidence in Mexico, in April, and in December reported that the charges were false; its report was titled Not Guilty.
  • Western communist parties defended the trials as necessary to destroy fascist agents, and some members left the party over the issue; the trials damaged the reputation of the Soviet system among liberals.
  • Stalin used the verdicts to discredit Trotsky, who was murdered in Mexico by an agent of the NKVD, Ramón Mercader, in August 1940.

The wider Great Terror, 1937-1938

Show trials were the visible tip of a mass campaign run by the NKVD and carried out largely in secret.

  • Nikolai Yezhov replaced Yagoda as head of the NKVD in September 1936, and the years 1937 and 1938 are often called the Yezhovshchina.
  • NKVD Order 00447 of 30 July 1937 set quotas by region for the arrest of 'former kulaks, criminals and anti-Soviet elements', with the first category to be shot and the second sent to camps; local officials asked for higher quotas, and the targets were exceeded.
  • National operations targeted ethnic groups with foreign connections, such as Poles, Germans and Latvians, and the Polish operation of 1937-38 was the largest.
  • NKVD records opened after 1989 show about 1.6 million arrests for 'counter-revolutionary' crimes in 1937 and 1938 and about 680,000 executions; some historians believe the true total of deaths was higher once camp deaths are added.
  • Many victims were ordinary workers, peasants and minorities rather than officials, and neighbours and colleagues were encouraged to denounce 'enemies of the people'.
  • According to Khrushchev's secret speech of 1956, 1,108 of the 1,966 delegates to the 1934 Congress were arrested and 98 of 139 members and candidates of the Central Committee elected at the Congress were shot.
  • By 1939 the Gulag held well over a million prisoners.
  • The Terror slowed in late 1938: Yezhov was removed in November 1938 and replaced by Lavrentiy Beria, and he was arrested in 1939 and shot in February 1940.

Key concepts in action, outcomes and impact on Soviet Russia

The trials illustrate three of the syllabus concepts.

  • Totalitarianism: the secret police, the control of the courts and the press, and the ability to make leading communists confess to invented crimes show a state that left no area outside political control.
  • Propaganda: newspapers, radio and public meetings across the USSR demanded death for the 'enemies of the people', and the confessions were used to explain shortages and accidents.
  • Personality cult: the trials presented Stalin as the vigilant leader who protected the people, Lenin's true heir, and denied the old Bolsheviks their share in the revolution.
  • The trials and the Terror were followed by the Short Course history of the party in 1938, which presented the defendants as agents of foreign powers from the beginning of the revolution.
  • The party's old guard was destroyed; Stalin's rivals and potential rivals were dead or in exile, and the new officials who replaced them owed their positions to him.
  • The economy lost managers, engineers and planners, and fear of being accused of wrecking made officials cautious and encouraged them to falsify results or avoid decisions.
  • The army and the security services were purged, and the scale of the loss of experienced officers is widely linked to the poor performance against Finland in 1939-40 and the German advance in 1941.
  • Society was affected through fear, denunciations and the arrest of relatives, and families of those arrested were stigmatised as relatives of enemies of the people.
  • Younger men promoted into the vacancies in the party, army and industry owed their careers to Stalin, which made the new elite loyal to him.
  • In 1956 Khrushchev condemned the Terror in his secret speech; mass rehabilitations followed, and Bukharin's was one of the last in 1988.

Assessment: the trials and the Terror

Judgements balance the consolidation of Stalin's personal power against the damage the Terror did to the state.

  • Achievement for Stalin: by 1939 the party, army and state were in the hands of officials loyal to him, and no organised opposition survived.
  • Limit for the regime: the loss of officers, managers and specialists weakened the army and the economy in the years before Barbarossa.
  • Contemporary supportive view: Soviet propaganda and foreign communists presented the trials as the exposure of real traitors, and visitors such as Davies and Feuchtwanger accepted the evidence.
  • Contemporary critical view: the Dewey Commission, liberal and socialist critics and many diplomats treated the trials as frame-ups.
  • Later view: after the opening of the archives in the 1990s historians treat the trials as stage-managed fabrications; they still debate how far Stalin planned the Terror, how much was driven from below by local officials, and how far it reflected paranoia or political calculation.
  • Later revisionist view: J. Arch Getty and others stress the disorder and local initiative in the Terror, while Robert Conquest and others emphasise central planning by Stalin.

Key terms

Show trial
A public trial in which the verdict is decided in advance and defendants are made to confess, so that the trial serves as propaganda.
Purge
The removal of members from a party, state or army, often by arrest and execution, to eliminate rivals or suspected opponents.
Totalitarianism
A system in which the state seeks to control every aspect of public and private life through a single party, secret police and propaganda.
Propaganda
The organised spread of information and images to shape opinion and promote a regime, its leader and its policies.
Personality cult
The promotion of a leader as an infallible and heroic figure, shown through propaganda, rituals and his image in public life.
NKVD
The Soviet people's commissariat for internal affairs, which combined the secret police with the labour camp system and carried out the arrests and executions of the Terror.
Gulag
The Soviet system of labour camps, administered by the NKVD, in which millions of prisoners were held and forced to work.
Great Terror
The campaign of mass arrests and executions of 1937 and 1938 that included the show trials, the military purge and the mass operations against 'enemies of the people'.
Old Bolsheviks
Members who had joined the party before 1917 and helped to make the revolution; most of those who led the opposition to Stalin were tried and shot.
Wrecking
The Soviet charge of sabotaging industry, used to blame managers and engineers for the failures of the Five-Year Plans.
Rehabilitation
The official clearing of the names of those wrongly condemned, begun under Khrushchev from 1956.
