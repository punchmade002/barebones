Catholic identity and the devotional revolution

From the 1840s the Catholic Church became more disciplined and its practice more uniform, a change the historian Emmet Larkin called the devotional revolution.

  • Church building expanded sharply after Emancipation, with new parish churches and cathedrals raised on money collected from the faithful.
  • St Patrick's College, Maynooth (1795) trained most of the clergy, and by mid-century the clergy were better educated and under closer episcopal discipline.
  • Religious orders and congregations grew, among them the Christian Brothers (Waterford, 1802), the Presentation Sisters, the Sisters of Mercy founded by Catherine McAuley in Dublin in 1831 and the Sisters of Charity; they ran schools, hospitals and orphanages.
  • Parish missions given by Redemptorists, Vincentians and Jesuits, together with the rosary, novenas, confraternities, benediction and devotion to the Sacred Heart and the Virgin Mary, spread new forms of piety.
  • Larkin argued that weekly Mass attendance rose from perhaps a third of Catholics before the Famine to the large majority by the 1870s.
  • Historians link the change to the Famine, which killed and removed the poor who had been least regular in practice, left a more prosperous farmer class and strengthened clerical authority.
  • Catholic identity became tied to nationality, as the Church's building programme and schools made it the main institution of the Catholic majority.

Protestant identity: evangelicalism, the Second Reformation and the Orange Order

Protestant identity was shaped by evangelical revival and by the defence of Protestant privilege.

  • Evangelicalism was a Protestant revival stressing personal conversion, Bible reading and missionary work; it grew in the Church of Ireland and among Presbyterians and Methodists from the 1820s.
  • The Second Reformation was the evangelical drive of the 1820s to 1850s to convert Catholics, using Bible societies, schools and missions.
  • Edward Nangle's mission colony at Achill (1834) and the Irish Church Missions of the 1840s were well-known examples.
  • In the Famine years, Protestant soup kitchens that required conversion led Catholics to coin the term souperism, and the bitterness lasted for generations.
  • The Orange Order, founded in 1795, was dissolved in 1836 and revived in the 1840s, and it expressed Protestant loyalty to the Crown, the Union and Protestant ascendancy.
  • Presbyterian leader Henry Cooke, an Ulster evangelical, promoted an alliance of Presbyterians and Anglicans against O'Connell and Catholic nationalism.
  • A great evangelical revival swept Ulster in 1859.
  • The Church of Ireland remained the established church until disestablishment took effect in 1871, though it served a small minority.

The nation, cultural nationalism and the decline of the Irish language

Young Ireland defined the nation through culture and history while the Irish language declined.

  • Young Ireland's idea of the nation was a community of shared history, culture and territory that could include Protestants as well as Catholics.
  • The Nation, founded in 1842, used poems, ballads and history to teach this idea.
  • O'Connell, though a native speaker, saw English as the language of progress, and had little interest in saving Irish.
  • The 1851 census, the first to ask about the Irish language, found roughly a quarter of the population reporting that they spoke it.
  • Irish was strongest in the west and south-west, where the Famine killed or scattered the poorest and the most Irish-speaking.
  • The national schools taught in English, and parents saw English as the key to a job or to emigration.
  • Cultural nationalism in the 1840s encouraged the study of Irish history, manuscripts and antiquities, but it did not stop the language declining.

Key personality: Cardinal Paul Cullen (1803–1878)

Cullen was the architect of the Romanisation of the Irish Church and the leading Catholic figure of the period.

  • Born in County Kildare in 1803, he studied in Rome, was ordained there and became rector of the Irish College in Rome in 1832.
  • He was appointed Archbishop of Armagh and Apostolic Delegate in 1850, the pope's representative with special powers, and presided at the Synod of Thurles in 1850.
  • He became Archbishop of Dublin in 1852 and was made the first Irish cardinal in 1866; he died in 1878.
  • He championed ultramontane devotion, strict clerical discipline, church building, religious orders, parish missions and Catholic schools.
  • He opposed the Queen's Colleges and backed the Catholic University founded under Newman in 1854.
  • He distrusted Young Ireland, the Tenant League and priests in politics, and opposed the Fenians; he refused to let the body of Terence Bellew MacManus lie in the Pro-Cathedral in 1861.
  • He favoured co-operation with the Liberals and supported Gladstone's disestablishment of the Church of Ireland.
  • Contemporaries admired his devotion to Rome and his organisation but nationalists and some bishops, notably MacHale, resented his authority.
  • Historians see him as the central figure of the devotional revolution but debate whether he shaped Catholic political conservatism or reflected what the Church wanted.

Case study: the Synod of Thurles, 1850 - background and causes

The Synod met to unify the Irish Church after decades of diocesan independence and to settle the education question.

  • It was the first national synod of the Irish Catholic Church for about two centuries, the last having been held at Kilkenny in 1642.
  • Before 1850 each bishop ran his own diocese with little central direction, and clerical discipline and practice varied widely.
  • Rome wanted uniform practice and a clear Irish policy, and Pope Pius IX wanted the Church to resist the secular and mixed education of the Queen's Colleges.
  • The bishops were divided: MacHale of Tuam opposed the colleges outright, while Daniel Murray of Dublin was more willing to accept them.
  • A papal rescript of 1847 had condemned the Queen's Colleges, and the Famine had weakened the old order and made Church leaders open to reform.
  • Rome made Paul Cullen Archbishop of Armagh in 1849 and Apostolic Delegate in 1850, to organise the Synod and carry out Roman policy.

Case study: the Synod of Thurles - proceedings and decrees

The Synod met in Thurles, County Tipperary, from 22 August to 9 September 1850.

  • Cullen presided as Apostolic Delegate, and the bishops of the four provinces attended.
  • The decrees condemned the Queen's Colleges as dangerous to faith and morals and forbade clergy to take posts in them.
  • It ordered the setting up of a Catholic University, which opened in 1854 with Newman as rector.
  • It laid down uniform liturgical practice and church discipline, with the Roman rite followed.
  • It regulated clerical dress, behaviour and the conduct of the sacraments, and increased the bishops' authority over priests.
  • It discouraged priests from political agitation, notably at the altar and election meetings.
  • Opposition came from MacHale and from lay Catholics and liberal clergy, who supported the Queen's Colleges and resented Roman control.
  • Rome supported Cullen, and the Synod's decrees were later confirmed there.

Case study: the Synod of Thurles - outcomes and significance

The Synod began the Romanisation of the Irish Church and shaped Catholic life and politics for the rest of the century.

  • Ultramontanism was the belief in strong papal authority over national churches and bishops, and the Synod made it the guiding principle of Irish Catholicism.
  • Romanisation meant closer links with Rome, uniform liturgy and devotions in the Roman style, and a centralised Church under the bishops.
  • The Catholic University failed to attract many students and had no state-recognised degrees, so the Synod's education policy had limited success.
  • Most Catholics who wished to attend university still entered Trinity or the Queen's Colleges, and the bishops later campaigned for a Catholic university with state support.
  • Cullen's authority over the bishops made him the dominant Catholic voice in public affairs for the next quarter-century.
  • Contemporaries disagreed: Protestants and some liberal Catholics saw an alien and authoritarian power, while supporters saw order and renewal.
  • Historians such as Emmet Larkin treat the Synod as the start of the devotional revolution, while others argue that the changes were already under way.

Key personality: Mother Mary Aikenhead (1787–1858)

Aikenhead founded the first Irish Catholic religious order devoted to caring for the poor outside a convent.

  • Born in Cork in 1787 to a Protestant father and a Catholic mother, she became a Catholic.
  • Guided by Archbishop Daniel Murray of Dublin, she trained at the Bar Convent in York and founded the Religious Sisters of Charity in 1815.
  • The Sisters visited the poor in their homes, ran schools and nursed the sick.
  • In 1834 she opened St Vincent's Hospital in St Stephen's Green, Dublin, the first hospital in Ireland run by nuns, which treated the sick poor of all creeds.
  • She directed the order from Stanhope Street in Dublin, much of the time as an invalid, until her death in 1858.
  • She was a participant, as the founder, in the growth of Catholic social services.
  • Contemporaries admired her charity and her courage in running an institution while an invalid.
  • Historians see her as a pioneer of active women's religious life and Catholic nursing and as a founder of institutions that shaped Irish health care.

Key personality: William Carleton (1794–1869)

Carleton was the leading chronicler of the Irish peasantry before the Famine.

  • Born in 1794 at Prillisk, County Tyrone, the son of a Catholic Irish-speaking tenant farmer, he went to Dublin in about 1817 and began writing.
  • He first wrote for the Christian Examiner, an evangelical Protestant paper, and his early stories had an anti-Catholic edge.
  • Traits and Stories of the Irish Peasantry appeared in 1830 and a second series in 1833, describing wakes, pilgrimages, faction fights and hedge schools.
  • Fardorougha the Miser (1839) and The Black Prophet (1847), a novel of famine, explored the life of the rural poor.
  • He was a witness to the world he described, rather than a political actor, and received a government pension in 1848.
  • Contemporaries admired his realism, but some Catholic nationalists resented his early anti-Catholic writing.
  • Historians value him as a source on pre-Famine rural life and as a realist who showed the violence and poverty as well as the humour.

Key personality: Thomas Davis and cultural nationalism

Davis shaped the cultural idea of the Irish nation; his political role is covered in the notes on O'Connell and Young Ireland.

  • Born in Mallow in 1814 into a Protestant family and educated at Trinity College Dublin, he co-founded The Nation in 1842 with Charles Gavan Duffy and John Blake Dillon.
  • His songs and ballads, including A Nation Once Again and The West's Asleep, put history and patriotism into popular verse.
  • He urged the study and teaching of Irish history and the Irish language, and edited the Library of Ireland series.
  • He argued that the nation should unite Catholic, Protestant and Dissenter on a shared Irish identity.
  • He died of scarlet fever in September 1845, aged 30.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a model of principled youth; critics thought his ideas were idealistic.
  • Historians credit him with laying the cultural foundations of Irish nationalism, though his appeal to Protestants was limited.

The creative arts: literature, music, painting and antiquarian scholarship

Writers, musicians and antiquarians drew on Irish history and folklore.

  • Thomas Moore's Irish Melodies, published in numbers from 1808 to 1834, set Irish airs to words and were popular in Britain, Ireland and America.
  • James Clarence Mangan wrote poems for The Nation, including Dark Rosaleen (1846), adapted from the Irish, and died in 1849 in the cholera of the Famine years.
  • George Petrie, painter and antiquarian, showed in an essay published in 1845 that the round towers were Christian in origin and collected traditional music in The Ancient Music of Ireland (1855).
  • The Ordnance Survey of the 1830s, directed by Thomas Larcom, employed scholars such as John O'Donovan and Eugene O'Curry to record Irish place names and to study manuscripts.
  • O'Donovan edited the Annals of the Four Masters (1848-51), and O'Curry became professor of Irish history and archaeology at the Catholic University.
  • The Royal Irish Academy and the Royal Dublin Society supported scholarship, and the Famine drew writers to themes of hunger, eviction and emigration.

Science and technology

Irish scientists made international contributions in mathematics, physics and astronomy.

  • William Rowan Hamilton, Royal Astronomer of Ireland at Dunsink, discovered quaternions on 16 October 1843 while walking by the Royal Canal at Broom Bridge.
  • William Parsons, 3rd Earl of Rosse, built the six-foot reflecting telescope called the Leviathan of Parsonstown at Birr Castle, completed in 1845, then the largest in the world.
  • With it he observed the spiral structure of the galaxy later known as M51, the Whirlpool Galaxy.
  • Nicholas Callan, a priest and professor at Maynooth, built an early induction coil in the 1830s, and developed powerful batteries.
  • George Boole was the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College Cork from 1849, and published The Laws of Thought (1854), the basis of Boolean algebra.
  • Robert Kane, first president of Queen's College Cork, wrote The Industrial Resources of Ireland (1844), urging the use of science for economic development.
  • The Queen's Colleges, the Royal Dublin Society and the Royal Irish Academy supported such work.

Assessment: religious and cultural identities, 1830-1870

Judgements turn on whether the changes were imposed from above or grew from below.

  • Achievement of the devotional revolution: a more disciplined clergy, more churches and schools, and a strong Catholic identity.
  • Limit: the Church's authoritarian leadership split bishops, alienated liberal Catholics and Protestants, and left the Catholic University weak.
  • Contemporary view: Protestants saw papal domination, while Catholic supporters saw renewal after the Famine.
  • Later view: historians broadly agree that the Church's changes gave Catholics a shared religious identity that later nationalism drew on.
  • Achievement of the arts and sciences: Moore, Carleton, Petrie, O'Donovan, Hamilton, Rosse and Boole won international recognition.
  • Limit: the Famine and emigration weakened the rural culture and the Irish language that cultural nationalists wished to save.

Key terms

Ultramontanism
The doctrine and movement that stressed the authority of the pope over national churches and local bishops, which guided Cullen's reforms.
Evangelicalism
A Protestant revival movement stressing personal conversion, Bible reading and missionary work, strong among Irish Protestants from the 1820s.
Nation
A community bound by shared history, culture and territory, which Young Ireland used to define Irish nationality.
Sectarianism
Hostility between religious communities, which shaped Irish politics, public processions and urban life.
Synod of Thurles
The 1850 national synod of the Irish Catholic bishops under Cullen, which condemned the Queen's Colleges and reformed Church discipline.
Devotional revolution
The change in Irish Catholic practice after the Famine toward regular Mass, new devotions and closer clerical discipline.
Romanisation
The process by which the Irish Catholic Church adopted Roman liturgy and closer links with Rome and the pope.
Second Reformation
The evangelical Protestant drive of the 1820s to 1850s to convert Irish Catholics through missions, schools and Bible societies.
Souperism
The charge, made by Catholics, that Protestant relief agencies in the Famine gave food in return for conversion.
Apostolic Delegate
The pope's special representative, a title Cullen held in Ireland from 1850.
Cultural nationalism
The belief that a nation is defined by its language, history, music and literature, promoted by The Nation.
