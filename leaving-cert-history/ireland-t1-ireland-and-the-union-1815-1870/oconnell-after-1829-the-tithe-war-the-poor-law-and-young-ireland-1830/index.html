O'Connell at Westminster and the Whig alliance, 1830–1841

In the 1830s O'Connell led a group of Irish Repeal MPs at Westminster and, when Repeal got nowhere, worked with the Whigs to win practical reforms.

  • Returned unopposed for Clare in July 1829, O'Connell entered the Commons in 1830 and gave up his legal practice; supporters funded him through an annual collection, the O'Connell Tribute.
  • He supported Earl Grey's Whig government over parliamentary reform; the Irish Reform Act of 1832 raised Ireland's MPs from 100 to 105 but left the Irish electorate small.
  • At the 1832 election about 39 MPs pledged to Repeal were returned, but his Repeal motion of April 1834 was defeated by 523 votes to 38.
  • The Lichfield House Compact (1835) was an informal agreement under which O'Connell and his MPs kept the Whigs under Lord Melbourne in office in return for Irish reforms and fair appointments.
  • Under the Lord Lieutenant Lord Mulgrave, Chief Secretary Lord Morpeth and Under-Secretary Thomas Drummond (1835–1840), Catholics and liberals were appointed to office, stipendiary (paid) magistrates were used and the police were centralised in the 1836 Irish Constabulary.
  • Drummond told Tipperary magistrates in 1838 that property has its duties as well as its rights, and the Orange Order dissolved itself in 1836 after a Commons inquiry.
  • The Municipal Corporations (Ireland) Act 1840 abolished most of the closed Protestant corporations and gave the rest an elected council on a £10 householder franchise.
  • O'Connell became Lord Mayor of Dublin in November 1841, the first Catholic to hold the office since the reign of James II.
  • The Whig alliance won no Repeal and only a limited tithe reform, and when Peel's Conservatives took office in 1841 O'Connell returned to agitation.

The Tithe War, 1830–1838

Tithe was a tax of about a tenth of agricultural produce, paid in money by the occupiers of land to support the clergy of the Church of Ireland, the church of a small minority.

  • Catholics resented paying for a church they did not attend; the Tithe Composition Act of 1823 converted tithe into fixed money payments and extended it to pasture, bringing graziers and strong farmers into the protest.
  • The campaign of non-payment began at Graiguenamanagh, Co. Kilkenny, in 1830–31, where farmers organised by the Catholic clergy refused payment and protected each other's livestock from seizure.
  • Collectors (tithe proctors) and process servers seized cattle and goods for arrears, escorted by police and soldiers; crowds gathered at the sound of horns and chapel bells to block them.
  • At Newtownbarry (Bunclody), Co. Wexford, in June 1831 yeomanry fired on a crowd resisting a seizure and killed more than a dozen people.
  • At Carrickshock, Co. Kilkenny, on 14 December 1831 a crowd attacked a process server and his police escort, killing the process server and more than ten policemen.
  • At Gortroe near Rathcormac, Co. Cork, on 18 December 1834 soldiers fired on a crowd trying to stop a seizure from a widow, killing about twelve people.
  • The protest made tithe uncollectable in much of the south: arrears mounted, and the government had to advance money to clergy left without income.
  • The Whigs split over the appropriation of surplus Church revenues to non-religious purposes, which O'Connell supported, so a settlement was delayed.
  • The Tithe Rentcharge Act (August 1838) cut the charge by about a quarter and made landlords responsible for paying it as part of the rent, which ended the open conflict; tithe was only abolished with disestablishment in 1869.

The Irish Poor Law, 1838

  • Ireland had no statutory system of poor relief before 1838; relief depended on charity, a few houses of industry and county infirmaries.
  • A royal commission chaired by the Church of Ireland Archbishop of Dublin, Richard Whately (1833–1836), argued that Irish poverty was too widespread for workhouses and recommended assisted emigration, public works and agricultural improvement.
  • The Whig government rejected the Whately report as too costly and sent George Nicholls, an English poor law commissioner, who toured Ireland in 1836 and recommended the English workhouse system.
  • The Irish Poor Law Act (July 1838) divided Ireland into 130 unions, each with a workhouse run by a board of guardians elected by ratepayers, with local magistrates sitting ex officio.
  • Relief was given only inside the workhouse (indoor relief), and conditions were made deliberately harsher than the life of the poorest labourer so that only the truly destitute would apply (the workhouse test).
  • Families were separated by sex and age on entry, inmates wore a uniform and worked, and admission carried a stigma that the poor deeply resented.
  • The cost was met by a poor rate on property, shared between landlord and occupier, and from 1843 landlords paid the whole rate on holdings valued at under £4.
  • The workhouses had room for about 100,000 people and were designed for normal destitution, not mass famine; most had only recently opened when the potato failed in 1845.

The Repeal campaign and Repeal Year, 1840–1843

  • O'Connell founded the Repeal Association in April 1840 (first called the National Association for Full and Prompt Justice or Repeal), with a penny-a-month Repeal Rent modelled on the Catholic Rent.
  • Repeal meant restoring a separate Irish Parliament under the Crown, not separation from Britain; O'Connell often hinted he would accept a federal compromise or substantial reform instead.
  • Support grew slowly until Dublin Corporation, led by O'Connell, debated Repeal in early 1843, and he declared 1843 Repeal Year.
  • The Catholic clergy again acted as local organisers, and Father Theobald Mathew's temperance movement, though separate, supplied disciplined crowds and bands.
  • About forty monster meetings were held in 1843, mostly on sites with historic associations such as Tara, Mullaghmast and Clontarf.
  • The largest was at the Hill of Tara on 15 August 1843; supporters claimed up to a million attended, and even hostile estimates ran to hundreds of thousands.
  • The Nation newspaper publicised the meetings and gave the campaign a younger, more militant tone.
  • In May 1843 Peel told the Commons that he would accept any alternative, even war, rather than the dismemberment of the empire, and the government dismissed Repeal magistrates and reinforced the army in Ireland.

Clontarf, the state trial and the decline of Repeal, 1843–1847

  • The government banned the monster meeting called for Clontarf on 8 October 1843 the evening before it was due, and troops and artillery occupied the site.
  • O'Connell cancelled the meeting and sent people home, fearing a massacre; this preserved his policy of moral force but exposed the fact that he would not go beyond the law.
  • He and other leaders, including Charles Gavan Duffy, were arrested in October 1843 and convicted of conspiracy in 1844 by a jury from which Catholics had been excluded.
  • Sentenced to a year in prison and a £2,000 fine, he spent about three months in comfortable confinement in the Richmond Bridewell before the House of Lords quashed the verdict in September 1844.
  • Peel combined coercion with conciliation: the Devon Commission on land (1843), the Charitable Bequests Act (1844), a permanent and larger grant to Maynooth College (1845) and the Queen's Colleges (1845).
  • O'Connell was 68 in 1843, and after prison his energy and health declined; he talked of a federal alternative, which confused supporters.
  • From 1845 the Famine destroyed the Repeal Rent and the mass following, and O'Connell renewed his alliance with the Whigs when Lord John Russell took office in 1846.
  • Why Repeal failed: both British parties, Parliament and Protestant Ulster opposed it; Peel called O'Connell's bluff at Clontarf; the movement was overwhelmingly Catholic; and Famine and the Young Ireland split followed.

Young Ireland and The Nation, 1842–1846

Young Ireland was a group of mostly young, middle-class writers within the Repeal movement who developed a cultural idea of the Irish nation.

  • Charles Gavan Duffy, a Catholic journalist from Monaghan, Thomas Davis, a Protestant barrister from Mallow, and John Blake Dillon, a Catholic barrister from Mayo, founded The Nation, first published on 15 October 1842.
  • The Nation became the most widely read paper in Ireland, passed from hand to hand and read aloud in reading rooms; it published history, ballads and essays.
  • Its idea of nationality was cultural and non-sectarian: a nation shared history, culture and, ideally, the Irish language, and included Protestants as well as Catholics.
  • The Library of Ireland, a series of cheap books on Irish history and literature launched by Duffy, extended the paper's educational work.
  • Young Ireland disagreed with O'Connell over federalism (1844), over his alliance with the Whigs, and over the Queen's Colleges (1845), which Davis welcomed as mixed education and O'Connell condemned as godless.
  • The final break came over the peace resolutions of July 1846, which O'Connell proposed so that the Association would declare the use of force never justified, clearing the way for his Whig alliance.
  • Thomas Francis Meagher's Sword Speech in reply, refusing to condemn force in all circumstances, led Young Ireland to walk out of the Repeal Association.

The Irish Confederation and the 1848 rising

  • The Young Irelanders founded the Irish Confederation in January 1847, led by William Smith O'Brien, a Protestant landowner and MP for Limerick, with Duffy and Meagher.
  • James Fintan Lalor argued in The Nation that the land question should be linked to national independence, an idea that influenced John Mitchel.
  • Mitchel broke with the Confederation's moderates, founded the United Irishman in February 1848 to preach open revolution, and urged tenants to refuse rent and resist food exports.
  • The February 1848 revolution in France, which overthrew Louis Philippe, encouraged the Confederates to plan a rising, and Smith O'Brien and Meagher took an address of support to Paris.
  • Mitchel was convicted under the new Treason Felony Act in May 1848 and transported for fourteen years, and in July 1848 the government suspended habeas corpus, forcing the leaders into a hasty rising.
  • On 29 July 1848 Smith O'Brien's small band besieged police in the Widow McCormack's farmhouse at Ballingarry, Co. Tipperary; two rebels were killed and the rest dispersed.
  • Reasons for failure: no preparation or weapons, a starving and demoralised population, strong opposition from the Catholic clergy, government foreknowledge, and leaders who would not risk lives or property.
  • Smith O'Brien, Meagher, Terence MacManus and Patrick O'Donohoe were sentenced to death for high treason at Clonmel; the sentences were commuted to transportation to Van Diemen's Land, while Dillon escaped to America.
  • James Stephens, wounded at Ballingarry, and John O'Mahony, who also took part in the rising, escaped to France and later founded the Fenian movement, so 1848 became a link in the physical force tradition.

Key personality: Daniel O'Connell, part 2 (1830–1847)

  • Aims after 1829: Repeal of the Union, the end of tithes, fair government and a share of office for Catholics, all by constitutional means.
  • Participant: he led the Irish party at Westminster, made the Lichfield House Compact, founded the Repeal Association, called Repeal Year and decided to cancel Clontarf.
  • He was shaped by events as much as he shaped them: Peel's firmness in 1843, the Famine from 1845 and Young Ireland's challenge set the limits of what he could achieve.
  • Strengths: an unrivalled orator and organiser, master of peaceful mass agitation, skilled parliamentarian and lawyer, and committed to religious liberty for all, including Jews and dissenters, and to the abolition of slavery.
  • Weaknesses: his reliance on bluff, his willingness to drop Repeal for Whig favours, rigid control of the movement, the patronage given to his family, and a Catholic-centred movement that alienated Protestants.
  • He died at Genoa on 15 May 1847 on his way to Rome; his heart was buried in Rome and his body in Glasnevin Cemetery, Dublin.
  • Contemporaries: to Catholic Ireland he was the Liberator; to the Times and British Tories he was a dangerous agitator; to Young Ireland in 1846 he was a tired leader who had sold Repeal to the Whigs.
  • Historians: Oliver MacDonagh and others see him as the founder of constitutional nationalism and mass democratic politics in Europe; critics note that Repeal failed and that his movement deepened the link between Catholicism and nationalism.

Key personality: Thomas Davis (1814–1845)

  • Born on 14 October 1814 in Mallow, Co. Cork, the son of an army surgeon, Davis was a Protestant who studied at Trinity College Dublin and was called to the bar in 1838.
  • He joined the Repeal Association in 1841 and co-founded The Nation in 1842, becoming its leading writer.
  • His essays argued for a nationality above religion, for the Irish language (he wrote that a people without a language of its own is only half a nation), for Irish history and for self-reliance.
  • His ballads, including A Nation Once Again and The West's Asleep, made romantic nationalism popular among the young.
  • He backed mixed, non-denominational education and welcomed the Queen's Colleges in 1845, which led to a bitter public clash with O'Connell.
  • He died of scarlet fever on 16 September 1845, aged 30, before the Famine took hold and before the peace resolutions split the movement.
  • Participant in the Repeal movement and Young Ireland, he was a witness rather than a leader in politics; his influence came through ideas and writing.
  • Contemporaries such as Duffy revered him as the soul of Young Ireland, while O'Connell's Catholic supporters distrusted his secular, mixed-education views.
  • Historians see him as the founder of cultural nationalism, an inspiration to Pearse and the Gaelic revival, and note that his ideal of a nation uniting Protestant and Catholic was never realised.

Assessment: O'Connell and Young Ireland, achievements and limits

  • O'Connell's achievement: he created mass constitutional politics, won practical reforms with the Whigs and gave Irish Catholics political confidence.
  • O'Connell's limit: Repeal, his central aim after 1829, failed completely, and the movement collapsed after Clontarf, his ill health and the Famine.
  • Tithe War achievement: popular resistance forced the 1838 settlement; limit: tithe was only shifted into rent, and the established Church survived until 1869.
  • Poor Law achievement: Ireland gained a national relief system and local elected boards; limit: the workhouse model failed when the Famine created mass rather than individual destitution.
  • Young Ireland's achievement: The Nation created a cultural, non-sectarian idea of the nation and a body of history and ballads that shaped later nationalism.
  • Young Ireland's limit: it split the national movement in 1846, its rising of 1848 was a fiasco, and its non-sectarian ideal won few Protestants.
  • Contribution to nationalism: O'Connell left the constitutional tradition later taken up by Butt and Parnell; Young Ireland left the cultural and physical force traditions taken up by the Fenians and the Gaelic revival.

Key terms

Nation
A people bound together by shared history, culture and language rather than by religion, the idea of Irish nationality that Thomas Davis and The Nation promoted from 1842.
Physical force republicanism
The belief that Irish independence could be won only by armed rebellion; Young Ireland's refusal to rule out force in 1846 and the 1848 rising passed this tradition to the Fenians.
Repeal
O'Connell's aim of dissolving the Union and restoring an Irish Parliament under the Crown, campaigned for by the Repeal Association from 1840.
Tithe
A tax on the occupiers of land, paid to support the Church of Ireland clergy, whose collection caused the Tithe War of the 1830s.
Lichfield House Compact
The informal agreement of 1835 in which O'Connell's MPs supported the Whig government in return for Irish reforms and fair appointments.
Monster meeting
One of the huge open-air Repeal rallies of 1843, held at historic sites such as Tara, to show the strength of popular support.
Peace resolutions
Resolutions O'Connell put to the Repeal Association in July 1846 declaring the use of force never justified, which caused the Young Ireland walk-out.
Workhouse test
The Poor Law principle that relief should be given only inside a workhouse whose conditions were harsher than the worst outside, so only the destitute would apply.
Board of guardians
The body, elected by ratepayers with ex officio magistrates, that ran each poor law union's workhouse and set the poor rate.
Cultural nationalism
Nationalism based on a distinctive history, literature and language, promoted by Young Ireland through The Nation.
