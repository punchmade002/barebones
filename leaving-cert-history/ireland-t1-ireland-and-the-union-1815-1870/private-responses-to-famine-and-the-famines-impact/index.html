Case study background: why private relief mattered, 1845–1849

  • Government relief was limited by laissez-faire and by the rule that Irish property should pay, so charity filled gaps in food, clothing, seed and medical care.
  • Private relief came from Irish local committees, landlords, the churches, the Society of Friends, British charities, emigrants and donors across the world.
  • Under Peel in 1845–1846 local relief committees of landlords, clergy and merchants raised subscriptions, and the government added grants to the sums they collected.
  • The worst-hit western districts had the fewest resident gentry and merchants, so the areas that most needed local charity could raise the least.
  • Newspaper reports and drawings from places such as Skibbereen, Co. Cork, in the winter of 1846–1847, especially in the Illustrated London News, made the Famine known in Britain and America and set off the great appeals of 1847.

Case study: the Society of Friends (Quakers)

  • Irish Quakers, a small community of prosperous merchants, set up the Central Relief Committee in Dublin on 13 November 1846, with Joseph Bewley and Jonathan Pim among its secretaries.
  • English Quakers formed a London committee, and William Forster and others, including the young James Hack Tuke, toured the west in the winter of 1846–1847 and reported on what they saw.
  • Their published letters and reports described cabins full of the dying and gave British readers the most trusted eyewitness accounts of the Famine.
  • The Friends opened soup kitchens in Cork and elsewhere in the winter of 1846–1847, and gave boilers, food and money to local committees regardless of religion.
  • American Quakers and other donors sent food shipments to the Committee, which distributed them through its network of local correspondents.
  • The Friends refused to proselytise and kept careful accounts, which earned them trust among Catholics and lasting gratitude.
  • From mid-1847 they withdrew from mass feeding, concluding that only the government had the resources, and turned to longer-term projects: seed distribution, fisheries and spinning schemes.
  • Their Transactions of the Central Relief Committee (1852) became a key source on conditions and relief.

Case study: the British Relief Association and international giving

  • The British Relief Association was founded in London on 1 January 1847 by bankers and merchants including Lionel de Rothschild to raise funds for Ireland and the Scottish Highlands.
  • A royal letter from Queen Victoria was read in churches in January 1847, the Queen gave £2,000, and the national day of fast and humiliation in March 1847 brought in large collections.
  • The Association raised about £470,000, the largest single private fund, and worked closely with the government, distributing food through Routh's commissariat.
  • Its agent, the Polish explorer Count Paul Strzelecki, worked in Westport and the west and used the funds to feed schoolchildren in the poorest unions in 1847–1848.
  • American relief included two warships lent by Congress, the Jamestown, which reached Cork in April 1847, and the Macedonian, both loaded with food raised by public subscription.
  • Donations also came from Irish emigrants in America and Britain, from India, Australia and Canada, from the Choctaw Nation in the United States and from the Ottoman Sultan.
  • Pope Pius IX issued the encyclical Praedecessores Nostros in March 1847, calling for prayers and collections for Ireland, and Catholic communities in Europe and America sent money.

Case study: the churches, local committees and souperism

  • Catholic priests sat on relief committees, wrote public letters describing conditions, ran collections and buried the dead; many died of fever.
  • Church of Ireland clergy, often the only resident educated men in poor parishes, were the backbone of many local committees and also died in large numbers.
  • Religious orders such as the Sisters of Charity and the Sisters of Mercy nursed fever victims and fed the poor in towns.
  • Souperism was the charge that some evangelical Protestants offered food, clothing or schooling only to those who attended Protestant services or sent children to Protestant schools.
  • Proselytising missions had begun before the Famine, as at Edward Nangle's Achill colony from the 1830s, and the Irish Church Missions founded by Rev. Alexander Dallas in 1849 expanded them in Connemara during and after the Famine.
  • Most Protestant relief, including the Quakers' and most Church of Ireland committees', was given without religious conditions, but the actions of a few tainted Protestant charity as a whole.
  • The legacy was lasting bitterness: taking the soup became a mark of shame in Catholic memory, converts were often ostracised, and the issue sharpened sectarian division.

Case study: landlord responses, from relief to clearance

  • Some landlords reduced or cancelled rents, imported food, set up soup kitchens and employed tenants, and a number of them were ruined by the cost or died of fever.
  • Others, burdened by debt, rent arrears and poor rates, cleared their estates, using the Gregory clause and the rule that landlords paid the rates on holdings under £4.
  • Mass evictions were recorded especially in Clare, Mayo and Galway; in the Kilrush union, Co. Clare, the poor law inspector Captain Arthur Kennedy reported the destruction of whole townlands.
  • Police returns, which began in 1849, recorded tens of thousands of families evicted between 1849 and 1854.
  • Assisted emigration: Major Denis Mahon of Strokestown, Co. Roscommon, paid passage to Canada for about 1,500 tenants in 1847; many died on the voyage or in quarantine, and Mahon was shot dead in November 1847.
  • Larger schemes followed, such as Lord Lansdowne's assisted emigration from his Kenmare estate, Co. Kerry, in the early 1850s, and many landlords found shipping tenants cheaper than supporting them in the workhouse.

Key personality: Asenath Nicholson (1792–1855)

  • Born Asenath Hatch on 24 February 1792 in Chelsea, Vermont, she was a teacher who, as a widow in New York, worked among the Irish poor and promoted temperance and a vegetarian diet.
  • She first came to Ireland in May 1844 and walked through almost every county, staying in cabins and reading the Bible to the people she met.
  • Her account of that tour, Ireland's Welcome to the Stranger (1847), described the poverty and the dependence on the potato before the Famine.
  • She returned in 1846 to give relief, set up a soup kitchen in Dublin's Liberties, then travelled to Ulster, Mayo, Erris and Arranmore, distributing food and clothing paid for by appeals in New York papers.
  • Participant and witness: she gave relief directly and independently rather than through a committee, and recorded what she saw at first hand.
  • Her Lights and Shades of Ireland (1850), whose Famine section was published as Annals of the Famine in Ireland (1851), condemned landlords, officials and proselytisers alike.
  • Contemporaries found her eccentric: some officials and clergy suspected her as a Protestant Bible reader, while the poor welcomed her; she left Ireland in 1848 and died in Jersey City in 1855.
  • Historians now value her as one of the most vivid and sympathetic eyewitnesses of the Famine, a woman relief worker who blamed the system rather than its victims.

Case study outcomes: what private relief achieved and why it shrank after 1847

  • Private relief saved many thousands of lives in 1846–1847, especially through Quaker soup kitchens and the British Relief Association's feeding of children.
  • It fed people faster than government schemes and reached remote places, and its reports exposed the scale of suffering to the public.
  • It could not match the scale of the crisis: total private giving was a small fraction of the roughly £7 million spent by the government, about half of it as loans.
  • Giving fell sharply after 1847 because of donor fatigue, the British commercial crisis of 1847, the belief that the Famine was over, and anger at the Young Ireland rising and at Irish ingratitude in 1848.
  • The Quakers concluded that private charity could not replace the state and withdrew from mass feeding, so by 1848 relief depended on the Poor Law.

The Famine's impact to 1870: population and land

  • Population fell from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.6 million in 1851 and about 5.4 million in 1871.
  • The cottier and labourer classes were the hardest hit by death and emigration, and the potato-dependent class largely disappeared.
  • Holdings of 1 to 5 acres fell sharply in number, while farms over 15 and 30 acres increased, as landlords and surviving farmers consolidated land.
  • The Encumbered Estates Act (1849) set up a court to sell estates whose owners were bankrupt; by the late 1850s several thousand estates, perhaps a quarter of the land of Ireland, had been sold.
  • Most buyers were Irish land speculators and businessmen rather than the British capitalists the government hoped for, and many new landlords raised rents and cleared tenants more readily than the old.
  • Agriculture shifted from tillage to pasture: cattle and sheep needed less labour and rising British demand made them more profitable.

The Famine's impact to 1870: society, language and politics

  • Subdivision ended: farmers now passed the whole farm to one son, while the other children emigrated, entered the Church or married into other farms.
  • Marriage came later and fewer people married, because a son had to wait to inherit the farm; the arranged match between farming families became the norm.
  • The dowry became central to the match: the bride's family paid money or stock to the groom's family, often used to give a dowry to his sister.
  • Emigration became a habit: remittances and prepaid tickets from relatives abroad paid the passage of later emigrants (see the notes on post-Famine Ireland).
  • The Irish language declined fastest, because its heartlands in the west and south lost most people; the 1851 census recorded about 1.5 million speakers, and English became the language of advancement.
  • The Famine left lasting hatred of landlordism and of British government among Irish Americans and at home, which fed Fenianism and, later, the Land War.
  • The Catholic Church gained from the changed society: the surviving farming class was more devout and better able to support priests, nuns and church building.

Assessment: private relief and the impact of the Famine

  • Achievement of private relief: speed, compassion and practical aid, above all by the Quakers, whose non-sectarian work is still remembered with respect.
  • Limit of private relief: it was too small, short-lived and unevenly spread, weakest where it was most needed, and shrank after 1847.
  • Damage: souperism and landlord clearances meant that some private responses deepened suffering and division rather than relieving them.
  • Impact during the Famine: about a million deaths, mass eviction and flight; impact after: a smaller, older, more commercial and more Catholic-devout rural society.
  • Contemporary British opinion often saw the Famine as an opportunity to modernise Irish agriculture; Irish nationalists, led by Mitchel, saw it as a crime of British government.
  • Historians stress continuity as well as change: consolidation, pasture and emigration had begun before 1845, but the Famine greatly speeded them up.

Key terms

Souperism
The giving of food or other relief on condition of attending Protestant services or schools, a practice of some evangelical missions that left lasting bitterness.
Central Relief Committee
The Society of Friends' Dublin committee, set up on 13 November 1846, which ran soup kitchens and non-sectarian relief.
British Relief Association
The London charity founded on 1 January 1847 that raised about £470,000 for Famine relief and fed schoolchildren through Count Strzelecki.
Assisted emigration
The payment of tenants' passage overseas by landlords or others to clear estates, as by Major Denis Mahon at Strokestown in 1847.
Encumbered Estates Act
The 1849 Act that set up a court to sell estates whose owners were bankrupt, bringing in new landlords.
Consolidation
The merging of small holdings into larger farms after the Famine, which ended subdivision and the cottier class.
Dowry
Money or stock brought by a bride to the groom's family under the arranged farm match that became the norm after the Famine.
Landlordism
After the Famine, the system of landlord ownership that many blamed for evictions and clearances, which made it a lasting target of nationalist anger.
