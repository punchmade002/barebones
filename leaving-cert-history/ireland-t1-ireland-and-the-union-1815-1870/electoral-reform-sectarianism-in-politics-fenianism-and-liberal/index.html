Electoral reform and the Irish franchise

The Irish electorate was cut sharply in 1829, widened a little in 1832 and widened again in the counties in 1850, but voting remained open and influenced by landlords and clergy.

  • Alongside the Emancipation Act of 1829, a second Act abolished the forty-shilling freeholders and raised the county franchise to £10, cutting the Irish county electorate to about 37,000.
  • The Irish Reform Act of 1832 raised Irish seats from 100 to 105 and gave the vote in the boroughs to £10 householders, but the electorate stayed small beside Britain's.
  • Voting was open, not secret, until the Ballot Act of 1872, so landlords, agents and priests could pressure tenants and parishioners.
  • The Irish Franchise Act of 1850 gave the county vote to occupiers rated at £12 or more and the borough vote to those rated at £8 or more.
  • The 1850 Act greatly enlarged the county electorate, which was several times larger than before, while the borough electorate stayed small and even fell.
  • A larger county electorate of tenant farmers helped the Tenant League win seats in 1852, but the Famine, emigration and eviction had already cut the number of small tenants.

Sectarianism in politics: the Orange Order, Dolly's Brae and the Ecclesiastical Titles Act

Religious rivalry shaped Irish politics at the end of the 1840s and in the early 1850s, with Orange marches and the Ecclesiastical Titles Act as flashpoints.

  • The Orange Order was founded in County Armagh in 1795, after the Battle of the Diamond, to defend the Protestant ascendancy and the link with Britain.
  • The Order was dissolved in 1836 after a parliamentary inquiry into its spread in the army, and was revived in Ulster in the 1840s.
  • On 12 July 1849 an Orange procession returning from Lord Roden's estate at Tollymore passed through Dolly's Brae near Castlewellan, County Down, where armed Catholic Ribbonmen had gathered.
  • Fighting followed and Catholics were killed, though the number is disputed (estimates range from a handful to dozens); Catholic homes nearby were burned.
  • An inquiry criticised the conduct of the Orangemen and the magistrate Lord Roden, and he was removed from the commission of the peace.
  • The Party Processions Act (1850) banned party marches, and it was repealed in 1872.
  • In September 1850 Pope Pius IX restored a Catholic hierarchy with territorial titles in England, and Lord John Russell, the Whig prime minister, denounced the move as papal aggression.
  • The Ecclesiastical Titles Act (1851) banned Catholic bishops from taking territorial titles in the United Kingdom; it was never enforced and was repealed in 1871.
  • Irish Catholic MPs and the Catholic Defence Association opposed the Act, which pushed Catholic and tenant-right politics together.

The Tenant League and the Independent Irish Party

A new alliance of tenant-right campaigners and Catholic MPs promised independent opposition in the 1850s and then collapsed.

  • The Irish Tenant League was founded in Dublin in 1850 by Charles Gavan Duffy, Frederick Lucas, a Catholic convert and editor of the Tablet, and others, including Presbyterian ministers from Ulster.
  • It demanded the three Fs: fair rent, fixity of tenure and free sale of the tenant's interest.
  • Catholic clergy, notably Archbishop MacHale and Fr Lavelle, supported it, but Cardinal Cullen distrusted its priests-in-politics.
  • At the 1852 election more than 40 MPs were returned pledged to independent opposition, forming the Independent Irish Party.
  • In December 1852 William Keogh and John Sadleir accepted posts in Lord Aberdeen's government, Keogh as solicitor-general for Ireland and Sadleir as a Lord of the Treasury, breaking the pledge of independence.
  • Duffy, Lucas and others denounced them as betrayers, and the party fractured as members drifted to the Liberals.
  • Lucas died in 1855 and Duffy emigrated to Australia in 1855, ending the movement; Sadleir died by suicide in 1856 after the collapse of his Tipperary Bank, and Keogh became a judge.
  • Many nationalists lost faith in parliamentary action, which helped turn young men towards Fenianism.

Fenianism: origins and organisation

Fenianism was a transatlantic revolutionary movement that aimed to end British rule by force and set up an Irish republic.

  • The Irish Republican Brotherhood (IRB) was founded in Dublin on 17 March 1858 by James Stephens, with Thomas Clarke Luby and others, as an oath-bound secret society.
  • In New York in the same year John O'Mahony founded the Fenian Brotherhood, which raised money and men in the United States and took its name from the legendary Fianna.
  • Roots lay in the failed 1848 rising, the Famine bitterness, the disillusion with parliamentary politics after the Independent Irish Party and the exile experience of emigrants.
  • The IRB was organised in circles under local leaders and reported to Stephens, who took the title of Chief Organiser of the Irish Republic.
  • Its members were mainly artisans, clerks, shopkeepers and farmers' sons, together with soldiers in the British army whom the IRB tried to recruit.
  • The funeral of Terence Bellew MacManus, a 1848 rebel, in Dublin in 1861 showed Fenian strength, and Cullen refused to allow his body to lie in the Pro-Cathedral.
  • The movement's newspaper, the Irish People, began in Dublin in 1863 with Luby, John O'Leary and Charles Kickham as editors and Jeremiah O'Donovan Rossa as manager.
  • American Fenians included veterans of the Civil War, whose military experience raised hopes of a rising in Ireland.

Suppression, the 1867 rising and Fenian actions abroad

The government crushed the Irish organisation before it could rise, and the 1867 rising failed, but Fenian actions in England and Canada kept the movement in the public eye.

  • In September 1865 the police raided the Irish People offices and arrested Luby, O'Leary, Rossa and Kickham, and in November Stephens himself was arrested.
  • Stephens escaped from Richmond Prison in Dublin in November 1865 with the help of warders, and went abroad.
  • Habeas corpus was suspended in Ireland in February 1866, allowing arrest without trial.
  • In 1866 Stephens postponed a rising he had promised, and in December American Fenian leaders removed him.
  • The rising took place in early March 1867 in bad weather: small risings in Dublin (Tallaght), Cork, Limerick and Tipperary were quickly crushed.
  • Informers had compromised the plans, and the Fenians lacked arms and trained leaders.
  • In September 1867 Fenians in Manchester freed two prisoners, Thomas Kelly and Timothy Deasy, from a prison van and killed a police sergeant, Charles Brett.
  • Three men, William Allen, Michael Larkin and Michael O'Brien, were hanged in November 1867 as the Manchester Martyrs and were mourned across Ireland.
  • In December 1867 a Fenian attempt to free a prisoner by blowing up a wall at Clerkenwell prison in London killed about a dozen people and turned British opinion against the Fenians.
  • Michael Barrett was hanged for Clerkenwell in 1868, the last public execution in England.
  • American Fenians launched raids into Canada, at Ridgeway in June 1866 and again in 1870, which failed to win Canadian territory.
  • Cardinal Cullen and most bishops condemned Fenianism as a secret society, and many priests denounced it.

Key personality: James Stephens (1825–1901)

Stephens founded and led the IRB, but his leadership failed at the decisive moment.

  • Born in Kilkenny in 1825, he took part in the 1848 rising at Ballingarry under William Smith O'Brien and escaped to Paris.
  • He returned to Ireland in 1856 and travelled the country, then founded the IRB in 1858 as its Chief Organiser.
  • He worked with O'Mahony's Fenian Brotherhood in America, which sent money and supporters to Ireland.
  • He organised the Irish People in 1863 and built a network by recruiting through secret circles, including in the British army.
  • He was arrested in November 1865, escaped within a fortnight and spent 1866 in the United States, failing to hold a rising on promised dates.
  • He was deposed by Fenian leaders in December 1866 and spent most of the rest of his life abroad in Paris; he returned to Ireland in 1891 and died in Dublin in 1901.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a gifted organiser and a charismatic figure but as autocratic, secretive and over-promising.
  • Historians credit him with building the first lasting revolutionary organisation, while criticising his indecision and his failure to launch the rising he had promised.

Key personality: Charles Kickham (1828–1882)

Kickham combined Fenian leadership with literature; he was a writer more than a conspirator.

  • Born in Mullinahone, County Tipperary, in 1828, he was left almost deaf and with poor sight by an accident with gunpowder as a boy.
  • He wrote for The Nation and joined the Fenians, becoming one of the editors of the Irish People.
  • Arrested in 1865, he was sentenced in 1866 to fourteen years' penal servitude and released in 1869 because of ill health, after a campaign for the release of Fenian prisoners.
  • He wrote the novel Sally Cavanagh (1869) about the eviction of a tenant family, and Knocknagow, or The Homes of Tipperary, which was very popular.
  • He became a leading member of the IRB's Supreme Council in the 1870s and died at Blackrock, near Dublin, in 1882.
  • A practising Catholic, he was caught between his faith and the bishops' condemnation of the movement.
  • Contemporaries admired his courage, honesty and endurance in prison.
  • Historians see him as the Fenian movement's voice for rural Ireland and its link between nationalism and literature.

Liberal reforms: Gladstone, the Church Act and the Land Act

After the Fenian scare, William Gladstone's first government (1868–74) tried to remove two Irish grievances.

  • Gladstone became prime minister in December 1868 and said that his mission was to pacify Ireland.
  • The Manchester executions and Clerkenwell showed that Irish discontent could threaten Britain and made reform politically urgent.
  • The Irish Church Act (1869) disestablished the Church of Ireland from 1 January 1871 and ended its status as the state church.
  • The Church lost most of its endowments, which passed to a commission, while serving clergy received compensation.
  • The Irish Land Act (1870) gave legal force to the Ulster custom of tenant right, which allowed outgoing tenants to sell their interest.
  • It gave tenants compensation for disturbance when evicted for reasons other than non-payment of rent, and for improvements.
  • Its Bright clauses offered loans to help tenants buy their holdings, but few did.
  • The Act did not stop evictions for non-payment, since landlords could raise rents, and so it failed to give security of tenure.
  • Fenian prisoners were released between 1869 and 1871 as the amnesty movement grew, and Isaac Butt founded the Home Government Association in 1870.

Assessment: Fenianism and Liberal reforms

Judgements turn on whether Fenianism or constitutional reform did more to change Irish politics by 1870.

  • Fenian achievement: it revived physical force republicanism, built a disciplined oath-bound organisation across Ireland, Britain and America, and kept the republican idea alive.
  • Fenian limit: it failed militarily in 1867, was heavily infiltrated by informers, split between Stephens and the American leaders, and was condemned by the Church.
  • Contemporary views: the British government saw a conspiracy; many Irish nationalists admired the martyrs, while bishops and moderate nationalists feared it.
  • Later views: historians argue that Fenianism, through the Manchester executions and Clerkenwell, prompted Gladstone's reforms, and that its legacy lasted into 1916.
  • Liberal achievement: disestablishment ended the Protestant church's privileged position, and the Land Act recognised tenants' claims for the first time.
  • Liberal limit: the Land Act failed to prevent eviction, and neither measure answered demands for tenant ownership or self-government.
  • Fenianism or Liberal reform: the first showed that violence could force reform; the second showed that reform could not fully remove the grievances behind violence.

Key terms

Fenianism
The revolutionary movement of the IRB in Ireland and the Fenian Brotherhood in the United States, which aimed to win an Irish republic by force.
Physical force republicanism
The belief that Irish independence as a republic could be won only by armed rebellion, which the Fenians adopted from 1858.
Sectarianism
Hostility between religious communities, here shown in Orange marches, Dolly's Brae and the reaction to the Ecclesiastical Titles Act.
Irish Republican Brotherhood
The secret oath-bound society founded in Dublin on 17 March 1858 by James Stephens to establish an Irish republic.
Fenian Brotherhood
The American sister organisation founded by John O'Mahony in 1858, which raised money and men for the cause.
Irish People
The Fenian newspaper founded in Dublin in 1863 and suppressed in September 1865.
Manchester Martyrs
Allen, Larkin and O'Brien, hanged in November 1867 after a police sergeant was killed in the rescue of two Fenian prisoners.
Tenant League
The tenant-right organisation founded in 1850 that demanded fair rent, fixity of tenure and free sale.
Independent Irish Party
The group of MPs elected in 1852 on a pledge of independent opposition, broken when Keogh and Sadleir took office.
Disestablishment
The ending of the Church of Ireland's status as the state church, passed in 1869 and in effect from 1 January 1871.
Ulster custom
The practice by which an Ulster tenant could sell his interest in a holding, legalised by the Land Act of 1870.
