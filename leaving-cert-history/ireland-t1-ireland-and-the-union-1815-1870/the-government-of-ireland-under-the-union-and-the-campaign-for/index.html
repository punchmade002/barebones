Westminster and Dublin Castle under the Union

The Act of Union (1800) abolished the Irish Parliament; from 1 January 1801 Ireland was part of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, governed by Westminster and a separate executive in Dublin.

  • Ireland sent 100 MPs to the House of Commons, a small minority of its roughly 650 members, so Irish measures depended on British votes.
  • Ireland was represented in the House of Lords by 28 Irish representative peers elected for life by the Irish peerage, and by four Church of Ireland bishops sitting in rotation.
  • The Union also united the Church of England and the Church of Ireland, so the Protestant Church of Ireland remained the established church of a mainly Catholic country.
  • William Pitt had hoped to follow the Union with Catholic Emancipation, but George III refused, and Pitt resigned in 1801; this broken expectation was the root of the Catholic question.
  • The Lord Lieutenant (viceroy), usually a British peer, headed the Irish executive and lived in Dublin Castle and the Viceregal Lodge in the Phoenix Park.
  • The Chief Secretary, a politician who sat in the Commons, ran Irish business in Parliament and became the most powerful figure in Irish government; Robert Peel held the post from 1812 to 1818.
  • The Under-Secretary was the senior permanent official in Dublin Castle, the name used for the whole Irish administration, which stayed separate from Whitehall.

Local government, justice and policing

  • Grand juries, chosen by the county sheriff from the leading Protestant landowners, levied the county cess (a local tax on land) and paid for roads, bridges, jails and courthouses.
  • Unpaid magistrates (justices of the peace), nearly all Protestant landowners, tried minor cases at petty sessions and quarter sessions, so most Catholics met the law through their landlords.
  • Town corporations such as Dublin Corporation were closed, self-electing Protestant bodies, which kept Catholics out of municipal office until the reforms of 1840.
  • Rural unrest by agrarian secret societies such as the Whiteboys and Ribbonmen led Peel to create the Peace Preservation Force in 1814, a paid force sent into disturbed districts at local expense.
  • The Irish Constabulary Act of 1822 set up a county constabulary under four provincial inspectors-general, Ireland's first regular rural police.
  • The Constabulary Act of 1836 merged these forces into the Irish Constabulary, an armed, centrally controlled national police under an inspector-general in Dublin; it became the Royal Irish Constabulary in 1867.
  • Ireland was policed more heavily and more centrally than Britain, which showed that the Union rested on coercion as well as consent.

The Catholic question in 1815: disabilities, the franchise and the veto

  • The Relief Act of 1793 had already given Catholics the vote on the same terms as Protestants, entry to the bar and to junior army commissions, and the right to sit on juries.
  • The county franchise belonged to forty-shilling freeholders, men who held land on a lease for life worth at least 40 shillings a year; landlords created many such tenancies so their tenants would vote as they directed.
  • Voting was open (spoken aloud at the poll), so a tenant who defied his landlord could be identified and evicted.
  • Catholics still could not sit in Parliament, because MPs had to take the oath of supremacy and declare against transubstantiation, which no Catholic could do.
  • They were also barred from the senior judiciary, the rank of King's Counsel, high government office and the senior ranks of the army and navy.
  • The veto controversy: from 1808 some supporters of relief proposed that the Crown should have a veto over the appointment of Catholic bishops as a security for Emancipation.
  • The Irish bishops rejected the veto, and in 1814 the Quarantotti rescript from Rome, which accepted it, was repudiated in Ireland; O'Connell led lay opposition, and the issue split the Catholic leadership and stalled the cause after 1815.

Case study: the Catholic Association and the Catholic Rent, 1823–1825

The Catholic Association turned a gentry petitioning campaign into the first mass political movement in Irish or British history.

  • Daniel O'Connell and Richard Lalor Sheil founded the Catholic Association in Dublin in May 1823; at first it was a small body of lawyers and gentry paying a full membership fee of one guinea a year.
  • In 1824 O'Connell introduced associate membership at a penny a month, the Catholic Rent, which brought in tenant farmers, labourers and townspeople in their hundreds of thousands.
  • The Rent was collected through the parishes by collectors under the supervision of the local priest, so the Church's parish network became the Association's organisation.
  • Its funds paid for lawyers to defend Catholics in the courts, petitions to Parliament, newspaper publicity and, later, election campaigns.
  • Priests read the Association's addresses from the altar and chaired meetings; Bishop James Doyle of Kildare and Leighlin (who signed himself JKL) was its most influential clerical supporter.
  • The Association widened its demands beyond Emancipation to tithes, the grand jury system and the treatment of Catholics in the courts, which made it attractive to the rural poor.
  • O'Connell insisted on strictly legal, non-violent methods while warning that the government could not ignore millions of organised Catholics, a tactic of moral force backed by implied threat.

Case study: suppression and the Waterford election, 1825–1826

  • Alarmed by its growth, the government passed the Unlawful Societies (Ireland) Act in 1825 to suppress the Association.
  • O'Connell evaded the Act by re-forming the body as the New Catholic Association in July 1825, limiting it to activities the law still allowed.
  • In 1825 Sir Francis Burdett's Catholic relief bill passed the Commons but was defeated in the Lords, showing that petitioning alone would not succeed.
  • At the general election of June 1826 the Association used the Rent organisation as election committees in County Waterford, with Thomas Wyse a leading organiser.
  • The forty-shilling freeholders, led to the poll by their priests, elected the pro-Emancipation Protestant Henry Villiers-Stuart and unseated Lord George Beresford, whose family had dominated the county.
  • Pro-Emancipation candidates also won in Louth, Monaghan and Westmeath, breaking the landlords' hold over tenant votes.
  • Landlords punished some freeholders who had voted against them, and the Association used part of the Rent to support those evicted; the lesson was that the Catholic vote could defeat the ascendancy at the polls.

Case study: the Clare election, 1828

  • In 1828 the Wellington government appointed William Vesey-FitzGerald, MP for County Clare, as President of the Board of Trade; taking office meant he had to stand for re-election.
  • Vesey-FitzGerald was a popular landlord who supported Emancipation, but the Association decided to oppose any member of a government that resisted it.
  • O'Connell himself stood, although as a Catholic he could not take the oath needed to sit if elected.
  • Priests marched their parishioners in orderly columns to the poll at Ennis, and the campaign passed off without violence.
  • O'Connell won on 5 July 1828 by 2,057 votes to 982, defeating a sitting minister.
  • The result faced the government with the prospect of Catholic candidates winning most Irish county seats at the next general election and being unable to take them.
  • O'Connell was not allowed to take his seat in 1828; he was returned unopposed for Clare again in July 1829, after the Relief Act, and entered the Commons in 1830.

Case study: why Wellington and Peel conceded

  • The Duke of Wellington (Arthur Wellesley), prime minister from 1828, and Robert Peel, Home Secretary, had both opposed Emancipation.
  • They feared that refusing it after Clare risked a rising or civil war in Ireland, where many soldiers of the army were themselves Irish Catholics.
  • The Lord Lieutenant, the Marquess of Anglesey, warned that Ireland was close to rebellion and openly favoured concession; he was recalled in early 1829.
  • The Commons had already voted for Catholic relief several times, so resistance depended on the Lords and the king.
  • The repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts in 1828, which had removed the disabilities of Protestant dissenters, weakened the argument that the constitution must remain exclusively Anglican.
  • Peel concluded that civil strife was a greater danger than Emancipation, and Wellington overcame George IV's resistance by threatening resignation in March 1829.
  • To make concession acceptable, Parliament first passed an act in February 1829 suppressing the Catholic Association, which O'Connell then dissolved.

Case study: the Catholic Relief Act, 1829, and reactions to it

  • The Roman Catholic Relief Act received royal assent on 13 April 1829.
  • Granted: Catholics could sit in both Houses of Parliament under a new oath of allegiance, and could hold almost all civil and military offices, including judgeships.
  • Withheld: Catholics could not be Lord Lieutenant of Ireland, Lord Chancellor or regent, or advise the Crown on appointments in the established church.
  • Restrictions: Catholic bishops could not take the titles of existing Church of Ireland sees, and the Act placed limits on religious orders, especially the Jesuits, and on public processions.
  • Its companion, the Parliamentary Elections (Ireland) Act 1829, raised the county franchise from forty shillings to £10, cutting the Irish electorate from about 216,000 to about 37,000.
  • The Act was not retrospective, so O'Connell had to seek re-election rather than take the Clare seat he had already won.
  • Protestant reaction: Brunswick Clubs had been formed in 1828 to defend the Protestant constitution, Orange opinion felt betrayed, and O'Connell called the Home Secretary Orange Peel.
  • Peel resigned his Oxford University seat in 1829 because he had reversed his position and wished to seek his constituents' approval; he lost the by-election and returned to Parliament for Westbury.
  • Wellington fought a duel with the Earl of Winchilsea in March 1829 after Winchilsea accused him of plotting against Protestantism; ultra-Tory anger split the Tory party and helped bring down Wellington's government in 1830.

Key personality: Daniel O'Connell, part 1 (to 1829)

  • Born on 6 August 1775 at Carhen near Cahersiveen, Co. Kerry, into a Catholic gentry family, he was raised by his uncle Maurice O'Connell of Derrynane and later inherited Derrynane.
  • He was educated at St Omer and Douai in France, which he left in 1793; the violence of the French Revolution left him with a lifelong hatred of political bloodshed, reinforced by the 1798 rebellion.
  • He was called to the Irish bar in 1798 and became the most successful barrister in Ireland, known for defending poor Catholics in the courts.
  • His first public speech, in January 1800, was against the Union, and he led the anti-veto party in the Catholic Committee and Catholic Board in the 1810s.
  • In 1815 he killed John D'Esterre in a duel arising from his attack on Dublin Corporation; he regretted it for the rest of his life.
  • Participant: he founded and led the Catholic Association, invented the Catholic Rent, decided to stand in Clare and was the campaign's chief speaker and strategist.
  • His methods were mass organisation, alliance with the clergy, legal agitation, monster petitions, public meetings, newspapers and the threat that the movement might slip out of control.
  • Contemporaries: Catholics hailed him as the Liberator; Protestants and the British press saw him as a dangerous demagogue and agitator.
  • Historians such as Oliver MacDonagh present him as the founder of modern mass democratic politics, while noting that Emancipation was conceded by Wellington and Peel to avoid a crisis, at the cost of the forty-shilling vote.

Assessment: how far was Emancipation O'Connell's victory, and what did it mean?

  • For O'Connell: the Rent, the Association and the Clare candidacy were his ideas, and without the crisis he created, Wellington and Peel would not have moved in 1829.
  • Against: the clergy supplied the organisation, Wyse and local leaders won Waterford, British Whigs and liberal Tories had long backed relief, and the final decision was the ministers' own.
  • Political dimension: Catholics could now enter Parliament and office, and a disciplined, peaceful mass movement had forced a reluctant government to change the constitution.
  • Political limits: the £10 franchise disfranchised most of the voters who had won the victory, and Protestants still dominated the magistracy, grand juries, corporations and Dublin Castle.
  • Religious dimension: the campaign fused Catholic and national identity and gave the Church a central place in Irish politics, which strengthened sectarian division.
  • Religious limits: the Church of Ireland stayed established and Catholics still paid tithes to it, the grievance behind the Tithe War of the 1830s.
  • Contemporary British view: a dangerous surrender to agitation; Irish Catholic view: a triumph that proved organised peaceful pressure worked.
  • Later view: the Emancipation campaign was O'Connell's greatest success because its aim was limited and could be granted within the Union, unlike Repeal later.

Key terms

The Union
The constitutional arrangement created by the Act of Union (1800), in force from 1 January 1801, under which Ireland lost its own parliament and was governed from Westminster and Dublin Castle as part of the United Kingdom.
Catholic Emancipation
The removal of the laws that kept Catholics out of Parliament and high office, achieved by the Roman Catholic Relief Act of April 1829.
Sectarianism
Division and hostility between religious communities, here between Catholics and Protestants, seen in the Protestant monopoly of power and in the Catholic and Brunswick Club mobilisations of the 1820s.
Catholic Association
The mass movement founded by O'Connell and Sheil in May 1823 to win Emancipation, funded by the Catholic Rent and organised through the parishes.
Catholic Rent
Associate membership of the Catholic Association at a penny a month, introduced in 1824 and collected in each parish.
Forty-shilling freeholder
A tenant with a life lease of land worth at least 40 shillings a year, who held the county vote until the franchise was raised to £10 in 1829.
Veto controversy
The dispute after 1808 over whether the Crown should be able to veto appointments of Catholic bishops in return for Emancipation, which the Irish bishops and O'Connell rejected.
Dublin Castle
The seat and shorthand name of the Irish executive under the Union, headed by the Lord Lieutenant, Chief Secretary and Under-Secretary.
Grand jury
A county body of leading landowners that raised the county cess and controlled local public works before the reform of local government.
Brunswick Clubs
Protestant associations formed in 1828 to resist Catholic Emancipation and defend the Protestant constitution.
