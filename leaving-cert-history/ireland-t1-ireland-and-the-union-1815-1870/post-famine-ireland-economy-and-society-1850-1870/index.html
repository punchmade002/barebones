Post-Famine agriculture and landlordism

The Famine left fewer, larger and more commercial farms, with livestock replacing tillage and a more prosperous stronger-farmer class.

  • Population fell from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.5 million in 1851, about 5.8 million in 1861 and about 5.4 million in 1871.
  • Tillage declined and pasture expanded, because cattle, sheep and dairy produce rose in price relative to grain and needed less labour.
  • Cattle were exported live to Britain, a trade carried by the new railways and steamships, and the growing British towns were the market.
  • Small holdings disappeared as cottiers died or emigrated and landlords consolidated plots; farms above fifteen acres became far more common.
  • The stronger farmers, holding large farms on long tenure, were the main gainers; they dominated parish life and later supplied much of the support for land and nationalist movements.
  • Agricultural labourers fell in number and remained poor, and the small farmers of the west stayed close to subsistence.
  • The Encumbered Estates Act (1849) and the Landed Estates Court (1858) sold bankrupt estates; some new owners were commercial investors who raised rents and cleared tenants.
  • Landlordism survived: the landlord class still owned almost all the land, and tenants had no legal security of tenure until the Land Act of 1870 gave limited protection.
  • Evictions fell from their Famine peak but remained a threat, so tenant right stayed a central grievance of the 1850s and 1860s.

Marriage, the dowry and family life

The Famine hardened a pattern of late marriage, celibacy and single-heir inheritance that kept farms intact and pushed other children abroad.

  • A dowry was money or land that a bride's family gave on her marriage; farmers used it to portion a sister, to buy out siblings or to pay debts.
  • Farms usually passed intact to one son, so a farmer could marry only when his father handed over the holding and a bride with a dowry was found.
  • Matchmaking was widespread, and the match depended on land and money more than on affection.
  • Marriage was late, often in the thirties, and a large share of men and women never married, especially in the west.
  • Younger sons and daughters without a dowry had to choose between emigration, farm labour, the priesthood or religious life, and domestic service.
  • Late marriage and celibacy kept the birth rate down, so the population did not recover after the Famine.

Emigration after 1850 (brief)

Large-scale emigration continued long after the Famine and became part of normal life; the diaspora is covered in detail in the notes on emigration and the Irish abroad.

  • About a million people left Ireland in the 1850s, and emigration stayed high through the 1860s.
  • The United States was the main destination, followed by Britain, Canada and Australia; Liverpool and Cork (Queenstown) were major ports of departure.
  • Emigrants were young and unmarried, and women left in numbers roughly equal to men.
  • Chain migration meant that relatives and neighbours already abroad sent news and prepaid passages, so people followed established routes to particular towns.
  • Remittances sent home helped families pay rent and passage costs for further relatives.
  • Emigration relieved pressure on land, but it drained the countryside of young people and fed the idea of exile in nationalist politics.

The national school system and primary education

The national school system, set up in 1831, became the main provider of primary education and a major influence on literacy and language.

  • The system was set up in 1831 through a letter from the Chief Secretary, Edward Stanley, and run by a board of Commissioners of National Education.
  • Its aim was combined literary and moral education for children of all denominations, with religious instruction given separately by each church.
  • The state paid grants, supplied textbooks and inspected schools, and a training college for teachers opened in Dublin.
  • The churches contested the plan: the Church of Ireland set up its own Church Education Society (1839), Presbyterians objected at first, and the Catholic bishops were divided between Archbishop Daniel Murray, who co-operated, and Archbishop John MacHale of Tuam, who opposed.
  • In practice most schools became denominational in character, because local clergy managed them and most pupils in each district were of one faith.
  • After 1850 Cardinal Cullen pressed for greater Catholic control of education.
  • The Christian Brothers, founded in Waterford in 1802 by Edmund Rice, ran their own free schools for poor Catholic boys and spread in the towns.
  • Lessons were in English, and the system reinforced the decline of the Irish language by teaching through English and treating Irish as no help to a career.
  • School attendance and literacy rose steadily, and by 1870 roughly a million children were on the rolls.

Higher education: the Queen's Colleges and the Catholic University

Higher education divided on religious lines, and the dispute fed into the Synod of Thurles.

  • Peel's government founded the Queen's Colleges at Belfast, Cork and Galway by an Act of 1845; they opened in 1849 and formed the Queen's University of Ireland in 1850.
  • They were non-denominational and had no chapel or chair of theology, so critics called them godless colleges.
  • The Catholic hierarchy was divided, but Rome condemned the colleges in 1847 and the Synod of Thurles in 1850 forbade Catholics to attend, and Cullen backed a Catholic alternative.
  • The Catholic University opened in Dublin in 1854 with John Henry Newman as first rector; it gave no state-recognised degrees and had few students, and Newman resigned in 1858.
  • Trinity College Dublin, an Anglican institution, remained the main university for Protestants, and Catholics were admitted to degrees but not to fellowships.
  • The Queen's College in Belfast served the Presbyterian middle class of Ulster and was the most successful of the three.

Railways and transport

The railway network was built from 1834 and was largely complete by about 1860, changing trade, towns and communications.

  • The Dublin and Kingstown Railway opened in December 1834 and ran about six miles from Westland Row in Dublin to Kingstown (now Dún Laoghaire); it was the first railway in Ireland.
  • Growth began slowly: only about 65 miles were open in 1845, but the network grew to nearly 1,000 miles by 1855 and the core was largely in place by 1860.
  • The Great Southern and Western reached Cork in 1849, the Midland Great Western reached Galway in 1851, and Belfast was linked to Dublin in the 1850s.
  • Ireland adopted a broad gauge of 5 feet 3 inches, a decision of the 1840s that set a different gauge from Britain.
  • Railways cut the cost and time of moving cattle, butter, grain and fish, and tied farmers to British markets through the ports.
  • Fairs and markets shifted to railway towns, and junctions such as Mallow, Limerick Junction and Athlone grew in importance.
  • Newspapers, mail and ideas travelled faster, so national newspapers reached the provinces and political movements could organise nationally.
  • Tourism grew, with the line to Killarney opening in 1853, and the rail and steamer link to Britain eased travel and emigration.
  • Some small ports lost trade as goods moved inland by rail.

Key personality: William Dargan (1799–1867)

Dargan was the most successful Irish railway contractor of his day and a public figure who promoted Irish industry.

  • Born in 1799 near Carlow town, he trained as a surveyor and engineer in England and worked under Thomas Telford on the Holyhead road.
  • He won the contract for the Dublin and Kingstown Railway, which opened in 1834, and went on to build lines including the Dublin and Drogheda and the Ulster Railway, becoming the leading contractor in Ireland.
  • He also built the Ulster Canal and dredged the Victoria Channel at Belfast, which created Queen's Island, later the Harland and Wolff site.
  • In 1853 he financed the Dublin Industrial Exhibition on Leinster Lawn, which drew over a million visitors, and Queen Victoria visited it.
  • He lost heavily on the Exhibition, and his reputation as an Irish industrial pioneer was the main reward; he reportedly refused the offer of a baronetcy.
  • A fall from a horse in 1866 and the failure of some of his ventures ended his career; he died in Dublin on 7 February 1867.
  • Contemporaries praised him as a self-made Catholic businessman who employed thousands and acted from public spirit, and the National Gallery of Ireland (1864) was founded partly as his memorial.
  • Historians see him as a symbol of Irish enterprise within the British economy, but his work depended on railway investment more than on a wider Irish industrial base.

Belfast and industrial development

Belfast became an industrial city of linen, engineering and shipbuilding, unlike the largely agricultural south.

  • Mechanised flax spinning arrived in the 1820s and 1830s, and linen became the dominant industry of Belfast and the Lagan valley.
  • The American Civil War (1861–65) cut off cotton supplies, and the cotton famine raised demand and prices for linen.
  • Belfast's population grew from about 100,000 in 1851 to over 170,000 by 1871, with migrants from the Ulster countryside and Famine-hit areas.
  • Harbour works, including Dargan's Victoria Channel, allowed larger ships to reach the port, and engineering firms grew up to serve the mills.
  • Edward Harland took over Robert Hickson's shipyard on Queen's Island in 1858, and in 1861 he and Gustav Wolff formed Harland and Wolff.
  • Ulster had skilled Protestant labour, capital from the linen trade, local business networks and strong trade links with Britain.
  • Belfast had no local coal or iron, but cheap imports by sea and rail made industry viable.
  • Industry in the south declined under competition from British factory goods once tariffs were gone, and Dublin and Cork kept only brewing, distilling and food processing.
  • Catholics, mostly migrants from the countryside, made up about a third of Belfast's population, and they were concentrated in unskilled jobs and certain districts such as the Pound.
  • Sectarian riots broke out in Belfast in 1857 and 1864, tied to Orange marches and Catholic rivalry for housing and work.

Assessment: how far did Ireland recover, 1850–1870

Judgements differ on whether post-Famine Ireland was a story of recovery or of continuing weakness.

  • Achievement: farm incomes and living standards of the stronger farmers rose, and railways opened national markets.
  • Limit: the population kept falling, emigration continued and the rural poor of the west gained little.
  • Achievement: the national school system spread literacy to most of the population.
  • Limit: schooling in English weakened the Irish language.
  • Achievement: Belfast industrialised and grew faster than any other Irish town.
  • Limit: industrialisation was confined to the north-east, and the rest of Ireland depended on agriculture and on exporting people.
  • Contemporary view: many saw the stronger farmers' prosperity and the railways as evidence of improvement under the Union.
  • Later view: historians argue that the Famine made Ireland a more commercial but more unequal and emigrant society, with a prosperous farmer class above a shrinking labouring poor.

Key terms

Dowry
Money or land given by a bride's family on marriage, which after the Famine decided who could marry and who could inherit a farm.
Landlordism
The system in which a small landlord class owned almost all the land and let it to tenants, who had no security of tenure and could be evicted.
Stronger farmer
A tenant with a large holding who gained in wealth and influence after the Famine as small holdings were consolidated.
Consolidation of holdings
The joining of small plots into larger farms after the Famine, which ended the cottier class.
Chain migration
Emigration in which settled relatives and neighbours sent passage money and information to draw others abroad.
National school system
The state-funded primary school system set up in 1831 to give combined literary education to children of all denominations.
Queen's Colleges
Non-denominational university colleges at Belfast, Cork and Galway, founded in 1845 and opened in 1849, which the Catholic Church condemned.
Catholic University
The Catholic institution founded in Dublin in 1854 under John Henry Newman as an alternative to the Queen's Colleges.
Encumbered Estates Act
The 1849 Act that allowed heavily indebted estates to be sold, bringing new owners to many Irish estates.
Linen
The cloth made from flax whose mechanised production was the basis of Belfast's industrial growth.
