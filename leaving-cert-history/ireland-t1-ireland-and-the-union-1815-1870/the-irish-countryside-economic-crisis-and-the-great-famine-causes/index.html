The Irish countryside in 1815: who held the land

Land was the basis of wealth and power, and rural society was a ladder running from a small, mainly Protestant landlord class down to millions of landless labourers.

  • About 10,000 landlords, mostly Protestant, owned nearly all the land; many held estates in more than one county.
  • Absentee landlords lived in England or Dublin and ran their estates through agents, so rents left the locality and improvements were rare.
  • Middlemen leased large farms from landlords on long leases and sublet them in smaller lots at higher rents; landlords increasingly ended such leases after 1815.
  • Strong farmers held 30 acres or more, employed labour and often grazed cattle; they were the most secure and prosperous tenants.
  • Smallholders held between about 5 and 30 acres, mostly as tenants-at-will with no lease, who could be evicted at short notice.
  • Cottiers rented a cabin and a potato patch, paying their rent mainly in labour for the farmer or landlord.
  • Landless labourers hired conacre, a patch of manured ground taken for one season to grow potatoes, often at very high rents.
  • Most tenants had no legal security of tenure, and any improvement they made to their holding belonged to the landlord, which discouraged investment.

Population, subdivision and the potato

  • Ireland's population rose from about 5 million around 1800 to about 8.2 million at the 1841 census.
  • Early marriage and large families were common among the poor, because a couple needed only a potato patch and a cabin to set up home.
  • Holdings were subdivided among sons, so that by 1841 nearly half of all holdings were under 5 acres.
  • The potato yielded more food per acre than any grain, grew on poor soil and, with buttermilk, gave a nutritious diet.
  • By the 1840s about 3 million people depended on the potato almost entirely; an adult labourer ate up to about 12–14 lb a day.
  • The heavy-cropping but poor-quality lumper variety had spread widely among the poor, and potatoes could not be stored from one season to the next.
  • The 1841 census classed about 40% of all houses as fourth-class: one-roomed mud cabins, the lowest category of housing.

Economic depression, 1815–1845

  • Wartime demand had pushed up grain prices and rents during the French Wars; after Waterloo in 1815 prices fell steeply, but rents fell slowly.
  • Tenants fell into arrears and many landlords, themselves heavily in debt from wartime spending and family settlements, could not afford to invest.
  • Landlords began clearing smallholders and middlemen to consolidate farms, especially for more profitable pasture.
  • Rural industry declined: hand spinning and weaving of linen outside north-east Ulster collapsed against factory competition, and the Irish cotton industry declined after protective duties ended in 1824.
  • Partial famines and fever followed harvest failures in 1816–1817, 1822 and the early 1830s, as well as several local failures in the late 1830s.
  • Agrarian secret societies such as the Whiteboys, Rockites and Ribbonmen attacked landlords, agents and new tenants who took farms after evictions.
  • The Devon Commission (1843–1845), a royal commission of landlords chaired by the Earl of Devon, found insecurity of tenure, lack of compensation for improvements and extreme poverty, but no reform followed.
  • Seasonal migration to Britain for the harvest, and emigration of about a million people between 1815 and 1845, were the poor's main escape routes.

The course of the Famine, 1845–1850

  • Phytophthora infestans, a fungus-like organism probably brought from North America, rotted potatoes in the ground and in storage; it was first reported in Ireland in September 1845.
  • 1845: between a third and a half of the crop was lost, causing severe hardship but few deaths in the first winter.
  • 1846: the blight destroyed almost the entire crop, and the worst winter in memory followed.
  • 1847 (Black '47): deaths peaked in the first half of the year; the small crop that year was healthy, but so few seed potatoes had been planted that hunger continued.
  • 1848: the blight returned and the crop failed again, and 1849 brought further failure in parts of the west and a cholera epidemic.
  • Most victims died of disease rather than starvation: typhus and relapsing fever (both spread by lice), dysentery, scurvy and, in 1849, cholera.
  • Doctors, clergy, relief workers and workhouse staff died in large numbers from fever caught from the people they treated.
  • Regional variation: Connacht and west Munster suffered most, with Mayo, Sligo, Roscommon, Galway, Clare and west Cork worst hit; the east and north-east suffered least, though parts of Ulster also lost heavily.
  • About one million people died between 1846 and 1851, and over a million emigrated; the population fell from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.6 million in 1851.

Peel's response, November 1845 – June 1846

  • Peel's Conservative government secretly bought about £100,000 of Indian corn (maize) in the United States through Baring Brothers in late 1845.
  • The maize was sold at cost through government depots to keep prices down; it was hard to grind, caused stomach pain if badly cooked, and was nicknamed Peel's brimstone for its yellow colour.
  • A Relief Commission, set up in November 1845 and later chaired by Sir Randolph Routh of the army commissariat, coordinated hundreds of local relief committees, which raised subscriptions and bought food.
  • The Board of Works ran public works such as road building to give the poor wages to buy food, with costs shared between the Treasury and the local county cess.
  • Peel used the crisis to repeal the Corn Laws in June 1846, which lowered grain prices in the long run but did little for Ireland immediately; his party split and he resigned.
  • Mortality in the first season was low, so Peel's measures were later judged the most effective of the Famine, although the failure of 1845 was only partial.

Russell's Whig government, laissez-faire and the public works, 1846–1847

  • Lord John Russell's Whig government took office at the end of June 1846, committed to laissez-faire: the belief that government should not interfere with trade or the food market.
  • It refused to import food or control prices in most of Ireland, leaving supply to private merchants; government depots opened only in the west and sold at market prices.
  • The Labouchere Act (August 1846) relaunched the public works with the whole cost to be repaid from local taxes, and wages were set below local rates so as not to compete with private employers.
  • Numbers on the works rose to about 700,000 by March 1847; payment was by task work, often late, and weakened, poorly clad people worked in the hard winter of 1846–1847.
  • Wages could not keep up with food prices, which more than doubled, and the works produced many roads that led nowhere.
  • Faced with rising deaths and costs, the government closed the works from March 1847 and switched to direct feeding.

Soup kitchens, the Poor Law Extension Act and the end of relief, 1847–1850

  • The Temporary Relief Act (the Soup Kitchen Act, February 1847) set up government soup kitchens run by local committees, giving free rations to the destitute.
  • By July 1847 about three million people a day received rations, the most effective and cheapest relief of the Famine, but the kitchens were closed by September 1847.
  • The Poor Law Extension Act (June 1847) made the poor law responsible for all further relief, so that Irish property would pay for Irish poverty, and allowed outdoor relief for the able-bodied.
  • Its Gregory clause, proposed by William Gregory, MP for Dublin, refused relief to anyone holding more than a quarter of an acre of land, forcing smallholders to surrender their land to qualify.
  • Landlords used the clause and the rising poor rates to clear estates, since they paid the rates on smaller holdings; evictions rose sharply from 1847.
  • Many western unions went bankrupt because their ratepayers were ruined, and workhouses, holding far more people than they were built for, became centres of fever.
  • The Rate-in-Aid Act (1849) imposed a national rate of 6d in the pound on every union to support about twenty-two bankrupt unions in the west; Ulster guardians protested that the north was paying for the south and west.
  • The government treated the Famine as over by 1850, although local distress and high mortality continued in the west.

Key personality: Charles Trevelyan (1807–1886)

  • Born in Taunton, Somerset, in 1807, Trevelyan served in the East India Company in Bengal before becoming Assistant Secretary to the Treasury, its senior permanent official, in 1840.
  • He ran the Treasury's Famine relief from 1845, controlling the purchase of Peel's maize, the public works, the food depots and the soup kitchens.
  • Participant: he was the civil servant most responsible for carrying out the Whig policy of 1846–1847, though ministers such as Russell and Charles Wood, Chancellor of the Exchequer, set its principles.
  • He was an evangelical Protestant and a firm believer in laissez-faire and political economy; he feared that generous relief would make the Irish dependent.
  • He saw the Famine as an act of Providence that would force changes in the Irish land system and population, and blamed Irish landlords and the moral character of the people.
  • He insisted that relief costs fall on Irish property, closed the soup kitchens in 1847 and declared the Famine over, and his article and book The Irish Crisis (1848) defended the policy.
  • Contemporaries: the government knighted him in 1848 for his work; Irish nationalists, many relief workers and some officials in Ireland condemned his rigidity.
  • Later career: co-author of the Northcote–Trevelyan Report (1854) that created a merit-based civil service, and Governor of Madras from 1859.
  • Historians: Cecil Woodham-Smith's The Great Hunger (1962) made him the villain of the Famine; Robin Haines (2004) argued he was a scapegoat for ministers and worked hard within his orders; most see his ideology as typical of the Whig government.

Assessment: how effective were government responses?

  • Strength: Peel's maize, relief committees and public works kept deaths low in 1845–1846.
  • Strength: the 1847 soup kitchens showed that direct feeding could reach three million people a day cheaply.
  • Weakness: the Whigs' faith in laissez-faire meant no control of food prices or exports, and the public works paid too little, too late, to people too weak to work.
  • Weakness: the decision that Irish property must pay, through the Poor Law from 1847, put the burden on the poorest unions just when they were bankrupt.
  • Weakness: the Gregory clause turned relief into a tool for clearance, and relief was cut back in 1847 while hunger continued.
  • Food exports: grain, cattle, pigs and butter continued to leave Irish ports, though from 1847 imports, mainly of maize, were several times greater than grain exports.
  • John Mitchel, writing in 1861, said that the Almighty sent the potato blight but the English created the Famine; this nationalist view made the Famine a central grievance against the Union.
  • Historians such as Cormac Ó Gráda and Christine Kinealy agree the scale of death was not inevitable and that a richer UK could have spent far more; most reject the charge of deliberate genocide but blame ideology, parsimony and indifference.

Key terms

Landlordism
The system in which a small, mainly Protestant class of landlords owned the land and let it to tenants without security of tenure, often through middlemen and agents.
Economic depression
The slump in Irish agriculture after 1815, when prices fell, rural industry collapsed and harvest failures left the poor more dependent on the potato.
Laissez-faire
The belief that government should not interfere with the economy or the market, which led Russell's Whig government to leave food supply to private traders during the Famine.
Famine
A catastrophic shortage of food causing mass death from hunger and disease; in Ireland, the Great Famine of 1845–1850 caused by repeated failures of the potato crop.
Conacre
Land rented for one season, usually manured ground for potatoes, the main means by which landless labourers fed their families.
Cottier
A rural labourer who rented a cabin and potato patch, paying the rent mostly in labour.
Middleman
A tenant who leased land from the landlord on a long lease and sublet it in smaller lots at a profit.
Gregory clause
The quarter-acre clause of the Poor Law Extension Act 1847, which denied relief to anyone holding more than a quarter acre of land.
Rate-in-aid
A national poor rate of 6d in the pound imposed on every union in 1849 to support the bankrupt western unions.
Public works
Government-funded relief schemes such as road building, which gave the poor wages to buy food in 1846–1847.
