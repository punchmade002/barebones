Why Germany industrialised quickly after 1871

After unification Germany combined abundant coal and iron with a large internal market, strong banks and a well-trained workforce. It overtook Britain in several industries before 1914.

  • Germany's population grew from about 41 million in 1871 to about 67 million in 1914, providing both labour and a large home market.
  • Coal and iron: the Ruhr coalfield was the heart of heavy industry, with further coal in the Saar and Silesia and iron ore in Lorraine, taken from France in 1871.
  • Political unification created a single market with a common currency (the mark, 1873), a central bank (the Reichsbank) and uniform commercial law. The Zollverein of the 1830s had already removed customs barriers between most states.
  • Railways, built mainly in the 1840s and 1850s, grew from about 21,000 km in 1871 to over 60,000 km by 1913, linking the Ruhr to ports, and later were run by the states, which kept rates low for industry.
  • Banks such as Deutsche Bank (1870) and Dresdner Bank (1872) were 'universal banks' that lent to industry for long periods and sat on company boards, so firms could raise capital for heavy investment.
  • Education: Germany's Technische Hochschulen, universities and technical schools trained engineers and chemists, and firms set up research laboratories.
  • The French indemnity of 1871 gave the new Reich a financial boost, and the boom of the early 1870s (the Gründerzeit) ended in a stock market crash in 1873. The long depression that followed encouraged cartels and tariffs.
  • Cartels, agreements among firms to fix prices and output, were common in coal, steel and chemicals. The Rhenish-Westphalian Coal Syndicate (1893) controlled most of the Ruhr coal supply.
  • By 1913 Germany produced more than twice as much steel as Britain, and was the world's second industrial power after the USA.
  • Hard coal output rose from about 30 million tonnes in 1871 to about 190 million tonnes in 1913, and German exports of machinery, chemicals and electrical goods competed with British goods across the world.
  • The Hamburg-America Line and the North German Lloyd of Bremen built up one of the largest merchant fleets in the world, supported by the growth of ports such as Hamburg.

The second industrial revolution

From the 1880s new industries based on steel, chemicals and electricity grew faster than the older coal and textiles, and Germany was often the leader.

  • Steel: the basic (Thomas-Gilchrist) process of 1878 allowed the phosphoric iron ores of Lorraine to be turned into steel, and steel production expanded rapidly in the Ruhr.
  • Chemicals: BASF, Bayer and Hoechst, all founded in the 1860s, led the world in synthetic dyes and pharmaceuticals. Fritz Haber's ammonia synthesis, developed with BASF's Carl Bosch (1909-13), made artificial fertilisers and explosives possible.
  • Electricals: Werner von Siemens founded Siemens and Halske in 1847 and developed the dynamo and electric railway. Emil Rathenau founded the Deutsche Edison-Gesellschaft in 1883, renamed AEG in 1887.
  • Electric lighting, trams and power stations spread in cities in the 1890s, and by 1914 Siemens and AEG dominated German electrical engineering and exported across Europe.
  • Motor vehicles and engines: Karl Benz, Gottlieb Daimler and Rudolf Diesel developed the motor car and the diesel engine in the 1880s and 1890s (see the notes on the motor car).
  • Government patronage, the army and navy as customers, and the relationship between industry and science helped German firms to exploit new technology.
  • Germany's share of world manufacturing output passed Britain's in the years before 1914.

Key personalities: the Krupp family - Alfred and Friedrich Alfred

The Krupp works at Essen grew from a small steel workshop into the largest industrial enterprise in Europe, and became synonymous with German arms and industry.

  • Friedrich Krupp founded a cast steel works at Essen in 1811. His son Alfred Krupp (1812-87) took over as a teenager in 1826 and made it a major enterprise.
  • Alfred exhibited a flawless 2-ton cast steel ingot at the Great Exhibition in London in 1851, and won international attention. He developed seamless steel railway wheel tyres and, with the Bessemer process, steel rails.
  • He moved into artillery, producing breech-loading steel guns that Prussia adopted in the 1860s and which proved effective against France in 1870-71. He sold guns to many foreign governments, earning the nickname 'the Cannon King'.
  • Alfred built the Villa Hügel (1873) as the family's home and expanded the works to a workforce of tens of thousands by the time of his death in 1887.
  • Welfare and control: he built housing colonies for workers from the 1860s, and provided sickness and pension funds, schools and shops. He also expected loyalty and discouraged political activity, so historians describe this welfare as paternalism.
  • Friedrich Alfred Krupp (1854-1902) took over in 1887 and expanded into naval armour plate and, in 1902, shipbuilding by buying the Germania shipyard at Kiel. The Kaiser was a close friend and the fleet was a large customer.
  • A scandal over Friedrich Alfred's private life in Capri was reported in the press in November 1902, and he died that month; whether by suicide or stroke is disputed. Wilhelm II attended the funeral.

Key personalities: the Krupp family - Bertha and Gustav

  • His daughter Bertha Krupp (1886-1957) inherited the firm at the age of 16 in 1902, and the firm was converted into a limited company (Fried. Krupp AG) in 1903, with Bertha as the main shareholder.
  • Bertha married Gustav von Bohlen und Halbach (1870-1950), a diplomat, in 1906. The Kaiser allowed him to take the name Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach. He chaired the board from 1909.
  • Under Gustav the firm grew with naval and army orders. In 1914 it produced the 42 cm 'Big Bertha' howitzer, named after Bertha Krupp, which was used against the Belgian fortresses at Liège and Namur in August 1914.
  • The firm made guns, shells, armour plate and submarines throughout the war and its workforce grew to well over 100,000, many of them women.
  • Contemporaries: Krupp was a national symbol of German industrial strength, admired by nationalists. Socialists attacked the family as 'merchants of death', and the Reichstag debated Krupp's profits and its bribery of officials in 1913-14.
  • Historians see the Krupps as a leading example of the links between heavy industry, the army and the state before 1914, and of industrial paternalism. The family's later role under the Nazis falls outside this period.

Urbanisation and the growth of the working class

  • Industrial growth drew millions from the countryside, mostly from the east, to the cities and the Ruhr. By 1910 around 60 per cent of Germans lived in towns, up from about a third in 1871.
  • Berlin grew from under a million in 1871 to about two million by 1910, and Essen, Dortmund and other Ruhr towns grew many times over.
  • Working-class families lived in overcrowded tenements (Mietskasernen) in Berlin, often with several to a room. Disease and poor sanitation were common, though public health improved from the 1890s.
  • Factory work meant long hours, strict discipline and little security, though real wages rose slowly in the 1890s and 1900s. Women and children also worked, and a law of 1891 restricted Sunday work and limited the hours of women and children.
  • The Ruhr miners' strike of 1889 involved about 90,000 workers, and a second strike followed in 1905, forcing the state to intervene in mining conditions.
  • Catholic and Polish migrants, often in the Ruhr, added religious and ethnic tension to the class divisions.

The SPD and the trade unions

  • The Social Democratic Party (SPD) was formed at Gotha in 1875 by uniting Marx's followers and Lassalle's General German Workers' Association.
  • The Anti-Socialist Law of 1878 banned the SPD's organisations and press until 1890, but socialists could still stand for the Reichstag, and their votes rose.
  • After the law lapsed, the SPD adopted the Erfurt Programme in 1891, which combined a Marxist analysis of capitalism with immediate demands for reform and democracy. Eduard Bernstein's revisionism, arguing for gradual reform, was rejected by the party in theory but accepted in practice.
  • In 1912 the SPD won about 4.25 million votes and 110 seats, becoming the largest party in the Reichstag, though the electoral system and the Bundesrat still excluded it from power.
  • The Free (socialist) trade unions grew to about 2.5 million members by 1913, bigger than the Christian and liberal unions.
  • Employers formed associations and used lockouts and blacklists in response, and the government used the courts and police against strikes.

Social tensions: Junkers, industrialists and workers

  • The Junkers, the landowning nobility east of the Elbe, dominated the army officer corps, the Prussian civil service and the Prussian parliament, where the three-class voting system gave the rich more weight.
  • Cheap imported grain from the USA and Russia in the 1870s and 1880s threatened Junker profits. The tariff of 1879 protected grain and iron and led to an alliance of 'iron and rye' between heavy industry and landowners.
  • Tariffs raised food prices and angered workers and consumers, and the left and liberal industries, such as finished goods and exporters, opposed them. In 1902 Bülow's tariff raised duties on grain still further.
  • In 1897 the Prussian minister Miquel promoted Sammlungspolitik, a 'policy of rallying together' landowners and industrialists behind the monarchy, the navy and Weltpolitik, against the left.
  • Industrialists resented Junker privilege and the aristocratic dominance of the state, while many middle-class Germans tried to imitate the nobility. Historians such as Hans-Ulrich Wehler argue that this 'feudalisation of the bourgeoisie' blocked democratic reform.
  • Geoff Eley and David Blackbourn replied in The Peculiarities of German History (1984) that the German middle class was confident and the Reich was a modern society, not a flawed one.

The state's response: welfare and repression

  • Bismarck's insurance laws created sickness insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old age and invalidity pensions (1889), partly to wean workers from the SPD.
  • Wilhelm II's 'new course' of 1890 started with factory laws on Sunday work, women and child labour, and a council to discuss labour matters, but he turned against the SPD when it kept winning votes.
  • Companies provided their own welfare. Krupp's housing, schools and funds are the best-known example, and other firms such as Siemens followed. Employers hoped for loyalty and an end to strikes.
  • Welfare did not stop the SPD vote from rising, and many workers were more loyal to their unions and party than to the state or firm.
  • Despite the growth in industrial wealth, workers had little political power in Prussia, where the three-class vote persisted until 1918.

Assessment: industrial success and social cost

  • Achievement: Germany became the leading industrial economy in continental Europe, strongest in steel, chemicals and electricals.
  • Limit: the growth was uneven. Agriculture and the east remained backward, and the economy was exposed to cycles of boom and slump.
  • Achievement: the Reich pioneered national social insurance, and real wages rose in the 1890s and 1900s.
  • Limit: welfare was paternalist and aimed at controlling the working class; political reform did not follow economic change.
  • Limit: heavy industry's dependence on arms orders linked the economy to militarism and the naval race.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists saw industrial power as proof of German greatness, while socialists saw it as exploitation. Later historians debate whether industrial Germany was a modern society with an unmodern politics.

Key terms

Second industrial revolution
The expansion from the 1880s of industries based on steel, chemicals, electricity and the internal combustion engine, in which Germany led.
Cartel
An agreement among firms in an industry to fix prices, share markets or limit output; common in German coal, steel and chemicals.
Universal bank
A German-style bank that lent to industry for the long term and took part in company management, such as Deutsche Bank.
Junkers
The landowning nobility of eastern Prussia, who dominated the army and Prussian state and relied on tariffs to protect agriculture.
Social Democratic Party (SPD)
The German socialist party founded at Gotha in 1875; it became the largest party in the Reichstag in 1912.
Paternalism
The practice of employers, as at Krupp, providing housing, schools and benefits in return for loyalty and discipline.
Sammlungspolitik
The policy of rallying landowners and industrialists together behind the monarchy, the navy and tariffs against the left.
Tariff of 1879
The protective tariff on grain and iron that linked heavy industry with the Junkers in an alliance of iron and rye.
