The war of movement, 1914

All the Great Powers expected a short war. Germany's plan was to defeat France in about six weeks and then turn on Russia, but the plan failed and the Western Front became a stalemate by the end of the year.

  • Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August 1914 and took Liège, then swept through Belgium and northern France under the Schlieffen Plan, modified by the younger Moltke, who weakened the right wing.
  • The British Expeditionary Force, a small professional army, fought its first major action at Mons on 23 August 1914 and retreated with the French.
  • The First Battle of the Marne (5-12 September 1914) halted the German advance about 40 kilometres from Paris. The Germans withdrew to the River Aisne and dug in.
  • Both sides then tried to outflank each other in the 'race to the sea', and the line of trenches reached the North Sea and the Swiss frontier by late 1914.
  • The First Battle of Ypres (October-November 1914) was a hard-fought defensive success for the British and French; the BEF lost much of its regular army and Britain had to rely on volunteers.
  • At Christmas 1914 troops along parts of the line took part in unofficial truces, which the high commands then forbade.
  • In the east, Russia invaded East Prussia, but General Hindenburg and his chief of staff Ludendorff destroyed the Russian Second Army at Tannenberg (26-30 August 1914), capturing about 90,000 men, and defeated the First Army at the Masurian Lakes in September.

Trench warfare: conditions, weapons and tactics

Defensive weapons were stronger than the means of attack, so battles on the Western Front produced very high casualties for small gains.

  • Trench systems had a front line, support and reserve trenches linked by communication trenches, with barbed wire and the shell-torn ground of no man's land between the opposing lines.
  • Soldiers lived with mud, rats, lice, cold and the constant danger of shelling and snipers. Trench foot and shell shock (what we now call combat stress) were common.
  • The machine gun and quick-firing artillery made frontal attacks costly; most battle casualties were caused by shellfire.
  • Attackers relied on long preliminary bombardments, which warned the enemy and churned up the ground. From 1916 the 'creeping barrage' moved ahead of infantry as they advanced.
  • Poison gas was first used on a large scale by Germany at the Second Battle of Ypres (22 April 1915), when chlorine was released against French colonial and Canadian troops. Phosgene and mustard gas followed, and gas masks reduced its effect.
  • Tanks were first used by Britain at Flers-Courcelette on 15 September 1916. They were slow and unreliable, but at Cambrai (November 1917) hundreds were used together with artillery and infantry.
  • Aircraft were used for reconnaissance, artillery spotting and, later, fighter combat and bombing. Germany used Zeppelins and Gotha bombers against British cities.
  • By 1918 both sides used combined arms: Germany developed stormtrooper infiltration tactics, and the Allies combined tanks, aircraft, artillery and infantry.

The Western Front, 1915–1917: attrition

In a war of attrition each side tried to wear down the other's manpower and resources. Generals on both sides believed a breakthrough was possible, and they continued costly offensives.

  • In 1915 the Allies launched offensives at Neuve Chapelle, Artois and Loos, and the Germans used gas at Ypres. None broke the line, and shell shortages in Britain caused a political crisis in May 1915.
  • Verdun (21 February to 18 December 1916): the German chief of staff Falkenhayn attacked the symbolically important French fortress, intending, according to his later account, to bleed the French army white. Both sides lost hundreds of thousands of men.
  • The French commander at Verdun, General Pétain, rotated divisions through the battle, and the road known as the Sacred Way supplied the front. Verdun became a symbol of French endurance.
  • The Somme (1 July to 18 November 1916) was a joint British-French offensive planned partly to relieve Verdun. On the first day the British Army lost about 57,000 casualties, of whom about 19,000 were killed, the worst single day in its history.
  • The Somme advanced the Allied line only a few kilometres and cost over a million casualties in total across all armies. It was the first big battle for Kitchener's volunteer 'Pals' battalions, and wiped out the male population of some local communities.
  • Nivelle's offensive in April 1917 along the Chemin des Dames failed with heavy losses, causing mutinies in the French army; Pétain replaced Nivelle and restored morale by improving conditions.
  • Passchendaele (the Third Battle of Ypres, July to November 1917) was fought in the Ypres salient, partly to clear German submarine bases in Belgium. Heavy rain and shell damage turned the ground to mud, and the final gain was only about 8 kilometres.
  • At Cambrai (20 November 1917) Britain used hundreds of tanks in a surprise attack; the gains were mostly lost to a German counter-attack.

The Eastern Front and Russia's exit

  • The Central Powers broke the Russian line at Gorlice-Tarnow in May 1915, forcing a great Russian retreat from Poland and Galicia.
  • The Brusilov Offensive (June 1916), led by General Brusilov against Austria-Hungary, was the most successful Allied offensive of the year. It took many prisoners and forced Germany to move troops east, but it cost Russia about a million casualties and could not be sustained.
  • Romania joined the Allies in August 1916 and was largely overrun by Germany and its allies by the end of the year.
  • Defeats, losses, shortages and the failure of the Tsar's government led to the February Revolution of 1917 (see the notes on Tsarist Russia). The Provisional Government continued the war, and the Kerensky Offensive of July 1917 collapsed.
  • After the Bolsheviks seized power in October 1917, they signed an armistice in December 1917 and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918, giving up large parts of Poland, the Baltic lands, Ukraine and Finland.
  • The treaty let Germany move over a million men west for the offensives of 1918, but it also tied down troops in the occupied east.

Other fronts

  • Gallipoli: after Ottoman entry into the war in October-November 1914, Britain and France tried to force the Dardanelles in March 1915. The land campaign began on 25 April 1915 with landings of British, French, Australian and New Zealand (ANZAC) troops. The campaign was stalemated and the troops were evacuated by 9 January 1916.
  • Gallipoli damaged the reputation of Churchill, who had promoted it, but helped to build the sense of national identity of Australia and New Zealand.
  • Italy joined the Allies in May 1915 under the Treaty of London, promised territory from Austria-Hungary. Eleven battles on the Isonzo brought little gain, and Italy suffered a disastrous defeat at Caporetto in October 1917 before recovering at Vittorio Veneto (October-November 1918).
  • In the Middle East, the British fought the Ottomans in Mesopotamia (surrender at Kut in April 1916) and in Palestine, where General Allenby captured Jerusalem in December 1917 and won at Megiddo in September 1918. The Arab Revolt was supported by T.E. Lawrence.
  • In the Balkans, Serbia was overrun by the Central Powers in 1915; the Allies maintained a front at Salonika. Bulgaria asked for an armistice on 29 September 1918, and the Ottomans and Austria-Hungary followed in October and November.
  • Germany's colonies were captured by the Allies, though German East Africa held out until the end of the war.

War at sea

  • Britain used its fleet to blockade Germany, stopping imports of food and raw materials. Germany suffered shortages, and the winter of 1916-17, known as the turnip winter, was especially severe.
  • The German High Seas Fleet was cautious. The only major fleet engagement, Jutland (31 May to 1 June 1916), cost Britain 14 ships and about 6,000 men, and Germany 11 ships and about 2,500 men. The British fleet kept control of the North Sea and the German fleet remained mostly in port.
  • Germany turned to submarines. The sinking of the liner Lusitania on 7 May 1915, with almost 1,200 dead including 128 Americans, outraged American opinion, and Germany limited its campaign.
  • On 1 February 1917 Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, hoping to starve Britain into surrender within months. British shipping losses were very high in spring 1917, and Britain had food stocks for only weeks.
  • The adoption of the convoy system in 1917 and improved anti-submarine methods reduced losses, and the campaign failed to knock Britain out of the war while bringing the USA in.

Conscription and the mobilisation of manpower

Mass war needed mass armies. Continental states already had conscription, but Britain had relied on volunteers and had to change.

  • Germany and France called up all fit men under the pre-war system of conscription. Russia did the same.
  • In Britain, Kitchener's recruiting campaign raised over two million volunteers by the end of 1915. As losses grew and numbers fell, the Military Service Act of January 1916 introduced conscription for unmarried men aged 18 to 41; it was extended to married men in May 1916.
  • Conscientious objectors could appear before tribunals, and about 16,000 men claimed that status. Some were jailed or sent to non-combatant units.
  • The Military Service Act of 1916 did not apply to Ireland. A proposal in April 1918 to extend conscription to Ireland provoked the Conscription Crisis, a nationwide campaign of opposition, and was never put into effect.
  • The USA introduced conscription through the Selective Service Act of May 1917. Germany passed the Auxiliary Service Law in December 1916, which required all men aged 17 to 60 to work for the war effort.
  • Conscription and casualties raised the question of 'reserved occupations': governments had to keep skilled workers in munitions, mines and railways, and sometimes replaced them with women (see the notes on women in the workforce).

The USA's entry and the end of the war, 1917–1918

  • The Zimmermann Telegram, a secret German offer to Mexico of an alliance against the USA, was intercepted by British intelligence and published in the American press in March 1917.
  • The USA declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917, after the submarine campaign sank American ships and the Zimmermann Telegram, as an 'associated power' rather than a formal ally. Its troops under General Pershing arrived in strength during 1918.
  • With Russia out, Ludendorff planned the Spring Offensive (Kaiserschlacht), launched on 21 March 1918 with 'Operation Michael'. It made large gains and came close to Amiens, but did not break the Allied line.
  • In response, the Allies set up a unified command under Marshal Foch in April 1918. German troops were exhausted and short of supplies, and a counter-attack at the Second Battle of the Marne (July 1918) turned the tide.
  • The Hundred Days began with the Battle of Amiens on 8 August 1918, which Ludendorff called the 'black day of the German army'. Allied tanks, aircraft and artillery pushed the Germans back to the Hindenburg Line, which was broken in late September.
  • On 29 September 1918 Ludendorff told the Kaiser that Germany needed an armistice at once. Germany asked for terms using Wilson's Fourteen Points, and the armistice was signed in a railway carriage at Compiègne at 5 a.m. on 11 November, taking effect at 11 a.m.
  • Military deaths in the war are usually put at about nine to ten million, with many more wounded, and civilian deaths in the millions.

Key personality: Douglas Haig

Douglas Haig commanded the British Expeditionary Force in France from December 1915 until the end of the war. He is the most controversial British commander of the war.

  • Born in Edinburgh in 1861 into a whisky-distilling family, Haig attended Sandhurst, joined the cavalry and served in the Sudan and the Boer War before becoming a corps commander in 1914.
  • He succeeded Sir John French as commander-in-chief of the BEF in December 1915 and was promoted field marshal in January 1917.
  • He commanded the Somme offensive of 1916, the Arras offensive and Passchendaele in 1917, and the defensive battles of 1918. On 11 April 1918 he issued his 'backs to the wall' order.
  • He led the Allied advance in the Hundred Days of 1918, in which British forces played the leading part and took many prisoners.
  • After the war he was made an earl and given a grant of money. He founded the British Legion in 1921 and supported the sale of poppies. He died in 1928, and his funeral was a national occasion.
  • Contemporaries: King George V and the army trusted him; Lloyd George distrusted him, thought his battles wasteful, and withheld reinforcements in early 1918. Many soldiers, and the public after the war, mourned him.
  • From the 1920s memoirs, notably Lloyd George's, criticised him. In the 1960s Alan Clark's The Donkeys popularised the phrase 'lions led by donkeys', which Clark misattributed to German generals.
  • Historians such as Denis Winter and Gerard De Groot depicted Haig as a callous and unimaginative butcher; John Terraine, Gary Sheffield and others argue that he adapted, learned from experience and led the army to victory in 1918.
  • Many historians now accept that his attritional strategy was shaped by circumstances: the strength of defences, the need to support France, and the poor communications that limited control over battles.

Assessment: how was the war won and lost?

  • Allied success: the blockade, attrition and the arrival of American troops in 1918 wore down Germany's capacity to fight, and 1918's coordinated tank, artillery and air attacks broke its army.
  • Limit: the Western Front battles of 1915-17 produced casualties out of proportion to gains, and Passchendaele and the Somme became symbols of waste.
  • German success: Germany held the Western Front for four years, knocked Russia out of the war and nearly won in spring 1918.
  • German failure: the Schlieffen Plan failed in 1914, unrestricted submarine warfare brought in the USA, and the army was exhausted by 1918.
  • Contemporary view: the troops themselves, and many officers, criticised the high command; later views range from Clark's 'donkeys' to Sheffield's 'Forgotten Victory'.

Key terms

World war
A war fought across several continents and at sea by many states, drawing in their empires, economies and entire populations.
War of attrition
A strategy of winning by wearing down the enemy's men and resources over time, as at Verdun and the Somme, rather than by a single decisive battle.
Conscription
Compulsory military service; Britain introduced it in January 1916 under the Military Service Act.
Trench warfare
Fighting from fortified lines of trenches protected by barbed wire, machine guns and artillery, which gave the defence the advantage.
Schlieffen Plan
Germany's pre-war plan to defeat France rapidly by sweeping through Belgium, before turning on Russia; it failed at the Marne in 1914.
Creeping barrage
An artillery barrage that moves forward in stages just ahead of advancing infantry, used from 1916.
Unrestricted submarine warfare
The German policy, resumed on 1 February 1917, of sinking ships without warning, including neutral and merchant vessels.
Blockade
The British naval policy of cutting off Germany's imports of food and materials, which caused severe shortages.
Kaiserschlacht
The German Spring Offensive of 1918, launched on 21 March, the last attempt to win before American troops arrived in force.
Armistice
The agreement signed at Compiègne that stopped fighting on the Western Front at 11 a.m. on 11 November 1918.
