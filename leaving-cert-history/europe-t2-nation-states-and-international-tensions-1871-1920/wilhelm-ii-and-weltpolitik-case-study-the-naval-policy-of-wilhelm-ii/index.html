Key personality: Wilhelm II

Wilhelm II was Kaiser from 1888 to 1918. His ambition to rule personally and his taste for a navy and a place in the world shaped German policy before 1914.

  • Wilhelm II was born on 27 January 1859, the eldest son of Frederick III and Victoria, the daughter of Queen Victoria. A difficult breech birth left him with a withered left arm.
  • He became Kaiser on 15 June 1888 at the age of 29 on his father's death, and forced Bismarck to resign in March 1890 because he wanted to rule personally.
  • He proclaimed a 'new course': he began by easing the Anti-Socialist Law and sponsoring workers' protection laws, then turned against the SPD when it kept growing.
  • He was first cousin to Tsar Nicholas II and George V and a grandson of Queen Victoria; he combined admiration for the Royal Navy with resentment of Britain.
  • He was impulsive and prone to indiscretion: his 'Hun speech' to troops departing for China (July 1900) and the Daily Telegraph interview (October 1908), which offended Britain, Japan and German opinion, damaged Germany's image.
  • After the Daily Telegraph affair the Reichstag demanded that the Kaiser submit to constitutional restraint, and Wilhelm withdrew from many public roles; Bülow resigned in 1909.
  • On 8 December 1912 he held a 'war council' with military leaders; historians disagree over whether it showed intent to start a war or merely a bellicose mood.
  • In 1914 he gave Austria-Hungary the 'blank cheque' on 5 July (see the notes on the outbreak of war) and he left for his annual cruise; he later tried to restrain Austria but could not stop the machinery of mobilisation.
  • He lost the confidence of the army in autumn 1918. His abdication was announced on 9 November 1918 and he went to the Netherlands, where he died at Doorn on 4 June 1941.
  • Contemporaries: nationalists and the navy admired him as the symbol of Germany's rise, while diplomats such as Holstein and politicians saw him as unpredictable and dangerous. Foreign cartoonists drew him as a menace.
  • Historians: John Röhl argues that Wilhelm exercised real personal rule and carries a heavy responsibility for 1914; Christopher Clark, in his biography and in The Sleepwalkers (2012), stresses his inconsistency and the shared responsibility of all the Great Powers.

Weltpolitik: world policy after 1897

Weltpolitik was the German drive, led by Wilhelm II, Bülow and Tirpitz, to turn Germany from a continental power into a world power through colonies, trade and a fleet.

  • Weltpolitik had economic roots: Germany was the leading industrial power in Europe, needed markets, raw materials and trade routes, and feared being shut out as Britain, France and Russia expanded.
  • It had domestic roots: conservatives and many liberals believed that imperial success would unite the classes behind the monarchy and distract from the rise of the SPD.
  • Bernhard von Bülow became Foreign Secretary in June 1897 and Chancellor in October 1900. In a speech to the Reichstag in December 1897 he claimed for Germany 'a place in the sun'.
  • Germany seized Kiaochow (Tsingtao) in China in November 1897 after two German missionaries were killed, and leased it for 99 years in 1898. It bought the Caroline, Marianas and Palau islands from Spain in 1899 and shared Samoa with the United States in the same year.
  • The Kruger telegram of 3 January 1896 congratulated the Transvaal president on defeating the Jameson Raid. It outraged British opinion and showed Germany courting anti-British feeling.
  • In 1898 Wilhelm II visited Constantinople and Damascus, declaring himself the friend of the world's Muslims. The Baghdad Railway concession (1899, expanded 1903) was meant to tie the Ottoman Empire to Germany and rival British and Russian influence in the Middle East.
  • Weltpolitik was backed by pressure groups: the Navy League, the Pan-German League (founded 1891) and the Colonial Society.
  • Attempts at Anglo-German alliance talks in 1898–1901 failed, and German pressure over Morocco (1905, 1911) gained little.

Naval policy case study: background and causes

Germany had a small coastal navy in 1890. A decision to build a large battle fleet was taken in 1897-98 for reasons of power, prestige and domestic politics.

  • The Kaiser had long admired Britain's navy and wanted a fleet that matched Germany's new status; as a boy he had been impressed by Queen Victoria's fleet at Spithead.
  • Alfred Thayer Mahan's The Influence of Sea Power upon History (1890) argued that national greatness depended on a strong battle fleet and command of the sea. Wilhelm II read it and ordered it placed on German warships.
  • Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz became state secretary of the Imperial Navy Office in June 1897 and drew up a plan for a battle fleet concentrated in the North Sea, not a cruiser fleet for overseas.
  • His 'risk theory' held that a German fleet strong enough to inflict serious damage on the Royal Navy would deter Britain from war, since even a British victory would leave it too weak against France and Russia.
  • Tirpitz also saw the fleet as a way of rallying the middle classes behind the Reich and of weakening the SPD; the navy would also give Germany bargaining power in colonial disputes.
  • The Navy Office launched a press campaign among professors and industrialists, and the German Navy League (Deutscher Flottenverein), founded in 1898, grew to over a million members and promoted the fleet.
  • Industrial interests such as Krupp (armour plate) and Blohm and Voss, as well as heavy industry in the Ruhr, stood to profit from orders.

Naval policy case study: the Navy Laws and the arms race

  • The First Navy Law (1898) authorised a fleet of 19 battleships, 8 large cruisers and 12 small cruisers; the Reichstag voted the money for several years at a time.
  • The Second Navy Law (June 1900) was passed amid British anger over the Boer War. It increased the planned fleet to 38 battleships (four squadrons of eight, plus a flagship and reserve) and 20 large cruisers.
  • The Kaiser and Tirpitz hoped to reach a ratio of about two German ships to three British ones, which they judged was enough to make the risk theory work.
  • British leaders reacted at first by reorganising: Admiral Fisher, First Sea Lord from 1904, brought home ships from distant stations and concentrated fleets against Germany. The British naval estimates grew.
  • HMS Dreadnought was launched on 10 February 1906. She was faster than any other battleship and carried ten 12-inch guns. She made existing battleships obsolete, so Germany had to start again on the same footing as Britain.
  • Germany responded with its own dreadnought class (Nassau, launched 1908) and passed a supplementary law in 1908 which sped up building to four capital ships a year.
  • In 1909 the British public, alarmed at German progress, demanded eight new dreadnoughts with the cry 'We want eight and we won't wait'; the government laid down more than usual in the 1909-10 programme.
  • A supplementary law of 1912 added further battleships and a third squadron to the active fleet. The Kiel Canal was widened to take dreadnoughts and reopened in June 1914.
  • After 1912 Germany could no longer afford to compete both in the navy and the army, and in 1912-13 the army received large increases; Britain kept a clear lead in capital ships.

Naval policy case study: Britain's response and failed talks

  • Britain ended its isolation by agreement with Japan (1902), France (1904) and Russia (1907), partly because of the German navy.
  • In 1904-05 Fisher redeployed the fleet to home waters, a change made possible by the agreement with France and Japan; the Royal Navy now faced the North Sea.
  • German proposals for naval talks in 1906-08 foundered on the fact that Germany would not accept limits on shipbuilding, while Britain would not give a promise of neutrality.
  • Bethmann Hollweg, Chancellor from 1909, wanted an agreement, and in 1911 and 1912 pursued the issue with British ministers.
  • The Haldane mission to Berlin (February 1912) failed because the Germans wanted a British promise of neutrality in a continental war, which would have left France and Russia to their fate, while Britain wanted a slowing of the building programme.
  • Churchill, First Lord of the Admiralty from October 1911, proposed a 'naval holiday' in 1912 and in 1912 the Royal Navy moved its Mediterranean battleships home, while France took on the Mediterranean (the Anglo-French naval agreement).
  • A détente on colonial and Baghdad Railway issues in 1913-14 brought some improvement, but the naval and military preparations of each side remained.

Naval policy case study: outcomes

  • The fleet failed in its main aim: it did not deter Britain from war. Instead it turned Britain from a possible friend into an opponent, and helped push it into the Entente with France and Russia.
  • The fleet cost vast sums and absorbed resources that could have gone to the army, at a time when Germany's land frontiers were threatened by France and Russia.
  • The costs raised the national debt and taxation, led to the Reich's finance crisis of 1908-09 and sharpened disputes between Conservatives, who opposed inheritance tax, and the left.
  • The navy remained largely in harbour in the war. The only major battle, Jutland (31 May 1916), was indecisive, and the British blockade of Germany was never broken.
  • Many German sailors came to resent the fleet's inactivity and harsh discipline, and the mutinies at Wilhelmshaven and Kiel in 1918 triggered the revolution that overthrew the Kaiser (see the notes on war and revolution in Germany).
  • The war-time British blockade and the Allied claim to German ships at Scapa Flow (scuttled by the Germans in June 1919) completed the fleet's fate.

Naval policy case study: significance and differing views

  • British contemporaries saw the German fleet as a challenge to the Empire; the Admiralty and press, including the Daily Mail and the Navy League in Britain, spread fear of invasion.
  • German contemporaries presented the fleet as essential for defence of overseas trade and as a symbol of national pride; Social Democrats and some Liberals objected to its cost.
  • Paul Kennedy, in The Rise of the Anglo-German Antagonism (1980), argues that the naval race was a major cause of the breakdown of relations between the two countries.
  • Fritz Fischer (Griff nach der Weltmacht, 1961) argued that Germany's leaders deliberately pursued world power by the fleet and then by war; critics say he exaggerated the coherence of German aims.
  • Others, such as Volker Berghahn, stress domestic motives: Tirpitz built the fleet to unite the right and to avoid reform at home, and the policy was a case of 'social imperialism'.
  • Some historians doubt that the naval race caused the war, noting that Anglo-German relations were improving in 1913-14 and that the Entente was formed first because of colonial concerns.

Assessment: Weltpolitik and the naval race

  • Achievement: Germany built the second-largest fleet in the world within about fifteen years and won prestige and domestic support for the Kaiser.
  • Limit: it never overtook Britain, and the cost of competing in both army and navy overstretched German finances.
  • Achievement: Weltpolitik gave Germany colonies and concessions such as Kiaochow, Samoa and the Baghdad Railway.
  • Limit: Weltpolitik was clumsy and provoked rival powers. Tangier (1905) and Agadir (1911) ended in humiliation, and Germany was left with its colonies small and unprofitable.
  • Achievement: the colonial and naval movement helped bring together Conservatives and National Liberals behind the government.
  • Limit: it deepened the sense of encirclement, which was a result of German policy, and made Britain more willing to stand with France and Russia in 1914.
  • Contemporary view: German nationalists regarded Weltpolitik as a necessity, while British opinion regarded it as a threat; later historians increasingly judge it a strategic mistake.

Key terms

Weltpolitik
German 'world policy' from the late 1890s: a drive for colonies, trade, a large navy and an equal place among the world powers.
Balance of power
A situation in which no one state can dominate Europe because rivals combine to check it; Germany's naval ambition disturbed it.
Nationalism
Pride in and loyalty to one's nation; in Germany it drove support for the navy through bodies such as the Navy League.
Risk theory
Tirpitz's argument that a fleet strong enough to damage the Royal Navy would deter Britain from war with Germany.
Dreadnought
The all-big-gun battleship launched by Britain in 1906, which made earlier battleships obsolete and began a new phase of the naval race.
Two-power standard
The British rule, from the Naval Defence Act of 1889, that the Royal Navy should equal the next two largest navies combined.
Navy League
The German Flottenverein, founded in 1898, a mass pressure group that campaigned for a bigger navy.
Encirclement
The German fear, strong before 1914, that France, Russia and Britain were hemming Germany in.
