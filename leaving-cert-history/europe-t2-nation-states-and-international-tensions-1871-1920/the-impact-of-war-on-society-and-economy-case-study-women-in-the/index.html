Total war: the state takes control

The scale and length of the war forced governments to direct the economy and the lives of civilians, far beyond anything seen in earlier wars.

  • Britain's Defence of the Realm Act (DORA), passed in August 1914 and extended repeatedly, gave the government power over property, labour, the press and public behaviour. It restricted pub opening hours, censored news, and led to British Summer Time (1916).
  • The government took control of the railways at the start of the war in August 1914, and took over the coal mines in 1917 and shipping, to coordinate supplies. New ministries were created: Munitions (1915), Labour and Food (1916) and Shipping (1916).
  • The 'Shell Crisis' of spring 1915, when the army ran short of shells, brought down the Liberal government. Lloyd George led the new Ministry of Munitions that took charge of the supply of war material.
  • Germany organised the economy through the War Raw Materials Department (1914) under Walther Rathenau and the Hindenburg Programme (1916), which aimed to increase munitions production and mobilise labour.
  • Propaganda was used to recruit soldiers (the 'Your Country Needs You' poster), sell war bonds and encourage civilians to accept shortages. Governments also censored letters and newspapers.
  • Rationing: Britain introduced compulsory rationing of sugar, meat and butter in early 1918, after the submarine campaign cut supplies and queues grew. Germany introduced bread rationing from 1915 and a wider system of price controls.
  • Civilians became targets: Scarborough was bombarded by German warships in December 1914, and Zeppelins and Gotha bombers attacked British towns.

Effects on the economy

  • States paid for the war through borrowing, war loans and printing money rather than taxation alone, and the national debts of the belligerents multiplied several times over.
  • Britain, once the world's greatest creditor, became a debtor to the USA, and the USA took over as the leading financial power.
  • Inflation: prices rose faster than wages in most countries, and savings lost value. In Germany, where the war was funded mainly by loans and money creation, this laid the ground for later inflation.
  • Germany's blockade-hit economy suffered severe shortages. Harvest failure in 1916 caused the 'Turnip Winter' of 1916-17, and an estimated several hundred thousand civilians died of malnutrition-related causes by the end of the war.
  • Industry converted to war production, and new industries such as chemicals, aircraft and engineering expanded. Peacetime trade collapsed, and Europe lost export markets to the USA and Japan.
  • Areas fought over, such as northern France and Belgium, were devastated. Russia's economy collapsed under the strain, contributing to revolution.
  • Agriculture lost labour and horses to the army, but governments encouraged food production, with measures such as the Corn Production Act 1917 in Britain.

Effects on society

  • Casualties: about nine to ten million soldiers died, including around 1.4 million French and about 2 million German dead, and about 750,000 British (about 900,000 from the whole British Empire). Many survivors were disabled, and there were hundreds of thousands of war widows.
  • Class barriers loosened a little: shared danger in the trenches and a need for national unity, and a wider welfare provision. Rural and urban workers who served in the army gained experience of other regions.
  • Trade unions gained strength and recognition by governments that needed their cooperation. British union membership roughly doubled between 1914 and 1920, though strikes continued over pay and conditions.
  • Governments also made reforms: the British Education Act 1918 (Fisher Act) raised the school leaving age to 14, and promised housing for returning servicemen.
  • The influenza pandemic of 1918-19 killed tens of millions worldwide, with estimates ranging widely from 17 to 50 million or more, striking young adults weakened by the war.
  • Soldiers returned to unemployment and unrest, and demobilisation led to some strikes and mutinies in 1919.

Case study: women before 1914

Before the war most women who earned wages were young and unmarried, and worked in a narrow range of badly paid jobs. Only the war made it possible for them to do men's work on a large scale.

  • Domestic service was the largest single employer of women in Britain, with well over a million servants, and the next largest sectors were textiles and clothing. Pay was typically half or less than that of men.
  • In France many women worked on family farms and in textile and clothing workshops; in Germany about a third of women worked, mostly in agriculture, domestic service and textiles.
  • Women were largely excluded from skilled trades and the professions, and many trade unions refused them membership. Married women were expected to work in the home.
  • Politically, women could not vote in national elections in Britain, France or Germany. The suffragettes of Emmeline Pankhurst's Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) used militant tactics from 1903, and the suffragists of Millicent Fawcett's NUWSS used peaceful campaigning.
  • In Germany women were banned from political associations until 1908, and the women's movement was divided between middle-class feminists and the SPD's women's organisation led by Clara Zetkin.

Case study: women in Britain's munitions factories

  • Men volunteered in huge numbers and recruitment for the army left gaps in the workforce. After the shell shortage, the Ministry of Munitions (1915) turned to women to fill the gap.
  • The Treasury Agreement of March 1915 between the government and unions accepted 'dilution' (employing unskilled workers on tasks done by skilled men) for the war's duration, and the Munitions of War Act of July 1915 supported dilution and banned strikes in munitions.
  • Hundreds of thousands of women worked in munitions by 1918, filling shells and fuses, making cordite and working in factories such as Woolwich Arsenal and the national filling factories. They were known as 'munitionettes'.
  • Conditions were hard: long shifts of 10 to 12 hours, shift work and the risk of explosion. At Chilwell in Nottinghamshire a huge explosion in July 1918 killed over 130 workers, and the Silvertown explosion of January 1917 killed 73.
  • TNT poisoning (toxic jaundice) turned skin yellow, earning women munition workers the name 'canary girls', and caused illness and some deaths.
  • The government appointed welfare supervisors, provided canteens and set up a Health of Munition Workers Committee, because poor conditions reduced output.

Case study: women in France and Germany

  • France lost its men to the army early in the war, and the harvest of 1914 was brought in largely by women, children and old men. Women ran family farms and businesses for the rest of the war.
  • From 1915 the French government under Albert Thomas, the undersecretary for munitions, encouraged women to work in the armaments industry. By the later war years, women made up a large share of the workers in French shell factories, including the Renault works at Billancourt. They were known as 'munitionnettes'.
  • Women in France also worked as tram conductors, postal workers and clerks, and as nurses in the army medical service.
  • In Germany the National Women's Service (Nationaler Frauendienst), organised in August 1914 by middle-class feminists such as Gertrud Bäumer, helped with food distribution and welfare.
  • As men were drafted, women increased in the metal and chemical industries, with Krupp, Siemens and other firms employing more women. The Hindenburg Programme and the Auxiliary Service Law of December 1916 mobilised male workers aged 17 to 60, increasing the pressure on women to fill gaps.
  • In Germany women bore the brunt of the food shortages, queuing for food, and women led food protests and riots in 1915-17.

Case study: offices, transport, farms and nursing

  • Women replaced men in banks, government offices and shops, and the numbers of women clerks and typists rose greatly in Britain, France and Germany.
  • Women worked on trams, buses and railways as conductors, ticket collectors and porters, and as postal workers, firefighters and police auxiliaries in several countries.
  • The Women's Land Army was formed in Britain in 1917 as the submarine campaign made food a priority, and around 20,000 or more women worked as farm labourers.
  • Voluntary Aid Detachments (VADs, founded 1909) supplied nurses and ambulance drivers, including Vera Brittain, who wrote Testament of Youth. Women served in field hospitals near the front.
  • Edith Cavell, a British nurse who ran a training school in Brussels, was executed by a German firing squad on 12 October 1915 for helping Allied soldiers escape, and became a symbol of Allied propaganda.
  • Auxiliary services were created: the Women's Army Auxiliary Corps (1917), the Women's Royal Naval Service (1917) and the Women's Royal Air Force (April 1918), in which women worked as clerks, drivers, cooks, mechanics and telephonists, releasing men for the front.

Case study: pay, unions and Pankhurst's wartime campaigning

  • Women were usually paid less than men even where they did the same work. Government rules in 1916 set women's rates for munitions below men's, though some women on piecework earned more than they had in domestic service.
  • Trade unions feared that employers would use women to undercut men's pay and skills. They agreed to dilution only on condition that pre-war practices were restored after the war.
  • In August 1914 Emmeline and Christabel Pankhurst suspended the WSPU's militant campaign, and the government released suffragette prisoners.
  • The Pankhursts supported the war effort. In July 1915 the WSPU organised a 'Right to Serve' march in London, backed by government money, to demand that women be given war work.
  • Sylvia Pankhurst, in contrast, opposed the war and worked for women in London's East End. Christabel founded the Women's Party in 1917.
  • Contemporary opinion changed: ministers and newspapers praised women's war work, and Asquith said in 1917 that women had earned the vote by their war work, and recognised the importance of the contribution.

What changed after 1918

  • The Representation of the People Act of February 1918 gave the vote in Britain to all men over 21 and to women over 30 who met a property qualification, around 8 million women. The Act applied to Ireland, and in the 1918 election Constance Markievicz became the first woman elected to Westminster.
  • The Equal Franchise Act of 1928 gave women the vote on the same terms as men, at 21.
  • In Germany a decree of 12 November 1918 gave all women over 20 the vote and the right to stand. Women voted in the January 1919 election and 37 women were elected to the National Assembly. The Weimar constitution declared men and women equal in civic rights 'in principle'.
  • France did not give women the vote until 1944, though French women had done as much war work as British.
  • Demobilisation brought pressure on women to leave their jobs. German decrees of 1918-19 required women to give up jobs to returning soldiers, and the Restoration of Pre-War Practices Act (1919) in Britain reinstated the old trade rules. Many women were dismissed from munitions, and some returned to domestic service.
  • The Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act of 1919 opened some professions and jury service to women in Britain, but marriage bars persisted in many jobs, and domestic service remained a major employer in the 1920s.

Assessment: did the war change women's position?

  • Achievement: war work showed that women could do skilled and heavy jobs, increased their public presence and confidence, and helped win the vote in Britain and Germany.
  • Limit: most war jobs ended in 1918-19, women's pay remained below men's, and traditional views of women's roles returned. The vote was limited in Britain to women over 30.
  • Arthur Marwick (The Deluge, 1965) argued that war was a catalyst of social change, including the status of women.
  • Gail Braybon (Women Workers in the First World War, 1981) and others argued that change was limited and temporary and that the vote owed as much to pre-war campaigning and to political calculations as to war work.
  • Contemporary view: many people thought women's war work proved their worth as citizens; others, including many male workers, regarded it as a temporary emergency.
  • Differences between countries: war work and the vote were linked in Britain and Germany, whereas France's women gained neither political rights nor lasting economic ones until the 1940s.

Key terms

Total war
A war in which whole societies and economies are mobilised and governments control industry, labour, food and propaganda.
Conscription
Compulsory enlistment of men for military service, which took men out of industry and created demand for women workers.
World war
A war fought across several continents by many states, whose effects reached civilians and economies as well as armies.
Munitionettes
The women who made shells, fuses and cordite in British factories in the First World War.
Dilution
The employment of unskilled or semi-skilled workers, including women, on jobs that formerly needed skilled men.
Defence of the Realm Act
The British law of August 1914 giving the government wide powers over labour, property, the press and public behaviour.
Rationing
The government control of food supplies by fixed allowances, introduced in Germany from 1915 and in Britain in 1918.
Women's Land Army
The British organisation formed in 1917 to place women as farm labourers and so increase food production.
