Tsarist Russia in 1871: autocracy and backwardness

Russia was the largest state in Europe, but it was poor, overwhelmingly rural and ruled by an autocratic tsar with no parliament.

  • Serfdom was abolished by Alexander II in 1861. Peasants had to pay redemption payments to the state over 49 years for the land they received, and these payments left many in debt.
  • Most peasants held land in the mir (village commune), which periodically redistributed strips among households. This discouraged investment, and farming methods were backward.
  • The census of 1897 showed a population of about 126 million, about 80 per cent of them peasants. Russia was an empire of many peoples, and Russians were less than half the population.
  • In 1871 Russia had little heavy industry, relatively few railways and a weak banking system; it exported grain and relied on foreign capital and technology.
  • Alexander II's reforms (zemstva, courts, army reform) were limited, and he was assassinated by the revolutionary group People's Will on 13 March 1881.
  • His son Alexander III (1881-94) and grandson Nicholas II (1894-1917) upheld autocracy, backed by the Orthodox Church, the nobility, the army and the secret police (the Okhrana), and pursued Russification of national minorities.
  • Nicholas II declared in 1895 that hopes for a constitution were 'senseless dreams'; revolutionary movements, including Social Revolutionaries (SRs) based on the peasantry and Marxist Social Democrats, developed underground.

Witte's industrialisation

Sergei Witte, Finance Minister from 1892 to 1903, led a state-directed 'great spurt' of industrial growth, paid for by foreign loans, taxation and grain exports.

  • Witte believed that Russia must industrialise to remain a Great Power, and the state built railways, ordered goods and protected industry with tariffs.
  • Railways were the key: the Trans-Siberian Railway began in 1891 and the main line to Vladivostok was open by about 1904 (completed in full in 1916). Rail mileage roughly doubled in the 1890s.
  • He put the rouble on the gold standard in 1897 to attract foreign investors. French and Belgian loans and investment paid for mines, ironworks and railways, helped by the Franco-Russian Alliance.
  • Coal, iron and steel grew in the Donbas in Ukraine, oil in Baku, and engineering and textiles in St Petersburg and Moscow. Output of pig iron and coal rose several times between 1890 and 1900, at a rate often put at about 8 per cent a year.
  • Industry was concentrated in huge factories such as the Putilov works in St Petersburg, which employed tens of thousands of workers, making labour organisation and protest easier.
  • The cost fell on the peasantry through high indirect taxes, and grain was exported even when people were hungry. Witte himself recognised the risk and warned about peasant discontent.
  • The industrial depression of 1900-03 caused unemployment and strikes. By 1914 Russia was the world's fifth industrial power by output, but its output per head was low and it relied on foreign capital.

The peasants and the 1891 famine

  • The peasant economy suffered from small plots, outdated methods, rapid population growth and redemption payments. Land hunger grew as families increased.
  • A harvest failure in 1891 caused a famine across the Volga region and the black earth provinces. A cholera epidemic in 1892 killed hundreds of thousands.
  • Government relief was slow, and private and zemstvo volunteers, including Tolstoy and Chekhov, played a major part. The famine weakened trust in the regime.
  • The famine encouraged educated Russians to form opposition groups: the Union of Liberation (1903), liberals who wanted a constitution, and the SRs.
  • Peasant unrest grew in the early 1900s with attacks on landlords' estates in 1902 in Kharkov and Poltava provinces, and violence rose again in 1905-06.
  • Workers were mostly first-generation peasants who kept links with their villages, which spread discontent between city and countryside.

The Russo-Japanese War, 1904–1905

  • Russia and Japan were rivals in Manchuria and Korea. Russia had leased Port Arthur from China in 1898, and the Trans-Siberian Railway gave it a route to the Far East.
  • Interior minister Plehve is reported to have wanted 'a short victorious war' to stop revolution, but it did the opposite.
  • Japan attacked Port Arthur without warning on 8 February 1904. The Russian army suffered defeats at Mukden (February-March 1905), and Port Arthur surrendered in January 1905.
  • Russia's Baltic Fleet sailed around the world and was destroyed by the Japanese at Tsushima on 27-28 May 1905.
  • The Treaty of Portsmouth (5 September 1905), mediated by US President Theodore Roosevelt, gave Japan southern Sakhalin, Port Arthur and a free hand in Korea.
  • The defeat humiliated the autocracy, revealed the weakness of its army, transport and administration, and was the immediate trigger for the 1905 revolution.

The 1905 Revolution

  • On Sunday 22 January 1905 (9 January in the old Russian calendar) a peaceful procession led by the priest Georgy Gapon went to the Winter Palace in St Petersburg to present a petition for reforms to the tsar. Troops opened fire, killing and wounding hundreds, in 'Bloody Sunday'.
  • The massacre destroyed the people's trust in the tsar as their protector. Strikes, peasant risings, mutinies (the battleship Potemkin in June 1905) and national movements in Poland and the Caucasus followed.
  • In October 1905 a general strike paralysed the railways and cities. The Petersburg Soviet (council) of workers' deputies was formed, with Trotsky as a leading member.
  • Witte advised the Tsar to grant concessions. The October Manifesto (17 October 1905) promised civil liberties, an elected Duma with legislative powers, and a wider franchise.
  • The Manifesto split the opposition: liberals (Octobrists) accepted it, while socialists did not. The army stayed loyal, the Soviet leaders were arrested in December, and an armed rising in Moscow was crushed.
  • A large French loan in April 1906 helped the government to survive without relying on the Duma.
  • The Fundamental Laws (April 1906) kept the Tsar's title of autocrat, his control of the army, foreign policy and ministers, and his right to veto laws and to dissolve the Duma and rule by decree between sessions.

The Dumas and Stolypin's reforms

Peter Stolypin, Prime Minister from 1906 to 1911, combined repression with agrarian reform in a bid to create a conservative, prosperous peasant class.

  • The First Duma (May-July 1906) demanded land reform and ministers answerable to it, and was dissolved after 73 days. The Second Duma (1907) was also dissolved.
  • In June 1907 the Tsar and Stolypin changed the electoral law, so that landowners and the rich dominated the Third Duma (1907-12) and the Fourth Duma (1912-17).
  • Stolypin crushed disorder with field courts-martial from 1906, which executed over a thousand people. The hangman's noose was called 'Stolypin's necktie'.
  • The land reform of November 1906 let peasants leave the commune, claim their land as private property and consolidate their strips. The Peasant Land Bank helped them buy land, and resettlement in Siberia was encouraged.
  • By 1914 about two million households (about a quarter) had left the communes, but most peasants stayed in the mir. Stolypin said he needed twenty years of peace.
  • He was shot by a revolutionary who was also a police informer, Dmitri Bogrov, at the Kiev opera in September 1911, and died days later.
  • After 1912 workers' protest revived, with strikes following the shooting of miners at the Lena goldfields in April 1912, and strikes in St Petersburg in summer 1914.

The impact of World War I and the February Revolution

  • Russia mobilised the largest army, but it was poorly led and short of rifles, shells and boots. Defeats at Tannenberg and the Masurian Lakes in 1914 and the great retreat of 1915 cost millions of casualties and prisoners. The Brusilov Offensive of 1916 was a success but cost heavily.
  • The war strained the economy: railways were overloaded, inflation rose as the government printed money, and food and fuel were short in the cities by winter 1916-17. Over fifteen million men were mobilised, reducing farm output.
  • Nicholas II took personal command of the army at the front in September 1915, leaving his wife Alexandra and Rasputin influential in government. Ministers changed rapidly. The Duma's Progressive Bloc demanded a government that had public confidence.
  • Grigori Rasputin, a peasant mystic whom the Tsarina trusted to ease the haemophilia of the heir, Alexei, was murdered by aristocrats in December 1916, a sign of the regime's isolation.
  • On 8 March 1917 (23 February in the Russian calendar), International Women's Day, women textile workers in Petrograd demanded bread. Strikes grew into a general strike, and the soldiers of the garrison joined the crowds instead of firing on them.
  • The Duma set up a Provisional Committee, and the Petrograd Soviet was revived. Army commanders told Nicholas to go, and he abdicated on 15 March 1917 (2 March in the Russian calendar), ending 300 years of Romanov rule. His brother Michael declined the crown.

The Provisional Government and the October Revolution

  • The Provisional Government, headed first by Prince Lvov and from July by Alexander Kerensky, shared power with the Soviet, an arrangement known as 'dual power'. Order No. 1 of the Soviet told soldiers to obey the Soviet's orders.
  • The Provisional Government introduced civil liberties but continued the war and delayed land reform until a Constituent Assembly was elected. The Kerensky offensive in July 1917 failed, and desertions rose.
  • Lenin returned from exile in Switzerland in April 1917 and issued the April Theses, demanding 'all power to the Soviets', peace, land to the peasants and bread for workers. The Bolsheviks grew in strength in the Soviets.
  • A Bolshevik rising in the July Days failed, but in August-September General Kornilov's attempted coup against Kerensky was defeated with the help of the Bolsheviks, who gained arms and prestige.
  • The Bolsheviks won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow Soviets by September. Trotsky chaired the Petrograd Soviet and organised the Military Revolutionary Committee.
  • On the night of 6-7 November 1917 (24-25 October in the old calendar) Bolshevik forces took bridges, stations and the telegraph, and the Winter Palace fell. Lenin announced the formation of a Soviet government under the Council of People's Commissars.
  • The new government issued decrees on peace and land and the right of nations to self-determination. In the Constituent Assembly election, the SRs won the most votes (about 40 per cent) and the Bolsheviks about a quarter; the Assembly met once in January 1918 and was dissolved by force.

Brest-Litovsk and the civil war

  • Lenin signed an armistice in December 1917 and, after Trotsky's attempt to stall, accepted the harsh Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918. Russia gave up Poland, the Baltic provinces, Ukraine and Finland, a huge loss of land, population, coal and industry.
  • The Bolsheviks moved the capital to Moscow in March 1918 and created the Cheka (December 1917) to crush opposition. The Romanov family were shot at Ekaterinburg in July 1918.
  • The civil war (1918-20) was fought between the Reds (Bolsheviks), organised by Trotsky as War Commissar into the Red Army, and the Whites, a loose mix of generals, monarchists and liberals such as Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich and Wrangel.
  • Britain, France, Japan and the USA sent troops to support the Whites and to guard supplies after 1918, but their effort was limited.
  • The Reds held central Russia with its railways, industry and capital and could move troops; the Whites were scattered and divided, and many peasants feared a return of the landlords.
  • War Communism (1918-21) meant seizing grain from peasants, nationalising industry and rationing, and the Red Terror targeted opponents.
  • The Red Army defeated Kolchak in Siberia (1919), Denikin and Yudenich (1919-20), and Wrangel in the Crimea in November 1920. In 1920 Poland defeated a Soviet advance on Warsaw. Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Poland became independent states.

Assessment: modernisation, crisis and revolution

  • Achievement: Witte's industrialisation built railways and heavy industry, and growth after 1906 was strong; Stolypin's reforms began to create a class of independent farmers.
  • Limit: industrial growth depended on foreign capital and on a poor peasantry, and it produced a discontented urban working class in huge factories.
  • Limit: political reform stopped short. The Dumas were weak, the autocrat could dismiss ministers, and Nicholas II mistrusted reformers and failed to use the chance of 1905-06.
  • Why the regime survived 1905 but not 1917: in 1905 the army stayed loyal and the opposition split; in 1917 the army was worn down by three years of defeat, the cities starved and the soldiers joined the strikers.
  • Historians' views: the optimists (such as Leopold Haimson and Hans Rogger in some respects) argue that Russia was modernising and that the war, not structural failure, caused collapse; pessimists argue that the autocracy was too weak to survive modernisation, and Orlando Figes stresses its failure to reform.
  • Contemporary views: liberals blamed the tsar's obstinacy; the Bolsheviks portrayed 1917 as a workers' revolution, though historians stress that it was also a coup by a disciplined party exploiting the failure of the Provisional Government.

Key terms

World war
A war fought across several continents by many states and empires, which in Russia's case led to military defeat, economic breakdown and revolution.
Self-determination
The principle that peoples may choose their own government and state; the Bolsheviks proclaimed it in November 1917, and Finland, Poland and the Baltic states became independent.
Autocracy
Rule by a monarch with unlimited power, as claimed by the tsars and preserved in the Fundamental Laws of 1906.
Mir
The Russian peasant commune, which held land collectively and redistributed strips among its households.
Duma
The Russian elected parliament created in 1905-06 with limited powers; four Dumas met before 1917.
October Manifesto
The decree of 17 October 1905 promising civil liberties and an elected Duma, issued on Witte's advice to end the revolution.
Soviet
A council of workers' and soldiers' deputies, first formed in St Petersburg in 1905 and revived in 1917.
Dual power
The shared authority in 1917 between the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet.
Bolsheviks
The faction of the Russian Social Democrats led by Lenin, who seized power in November 1917.
War Communism
The Bolshevik economic policy during the civil war of requisitioning grain, nationalising industry and rationing.
