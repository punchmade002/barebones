Serbia as a fulcrum of Great Power rivalry

Serbia became fully independent in 1878. Its drive to unite the South Slavs threatened Austria-Hungary, and its link with Russia made a Serbian quarrel a Great Power matter.

  • The Congress of Berlin (1878) recognised Serbia's full independence from the Ottoman Empire and enlarged its territory. Serbia became a kingdom in 1882 under the Obrenović dynasty.
  • Austria-Hungary dominated Serbia's economy, which depended on exporting pigs and other livestock to the Habsburg market, and it kept Serbia in its sphere until the early 1900s.
  • Serbian nationalists dreamed of uniting the South Slavs (Serbs, Croats, Slovenes and the Slavs of Bosnia) in a single state. This threatened a Habsburg empire with millions of South Slavs.
  • In June 1903 a group of army officers murdered King Alexander Obrenović and Queen Draga in Belgrade. Peter I of the Karadjordjević dynasty became king.
  • The new regime turned away from Vienna and towards Russia and France. Russia, the great Slav and Orthodox power, saw itself as protector of the Balkan Slavs and wanted to prevent Austrian control of the Balkans.
  • The Pig War began in 1906, when Austria-Hungary closed its market to Serbian livestock to punish its trade links with France. Serbia found other markets, including Germany, Italy and France, and the war weakened Austrian influence.
  • Austria-Hungary feared that a strong Serbia could encourage the Slav nationalities of the empire to leave, and Chief of Staff Conrad von Hötzendorf repeatedly urged a preventive war against it.

The Bosnian annexation crisis, 1908–1909

  • Austria-Hungary had occupied and administered Bosnia-Herzegovina since 1878, though it remained under nominal Ottoman rule.
  • The Young Turk revolution of July 1908 in the Ottoman Empire raised the possibility that Bosnia would be reclaimed, so Austria acted.
  • In September 1908 the Austrian foreign minister Aehrenthal and the Russian foreign minister Izvolsky met at Buchlau and discussed a swap: Austria would annex Bosnia, and Russia would gain support for opening the Straits to its warships.
  • Austria announced the annexation on 6 October 1908, before Izvolsky had won agreement on the Straits, and Russia felt cheated.
  • Serbia, which regarded Bosnia as the heartland of Serb population, mobilised and demanded compensation; Serbian nationalism and anti-Austrian feeling grew, and the Narodna Odbrana society was formed to resist Austrian rule.
  • In March 1909 Germany told Russia to accept the annexation and drop its support for Serbia, or face consequences. Russia, still weak after the war with Japan and revolution of 1905, gave way; Serbia also had to accept.
  • The crisis humiliated Russia and Serbia, and left both resolved not to back down again. Russia accelerated its rearmament, and Austria-Hungary and Germany drew closer.

The Balkan Wars, 1912–1913

  • In 1912 Russia helped to build the Balkan League of Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro, aimed at the Ottoman Empire. Italy had just taken Libya from the Ottomans in 1911–12.
  • The First Balkan War (October 1912 to May 1913) saw the League defeat the Ottomans and take most of their European territory.
  • The Great Powers meeting at the London Conference (1912–13) created an independent Albania, supported by Austria-Hungary and Italy, which denied Serbia the Adriatic port it wanted.
  • The League then quarrelled over the spoils. In the Second Balkan War (June to August 1913) Bulgaria attacked Serbia and Greece and was beaten by them, with Romania and the Ottomans joining in.
  • By the Treaty of Bucharest (August 1913) Serbia doubled in size and took much of Macedonia, including Kosovo.
  • A stronger Serbia, backed by Russia and unsatisfied over the Adriatic, alarmed Austria-Hungary further, and Conrad pressed for war against it.
  • The Balkan Wars showed that a regional conflict could be contained by Great Power conference diplomacy (London 1912–13); but they left Austria-Hungary feeling that the next time it would have to fight.

The Black Hand and the assassination at Sarajevo

  • The Black Hand (Unification or Death) was a secret society of Serbian army officers founded in May 1911. Its leader was Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, chief of Serbian military intelligence.
  • It sought a Greater Serbia and trained and armed Bosnian nationalists, particularly those of the Young Bosnia movement, who wanted to end Habsburg rule over the South Slavs.
  • Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, went to Sarajevo to inspect army manoeuvres, on 28 June 1914. It was St Vitus's Day (Vidovdan), the anniversary of the Serbian defeat at Kosovo in 1389.
  • A first attempt in the morning, a bomb thrown by Nedeljko Čabrinović, failed to kill the Archduke. Later his driver took a wrong turn, and the car stopped near Gavrilo Princip, who shot Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie.
  • Princip was a 19-year-old Bosnian Serb. He and the other conspirators had been given weapons and training in Serbia, but how much the Serbian government knew of the plot is disputed.
  • The assassins were arrested; Princip was too young for the death penalty and died of tuberculosis in prison in 1918.
  • Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and decided to use the murder to destroy it, but first needed to know how Germany would respond.

The July crisis, 1914

  • On 5 and 6 July Germany gave Austria-Hungary its 'blank cheque', a promise of unconditional support in whatever action it took against Serbia. The Kaiser and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg hoped for a short, localised war.
  • French President Poincaré and his prime minister visited St Petersburg from 20 to 23 July, reaffirming French support for Russia.
  • On 23 July Austria-Hungary presented an ultimatum to Serbia with ten demands and a 48-hour deadline. They included the suppression of anti-Austrian propaganda and a role for Austrian officials in the investigation within Serbia.
  • Serbia accepted most demands on 25 July but refused Austrian officials a role on its soil. Austria broke off relations and declared war on Serbia on 28 July.
  • Russia ordered partial mobilisation on 29 July and general mobilisation on 30 July. Germany demanded that Russia stop; when it did not, Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August.
  • Germany declared war on France on 3 August. Its Schlieffen Plan, which required a rapid defeat of France before Russia mobilised, meant it would attack France through Belgium whether or not France was the aggressor.
  • Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August after Belgium rejected an ultimatum. Britain, bound by the Treaty of London (1839) guaranteeing Belgian neutrality and concerned for the balance of power, declared war that day.
  • Bethmann Hollweg dismissed the treaty to the British ambassador as 'a scrap of paper'. Britain's entry made it a European war and, through the empires, a world war.

The Second International

The Second International (1889) was a federation of socialist and labour parties. Its members believed that workers had more in common across borders than with their own ruling classes, but in 1914 national loyalty proved stronger.

  • It was founded in Paris in 1889, with a permanent International Socialist Bureau in Brussels from 1900. The largest member was the German SPD, which in 1912 won 110 seats and became the biggest party in the Reichstag.
  • Its parties disagreed over reform or revolution, and over how to act in a war. Rosa Luxemburg and Lenin argued for revolutionary action; Jean Jaurès of France backed a general strike against war.
  • The Stuttgart Congress (1907) resolved that socialists should do everything to prevent war, and if it came, to end it quickly and use the crisis to hasten the fall of capitalism. The last part was added after amendments by Luxemburg, Lenin and Martov.
  • The Basel Congress (24-25 November 1912), called during the first Balkan War, issued a manifesto against war and threatened that governments would face revolution if they started one.
  • Jaurès, the main French pacifist, was assassinated in Paris by a nationalist on 31 July 1914.
  • On 4 August 1914 the SPD Reichstag group voted for war credits. French socialists backed the 'sacred union' and joined the government, and British Labour largely supported the war.
  • The International collapsed. Socialists divided into those who backed their governments, pacifists, and a revolutionary minority (including Luxemburg, Liebknecht and Lenin) who opposed it as an imperialist war.
  • Anti-war socialists met at Zimmerwald (September 1915) and Kienthal (April 1916); the split led directly to the communist parties and the Third International (Comintern) in 1919.

Key personality: Rosa Luxemburg

Luxemburg was a Marxist theorist, speaker and journalist on the left of the SPD, a participant in the International's debates and a leading opponent of the war.

  • She was born into a Jewish family on 5 March 1871 in Zamość in Russian-ruled Poland, and studied at Zurich. Her disability, a limp from childhood illness, did not prevent her from speaking in public.
  • She helped found a Polish socialist party (later the SDKPiL) in 1893 and moved to Germany in 1898, where she gained German citizenship by marriage and joined the SPD.
  • In Reform or Revolution (1899) she attacked the revisionism of Eduard Bernstein, who wanted socialism by gradual reform. In 1905-06 she travelled to Warsaw to take part in the Russian revolution.
  • She taught at the SPD's party school in Berlin (1907-14) and wrote The Accumulation of Capital (1913), an economic study of imperialism.
  • She spoke against war before 1914 and was jailed in 1914 for urging soldiers to disobey orders. When the SPD voted for war credits on 4 August 1914 she was shocked by its capitulation.
  • With Karl Liebknecht she formed the International Group, later the Spartacus League (1916). From prison she wrote the Junius pamphlet (1915, published 1916), condemning the war as imperialist and the SPD's stance as betrayal.
  • She spent most of the war in prison or protective custody, from 1915 to 1916 and from July 1916 until November 1918, and was freed by the German revolution.
  • On the Russian Revolution of 1917 she welcomed the Bolsheviks' seizure of power but criticised their suppression of democracy and the Constituent Assembly.
  • She helped found the Communist Party of Germany (KPD) at the end of December 1918, but opposed the Spartacist rising of January 1919, which she thought premature. She was murdered by Freikorps soldiers on 15 January 1919 and her body thrown into a Berlin canal.
  • Contemporaries: the SPD leaders and conservatives viewed her as a dangerous agitator, 'Red Rosa'; to supporters, she was a courageous principled critic.
  • Historians now see her as a major Marxist thinker and a critic of both militarism and dictatorship, although they argue over whether her ideas would have worked in practice.

Causes of the First World War: long-term and short-term

  • Alliances: Europe was divided into the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente, so a local quarrel could bring in all the Great Powers.
  • Militarism and plans: the powers built up large armies and conscription, and had mobilisation plans that left little time for diplomacy; the Schlieffen Plan required a swift attack on France.
  • Naval and colonial rivalry: the Anglo-German naval race and the Moroccan crises had poisoned relations between the powers.
  • Nationalism: South Slav nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary, French revanche aimed at Alsace-Lorraine, and pan-Slavism and pan-Germanism pushed Russia and Germany towards confrontation.
  • Short-term: the Bosnian crisis, the Balkan Wars, the assassination at Sarajevo, the blank cheque, the ultimatum and the sequence of mobilisations.
  • The Balkans were where the interests of Austria-Hungary and Russia collided, and each feared that backing down would cost it Great Power status.

Assessment: who was responsible?

  • Contemporary German view: Germany had been encircled and was forced into a defensive war by Russian mobilisation; this became the basis of German resistance to the war guilt clause later.
  • Contemporary Entente view: Germany and Austria-Hungary were aggressors, shown by the blank cheque, the ultimatum, and the invasion of Belgium.
  • Fritz Fischer (1961) argued that German leaders deliberately risked war in July 1914 to achieve world-power aims; his view raised an enduring debate about German war aims.
  • Christopher Clark, in The Sleepwalkers (2012), argues that the leaders of all the powers, including Serbia, Russia and France, stumbled into a war none fully intended, stressing the Balkans and contingency.
  • Austria-Hungary's decision to crush Serbia, Serbian-backed nationalism, Russian backing for Serbia and Germany's blank cheque were all necessary links in the chain; historians differ on the weight of each.
  • The war-guilt question became part of the peace settlement (see the notes on the Peace Settlement).

Key terms

Nationalism
Loyalty to a nation and the belief that it should be united and self-governing; South Slav nationalism in Serbia and Bosnia threatened Austria-Hungary.
Balance of power
A situation in which no single state can dominate Europe because rivals combine to check it; Britain's entry in 1914 was justified by this principle and the neutrality of Belgium.
Second International
The federation of socialist and labour parties founded in Paris in 1889, which passed anti-war resolutions but collapsed in August 1914.
Black Hand
The Serbian officers' secret society, Unification or Death, founded in 1911 and led by Apis, which supported the Bosnian conspirators.
Young Bosnia
The movement of young Bosnian Serb and South Slav nationalists, to which Gavrilo Princip belonged, who wanted to end Habsburg rule.
Blank cheque
Germany's unconditional promise of support to Austria-Hungary on 5-6 July 1914.
Schlieffen Plan
The German plan to defeat France quickly by attacking through Belgium, before turning against Russia.
Mobilisation
The summoning and deployment of an army for war; in 1914 the timetable of mobilisations left little time for diplomacy.
Spartacus League
The revolutionary socialist group formed in 1916 by Luxemburg and Liebknecht to oppose the war; it became the core of the German Communist Party.
