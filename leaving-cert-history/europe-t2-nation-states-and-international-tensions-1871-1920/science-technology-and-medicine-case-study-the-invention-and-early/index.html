Key personality: Marie Curie

Marie Curie was a Polish-born physicist and chemist who worked in France. Her research on radioactivity made her one of the most important scientists of the period and the first woman to win a Nobel Prize.

  • Maria Skłodowska was born in Warsaw on 7 November 1867, when Poland was under Russian rule. Women could not attend university there, so in 1891 she went to study physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne in Paris.
  • In 1895 she married the physicist Pierre Curie. After Henri Becquerel found in 1896 that uranium gave off penetrating rays, she began research on them for her doctorate and coined the term 'radioactivity'.
  • She found that the mineral pitchblende was more radioactive than the uranium it contained and concluded that it must hold new elements. In July 1898 the Curies announced polonium, named after her homeland, and in December 1898 radium.
  • They processed tonnes of pitchblende in a shed to separate radium salt, which she achieved by 1902. Pure radium metal was isolated with André Debierne in 1910.
  • Marie Curie shared the 1903 Nobel Prize for Physics with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel for the study of radioactivity, becoming the first woman to win a Nobel Prize.
  • Pierre was killed in a street accident in Paris in April 1906. Marie took over his post and became the first woman professor at the Sorbonne.
  • She won the 1911 Nobel Prize for Chemistry for the discovery of polonium and radium. She is the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
  • In 1911 the French Academy of Sciences rejected her membership by a narrow vote, and the press attacked her over a relationship with the physicist Paul Langevin, partly on xenophobic grounds.
  • During the First World War she organised mobile X-ray units, known as 'petites Curies', for field hospitals and trained women to run them, with her daughter Irène. She also set up fixed radiological posts.
  • She founded the Radium Institute in Paris (opened 1914) and died on 4 July 1934 of aplastic anaemia, probably caused by long exposure to radiation. Her notebooks are still radioactive.
  • Contemporaries admired her as an exceptional scientist, though some were hostile to a foreign woman in a leading scientific position. Historians regard her as a founder of the study of radioactivity and a pioneer for women in science.

Medicine: germ theory, vaccines and antiseptics

The discovery that microbes cause disease transformed medicine and surgery between 1870 and 1920.

  • Louis Pasteur showed in the 1860s that microbes cause fermentation and decay, and argued that they cause disease. Pasteurisation (heating liquids to kill microbes) was developed from this work.
  • He developed vaccines using weakened microbes against chicken cholera (about 1879), anthrax (1881) and rabies. In July 1885 he treated a nine-year-old boy, Joseph Meister, who had been bitten by a rabid dog, and the boy survived.
  • The Pasteur Institute was founded in Paris in 1887-88 to continue research and make vaccines.
  • Robert Koch in Germany developed methods to isolate bacteria in pure culture. He identified the anthrax bacterium (1876), the tuberculosis bacillus (announced on 24 March 1882) and the cholera bacterium (1883), and won the Nobel Prize for Medicine in 1905.
  • Emil von Behring and Kitasato Shibasaburo developed antitoxins against diphtheria and tetanus in 1890, and Behring received the first Nobel Prize for Medicine in 1901.
  • Paul Ehrlich developed Salvarsan, the first effective drug against syphilis, in 1909. It was the first modern chemotherapy.
  • Joseph Lister, a Scottish surgeon, used carbolic acid to clean wounds and instruments and spray the operating theatre from 1865-67. This antiseptic surgery cut deaths from infection.
  • Anaesthetics, ether (1846) and chloroform (1847, used by Queen Victoria in 1853), had made longer operations possible, and with asepsis (sterile conditions, developed in Germany in the 1880s-90s) surgery became much safer.
  • Public health measures such as clean water supplies and sewers, built from the 1850s, as well as better food and housing, did much to cut deaths from cholera and typhoid in European cities.

X-rays, electricity and communications

  • Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays in Würzburg in November 1895. Doctors quickly used them to find broken bones and bullets, and he won the first Nobel Prize for Physics in 1901.
  • In the First World War X-ray equipment became standard in military hospitals, and Curie's mobile units brought it close to the front.
  • Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876, and telephone exchanges and networks spread rapidly in cities in the 1880s and 1890s.
  • Thomas Edison invented the phonograph in 1877 and developed a long-lasting electric light bulb in 1879. The first power stations followed in the 1880s, and electric lighting, trams and underground railways spread in cities.
  • Guglielmo Marconi, who had an Irish mother, Annie Jameson, sent wireless signals over longer distances from 1895-96. In December 1901 he claimed to receive a signal across the Atlantic in Newfoundland, sent from Poldhu in Cornwall. He shared the 1909 Nobel Prize for Physics.
  • Wireless became important at sea: it was used to call for help when the Titanic sank in April 1912, and it was used in military communication in the First World War.
  • The Nobel Prizes were first awarded in 1901 under the will of the Swedish chemist Alfred Nobel, who invented dynamite, and several early winners were German or French scientists.

Flight and other advances

  • Count Zeppelin built rigid airships from 1900, and the Wright brothers made the first controlled, powered flight of a heavier-than-air machine at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, on 17 December 1903.
  • Louis Blériot flew across the English Channel on 25 July 1909. Aviation meetings and prizes attracted publicity across Europe.
  • In the First World War aircraft were used for reconnaissance, artillery spotting, bombing and air combat. Production rose from hundreds to tens of thousands, and engines improved greatly.
  • John Alcock and Arthur Whitten Brown made the first non-stop transatlantic flight in June 1919, from Newfoundland to a bog near Clifden in Co. Galway.
  • Rudolf Diesel patented the diesel engine in 1892, and Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch developed the synthesis of ammonia (1909-13), which made artificial fertilisers and explosives possible.
  • Albert Einstein published the special theory of relativity in 1905 and the general theory in 1915.

Case study: the origins of the motor car

The motor car emerged in Germany and France in the 1880s from the internal combustion engine, and from the bicycle and carriage industries. Steam and electric vehicles had been tried, but petrol won out.

  • Nikolaus Otto built the first practical four-stroke internal combustion engine in 1876, burning gas. It became the basis of the petrol engine.
  • Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach, who had worked for Otto's company, developed a small, light, high-speed petrol engine in the 1880s. In 1885 they fitted it to a wooden motorcycle, and in 1886 to a carriage.
  • Karl Benz founded Benz and Cie in Mannheim in 1883 and built a three-wheeled vehicle with a single-cylinder engine, running in 1885. He applied for a patent for his 'Motorwagen' on 29 January 1886, and it was granted in November 1886. It is regarded as the first practical car designed as a whole.
  • Benz and Daimler worked independently and never met. Their firms merged in 1926 to form Daimler-Benz.
  • Bertha Benz, with her sons Eugen and Richard, drove a Motorwagen from Mannheim to Pforzheim, about 106 km, in August 1888, without telling her husband. She bought fuel (ligroin) at a pharmacy in Wiesloch and made repairs on the way. The journey proved the car's usefulness and publicity helped sales.
  • In France, Panhard et Levassor and Peugeot began building cars in 1890-91 using Daimler engines. France led early car production, with races such as Paris-Rouen (1894) boosting interest. Louis Renault built his first car in 1898 and the Renault company followed in 1899.
  • John Boyd Dunlop, a Scottish veterinary surgeon in Belfast, patented the pneumatic tyre in 1888 for his son's tricycle. Dunlop tyres helped bicycles and cars run more smoothly, and the Michelin brothers brought out a detachable pneumatic tyre in 1891.
  • In Britain the 'red flag' law limited early vehicles to walking pace until 1896. The Locomotives on Highways Act of 1896 raised the limit to 12 mph and was celebrated by the Emancipation Run from London to Brighton in November 1896.
  • Daimler died in 1900. In 1901 the Daimler company produced the Mercedes, named after the daughter of the businessman Emil Jellinek, a car with a lighter engine and a lower body that set the pattern for later cars.
  • Charles Rolls and Henry Royce met in 1904, and the Rolls-Royce Silver Ghost (1906) made Britain a leader in luxury cars.

Case study: Ford, mass production and the Model T

  • Early cars were handmade luxury goods for the rich. Henry Ford founded the Ford Motor Company in 1903 in Detroit with the aim of building a cheap car for ordinary people.
  • The Model T, launched in October 1908, was simple, strong and easy to repair. The price fell from about 850 dollars to under 300 dollars by the early 1920s as output rose.
  • In 1913 Ford introduced the moving assembly line at Highland Park, Michigan, in which the chassis moved past workers who each did one task. The time to build a chassis fell from over twelve hours to about ninety minutes.
  • Repetitive work caused high turnover. In January 1914 Ford announced a wage of five dollars for an eight-hour day, about double the usual pay, which kept workers and made them potential customers.
  • By 1920 the USA had millions of cars and produced most of the world's cars. Ford's mass production methods, later called Fordism, were studied and copied by European firms.
  • In Ireland the Gordon Bennett motor race was held in Co. Kildare and Carlow in July 1903, and the Motor Car Act 1903 introduced registration and a speed limit of 20 mph in Britain and Ireland.

Case study: economic and social effects of the motor car

  • The car industry drove demand for steel, rubber, glass, petrol and machine tools, and created work in manufacturing, garages and repair. Oil companies and tyre firms expanded.
  • Roads were improved and tarred for motor traffic, and governments passed traffic laws, licences and speed limits. Early motorists faced hostility from pedestrians, farmers and cyclists.
  • Cars were first status symbols for the rich. Motoring clubs and early tourism grew, and doctors, salesmen and country priests used cars before 1914.
  • In the First World War, motor lorries, cars and ambulances were vital. About 600 Paris taxis carried troops to the Marne battle in September 1914, and motor lorries supplied Verdun along the 'Sacred Way' in 1916.
  • War made engines and lorries more powerful, and the war also gave rise to the tank and armoured car, as well as aircraft engines.
  • After the war ex-army lorries and cheap cars allowed bus services and road haulage to compete with the railways in the 1920s.
  • Cars were still too expensive for most Europeans by 1920, and they were much less common than in the USA, where the Model T made them part of everyday life.

Assessment: science and technology as drivers of change

  • Achievement: germ theory, antiseptics and vaccines saved millions of lives, and new medical technologies such as the X-ray changed diagnosis.
  • Limit: knowledge of radiation danger came too late for many pioneers, including Curie, and the benefits of medicine reached the poor only slowly.
  • Achievement: electricity, the telephone and wireless changed communication, and the car and aeroplane transformed transport.
  • Limit: these technologies also served war. Aircraft, motor vehicles, chemistry and wireless were used in the First World War, producing deadlier weapons.
  • The car's early history: Germany and France invented it, but the USA, through the Model T and mass production, made it a mass consumer product.
  • Contemporary view: people saw science as progress and as a source of national prestige, as in Curie's French and Polish recognition. Later historians stress that the social effects of the car came mostly after 1920.

Key terms

Radioactivity
The spontaneous emission of rays by certain elements such as uranium, polonium and radium; Marie Curie named and studied the phenomenon.
Germ theory
The principle that many diseases are caused by microbes, established by Pasteur and Koch in the 1860s-1880s.
Antiseptic
A chemical such as carbolic acid, used from the 1860s by Lister to kill microbes on wounds and instruments.
Vaccine
A preparation of weakened or killed microbes that gives immunity to a disease, as in Pasteur's vaccines for anthrax and rabies.
X-rays
Penetrating rays discovered by Röntgen in 1895, quickly used in medicine to see bones and foreign objects.
Internal combustion engine
An engine in which fuel burns inside a cylinder to drive a piston; Otto's four-stroke engine of 1876 made it practical.
Pneumatic tyre
An air-filled tyre patented by John Boyd Dunlop in Belfast in 1888, which made bicycles and cars smoother.
Assembly line
A system in which the product moves past workers who each perform one task; Ford introduced the moving line at Highland Park in 1913.
Mass production
The manufacture of standard products in large quantities with specialised machines and labour, lowering the cost per unit.
