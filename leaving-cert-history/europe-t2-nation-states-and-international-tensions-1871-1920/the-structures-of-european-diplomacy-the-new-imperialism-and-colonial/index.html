The end of Bismarck's system: the Franco-Russian Alliance

After Bismarck's fall, Germany's new leaders let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse. Russia and France, both isolated, drew together and ended France's isolation.

  • Chancellor Caprivi and the Foreign Office official Holstein decided not to renew the Reinsurance Treaty in 1890, believing it was incompatible with the Dual Alliance and with good relations with Britain.
  • Russia then turned to France: French banks began large loans to Russia from 1888 and the powers agreed to consult each other in a political understanding in 1891, after a French naval squadron visited Kronstadt.
  • A military convention was agreed in August 1892 and the Franco-Russian Alliance was formally ratified in January 1894. It was a defensive alliance directed at the Triple Alliance powers.
  • If France was attacked by Germany, or by Italy supported by Germany, Russia would attack Germany; if Russia was attacked by Germany, or by Austria-Hungary supported by Germany, France would attack Germany. Both would mobilise if any Triple Alliance member mobilised.
  • The alliance gave Germany the risk of a war on two fronts, the danger Bismarck had tried to avoid, and it later shaped the Schlieffen Plan.
  • The alliance was cemented by French investment in Russian railways and industry, and lasted until 1917.

The Entente Cordiale, the Anglo-Russian Entente and the two blocs

Britain stayed outside permanent alliances until the early twentieth century. The Boer War showed it had few friends, and it began to settle quarrels with its imperial rivals instead.

  • Britain's policy of 'splendid isolation' lost its value after the Boer War, when most European opinion was hostile, and after Germany began to build a large navy.
  • The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902) gave Britain support in the Far East against Russia and allowed it to concentrate its navy nearer home; it was renewed in 1905 and 1911.
  • The Entente Cordiale (8 April 1904) settled colonial disputes: Britain was recognised in Egypt, France in Morocco, and the two settled questions over Siam, Madagascar, and the Newfoundland fisheries.
  • The Entente was not an alliance, but after the first Moroccan crisis British and French military staffs began secret talks in 1906, and in 1912 their navies agreed to divide responsibilities in the Channel and the Mediterranean.
  • The Anglo-Russian Entente (31 August 1907) settled rivalries in Asia: Persia was divided into a Russian northern zone, a British southern zone and a neutral middle; Afghanistan was recognised as a British sphere; both promised to stay out of Tibet.
  • The Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia was therefore a loose understanding, without a common military commitment, facing the formal Triple Alliance.
  • The Triple Alliance was weaker than it seemed: Italy signed a secret agreement with France in 1902 (the Prinetti-Barrère agreement) promising neutrality if France was attacked, and attacked the Ottoman Empire for Libya in 1911–12.
  • From 1906 to 1914 each bloc grew more fearful of the other, and Germany spoke of 'encirclement' while Britain and France feared German ambitions; this made each crisis seem part of a struggle between the blocs.

Causes of the New Imperialism

From about 1880 the European powers competed to acquire territory in Africa, Asia and the Pacific. Historians group the causes into economic, strategic, nationalist, missionary and ideological motives.

  • Economic: industrial states needed raw materials such as rubber, cotton, palm oil, copper and later oil, and wanted protected markets when tariffs rose in Europe from the 1870s.
  • Economic theory: the British writer J.A. Hobson argued in Imperialism: A Study (1902) that capitalists invested overseas because of surplus capital at home; Lenin built on this in Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1916). Historians dispute it, since most trade and investment went to areas outside the empires.
  • Strategic: states sought naval bases, coaling stations and secure routes. Britain occupied Egypt in 1882 to protect the Suez Canal, the route to India, and then moved to control the Nile valley.
  • Nationalist: colonies were seen as a mark of Great Power status, and newspapers, colonial societies and the public cheered each acquisition. After defeat in 1871, France sought compensation overseas under Jules Ferry.
  • Missionary and humanitarian: churches wanted to spread Christianity and end the slave trade, and explorers such as David Livingstone and H.M. Stanley made Africa known to the public.
  • Ideological: ideas of racial and cultural superiority, including Social Darwinism, and a belief in a duty to 'civilise' other peoples (France's mission civilisatrice; Kipling's poem The White Man's Burden, 1899) were used to justify rule.
  • Technology made conquest easy: quinine against malaria, steamships, the telegraph and rifles and machine guns such as the Maxim gun gave Europeans overwhelming military advantage.
  • Competition fed on itself: once one power claimed territory, others felt that they had to do the same or be shut out.

The Scramble for Africa

  • In 1880 only about a tenth of Africa was under European control, mostly coastal areas such as Algeria, the Cape and Senegal; by 1914 about nine tenths of the continent had been taken.
  • The scramble was triggered by Leopold II's claims on the Congo, France's advance in West Africa and Tunisia (1881), and Britain's occupation of Egypt in 1882.
  • The Berlin Conference (November 1884 to February 1885), called by Bismarck and attended by fourteen powers including the United States, set rules for claims: notice to other powers, effective occupation, and freedom of trade on the Congo and Niger rivers.
  • No African ruler was represented at the Conference, and it did not itself divide Africa, but it made the partition faster and gave it a legal shape.
  • Britain's aim was a belt of territory from the Cape to Cairo, promoted by Cecil Rhodes, Prime Minister of the Cape Colony (1890–96); France sought to link its territories from Senegal to the Red Sea.
  • These two ambitions crossed at Fashoda in 1898. Germany took South West Africa, Togo, Cameroon and East Africa; Italy took Eritrea and Somaliland and, in 1911–12, Libya.
  • Africans resisted: Ethiopia defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa (1 March 1896), and only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent by 1914.

Leopold II and the Congo

  • Leopold II, King of the Belgians, founded the International African Association in 1876 and used Stanley to claim the Congo basin for himself.
  • The Berlin Conference recognised the Congo Free State in 1885 as Leopold's personal possession, on the promise that he would end slavery and keep free trade.
  • From the 1890s the state forced Africans to collect wild rubber under quotas, as demand rose with the bicycle and the motor car; failure was punished by hostage-taking, beatings, mutilation and the burning of villages.
  • The Force Publique, the colonial army, enforced the system. Mortality rose sharply from violence, disease and starvation; the population fell by millions, though estimates vary widely.
  • E.D. Morel, a shipping clerk who noticed the Congo's trade figures, and the British diplomat Roger Casement (whose 1904 report documented the abuses) led the Congo Reform Association (1904).
  • Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness (1899) was based on his own time in the Congo, and Mark Twain wrote a satire on Leopold's rule.
  • International pressure forced the Belgian parliament to take over the colony in 1908, ending Leopold's personal rule.

Imperial rivalry in Asia and the Pacific

  • France created French Indochina from Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos (1880s–1890s); Britain added Burma and Malaya; the Dutch held the East Indies.
  • China was defeated by Japan in the Sino-Japanese War (1894–95). The powers then forced concessions: Germany leased Kiaochow (Tsingtao), Russia Port Arthur, Britain Weihaiwei and France Guangzhouwan, and each claimed a sphere of influence.
  • The United States, which had defeated Spain in 1898 and taken the Philippines, Guam and Puerto Rico, proposed an 'Open Door' for trade in China (1899).
  • The Boxer Rising (1899–1901) was a Chinese uprising against foreigners and Christians, which besieged the foreign legations in Beijing in 1900; an eight-nation force relieved them.
  • Japan modernised after 1868, built a strong army and navy and defeated Russia in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05), the first time an Asian power beat a European Great Power in modern times.
  • The defeat turned Russia's attention back towards the Balkans and Europe, and contributed to the revolution of 1905 (see the notes on Tsarist Russia).

Fashoda (1898) and the Boer War (1899–1902)

  • Fashoda: in July 1898 a small French expedition under Captain Marchand reached Fashoda on the Upper Nile in Sudan, hoping to claim a French East-West belt of Africa.
  • In September 1898, after his victory at Omdurman, General Kitchener arrived with a far stronger force. Both sides refused to withdraw and war was feared.
  • France, isolated and facing the Dreyfus Affair at home, backed down in November 1898, and an agreement in March 1899 set the boundary between British and French spheres.
  • Fashoda poisoned Anglo-French relations until the settlement of 1904; but it also showed the danger of colonial clashes and encouraged both powers to solve their disputes.
  • The Boer War began in October 1899 after disputes over British claims on the Boer republics, the Transvaal and the Orange Free State, whose gold and rights of foreign settlers were at issue; the failed Jameson Raid of 1895–96 had already soured relations.
  • The Boers won early successes (Black Week, December 1899). Britain sent large reinforcements and, under Kitchener, burned farms and interned Boer civilians in camps, where over 20,000 died.
  • The Treaty of Vereeniging (31 May 1902) ended the war and the two republics were absorbed into the British Empire.
  • The war damaged British prestige, aroused hostility in Europe (Germany's Kruger telegram of 1896 had already caused outrage), and persuaded Britain to end its isolation.

The Moroccan crises, 1905–1906 and 1911

  • After the Entente Cordiale of 1904, France moved to control Morocco, one of the last independent states in Africa.
  • Wilhelm II landed at Tangier on 31 March 1905 and spoke in support of Moroccan independence. Germany hoped to test the Entente and to win concessions from France.
  • German pressure forced the resignation of the French foreign minister Delcassé in June 1905, but the crisis ended at the Algeciras Conference (January to April 1906).
  • Algeciras was a diplomatic defeat for Germany: only Austria-Hungary supported it, Britain, Russia, Italy and the United States backed France, and France and Spain received the policing of Morocco's ports.
  • The crisis strengthened the Entente: British and French generals began secret staff talks.
  • In May 1911 France sent troops to Fez. Germany, in July 1911, sent the gunboat Panther to Agadir, demanding compensation, and Lloyd George warned in his Mansion House speech (21 July) that Britain would not be ignored.
  • The Agadir crisis was settled in November 1911: Germany accepted French control of Morocco in return for part of the French Congo (the 'New Cameroon' strip).
  • German opinion saw the settlement as a humiliation and Britain and France drew closer; the crisis led directly to the naval talks of 1912 between Britain and France.

Assessment: did imperial rivalry cause tension in Europe?

  • Imperial rivalry created crises (Fashoda, the Boer War and Morocco) that raised tension and made Britain and Germany suspicious of each other.
  • Imperial agreements also reduced tension, as the Entente Cordiale and the Anglo-Russian Entente show; the powers were often able to settle colonial quarrels by compromise.
  • Imperialism made the alliance blocs more rigid: the Moroccan crises turned the Entente Cordiale from a colonial settlement into a quasi-alliance.
  • Imperial rivalries were mostly settled peacefully, so the war in 1914 came from the Balkans and Europe's own alliance system rather than from colonial clashes.
  • Contemporaries in Germany blamed British and French encirclement for their sense of insecurity, while the British and French blamed German pressure for the Moroccan crises.
  • Lenin and Hobson, writing from different positions, saw capitalism and imperialism as an underlying cause of the war; many later historians see imperialism as one factor alongside nationalism, militarism and alliances.

Key terms

New Imperialism
The competition from about 1880 among European powers, and later the United States and Japan, to acquire colonies and spheres of influence in Africa, Asia and the Pacific.
Balance of power
A situation in which no one state can dominate Europe because rival states ally or act together to check it; the alliance blocs of 1890-1914 were meant to preserve it.
Nationalism
Pride in and loyalty to one's own nation, which made colonies a matter of prestige and strengthened rivalry between the powers.
Triple Entente
The loose grouping of Britain, France and Russia, formed by the Franco-Russian Alliance (1894), Entente Cordiale (1904) and Anglo-Russian Entente (1907).
Entente Cordiale
The 1904 agreement settling colonial disputes between Britain and France, giving Britain a free hand in Egypt and France in Morocco.
Social Darwinism
The application of the idea of the survival of the fittest to nations and races, used to justify conquest and the idea of a civilising mission.
Scramble for Africa
The rapid European partition of Africa between about 1880 and 1914, which left only Ethiopia and Liberia independent.
Effective occupation
The rule agreed at the Berlin Conference that a power had to administer and control territory, not merely claim it, for its claim to be recognised.
Sphere of influence
A region, such as parts of China or Persia, in which one power held special economic or political privileges without formal annexation.
Splendid isolation
Britain's policy of avoiding permanent alliances with other European powers, abandoned in the early 1900s.
