Expressions of national identity in the arts

Between 1871 and 1914 composers, writers, architects and painters presented national history, folk tradition and landscape as proof of a people's distinct identity.

  • Richard Wagner's operas drew on Germanic myth. His Ring cycle was first staged in full at his own theatre in Bayreuth in 1876, and the Bayreuth Festival continued after his death in 1883 as a shrine of German culture.
  • Germany's new national monuments expressed the Reich's identity: the Hermann Monument in the Teutoburg Forest (1875) honoured the Germanic leader who defeated the Romans, and the Monument to the Battle of the Nations at Leipzig was unveiled in 1913.
  • Bedřich Smetana's cycle of symphonic poems Má vlast (My Country, 1874-79), which includes The Moldau, celebrated Bohemian history and landscape at a time when the Czechs sought more autonomy within Austria-Hungary.
  • Jean Sibelius's Finlandia (1899-1900) became a symbol of Finnish resistance to Russian control, and Edvard Grieg used Norwegian folk music in Peer Gynt (1876).
  • Edward Elgar's Pomp and Circumstance March No. 1 (1901) and the Coronation Ode (1902), with its 'Land of Hope and Glory', expressed British imperial confidence.
  • Rudyard Kipling celebrated British soldiers and the Empire in poems such as Recessional (1897) and The White Man's Burden (1899). His only son John was killed at Loos in 1915, and Kipling later wrote for the Imperial War Graves Commission.
  • Across Europe, governments and nationalist societies founded national museums, galleries and theatres, taught national history in schools and fostered national languages.

The literature of World War I

Writers' responses to the war moved from patriotic enthusiasm in 1914-15 to disillusionment and protest by 1917, and the post-war view of the war was shaped by the protest writers.

  • Rupert Brooke's 1914 sonnets, including The Soldier, expressed idealism about dying for England. Brooke died of blood poisoning on the way to Gallipoli on 23 April 1915 and was celebrated as a hero. Julian Grenfell and the Canadian John McCrae (In Flanders Fields, 1915) wrote in a similar spirit.
  • Siegfried Sassoon won the Military Cross, then in July 1917 published a statement condemning the prolongation of the war. Instead of a court martial, he was sent to Craiglockhart War Hospital in Edinburgh. His Counter-Attack (1918) used satire against generals and the home front.
  • Other soldier-poets included Isaac Rosenberg (killed in 1918), Edmund Blunden and Ivor Gurney. Robert Graves wrote the memoir Goodbye to All That (1929).
  • Henri Barbusse's novel Le Feu (Under Fire, 1916), which won the Prix Goncourt, described the lives of French infantry squad in the trenches and attacked the war's profiteers.
  • Ernst Jünger's Storm of Steel (1920) took the soldier's viewpoint of a German officer who found meaning in combat, and contrasts with later pacifist works.
  • Erich Maria Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front (1929), a German bestseller, was a post-war novel that showed young soldiers ruined by war. Together with Graves and Vera Brittain's Testament of Youth (1933), it shaped later memory of the war.
  • Wartime censorship limited what could be published at the front, so the most critical works came out in 1918 or after.

Key personality: Wilfred Owen

Owen was a participant in the war who became its most famous poetic witness. Nearly all of his work was published after his death.

  • Wilfred Owen was born in Oswestry, Shropshire, on 18 March 1893, the eldest child of a railway official. He worked as a lay assistant to a vicar and as a teacher in France before the war.
  • He enlisted in the Artists Rifles in October 1915 and was commissioned in the Manchester Regiment in 1916. He fought on the Somme and in the Beaumont-Hamel area in January 1917.
  • After concussion and weeks in a shell-hole he was diagnosed with neurasthenia (shell shock) and sent to Craiglockhart War Hospital near Edinburgh in June 1917.
  • At Craiglockhart he met Siegfried Sassoon in August 1917. Sassoon read his drafts, advised him to write plainly and introduced him to literary circles, including Robert Graves.
  • Between autumn 1917 and 1918 he wrote or finished his best poems, including Dulce et Decorum Est (an attack on 'the old Lie' that it is sweet and fitting to die for one's country), Anthem for Doomed Youth, Strange Meeting, Futility and Exposure.
  • He used pararhyme (consonance in place of full rhyme) and graphic detail of gas, mud and death. He wrote in a draft preface that 'the poetry is in the pity'.
  • Owen returned to the front in 1918 and won the Military Cross for his actions near Joncourt in October. He was killed on 4 November 1918 in an attack on the Sambre-Oise canal, a week before the armistice, aged 25.
  • Only a few of his poems appeared in his lifetime. Sassoon and Edith Sitwell published a first edition in 1919-20, and Edmund Blunden produced a fuller edition in 1931.
  • Contemporaries: in the 1920s Owen was one of several war poets, and the public were drawn at first to Brooke. Interest grew after the 1930s as pacifist feeling spread.
  • Historians now regard Owen as the main English voice of the war's suffering. Some, such as Dominic Hibberd, have stressed his complex character. Others, like Jon Stallworthy, stressed his craft. Some critics note that the 'poets' war' view of 1914-18 was shaped by an educated officer class, and that most soldiers did not write such poetry.

Church and state in Germany: the Kulturkampf

  • The Reich was two-thirds Protestant, and about a third Catholic. After the First Vatican Council declared papal infallibility (1870), Bismarck feared that Catholics owed their loyalty to Rome and saw the Catholic Centre Party (founded 1870) as an enemy of the new state.
  • Bismarck also feared Polish Catholics in the east, and liberals welcomed an attack on the Church as a defence of modern secular society. The campaign was called Kulturkampf ('struggle for civilisation') by the liberal Rudolf Virchow.
  • In 1871 the pulpit law made it an offence for clergy to criticise the state in sermons, and the Catholic department of the Prussian ministry of culture was abolished. The Jesuits were expelled from Germany in 1872, and in March 1872 the School Supervision Law put schools under state inspection.
  • The May Laws of 1873, piloted by the Prussian culture minister Adalbert Falk, required priests to study at state universities and pass state exams, and made state approval necessary for church appointments. Civil marriage became compulsory in 1875.
  • Pope Pius IX condemned the laws in 1875. Bishops were imprisoned or exiled, and by 1876 many parishes had no priest. Catholics resisted, and the Centre Party doubled its seats, becoming the second-largest party.
  • Bismarck turned to the Centre in 1878-79 for support for tariffs and against socialism. He opened talks with the new pope, Leo XIII (elected February 1878), and in 1880-83 and 1886-87 most laws were repealed or relaxed. The Jesuit ban lasted until 1917.
  • The Kulturkampf failed. It strengthened Catholic political unity, made the Centre Party a permanent force in the Reichstag, and showed the limits of Bismarck's power.

Church and state in Italy: the Roman Question

  • The Kingdom of Italy, created in 1861, took the Papal States, and on 20 September 1870 Italian troops entered Rome, completing unification. The Pope lost his temporal power.
  • The Law of Guarantees (May 1871) declared the Pope's person inviolable, gave him use of the Vatican and Lateran palaces and a yearly income. Pius IX rejected it and refused to recognise the Italian state, calling himself a prisoner in the Vatican.
  • This dispute was known as the Roman Question. The papacy's refusal to accept the loss of Rome divided Italians between the liberal, anticlerical state and the Catholic population.
  • The non expedit instruction (1868, reinforced in 1874) forbade Catholics from voting in or standing for parliamentary elections, so that most Catholics stayed out of national politics, while Catholics played an active role in local government and social action.
  • The state expanded civil marriage, secular education and the closure of religious orders, while Catholics built their own organisations: Catholic Action, banks, newspapers and cooperatives.
  • The ban was relaxed gradually; in the 1913 Gentiloni Pact Catholic voters supported Giolitti's liberal candidates, and in 1919 Don Luigi Sturzo founded the Catholic Popular Party. The Roman Question was only settled in the Lateran Treaties of 1929.

Key personality: Pope Leo XIII

Leo XIII, pope from 1878 to 1903, tried to renew the church's relations with the modern world after the confrontational reign of Pius IX.

  • Vincenzo Gioacchino Pecci was born on 2 March 1810 and elected pope on 20 February 1878. He had been Archbishop of Perugia and a cardinal, and was a diplomat and scholar.
  • He ended the Kulturkampf by negotiation with Bismarck in the 1880s, and the Centre Party's cooperation with the government helped to repeal most of the laws.
  • In Rerum Novarum (15 May 1891) he addressed the 'condition of the working classes'. He defended private property and rejected socialism and class war, but upheld the right of workers to a just wage, decent conditions and to form trade unions, and called on states to protect the poor.
  • Rerum Novarum became the foundation of modern Catholic social teaching and encouraged Catholic trade unions and Christian democratic parties in Germany, Belgium, Italy and elsewhere.
  • His ralliement policy of 1890-92 urged French Catholics to accept the Third Republic and work within it. Monarchist Catholics largely refused, and the Dreyfus Affair and the anticlerical laws of the early 1900s, ending in the 1905 separation of church and state, undercut the policy.
  • He encouraged the study of Thomas Aquinas (Aeterni Patris, 1879) and opened the Vatican archives to scholars in 1883. In 1888 the Vatican condemned the Plan of Campaign and boycotting in Ireland.
  • He refused to accept the loss of the Papal States and kept the 'prisoner in the Vatican' stance, so the Roman Question remained open.
  • Contemporaries: liberals and many workers respected him as a thoughtful reformer, while conservative Catholics distrusted his accommodation with republics and the modern world. He died on 20 July 1903.
  • Historians: he is regarded as a pioneer of the modern papacy and of Catholic social thought, though critics argue that Rerum Novarum was cautious and that the ralliement failed in France.

Anti-Semitism in France

Anti-Jewish prejudice was old in Europe, but after 1870 racial and political anti-Semitism spread. The word anti-Semitism was coined by Wilhelm Marr in Germany in 1879.

  • Economic anxiety, nationalism and the humiliation of 1871 in France led nationalists to blame Jews and Freemasons for the country's weakness. Édouard Drumont's La France juive (1886) was a bestseller portraying Jews as a threat to France, and in 1892 he founded the paper La Libre Parole.
  • In October 1894 Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish artillery officer from Alsace, was arrested and accused of passing secrets to Germany, convicted by a secret court martial in December 1894 and sent to Devil's Island in French Guiana.
  • In 1896 Colonel Picquart, head of army intelligence, found that the real author of the leaked memorandum (the bordereau) was Major Esterhazy. The army suppressed this and Esterhazy was acquitted in January 1898; a forgery by Colonel Henry was later exposed.
  • On 13 January 1898, Émile Zola published the open letter 'J'accuse' in the newspaper L'Aurore, accusing the army of covering up the truth. Zola was convicted of libel and fled to England.
  • France divided into Dreyfusards (republicans, socialists such as Jaurès, many intellectuals) and anti-Dreyfusards (the army, much of the Catholic Church, nationalists and monarchists).
  • A second court martial at Rennes in 1899 found Dreyfus guilty again, with 'extenuating circumstances'; President Loubet pardoned him. In 1906 the court of appeals cleared him and he was reinstated in the army.
  • The Affair weakened the army and the Church's reputation and led to the anticlerical measures of the early 1900s. It also influenced Theodor Herzl, who covered the case as a journalist and published Der Judenstaat (1896); the First Zionist Congress met at Basel in 1897.

Anti-Semitism in Russia

  • About five million Jews lived in the Russian Empire in the late 1800s, the world's largest Jewish community. Most were confined to the Pale of Settlement, set up in 1791, and many were poor artisans and traders.
  • After Alexander II was assassinated in March 1881, rumours blamed Jews and pogroms (violent attacks) swept southern Russia, starting at Elizavetgrad in April 1881. Police and troops were slow to intervene.
  • The May Laws (Temporary Rules) of 1882 banned Jews from settling in rural areas of the Pale and from owning land. Quotas limited Jewish places in schools and universities from 1887, and Jews were expelled from Moscow in 1891-92.
  • Government officials such as Pobedonostsev, adviser to Alexander III and Nicholas II, supported discrimination and Russification, and the Orthodox Church and the Black Hundreds nationalist groups spread anti-Semitic propaganda.
  • The Kishinev pogrom (6-7 April 1903) killed 49 Jews and injured hundreds, and caused international outrage. More pogroms followed in 1905-06 during the revolution.
  • The Protocols of the Elders of Zion, which claimed a Jewish plot to dominate the world, was first published in Russia in 1903 and was later shown to be a forgery drawing on earlier fiction. It spread widely after 1917.
  • Between 1881 and 1914 about two million Jews emigrated from the Russian Empire, most to the USA, and others to Britain and Palestine. Many Jews also joined socialist movements such as the Bund (1897) and Zionism.

Assessment: culture, faith and exclusion

  • Cultural nationalism gave peoples without a state, such as the Czechs and Finns, a means of asserting their identity, but in Germany, Britain and Russia it also supported imperial and exclusive nationalism.
  • War literature: contemporaries read Brooke's patriotism in 1914-15 and the protest poets only after 1918; later readers see Owen and Sassoon as the war's authentic voices, but historians remind us that many soldiers still saw the war as justified.
  • Kulturkampf: a success in asserting state power over schools and marriage, but a political failure that strengthened Catholic unity and left Bismarck dependent on the Centre.
  • Italy: the Roman Question showed the limits of a liberal state's authority in a Catholic country, and kept Catholics out of national politics for decades.
  • Leo XIII: achievements in ending the Kulturkampf and in social teaching, but limits in the failure of the ralliement and the rejection of unification.
  • Anti-Semitism: the Dreyfus Affair ended in justice for one man and a republican victory, but the prejudice remained strong; in Russia state policy encouraged it, and the pogroms drove mass emigration.

Key terms

Anti-Semitism
Hostility towards or discrimination against Jews, which took political, legal and violent forms in France and Russia between 1871 and 1914.
Nationalism
Loyalty to the nation and belief that it should be strong and self-governing, expressed in music, literature, monuments and religion.
Kulturkampf
Bismarck's conflict with the Catholic Church in Germany, 1871-87, to bring schools, marriage and clergy under state control.
Centre Party
The Catholic party founded in Germany in 1870, which defended the Church and grew stronger during the Kulturkampf.
Roman Question
The dispute between the Italian state and the papacy after Italy seized Rome in 1870, unresolved until the Lateran Treaties of 1929.
Non expedit
The papal instruction forbidding Italian Catholics to vote in or stand for national elections.
Ralliement
Leo XIII's policy of urging French Catholics to accept the Third Republic.
Rerum Novarum
Leo XIII's 1891 encyclical on the condition of the working classes, defending a just wage and trade unions while rejecting socialism.
Pogrom
A violent mob attack on Jewish communities, tolerated or encouraged by officials, as in Russia in 1881-82 and 1903.
Pale of Settlement
The western region of the Russian Empire to which most Jews were confined by law from 1791 to 1917.
