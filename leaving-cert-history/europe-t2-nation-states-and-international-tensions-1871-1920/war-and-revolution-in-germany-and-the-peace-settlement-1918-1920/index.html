The collapse of the Central Powers and the German revolution

By autumn 1918 the German army could not win and the home front was exhausted by blockade and hunger. Defeat, mutiny and revolution brought down the Kaiser.

  • On 29 September 1918, with the Hindenburg Line broken and Bulgaria out of the war, Ludendorff told the Kaiser that Germany needed an armistice at once.
  • To please Wilson, the Kaiser appointed the liberal Prince Max of Baden as Chancellor on 3 October 1918, with Social Democrats and Centre Party members in the government, and Germany asked Wilson for an armistice on the basis of the Fourteen Points.
  • Wilson's replies insisted that Germany must end its military autocracy, which encouraged calls for the Kaiser to go. The constitution was changed in late October to make the Chancellor responsible to the Reichstag.
  • Ludendorff resigned on 26 October. At the end of the month the naval command ordered the fleet to sail for a last battle; sailors at Wilhelmshaven refused, and a mutiny at Kiel spread from 3 November to ports and cities across Germany.
  • Sailors, soldiers and workers formed councils on the Russian model. Kurt Eisner declared a Bavarian republic on 7 November.
  • On 9 November, with a general strike in Berlin, Prince Max announced the Kaiser's abdication without his consent and handed the chancellorship to the Social Democrat Friedrich Ebert. Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed a German republic from the Reichstag window, and Karl Liebknecht proclaimed a socialist republic from the Berlin palace.
  • Wilhelm II fled to the Netherlands on 10 November. On 11 November German representatives, led by the Centre politician Matthias Erzberger, signed the armistice at Compiègne.
  • The armistice required Germany to evacuate occupied territory, hand over weapons and ships, accept Allied occupation west of the Rhine, and gave up the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk. The Allied blockade continued until the peace was signed.

The new republic and the Spartacist rising

  • A Council of People's Representatives of Majority and Independent Social Democrats ruled until elections. On 10 November Ebert made a pact with General Groener of the army: the army would support the government in return for its help in keeping order and respecting officers' authority.
  • Employers and trade unions agreed (the Stinnes-Legien agreement, November 1918) to recognise unions and introduce the eight-hour day.
  • The Communist Party of Germany (KPD) was founded around 30 December 1918 by Luxemburg, Liebknecht and others from the Spartacus League. The left wanted a council republic like Russia's; the SPD leaders wanted an elected parliament.
  • In the Spartacist rising or January uprising (5-12 January 1919), radicals occupied newspaper buildings in Berlin. The defence minister Gustav Noske crushed it with regular troops and Freikorps, volunteer units of ex-soldiers.
  • Luxemburg and Liebknecht were arrested and murdered by Freikorps officers on 15 January 1919. Communist risings in Bavaria, where a Soviet republic existed for a few weeks in April 1919, were also put down.
  • Elections to the National Assembly on 19 January 1919, the first in which women voted, made the SPD the largest party. The Assembly met at Weimar in February 1919 to avoid Berlin's unrest and adopted the Weimar constitution in August.
  • The republic began with the burden of defeat: right-wing nationalists spread the 'stab in the back' myth, that the army had been betrayed by socialists and Jews at home, though the army leadership itself had asked for an armistice.

Wilson's Fourteen Points and the armistice

  • Wilson set out the Fourteen Points in an address to Congress on 8 January 1918, as a statement of American war aims.
  • They called for open covenants of peace openly arrived at, freedom of the seas, free trade, reduction of armaments and an impartial adjustment of colonial claims, with the interests of the peoples concerned counting equally.
  • Points 6 to 8 dealt with the evacuation of Russia and Belgium and the return of Alsace-Lorraine to France. Points 9 to 13 set frontiers for Italy, Austria-Hungary, the Balkans, the Ottoman Empire and an independent Poland with sea access, broadly on national lines.
  • Point 14 called for a general association of nations to guarantee political independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike. This became the League of Nations.
  • Germany's request for an armistice in October 1918 relied on the Fourteen Points. Britain and France accepted them as a basis in November, but Britain reserved its position on freedom of the seas and the Allies insisted on compensation for civilian damage.
  • Many Germans later felt that the Allies had broken the pre-armistice agreement by imposing the harsher Treaty of Versailles.

The Paris Peace Conference, 1919

The conference opened in Paris on 18 January 1919. Over thirty states attended, but the defeated powers were excluded and the key decisions were taken by the Big Four.

  • The Big Four were Woodrow Wilson (USA), David Lloyd George (Britain), Georges Clemenceau (France) and Vittorio Orlando (Italy). The Council of Four took the main decisions.
  • Wilson wanted a just peace based on self-determination and a League of Nations, and he was the first US president to travel to Europe in office.
  • Clemenceau, nicknamed 'the Tiger', wanted security for France, which had been invaded twice in fifty years: a weak Germany, a Rhineland buffer, and heavy reparations.
  • Lloyd George had won the December 1918 election on slogans demanding that Germany pay, but he wanted a treaty that was not so harsh that it would provoke revenge, and he wanted to protect British trade and naval power and avoid French dominance on the continent.
  • Orlando wanted the territory promised to Italy in the secret Treaty of London (1915), and left the conference in April 1919 when Fiume was refused. Italians spoke of a 'mutilated victory'.
  • Japan, an ally, sought a racial equality clause, which was rejected, and gained German rights in Shandong. Russia, now Bolshevik, was not invited.
  • Ireland's Sinn Fein sent Seán T. O'Kelly to Paris to seek a hearing for Irish self-determination, but Wilson and the Allies refused to admit the Irish case.
  • Terms were drawn up by commissions and handed to the Germans in May 1919, with no negotiation, under a threat of resuming war if they refused to sign.

The Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919)

The treaty was signed by the German delegates in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, five years to the day after the assassination at Sarajevo.

  • Article 231, the war guilt clause, stated that Germany and its allies accepted responsibility for all loss and damage caused by the war that was imposed on the Allies by the aggression of Germany and its allies. It was the legal basis for reparations.
  • Territory: Alsace-Lorraine returned to France; Eupen-Malmedy went to Belgium; North Schleswig, after a plebiscite, to Denmark; and most of West Prussia and Posen to Poland, which gave Poland a corridor to the Baltic that split East Prussia from the rest of Germany. Germany lost about 13 per cent of its pre-war European territory and about a tenth of its population.
  • Danzig became a Free City under League of Nations protection, and Memel was placed under Allied control. The Saar was governed by the League for fifteen years, with its coal mines going to France, and a plebiscite was due in 1935.
  • Colonies: all of Germany's colonies were taken and became League of Nations mandates under Britain, France, Japan and others.
  • Military clauses: the army was limited to 100,000 volunteers, conscription ended, the navy limited to a few small ships and no submarines, there was no air force, no tanks or heavy artillery, and the Rhineland was demilitarised with Allied occupation for up to fifteen years.
  • Union of Germany and Austria (Anschluss) was forbidden.
  • Reparations: the sum was not set in the treaty. The Reparations Commission fixed a total of 132 billion gold marks in 1921, in a schedule of payments with three classes of bond; Germany was required to pay in practice about 50 billion.
  • Article 227 called for the trial of the Kaiser, but the Dutch refused to hand him over. The treaty also included the Covenant of the League of Nations.
  • Germans resented the treaty as a 'Diktat' because they were not allowed to negotiate. Scheidemann's government resigned rather than sign, and the Assembly accepted it under the threat of invasion.

The other treaties and the break-up of Austria-Hungary

  • Austria-Hungary broke up in October and November 1918, before the peace. Czechoslovakia declared independence on 28 October, and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (Yugoslavia) was proclaimed on 1 December. Poland, Hungary and Austria formed separate states.
  • Emperor Charles I withdrew from state affairs on 11 November 1918. Austria became a republic on 12 November.
  • The Treaty of St Germain with Austria (10 September 1919) recognised the new states, limited Austria's army to 30,000 and forbade union with Germany. Austria lost South Tyrol to Italy and the Sudeten German areas to Czechoslovakia.
  • The Treaty of Trianon with Hungary (4 June 1920) cut Hungary to about a third of its former territory, giving Transylvania to Romania, Slovakia to Czechoslovakia and Croatia to Yugoslavia. Over three million Hungarians were left outside its borders, causing bitter resentment.
  • The Treaty of Neuilly with Bulgaria (27 November 1919) took Western Thrace, which gave Bulgaria's Aegean outlet to Greece, imposed reparations and limited its army to 20,000.
  • The Treaty of Sèvres with the Ottoman Empire (10 August 1920) divided up the empire, with Armenia and a Kurdish area promised autonomy. Mustafa Kemal's nationalists rejected it and won the war against Greece, and the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) replaced it.
  • The Middle East was placed under League mandates: Britain received Palestine, Iraq and Transjordan; France received Syria and Lebanon.

Self-determination applied and denied

  • Applied: the settlement created or restored Poland, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, and recognised Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, which had broken away from the Russian empire.
  • Plebiscites were held in some contested areas: North and Middle Schleswig (1920), Allenstein and Marienwerder, Upper Silesia (1921, split after a rising and a League decision in 1922) and Carinthia, and Burgenland went to Austria.
  • Denied: Germans and Austrians were forbidden to unite, though Austria's parliament had voted for union with Germany. About three million Germans of the Sudetenland were placed in Czechoslovakia.
  • Denied: South Tyrol, whose population was German-speaking, was given to Italy for strategic reasons; Hungarians in Romania, Slovakia and Yugoslavia were not given a choice.
  • Eastern Europe's new states were multi-ethnic: Poland and Czechoslovakia contained large German, Ukrainian, Slovak and other minorities, protected only by minority-rights treaties with the League.
  • The principle was not applied to colonies, which became mandates, nor to Ireland, Egypt or India, a fact noted by nationalists there.

The League of Nations and the US Senate

  • The League of Nations came into being on 10 January 1920, with its seat in Geneva. Its main organs were the Assembly, the Council and the Secretariat, and it also had the Permanent Court of International Justice and the International Labour Organisation.
  • Its Covenant promised collective security: members agreed to respect each other's independence and to use arbitration, then economic sanctions and possibly force against an aggressor (Article 10 and Article 16).
  • The defeated powers and Soviet Russia were excluded at first. Germany joined in 1926 and the Soviet Union in 1934.
  • Wilson toured the USA in September 1919 to rally support, but suffered a stroke on 2 October 1919.
  • The Republican Senate, led by Henry Cabot Lodge, had objections to Article 10, which could oblige the USA to defend other states. The Senate rejected the treaty in November 1919 and again in March 1920.
  • The USA made a separate peace with Germany in 1921. Without the USA, the League lacked the strongest Western power, and France and Britain had to enforce the treaty alone.

Key personality: Woodrow Wilson

Woodrow Wilson was President of the USA from 1913 to 1921. His Fourteen Points and the League of Nations shaped the Peace Settlement, but he could not win American support for it.

  • Wilson was born on 28 December 1856 in Staunton, Virginia. A historian and political scientist, he became president of Princeton University and Governor of New Jersey (1911-13) before winning the presidency in 1912.
  • He kept the USA neutral from 1914 and was re-elected in 1916 on the slogan that he had kept the country out of the war.
  • After the resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram, he asked Congress on 2 April 1917 to declare war so that the world might be made safe for democracy. Congress did so on 6 April.
  • He announced the Fourteen Points in January 1918 and was the leading figure at Paris, where he insisted that the League Covenant be part of the treaty.
  • He made compromises: he accepted reparations and Article 231, the Saar and Japanese gains in Shandong to win agreement on the League and mandates.
  • He refused to compromise with Republican senators led by Lodge, who wanted reservations to the Covenant, and the treaty failed in the Senate. His stroke in October 1919 left him incapacitated for the rest of his term.
  • Contemporaries: he was welcomed by huge crowds in Europe in December 1918 as a prophet of a new order, though Clemenceau is said to have mocked his idealism. Many Americans came to see him as having entangled the USA in European affairs.
  • Historians: admirers see him as the founder of liberal internationalism, whose ideas shaped the United Nations; critics find him self-righteous, inconsistent in applying self-determination, and politically inept in handling the Senate.

Assessment: the Peace Settlement and post-war Europe

  • Achievement: new democratic states appeared where empires had ruled, and the League provided a forum for settling disputes, such as the Aaland Islands in 1921.
  • Limit: the new states contained large minorities, and revision was demanded by Germany, Hungary, Bulgaria and Italy, who felt cheated.
  • Limit: the treaty was too harsh to be accepted by Germany but not harsh enough to stop it from recovering. Enforcement depended on Britain and France, which disagreed, and the USA and Russia were outside the system.
  • Problems facing Europe after the war: economic dislocation, war debts and inflation, refugees and the influenza epidemic of 1918, the Russian Civil War and fear of Bolshevism, disputed borders such as the Polish-Soviet war of 1920, and the fragile new democracies.
  • Contemporary view: Keynes, a member of the British delegation who resigned, argued in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919) that the reparations were unrealistic and would ruin European recovery; Marshal Foch is credited with saying that it was only an armistice for twenty years.
  • Later view: some historians say Germany's grievance was exaggerated and its terms were milder than Brest-Litovsk; others, such as Margaret MacMillan, see a serious attempt at a difficult task and argue that later failures, not the treaty itself, caused the Second World War.

Key terms

Self-determination
The principle, promoted by Wilson, that peoples should choose their own governments and frontiers, usually on national lines; it was applied unevenly in 1919-20.
War guilt
The principle, in Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, that Germany and its allies were responsible for the war and for all loss and damage, justifying reparations.
Reparations
Payments imposed on Germany to compensate the Allies; the total was set at 132 billion gold marks in 1921.
League of Nations
The international organisation, founded on 10 January 1920 and based in Geneva, to preserve peace through collective security.
Fourteen Points
Wilson's statement of war aims and peace principles to Congress on 8 January 1918, which formed the basis for the armistice.
Diktat
A dictated peace; the German term for the Treaty of Versailles, which Germany was not allowed to negotiate.
Mandate
A territory taken from Germany or the Ottoman Empire and governed by a victorious power on behalf of the League of Nations.
Freikorps
Volunteer units of ex-soldiers who fought left-wing risings in Germany in 1919 and murdered Luxemburg and Liebknecht.
Stab in the back
The nationalist myth that the German army was undefeated in the field and was betrayed by politicians and revolutionaries at home.
