The Second Reich: how it was founded and governed

The German Empire was proclaimed in 1871 after Prussia's wars against Denmark, Austria and France. It combined a strong monarchy with an elected parliament, and it upset the old balance of power in Europe.

  • The German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, while Paris was still under siege in the Franco-Prussian War; King Wilhelm I of Prussia became German Emperor (Kaiser).
  • The Empire was a federation of 25 states: four kingdoms (Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg), smaller duchies and principalities, and three free cities. Prussia held about two thirds of the territory and population.
  • The Kaiser was hereditary King of Prussia, commanded the army, appointed and dismissed the Chancellor and could declare war with Bundesrat consent.
  • The Chancellor was responsible to the Kaiser, not to parliament. Bismarck held the office from 1871 to 1890 and was also Prussian Minister-President for almost all of that time.
  • The Bundesrat (federal council) represented the state governments. Prussia held 17 of its 58 votes in 1871, enough to block any proposed constitutional change.
  • The Reichstag was elected by universal male suffrage (men aged 25 and over) by secret ballot. It could pass or reject laws and the budget, but it could not appoint or dismiss the Chancellor or ministers.
  • The Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871) made France pay an indemnity of 5 billion francs and cede Alsace and part of Lorraine, including Strasbourg and Metz; German troops stayed until it was paid, which France did by 1873.
  • A new Great Power in the centre of Europe, with a growing population and industry, changed the balance of power: France was weakened and embittered, while Britain, Russia and Austria-Hungary had to adjust to a united Germany.

Bismarck's domestic policy, 1871–1890

Bismarck governed by managing parliament and identifying 'enemies of the Reich' in turn. The Kulturkampf against the Catholic Church is covered in the notes on church-state tensions.

  • Until 1878 Bismarck relied on the National Liberals in the Reichstag, who supported free trade and the Reich's unity.
  • After two attempts on the life of Wilhelm I in May and June 1878 (by Hödel and Nobiling), Bismarck used the fear of revolution to pass the Anti-Socialist Law in October 1878. It banned socialist organisations, meetings and newspapers but still let socialists stand for the Reichstag.
  • The law did not stop the SPD: the party's vote kept rising, and by the 1890 election it won about 1.4 million votes and 35 seats.
  • The tariff of 1879 ended free trade by protecting German iron and steel and agricultural products. It split the National Liberals, and Bismarck now relied on the Conservatives and the Catholic Centre Party.
  • To win workers away from socialism, Bismarck introduced state insurance: sickness insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old age and invalidity insurance (1889). Germany was the first major state to create such a scheme.
  • Bismarck's mix of repression and welfare is often described as the 'carrot and stick'. The insurance schemes were limited in coverage and benefits, and they failed to reduce support for the SPD.
  • Bismarck won army funding by repeated confrontations with the Reichstag; in the 1887 election, held after the Reichstag rejected the army bill (the Septennate), the pro-government Kartell of Conservatives and National Liberals won a majority.

Bismarck's aims and the isolation of France

After 1871 Bismarck declared Germany a 'satiated' power that wanted no more territory in Europe. His aim was to keep the new Reich safe from a hostile coalition, above all one that included France.

  • French feeling after the loss of Alsace-Lorraine produced revanche, the desire for revenge and the recovery of the lost provinces; Bismarck assumed that France would never become a reliable friend.
  • His strategy was to keep France without allies by keeping the other powers friendly with Germany or at odds with each other. Contemporaries spoke of his fear of coalitions against Germany.
  • Bismarck wanted the Great Powers to feel that they needed good relations with Berlin more than with each other; he also steered rivalries towards the periphery of Europe, such as the Balkans and Africa.
  • In the War-in-Sight crisis of spring 1875 a German newspaper article suggested that Germany might attack France to stop its rearmament (France had passed a new army law). Britain and Russia warned Germany to hold back; historians disagree on whether Bismarck was really planning war or merely bluffing.
  • The crisis showed Bismarck that Britain and Russia would not allow France to be crushed a second time, and it fed his fear of a hostile coalition.
  • Franco-German tension flared again in 1886–87 under General Boulanger, a popular war minister who stirred up revanchist feeling, and in the Schnaebelé affair (April 1887), when a French frontier official was arrested by the Germans and later released.
  • Bismarck backed French colonial expansion, such as the occupation of Tunisia in 1881, and cooperated briefly with France against Britain over Egypt and Africa in 1884–85, to turn French attention away from Alsace-Lorraine.

The Three Emperors' League and the Eastern Question

  • The Three Emperors' League (October 1873) linked Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia as a conservative understanding to resist revolution and settle disputes among the three monarchies.
  • The League was only an understanding, and it came under strain in the Balkan crisis of 1875–78, when Christian peoples rose against Ottoman rule and Russia fought Turkey in 1877–78.
  • The Treaty of San Stefano (March 1878) created a large Bulgaria and gave Russia great influence in the Balkans. Austria-Hungary and Britain refused to accept it.
  • At the Congress of Berlin (13 June to 13 July 1878), chaired by Bismarck, who called himself an 'honest broker', the powers cut Bulgaria down and divided it into three parts.
  • The Congress recognised the full independence of Serbia, Montenegro and Romania. Austria-Hungary was allowed to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina, and Britain took Cyprus; Russia kept Bessarabia and gains in the Caucasus.
  • Russians felt that Bismarck had sided against them at Berlin and had cheated them of the fruits of their victory over Turkey; Russian newspapers attacked Germany, and the League lapsed.
  • The League was renewed secretly in June 1881 for three years (and again in 1884), with promises of neutrality if one member went to war with a fourth power and agreed Balkan spheres. It broke down in 1887 over the crisis in Bulgaria.

The Dual Alliance (1879) and Triple Alliance (1882)

  • The Dual Alliance of 7 October 1879 was a secret defensive treaty between Germany and Austria-Hungary: each promised to help the other if attacked by Russia, and to stay neutral if attacked by another power, unless Russia joined that power.
  • Bismarck signed it to guard against a Russian attack and to bind Austria-Hungary to Germany; Wilhelm I accepted it reluctantly because it seemed to threaten his friendship with Tsar Alexander II.
  • The Dual Alliance lasted until 1918 and was the permanent core of Germany's alliance system.
  • The Triple Alliance (20 May 1882) added Italy, which was angered by France's occupation of Tunisia in 1881 and wanted support against France and recognition as a Great Power.
  • Under the Triple Alliance, Germany and Austria-Hungary promised help if Italy was attacked by France; Italy would help Germany if attacked by France, and all three would act together if any faced two or more Great Powers.
  • Italy declared that it would not fight Britain, and its relations with Austria-Hungary were poor because of the Italian-speaking territories (Trentino and Trieste) in Habsburg hands.
  • The treaty was renewed regularly until 1914; Italy stayed out of the war in August 1914 and joined the Allies in May 1915.

The Reinsurance Treaty and the last years of the system

  • The crisis in Bulgaria (1885–87), where Russia and Austria-Hungary competed for influence after Prince Alexander of Battenberg was forced to abdicate in 1886, ended the Three Emperors' League in 1887.
  • Bismarck replaced it with the Reinsurance Treaty of 18 June 1887, a secret agreement between Germany and Russia for three years. Each promised neutrality if the other was at war with a third power, except if Germany attacked France or Russia attacked Austria-Hungary.
  • Germany also recognised Russia's interests in Bulgaria and promised diplomatic support over the Straits.
  • Bismarck did not tell Austria-Hungary or Italy of the treaty, and it can be argued that it conflicted in spirit with the Dual Alliance.
  • In November 1887 Bismarck banned the Reichsbank from accepting Russian bonds as security for loans (the Lombardverbot). Russia turned to France for loans, which helped to bring the two powers together.
  • Britain, Italy and Austria-Hungary signed the Mediterranean Agreements in 1887 to hold the status quo in the Mediterranean and Near East, with Bismarck's encouragement. This tied Britain loosely to the Triple Alliance powers.
  • The Reinsurance Treaty was not renewed in 1890 after Bismarck's fall, and Russia moved towards France (see the notes on the structures of European diplomacy).

Colonies and relations with Britain

Bismarck was not an enthusiast for colonies. He changed his mind in 1884, partly because of pressure from merchants and the colonial movement and partly for diplomatic reasons.

  • In 1884–85 Germany claimed South West Africa (Lüderitz), Togoland, the Cameroons, German East Africa and territory in the Pacific including north-east New Guinea.
  • Bismarck preferred protectorates run by chartered companies at their own cost rather than colonies governed by the state.
  • The colonial claims brought friction with Britain, especially over South West Africa and East Africa, and the Gladstone government protested; Bismarck used the disputes to build pressure on Britain and to cooperate with France.
  • Bismarck summoned the Berlin Conference (November 1884 to February 1885) on West Africa; it set rules for the partition of the continent (see the notes on the New Imperialism).
  • Relations with Britain improved after Lord Salisbury took office in 1885. Bismarck tried to keep Britain friendly through the Mediterranean Agreements and hoped that colonial quarrels would not damage the relationship.
  • Anglo-German tension was also dynastic: the future Frederick III's wife, Victoria, was Queen Victoria's daughter, and Bismarck distrusted the liberal influence of the English-born crown princess.
  • Bismarck is said to have told an explorer in 1888 that his map of Africa lay in Europe, meaning that colonies mattered to him mainly for their effect on European diplomacy.

The fall of Bismarck, 1888–1890

  • Wilhelm I died on 9 March 1888 aged 90. His son Frederick III, a liberal who was fatally ill with throat cancer, reigned for 99 days and died on 15 June 1888.
  • The new Kaiser, Wilhelm II, aged 29, wanted to rule personally and disagreed with Bismarck about handling the workers; he wanted to ease the anti-socialist laws, which Bismarck wanted to make permanent.
  • The Anti-Socialist Law was due to expire in September 1890. After the Reichstag election of February 1890, in which the Kartell parties lost seats and the SPD vote grew, Bismarck mooted a coup d'etat to change the constitution.
  • Wilhelm II also objected to Bismarck's attempt to limit the ministers' access to the Kaiser through the Cabinet Order of 1852, and to the Chancellor's talks with the Catholic Centre leader Windthorst.
  • Bismarck offered his resignation on 18 March 1890, after the Kaiser made it clear that he would be dismissed otherwise. It was accepted two days later, and General Leo von Caprivi became Chancellor.
  • The British magazine Punch published the cartoon 'Dropping the Pilot' by John Tenniel on 29 March 1890, showing the Kaiser watching the old pilot leave the ship.
  • In the following months the Reinsurance Treaty was not renewed and Germany signed the Heligoland-Zanzibar treaty with Britain (July 1890).

Key personality: Otto von Bismarck (Chancellor, 1871–1890)

Bismarck made the unification of Germany possible in the 1860s, and then governed it as Chancellor until 1890. This block covers the Chancellorship; his earlier career belongs to the notes on unification.

  • Bismarck (1815–98) came from a Junker landowning family at Schönhausen in Prussia, and became Minister-President of Prussia in 1862.
  • He was made Prince in 1871 and served as Chancellor under Wilhelm I, Frederick III and Wilhelm II. He controlled foreign policy personally, and most of the major policy decisions of the Empire were his.
  • He saw himself as a conservative protecting the monarchy and Prussian power; he used nationalism, tariffs and social insurance to keep the new Reich stable.
  • Contemporaries admired the 'Iron Chancellor' who had made Germany great: nationalists and conservatives adored him, while Catholics, Liberals and socialists suffered under his measures and called them persecution.
  • Foreign observers respected him as a master diplomat. Punch's cartoon of 1890 and the many Bismarck monuments erected after his death show how large he loomed.
  • After his dismissal he used the press to criticise Wilhelm II's governments, and published his memoirs, Thoughts and Recollections. He died on 30 July 1898 at Friedrichsruh.
  • Historians are divided. A.J.P. Taylor and others praised his skill as a diplomat who kept the peace; Lothar Gall called him a 'white revolutionary' who changed Europe from above.
  • Hans-Ulrich Wehler argued that Bismarck's rule was a kind of Bonapartist dictatorship that kept the old elites in power by 'negative integration', uniting the Reich against domestic 'enemies' such as Catholics and socialists; critics say it left Germany with a weak parliamentary tradition.

Assessment: Bismarck's foreign and domestic record

  • Foreign success: Germany fought no war after 1871 and avoided a two-front war while Bismarck was in office.
  • Foreign limit: the system was complicated and depended on Bismarck personally; the Reinsurance Treaty and the Dual Alliance pulled in opposite directions, and a successor could not keep all the balls in the air.
  • Foreign limit: the Dual Alliance committed Germany to Austria-Hungary in a Balkan region where Habsburg and Russian interests clashed, and the treatment of Russia in 1878 and 1887 helped to push it towards France.
  • Foreign success: the Congress of Berlin avoided a European war in 1878, and Bismarck was able to turn colonial rivalry to Germany's advantage.
  • Domestic success: he created a unified state, a national market, a common currency (the mark, 1873), a central bank (the Reichsbank) and the first national state insurance system in Europe.
  • Domestic failure: the Kulturkampf and the Anti-Socialist Law did not break the Catholic Church or the SPD; both emerged stronger, and the Reichstag remained weak.
  • Contemporary view: Germans in 1890 saw a statesman who had made Germany one of the strongest states in Europe.
  • Later view: historians stress the unfinished nature of his domestic settlement, which left his successors with an unreformed constitution and a divided society.

Key terms

Balance of power
A situation in which no one state can dominate Europe because rival states ally or act together to stop it; Bismarck tried to manage it in Germany's favour after 1871.
Second Reich
The German Empire of 1871–1918, a federation of 25 states led by Prussia, with a hereditary Kaiser, a Chancellor responsible to him, a Bundesrat and a Reichstag.
Reichstag
The German parliament, elected by universal male suffrage, which could pass laws and the budget but could not appoint or dismiss the Chancellor.
Bundesrat
The federal council of the German Empire, made up of representatives of the state governments, in which Prussia held 17 of the 58 votes in 1871.
Revanche
The French desire for revenge against Germany and the recovery of Alsace-Lorraine, lost in 1871.
Three Emperors' League
The conservative understanding between the rulers of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia, begun in 1873 and renewed in 1881, which broke down over the Balkans.
Dual Alliance
The secret defensive alliance of 1879 between Germany and Austria-Hungary, aimed at the risk of a Russian attack.
Triple Alliance
The defensive alliance of 1882 linking Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, aimed chiefly at France.
Reinsurance Treaty
The secret 1887 treaty of neutrality between Germany and Russia, kept from Austria-Hungary and not renewed in 1890.
Anti-Socialist Law
The law of 1878 that banned socialist societies, meetings and newspapers in Germany until 1890 but left socialists free to stand for the Reichstag.
Honest broker
Bismarck's description of his role as chairman of the Congress of Berlin in 1878, claiming to act as a neutral mediator.
