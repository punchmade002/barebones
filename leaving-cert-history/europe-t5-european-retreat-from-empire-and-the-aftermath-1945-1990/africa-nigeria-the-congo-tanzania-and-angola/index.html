Nigeria: colonial amalgamation and federal independence, 1914–1960

Britain joined separate northern and southern territories into one colony in 1914, and independence in 1960 was given to a federation of three regions.

  • Lord Lugard amalgamated Northern and Southern Nigeria into one colony in 1914, and the colonial government ruled much of the north through existing emirs under the system of indirect rule.
  • Nigeria's three main groups were the Hausa-Fulani in the north, the Yoruba in the west and the Igbo in the east; the north was predominantly Muslim, and the south had large Christian populations.
  • The 1954 constitution created a federation of three regions, Northern, Western and Eastern, with Lagos as a federal territory.
  • Nigeria became independent on 1 October 1960 as a member of the British Commonwealth, with Abubakar Tafawa Balewa as prime minister; the Queen remained head of state, represented by a governor-general, Nnamdi Azikiwe from November 1960.
  • The regions had their own parliaments and revenue, and the regional parties drew support on ethnic lines, which made federal politics tense.
  • Nigeria became a republic on 1 October 1963 with Azikiwe as president, and a fourth region, the Mid-West, was created in 1963.
  • In the 1959 federal elections the Northern People's Congress won the most seats and formed a coalition with Azikiwe's National Council of Nigeria and the Cameroons, and its leader Abubakar Tafawa Balewa became federal prime minister.
  • Nigeria's oil came from Oloibiri, discovered by Shell-BP in 1956, and exports began in 1958; the oil lay in the Eastern Region, which became a source of dispute in 1967.

Nigeria: coups, massacres and the Biafran War, 1966–1970

  • On 15 January 1966 a group of army officers led by Major Chukwuma Nzeogwu killed the federal prime minister and several regional leaders, and the coup was seen as dominated by Igbo officers.
  • General Johnson Aguiyi-Ironsi took power, but a counter-coup in July 1966 brought Lieutenant-Colonel Yakubu Gowon to power.
  • Anti-Igbo massacres in the north in 1966 killed many thousands of Igbo people, and large numbers fled to the east.
  • The eastern region declared itself the Republic of Biafra on 30 May 1967 under Colonel Odumegwu Ojukwu, and the federal government responded with a blockade.
  • The Biafran War, from July 1967 to 15 January 1970, caused widespread famine in Biafra; estimates of the total death toll range from several hundred thousand to more than two million, most from starvation and disease.
  • Biafra was recognised by only Tanzania, Gabon, the Ivory Coast, Zambia and Haiti, while Britain and the Soviet Union supplied arms to the federal government; France and Portugal gave covert support to Biafra.
  • Gowon created twelve states on 27 May 1967, three days before Biafra's declaration, which broke up the Eastern Region and cut the Igbo off from most of the oil; an earlier attempt to settle the crisis, the Aburi meeting in Ghana in January 1967, had failed.
  • Gowon announced a policy of reconciliation, rehabilitation and reconstruction at the end of the war on 15 January 1970 and declared that there was no victor and no vanquished.
  • Many outside Nigeria were moved by images of starving Biafran children; the relief flights of Joint Church Aid, Irish missionaries among them, kept supplies reaching the enclave.

The Belgian Congo, 1908–1960

Belgium ran the Congo as a colony for profit, and the country had very few trained Congolese when independence came suddenly in 1960.

  • King Leopold II ruled the Congo Free State from 1885 as his personal possession, and forced rubber collection caused widespread abuses, exposed by Roger Casement and E. D. Morel, that led to international protest.
  • The Belgian government took over the colony in 1908, and it was ruled by a paternalistic administration that gave little political role to Congolese.
  • The colony's wealth lay in copper, cobalt, tin and diamonds, much of it extracted in Katanga by the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga, a Belgian company.
  • By 1960 only a handful of Congolese had a university degree, and the Force Publique, the colonial army, had no Congolese officers, with Belgians commanding all the ranks above sergeant.
  • Congolese political parties emerged in the late 1950s, including Patrice Lumumba's National Congolese Movement (MNC), which won the most seats in the 1960 elections.
  • Riots in Léopoldville in January 1959 shocked Belgian opinion, and King Baudouin soon promised independence at an unspecified date.
  • The Round Table in Brussels in January-February 1960 fixed independence for 30 June 1960, only about five months later.

Congolese independence and the Lumumba crisis, 1960–1961

  • Joseph Kasa-Vubu became head of state and Patrice Lumumba prime minister at independence on 30 June 1960.
  • At the independence ceremony King Baudouin praised Belgium's colonial work; Lumumba replied with a speech attacking the humiliations of colonial rule, which offended Belgian opinion.
  • Soldiers of the Force Publique mutinied against their Belgian officers on 5–6 July 1960, and attacks on Europeans led Belgium to send troops back to the Congo.
  • Katanga seceded on 11 July 1960 with Belgian backing, and South Kasai declared its own state in August 1960.
  • The UN Security Council authorised the UN operation in the Congo (ONUC) in July 1960; at its height it had about 20,000 troops, and it was the largest UN operation of its time.
  • Kasa-Vubu dismissed Lumumba on 5 September 1960 and Lumumba tried to dismiss Kasa-Vubu, which gave Mobutu his pretext to intervene.
  • Colonel Joseph Mobutu, army chief of staff, took power on 14 September 1960; Lumumba, who had appealed to the Soviet Union for help, was confined to his residence under UN guard.
  • Lumumba left his residence in late November 1960, was captured in December, and was flown to Katanga and murdered there on 17 January 1961 in the presence of Belgian officers; Belgium's parliament concluded in 2002 that it bore a moral responsibility.

Mobutu's Zaire, 1965–1990

  • Mobutu seized power in a second coup on 24 November 1965, with Kasa-Vubu deposed and the army in charge of the state.
  • The country was renamed Zaire in 1971, and Mobutu's regime promoted authenticité, which banned Christian first names and European dress for men in favour of the abacost.
  • In 1973 Zairianisation transferred foreign-owned plantations and businesses to Zairian owners, mostly Mobutu's associates, and the economy collapsed as the copper price fell in 1974-75.
  • The one-party state, the Popular Movement of the Revolution (MPR), was the only legal party, and political opposition was suppressed.
  • Mobutu was supported by the United States, Belgium and France as a barrier to communism in central Africa, and their military help defeated the Katangan exile invasions of Shaba in 1977 and 1978.
  • Mobutu announced the end of the one-party state in April 1990 under pressure from protests and from the end of Cold War support, though he stayed in power until 1997.

Tanganyika and Tanzania: peaceful independence under Nyerere, 1961–1964

Tanganyika moved to independence without armed conflict under Julius Nyerere and then united with Zanzibar.

  • Tanganyika had been German East Africa until 1918, then a British mandate under the League of Nations and a UN trust territory after 1946.
  • Julius Nyerere founded the Tanganyika African National Union (TANU) in 1954 and led it to a majority in the 1960 elections.
  • Tanganyika became independent on 9 December 1961 with Nyerere as prime minister, and it became a republic with Nyerere as president in December 1962.
  • Zanzibar became independent in December 1963, but a revolution in January 1964 overthrew its Arab-led government, and the new regime was dominated by the Afro-Shirazi Party.
  • Tanganyika and Zanzibar united on 26 April 1964 to form the United Republic, renamed Tanzania in October 1964; Zanzibar kept its own government and president, Abeid Karume of the Afro-Shirazi Party.
  • Tanzania was a multi-ethnic state with more than a hundred ethnic groups and no dominant majority, and Nyerere made Swahili the national language and avoided ethnic parties.
  • In January 1964 the Tanganyika Rifles mutinied against British officers; Nyerere asked Britain for help and Royal Marines put down the mutiny, after which the army was rebuilt.
  • Nyerere broke off diplomatic relations with Britain in December 1965 over its failure to act against the white minority regime in Southern Rhodesia, though Tanzania stayed in the Commonwealth.

Angola: Portuguese rule and the war of independence, 1961–1975

  • Portugal under the Estado Novo regime of António de Oliveira Salazar regarded Angola as an overseas province rather than a colony, and it refused to grant independence.
  • Armed attacks in Luanda on 4 February 1961 began the Angolan war of independence, followed by a rising in the north on 15 March 1961 in which many Europeans and Africans were killed and Portuguese reprisals were severe; the war lasted until 1974.
  • Three nationalist movements fought the Portuguese: the MPLA, led by Agostinho Neto, founded in 1956 and strongest among the Mbundu and in Luanda; the FNLA, led by Holden Roberto and based among the Bakongo in the north; and UNITA, founded in 1966, led by Jonas Savimbi and based among the Ovimbundu.
  • The Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 in Lisbon overthrew the Portuguese regime, and the new government agreed to grant independence to Angola.
  • The Alvor Agreement of January 1975 set up a transitional government in which the three movements shared power, but it broke down into fighting.
  • Angola became independent on 11 November 1975, with the MPLA under Neto holding Luanda; the civil war that followed between the MPLA and UNITA and the FNLA lasted until 2002, with Cuban troops and Soviet aid for the MPLA, and South African and American support for its opponents.
  • Most of the roughly 300,000 Portuguese settlers left Angola in 1975, taking skills and capital with them, and oil from Cabinda and diamonds became the main sources of revenue.

Relations with the former colonial powers

  • Nigeria kept the Queen as head of state until becoming a republic in 1963, and stayed in the Commonwealth, with British companies such as Shell-BP dominating the oil industry.
  • Belgium retained large economic interests in the Congo through companies such as the Union Minière, and Mobutu nationalised the Union Minière's Katanga assets in 1966-67 before reaching a settlement with it in 1969.
  • Portugal's refusal to decolonise until 1974 meant that Angola faced a long war, and Portugal's exit left Angola with few trained administrators.
  • Tanzania remained in the Commonwealth and received British aid, but Nyerere pursued non-alignment and criticised Western economic influence; Britain also supplied arms to the federal side in Nigeria's war.
  • France kept influence in its former West and Equatorial African colonies through co-operation accords, the franc zone and military agreements, an arrangement later called Françafrique.

Tribalism and ethnic politics after independence

  • Colonial rulers defined and fixed ethnic identities through census categories, languages and indirect rule, and the term tribalism was used by colonial officials to describe ethnic loyalty.
  • In Nigeria, the three main parties each drew their support from one region and one ethnic group, and the army's officer corps divided on regional lines, which contributed to the coups of 1966 and the massacres that followed.
  • In the Congo, the leading politicians drew their support from particular regions and ethnic communities, such as Lumumba's base in the east, Kasa-Vubu's Kongo base in the west and the Lunda of Katanga.
  • Katanga's secession was partly based on the claim that the Katangan people had a separate identity from the rest of the Congo, a claim that the national government rejected.
  • Tanzania's single language, Swahili, and the policy of building a national identity were among the factors that reduced ethnic conflict after independence.

Assessment: the problems and opportunities of independence

  • Opportunity: independence gave Africans control of government, and Nyerere's Tanzania pursued national unity and African development without a civil war.
  • Problem: the Belgian Congo's rapid independence left the new state with almost no trained administrators or officers, and the army's mutiny and the secession of Katanga followed within weeks.
  • Opportunity: Nigeria's oil revenue, which grew rapidly after the 1973 oil price rise, gave the federal government resources for reconstruction after the Biafran War.
  • Problem: the Biafran War and the 1966 coups showed how the federal structure could fail to hold ethnic groups together.
  • Contemporary view: many African leaders saw independence as the end of colonial domination, while the former colonial powers often saw it as the start of a new relationship of trade and aid.
  • Later view: historians stress that colonial borders, ethnic policies and the Cold War constrained the new states, but they also stress African agency in shaping the outcomes.
  • Problem: Angola's long war of independence ended in civil war because the three movements had different ethnic bases and different foreign backers.

Key terms

Colonial rule
The control of a territory by an external power that governed it for the benefit of the imperial state.
British Commonwealth
The association of Britain and its former colonies, which many African states joined on independence while keeping their own republics.
Tribalism
A term used by colonial officials for loyalty to ethnic groups, which colonial policies often defined, fixed and exploited.
Force Publique
The colonial army of the Belgian Congo, whose Congolese soldiers mutinied against their Belgian officers in July 1960.
ONUC
The UN operation in the Congo, set up in July 1960 to restore order, prevent foreign intervention and oppose secession.
Carnation Revolution
The coup of 25 April 1974 in Lisbon that overthrew the Portuguese dictatorship and led to the independence of Angola and Mozambique.
Secession
The withdrawal of a region from a state to form a separate state, as in Katanga in 1960 and Biafra in 1967.
Indirect rule
The British system of governing through existing local rulers and institutions, associated with Lord Lugard in Nigeria.
Federation
A state in which power is divided between a central government and regional governments, as in Nigeria's three regions from 1954.
Estado Novo
The authoritarian Portuguese regime founded by Salazar, which treated Angola as an overseas province and fought colonial wars until 1974.
MPLA
The People's Movement for the Liberation of Angola, founded in 1956, led by Agostinho Neto, which held power from independence in 1975.
Zairianisation
Mobutu's policy of 1973 that transferred foreign-owned businesses and plantations to Zairian owners.
