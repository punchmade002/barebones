Commonwealth migration to Britain, 1948–1962

Britain's post-war labour shortage and the Commonwealth citizenship laws brought large numbers of migrants from the Caribbean and South Asia.

  • The British Nationality Act of 1948 gave citizens of Commonwealth countries the right to enter and settle in Britain as British subjects.
  • The SS Empire Windrush docked at Tilbury on 22 June 1948 with about 1,000 passengers, most of them from Jamaica and other Caribbean islands, and it became a symbol of this migration.
  • Post-war labour shortages led Britain to recruit workers for the National Health Service, London Transport and the postal service, many of them from the Caribbean.
  • Migration from India and Pakistan grew in the 1950s, with many migrants from Punjab and Gujarat settling in Birmingham, Leicester, Bradford and London.
  • Migration peaked in 1961–62 as migrants rushed to enter before new restrictions took effect; the migrant population was concentrated in a few cities, especially London, the West Midlands and Yorkshire.
  • The Commonwealth citizenship status of most migrants meant they had the right to vote and to live in Britain, but they faced discrimination in housing and jobs.
  • Caribbean migrants met a colour bar in lodgings; in Notting Hill the landlord Peter Rachman became notorious for overcrowded, exploitative lettings to newcomers.

Racism and the first race riots, 1958–1959

  • In August and September 1958 white youths attacked West Indian residents in the Notting Hill and Nottingham areas, in the worst racial violence in post-war Britain up to that time.
  • The disturbances were caused by overcrowded housing, competition for jobs and housing, and the activities of far-right groups in the area, such as Oswald Mosley's Union Movement and the White Defence League.
  • The Trinidadian journalist Claudia Jones organised an indoor carnival in London in January 1959, which became a precursor of the Notting Hill Carnival.
  • Kelso Cochrane, an Antiguan-born carpenter, was murdered in a racist attack in Notting Hill in May 1959, and his death increased pressure for a legal response.

Restrictive laws and the rise of anti-immigrant politics, 1962–1971

  • The Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962 introduced employment vouchers, which restricted the entry of Commonwealth citizens without jobs in Britain.
  • In 1968 a new Commonwealth Immigrants Act was rushed through Parliament in a few days to limit the entry of Kenyan Asians holding British passports, who faced discrimination and expulsion in Kenya.
  • Enoch Powell, a Conservative MP, warned in a speech to a Conservative meeting in Birmingham on 20 April 1968, known as the 'rivers of blood' speech, that continued immigration would lead to violence, and Edward Heath dismissed him from the Shadow Cabinet; London dockers marched in his support.
  • The Immigration Act of 1971 introduced the concept of patriality, which gave the right of abode to those with a parent or grandparent born in Britain.
  • The National Front, founded in 1967, campaigned for an end to immigration, stood candidates in the general elections of 1974 and 1979 and never won a parliamentary seat; the Battle of Lewisham (August 1977) and the death of the teacher Blair Peach at an anti-NF protest in Southall in April 1979 marked its confrontations with opponents.
  • President Idi Amin gave Asians in Uganda 90 days to leave in August 1972; about 27,000 of them, many holding British passports, settled in Britain, helped by a government resettlement board.
  • The Anti-Nazi League and Rock Against Racism, founded in 1976-77, organised counter-campaigns against the National Front.

Anti-discrimination law: the Race Relations Acts, 1965–1976

  • The Race Relations Act of 1965 made discrimination on grounds of colour, race or ethnic origin illegal in public places, and it created a Race Relations Board.
  • The Race Relations Act of 1968 extended the ban on discrimination to housing and employment, and it created the Community Relations Commission.
  • The Race Relations Act of 1976 extended the law to indirect discrimination and created the Commission for Racial Equality.
  • The laws were enforced through complaints and conciliation, and critics argued that their effectiveness was limited by the difficulty of proving discrimination in practice.
  • Home Secretary Roy Jenkins said in 1966 that integration should not mean assimilation but equal opportunity together with cultural diversity, which set the direction of British policy.

Discrimination, policing and the 1981 riots

  • Police stop-and-search powers under the ‘sus’ laws (the Vagrancy Act of 1824) were used heavily against young black men, which many black residents saw as harassment.
  • The Brixton riots of 10-12 April 1981 followed Operation Swamp 81, in which police stopped and searched nearly a thousand people, mostly young black men, in a week; the riots caused widespread damage and injured hundreds.
  • Riots broke out in Toxteth in Liverpool and Moss Side in Manchester in July 1981, as well as in other cities; police used CS gas on the British mainland for the first time in Toxteth.
  • The Scarman Report of November 1981 identified racial disadvantage, unemployment and poor relations between police and the community as causes of the riots; it found no institutional racism in the police but accepted that individual officers had acted improperly.
  • The Scarman Report recommended more accountable policing and community consultation, and it led to the Police and Criminal Evidence Act of 1984; the 'sus' law had already been repealed in 1981.

Multi-racial and multicultural Britain

By the 1980s Britain had large Caribbean, Indian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi communities whose culture changed British life in many areas.

  • Caribbean communities shaped British music, including reggae, ska and calypso, and Caribbean food and festivals became part of urban life.
  • Notting Hill Carnival began as a street festival in 1966 and became one of the largest street festivals in Europe.
  • Muslims from South Asia built mosques in the 1960s and 1970s, and Sikh and Hindu communities built gurdwaras and temples in Birmingham, Leicester and London.
  • Britain's policy of multiculturalism, supported by local councils and the Race Relations Acts, encouraged the recognition of distinct cultures within a common framework of laws.
  • The Rushdie affair of 1988-89 showed the tensions between religious identity and freedom of expression: copies of The Satanic Verses were burned in Bradford in January 1989, and Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa against Salman Rushdie in February 1989.
  • Black and Asian candidates entered Parliament: Diane Abbott, Paul Boateng, Bernie Grant and Keith Vaz were elected in 1987, and the Rampton Report (1981) and the Swann Report (1985) argued for schools to recognise the needs of ethnic minority children.

France: migration from North Africa and West Africa, 1945–1974

  • Algerians held French nationality until Algerian independence in 1962, and they could move freely between Algeria and France, so Algerian labour migration to mainland France was not legally immigration before that date.
  • Post-war reconstruction created labour shortages, and migrants from Portugal, Spain, Italy, Morocco, Tunisia and West Africa filled jobs in factories, mines and construction.
  • Many migrants lived in bidonvilles, makeshift shanty settlements on the edges of French cities, such as Nanterre near Paris, where living conditions were poor.
  • Algerian migration continued after 1962, and it brought the Algerian war's legacy into French politics; the harkis who came to France faced hostility and poor housing.
  • In July 1974 the French government suspended labour immigration, and in 1976 it allowed family reunification for migrants who had been living in France.
  • Migrants from North and West Africa formed the second generation, the ‘beurs’, who grew up in the banlieues and by the 1980s were a focus of French debate about integration.
  • The Franco-Algerian accord of 27 December 1968 gave Algerian nationals preferential rights to enter and work in France.
  • The harkis and their families were housed in camps known as hameaux de forestage in France, and some camps were still in use in the mid-1970s.
  • The French government set up the National Immigration Office in 1945, and Sonacotra, founded in 1956, built hostels for Algerian workers.
  • On 17 October 1961 Paris police under Maurice Papon attacked a demonstration by Algerians against a curfew, killing dozens or more; the number remains disputed.
  • After 1977 the government offered payments to migrants who agreed to go home, with little effect.

The French model: assimilation and laïcité

  • The French model of integration was based on universal citizenship: all citizens were equal before the law, and the state did not recognise ethnic or religious communities as such.
  • Assimilation meant that migrants were expected to adopt the French language, values and civic practices, and to keep their ethnic identities in private.
  • The law on the separation of church and state of 1905 established laïcité, a principle of state secularism that shaped how public religious expression was regulated.
  • France did not collect statistics on ethnic origin, which meant that discrimination was harder to measure and debate than in Britain.
  • The Pleven law of 1972 was France's main anti-racist legislation, and it made incitement to racial hatred a criminal offence.

Comparison: Britain and France

  • Similarity: both states restricted immigration in the 1970s, both had far-right parties that gained support by the late 1970s and 1980s, and both had racial violence in urban areas.
  • Difference: most migrants to Britain were Commonwealth citizens with a legal right of entry, while migrants to France came from southern Europe as well as North and West Africa, and North African migrants had a complex legal status.
  • Difference: Britain developed a policy of multiculturalism that recognised ethnic groups, while France used the assimilation model that recognised individuals as citizens.
  • Difference: the colonial histories differed, since Britain's migrants came largely from its former colonies in the Caribbean and South Asia, and France's came from its former North African and West African colonies.

Assessment: integration and exclusion in Britain and France

  • Achievement: Britain's anti-discrimination laws gave migrants a legal basis for challenging discrimination in jobs, housing and public services.
  • Limit: the laws did not end disadvantage, and the 1981 riots showed that unemployment and police practices remained sources of conflict.
  • Achievement: France's universal citizenship gave second-generation migrants a legal claim to French nationality, and many took part fully in French public life.
  • Limit: the assimilation model made it difficult to recognise ethnic discrimination, and the headscarf question and the banlieues showed the tensions it created.
  • Contemporary view: many politicians in both countries saw migration mainly as an economic and security problem, while migrant communities often saw discrimination as the main issue.
  • Later view: scholars contrast Britain's race-relations model, which counted ethnic groups and tackled discrimination through the law, with France's republican model, which counted no one by origin; each has been criticised for failing to end disadvantage.

Key terms

Racism
The belief that people can be ranked by race and treated differently on that basis, expressed in discrimination, violence and exclusion.
Cultural diversity
The presence of many cultures, languages, religions and traditions within one society, which post-war migration brought to Britain and France.
Identity
A person's sense of who they are, shaped by ethnicity, nationality, religion, language and place, and often contested by migrants and their children.
Assimilation
The expectation that migrants and minorities adopt the language, values and civic culture of the majority society.
Multiculturalism
The policy of recognising and supporting distinct cultures and communities within one state, as developed in Britain from the 1960s.
Patriality
The Immigration Act of 1971 concept that gave the right of abode in Britain to people with a parent or grandparent born there.
Bidonville
A makeshift shanty settlement on the edge of a French city, where many migrant workers lived in poor conditions after the Second World War.
Laïcité
The French principle of state secularism, established by the 1905 law separating church and state, which keeps public institutions free of religious influence.
Family reunification
The right of migrant workers to bring spouses and children to join them, as allowed in France from 1976 after labour migration was halted.
Banlieue
The suburban areas on the edge of French cities, with large housing estates where many migrant families lived.
Commonwealth Immigrants Acts
The British laws of 1962 and 1968 that restricted the right of Commonwealth citizens to enter and settle in Britain.
