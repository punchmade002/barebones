Colonial economies and their legacy

Colonial rule built economies around exporting raw materials to Europe, and these structures outlived independence.

  • Colonial economies were organised around cash crops and minerals: cocoa in the Gold Coast, groundnuts in Senegal, cotton in Uganda and copper in Katanga.
  • Railways and ports were built to carry exports to the coast, so most African transport networks linked interior mines and farms to ports rather than to each other.
  • Colonial powers controlled trade through their own companies and currencies, and African states inherited trading patterns that tied them to a few markets.
  • Development economists such as Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer argued from 1950 that the terms of trade, the ratio of export prices to import prices, tend to move against primary producers, so African countries have to export more to buy the same manufactured goods; commodity prices fell sharply in the 1980s.
  • Dependence on one or two commodities made African economies vulnerable to price falls, as with copper prices in the 1970s and cocoa and coffee prices in the 1980s.
  • Colonial governments in the Belgian Congo and French West Africa required peasants to grow set quantities of cotton and other cash crops, which were bought at fixed low prices.
  • Multinational companies such as Unilever's United Africa Company, Shell-BP and the Union Minière kept control of trade, oil and mining after independence, which is why the topic is linked to the idea of a transnational economy.

The Yaoundé and Lomé Conventions

The European Economic Community set up trade and aid agreements with former African colonies, which later became the Lomé Conventions.

  • The Treaty of Rome of 1957 associated the overseas territories of the EEC members, mostly in Africa, with the Community.
  • The Yaoundé Convention of 1963 (with a second in 1969) between the EEC and 18 associated African states gave their exports duty-free access to European markets in return for limited reciprocal opening of their own markets.
  • Lomé I was signed on 28 February 1975 in Lomé, Togo, between the EEC and 46 African, Caribbean and Pacific (ACP) states, after Britain's entry in 1973 brought in Commonwealth countries; the ACP group as an organisation was formed later that year.
  • Lomé I gave ACP exports largely duty-free access to the EEC without requiring reciprocal concessions, which was a new departure in trade relations.
  • STABEX was a fund set up under Lomé I to compensate ACP countries when earnings from specified agricultural exports fell below a set level.
  • Later Conventions were signed in 1979 (Lomé II), 1984 (Lomé III) and 1989 (Lomé IV), and they increasingly added conditions on human rights and governance.
  • Lomé I also included a Sugar Protocol, which guaranteed quotas for ACP sugar at European prices, and Lomé II (1979) added SYSMIN, a fund to help mineral exporters; the Conventions were replaced by the Cotonou Agreement in 2000.

Debt, the IMF and the World Bank, 1970s–1980s

  • The IMF and the World Bank were created at the Bretton Woods conference of 1944; the IMF lent to countries with balance-of-payments problems and the World Bank funded development projects.
  • Oil price rises in 1973 and 1979 raised import costs for oil-importing African states, and many governments borrowed heavily from banks and international lenders in the 1970s.
  • When interest rates rose in the early 1980s and commodity prices fell, African countries struggled to repay, and Mexico's default in 1982 marked the start of the wider debt crisis.
  • From the early 1980s the IMF and World Bank made new loans conditional on structural adjustment programmes: devaluation, cuts in public spending, removal of subsidies and the sale of state enterprises.
  • The World Bank's Berg Report of 1981 blamed African governments' own policies, such as overvalued currencies and state marketing boards, while critics argued that structural adjustment reduced spending on health and education; UNICEF's 1987 report 'Adjustment with a Human Face' made the case for protecting the poor.
  • Ghana under Jerry Rawlings adopted an IMF-backed recovery programme in 1983, which was widely cited as an early success even though it brought hardship.

Trade, aid and dependence

  • Aid came in two forms: bilateral aid given government to government by a donor state, and multilateral aid channelled through bodies such as the World Bank and the UN.
  • Tied aid required recipients to spend the money on goods and services from the donor country, which increased its cost and reduced its value.
  • Many former colonies received aid from their former rulers, so aid often reinforced existing trade and political links rather than diversifying them.
  • Aid was criticised for supporting corrupt or authoritarian governments, such as Mobutu's Zaire, and for creating dependence on donors for budgets and food.
  • Some African governments tried to reduce dependence by promoting local industry and regional trade, but many national markets remained small and fragmented.
  • The United Nations set a target in 1970 that rich countries give 0.7 per cent of national income as aid; few donors met it.
  • Cold War rivalry shaped aid: China built the TAZARA railway linking Zambia to the Tanzanian port of Dar es Salaam between 1970 and 1975, and the Soviet Union and the United States each aided their allies.

Famine: Biafra, the Sahel and Ethiopia

Famines in the post-colonial period were caused by war, drought and state policy, and they drew international relief efforts.

  • During the Biafran War, blockade and fighting caused a famine in Biafra in 1968–70, and the images of starving children drew wide attention in Europe and North America.
  • Relief flights into Biafra ran at night from São Tomé to the Uli airstrip, and they were limited by the federal government's control of the airspace; in June 1969 a Red Cross aircraft was shot down.
  • A drought in the Sahel from the late 1960s to the mid-1970s caused a famine across Mali, Niger, Chad, Mauritania, Upper Volta (now Burkina Faso) and Senegal, with large loss of livestock; the Wollo famine of 1972-74 helped bring down Ethiopia's Emperor Haile Selassie in 1974.
  • The Ethiopian famine of 1983-85 killed hundreds of thousands, with estimates from about 300,000 to one million; drought combined with the Derg regime's policies and the wars in Eritrea and Tigray, and a BBC report by Michael Buerk in October 1984 brought it to Western attention.
  • The British singer Bob Geldof organised the Band Aid charity single 'Do They Know It's Christmas?', released in December 1984, and the Live Aid concerts in London and Philadelphia on 13 July 1985 raised about 150 million pounds for famine relief.
  • Famine relief was also criticised for distorting local markets and for prolonging wars when food aid went to armed groups.
  • The Ethiopian government's resettlement programme of 1984-86 moved about 600,000 people from the drought-hit north, and tens of thousands died during the moves.

NGOs and Irish aid

  • Non-governmental organisations (NGOs) are non-state charities and campaigning bodies that deliver aid and work on development projects.
  • Oxfam was founded in Oxford in 1942 as the Oxford Committee for Famine Relief, and it later expanded into development work across Africa and Asia.
  • Concern was founded in Dublin in 1968 by John and Kay O'Loughlin Kennedy in response to the Biafran famine, and it became one of Ireland's main aid agencies.
  • Trócaire was founded in 1973 by the Catholic bishops of Ireland as the Irish Catholic development agency, and it works through local partners in Africa.
  • NGOs distinguish between emergency relief and long-term development, and many shifted in the 1980s towards community projects and advocacy.
  • Trócaire's annual Lenten campaign raised money and awareness in Irish parishes, and it became one of the best-known development campaigns in Ireland.
  • GOAL was founded in Dublin in 1977 by John O'Shea, and Médecins Sans Frontières was founded in France in 1971 by doctors who had worked in Biafra.

Nyerere's ujamaa and the Arusha Declaration

Julius Nyerere tried to build African socialism in Tanzania through state control and rural villages.

  • The Arusha Declaration of 5 February 1967 committed Tanzania to ujamaa (familyhood or African socialism), self-reliance and state control of the main means of production.
  • The government nationalised the banks and major industries and insurance companies in 1967, and it introduced a policy of ‘Education for Self-Reliance’ to shape schooling around rural work.
  • The ujamaa villages were voluntary cooperative settlements at first, but from 1973-74 the government moved millions of people into villages, often by force, in a policy called villagisation.
  • Villagisation disrupted farming, and food shortages followed; the oil price rise, drought and the cost of the war to overthrow Idi Amin of Uganda in 1978-79 added to the crisis, the government had to import food and the economy declined by the early 1980s.
  • Tanzania made important gains in health and literacy, with adult literacy rising to a high proportion of the population by the early 1980s.
  • Nyerere stepped down as president in 1985, and his successor Ali Hassan Mwinyi accepted an IMF programme in 1986 that reversed many of the socialist policies.
  • Nyerere, known as Mwalimu (teacher), died in 1999; supporters stress his gains in education, health and national unity and his refusal to rule by ethnic favour, while critics stress the collapse of farm output, the use of compulsion and the one-party state.
  • TANU's Mwongozo guidelines of 1971 required party members to work for their living and to avoid exploitation and privilege.
  • Universal primary education launched in 1974 aimed to put every child in school by 1977, and primary enrolment rose rapidly.

Key personality: a development worker in Africa

Development workers, including missionaries, volunteers and agency staff, carried out most of the day-to-day aid work in rural Africa from the 1960s.

  • Development workers came from churches, voluntary bodies such as Britain's Voluntary Service Overseas (1958), government programmes such as the American Peace Corps (1961), and NGOs such as Concern and Trócaire.
  • Their typical work included well-digging, agricultural advice, primary health care and vaccination, the supply of food in famines, and training of local staff.
  • Many arrived as young volunteers on short contracts, and they often lived in rural communities with limited access to medicine, transport and communications.
  • Their problems included poor roads and supplies, cultural misunderstandings with local communities, the risk of violence during civil wars, and the difficulty of keeping projects running after the donor left.
  • They were participants in the aid system but also witnesses to its results, and their reports were important sources for NGO campaigns in Europe and North America.
  • Contemporaries in Europe often saw development workers as humanitarian heroes, while African critics sometimes saw them as agents of dependence on foreign money.
  • Historians have criticised ‘top-down’ development projects of the 1960s and 1970s, and they have praised the shift in the 1980s towards projects planned with local people.
  • Irish missionary orders such as the Holy Ghost Fathers and the Medical Missionaries of Mary ran schools, hospitals and clinics in Nigeria, Kenya, Tanzania and other countries, and the Irish government began an official aid programme in 1974.

Assessment: did the economic legacy of decolonisation help Africa?

  • Achievement: the Lomé Conventions gave ACP states preferential access to European markets and a stabilisation fund, which was a recognised innovation in trade policy.
  • Limit: despite these trade preferences, most ACP exports stayed concentrated on a few primary commodities, and commodity prices fell in the 1980s.
  • Achievement: international health campaigns achieved the global eradication of smallpox by 1977, the last natural case being in Somalia, and aid and NGO work improved access to water and primary schooling in many areas.
  • Limit: debt and structural adjustment left many governments with cuts in public services, and the famines of the 1970s and 1980s showed how fragile food supply remained.
  • Achievement: Tanzania's rise in literacy and primary education showed what a government with limited resources could achieve in social services.
  • Limit: villagisation and the collapse of agricultural output showed that state-directed development could cause serious hardship.
  • Later view: dependency writers such as Walter Rodney (How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, 1972) blamed colonial exploitation, while the World Bank's Berg Report of 1981 blamed domestic policies; most historians now combine colonial legacies, falling commodity prices, Cold War pressures and government choices.

Key terms

IMF
The International Monetary Fund, founded at Bretton Woods in 1944, which lends to countries with balance-of-payments problems and sets conditions for its loans.
World Bank
The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, founded at Bretton Woods in 1944, which funds development projects in poorer countries.
Transnational economy
An economy in which trade, finance and production cross national borders, through multinational companies and international lenders.
NGO
A non-governmental organisation, a non-state charity or campaigning body that delivers aid and carries out development work.
Structural adjustment
The conditions of IMF and World Bank loans from the early 1980s, requiring devaluation, cuts in state spending and privatisation.
Lomé Conventions
The trade and aid agreements between the EEC and the ACP states, beginning with Lomé I in 1975, which gave ACP exports preferential access to European markets.
Ujamaa
Julius Nyerere's policy of African socialism and self-reliance in Tanzania, based on cooperative rural villages.
Terms of trade
The ratio of the prices a country receives for its exports to the prices it pays for its imports, which fell against primary producers in the decades after 1950.
ACP states
The African, Caribbean and Pacific countries, former colonies of EEC members, that signed the Lomé Conventions with the Community.
STABEX
The scheme under Lomé I that compensated ACP countries when earnings from specified agricultural exports fell below a set level.
Villagisation
Tanzania's policy from 1973-74 of moving rural people into planned villages, often by compulsion, to carry out ujamaa.
Tied aid
Aid that the recipient must spend on goods and services from the donor country.
