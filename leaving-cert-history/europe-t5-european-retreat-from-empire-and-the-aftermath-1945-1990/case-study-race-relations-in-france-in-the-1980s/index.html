Background: the banlieues and the second generation

By the early 1980s the children of North African and West African migrants lived in the banlieues, where unemployment and policing were the main grievances.

  • The banlieues were the working-class suburbs and high-rise estates around French cities, many built in the 1960s to house migrant families and urban workers.
  • Les Minguettes, an estate in Vénissieux near Lyon, was one of the best-known banlieues, and it became the scene of unrest in 1981.
  • The second generation, known as ‘beurs’ (a verlan term for Arabs), had been born or raised in France and had French citizenship or a right to it.
  • Youth unemployment in the banlieues was far above the national average, and many young people were in low-skilled work or outside the labour market.
  • Relations between young people and the police were poor, and identity checks and stop-and-search practices were a frequent complaint in the banlieues.
  • The oil shock of 1973 ended the post-war economic boom, and migrant workers in the factories were among the first to lose their jobs.
  • Many banlieue families lived in HLM estates, public housing built from the 1950s and 1960s that had become rundown by the 1980s.
  • By the 1982 census about 800,000 Algerians lived in France, making them the largest foreign nationality, ahead of Portuguese and Moroccans.

The Minguettes unrest of 1981

  • In the summer of 1981 young men in the Minguettes estate stole and burned cars in what the press called 'rodéos' and clashed with police, in an episode that had no single cause and drew national television coverage.
  • The unrest spread to other banlieues in Lyon and elsewhere, and it drew national attention to the living conditions of second-generation migrants.
  • The Socialist government of François Mitterrand, elected in May 1981, responded with social programmes for the banlieues and promised reforms to immigration law.
  • The government created Priority Education Zones (ZEP) in schools in 1981 and the 'Banlieues 89' regeneration project in 1983, which were criticised as too limited to address the causes of disadvantage.
  • The Dubedout report of 1983 recommended local regeneration and greater involvement of residents in their neighbourhoods, and it led to local development programmes.
  • Mitterrand's government regularised the status of about 130,000 undocumented migrants in 1981-82, and a law of 1984 created a ten-year residence card.
  • Mitterrand had promised local voting rights for foreign residents and spoke of a 'right to be different', but the voting-rights promise was never carried out.

The March for Equality and against Racism, 1983

  • The March for Equality and against Racism, which the press called the Marche des Beurs, set out from Marseille on 15 October 1983 with about 30 people, after Toumi Djaïdja, a young man from the Minguettes, was shot and wounded by a police officer in June 1983; the priest Christian Delorme and the pastor Jean Costil helped organise it.
  • The march grew as it crossed France, reached Paris on 3 December 1983 with tens of thousands of supporters, and a delegation was received by President Mitterrand, who promised the ten-year residence card.
  • The march's demands included equal treatment in jobs, housing and policing, and the right of second-generation migrants to be treated as French citizens.
  • The march was organised with local associations and churches, and it showed that second-generation migrants could organise a national political campaign.

SOS Racisme and the anti-racist movement, 1984

  • SOS Racisme was founded in Paris in 1984, launched in October, by young activists including Harlem Désir and Julien Dray, and it quickly grew into a national organisation with local branches.
  • Its slogan, ‘Touche pas à mon pote’ (Hands off my pal), was printed on badges worn widely by young people, and it expressed a message of solidarity across ethnic lines.
  • SOS Racisme was supported by the Socialist government and by celebrities and trade unions, and it organised concerts and campaigns against racism in schools and workplaces.
  • Critics on the left argued that SOS Racisme focused on individual racism and did not address the social causes of disadvantage in the banlieues.
  • A free concert of SOS Racisme in the Place de la Concorde on 15 June 1985 drew hundreds of thousands of young people.
  • The Left's critics, including some of the 1983 marchers, accused the Socialists of using SOS Racisme to take over the anti-racist movement.

The rise of the Front National, 1972–1989

  • The Front National (FN) was founded in October 1972 by Jean-Marie Le Pen, a former paratrooper who had served in Algeria, with the backing of far-right groups including Ordre Nouveau, and it drew on supporters of the French Algeria cause.
  • Its policies included the restriction of immigration, the expulsion of illegal migrants and the preference for French citizens in jobs and housing, known as ‘national preference’.
  • The FN's breakthrough came in the Dreux municipal by-election of September 1983, where its list led by Jean-Pierre Stirbois won about 17 per cent in the first round and then joined a right-wing list that took the town, ending the taboo against allying with the FN.
  • In the 1984 European elections the FN won about 11 per cent of the vote and sent 10 deputies to the European Parliament.
  • In the 1986 parliamentary elections, held under a proportional system, the FN won 35 seats in the National Assembly.
  • The FN's support was strongest in the south and east of France, in areas with high unemployment and large migrant communities, such as Marseille and Nice.
  • In the 1988 presidential election Jean-Marie Le Pen won about 14.4 per cent of the first-round vote, over four million votes, while under the return to two-round constituency voting the FN won only one seat in the 1988 parliamentary election.
  • Le Pen was elected as the youngest deputy in 1956 as a Poujadist, and in September 1987 he called the Nazi gas chambers a 'detail' of the history of the Second World War, a remark that brought him a conviction and wide condemnation.

Government responses: the 1986 Pasqua laws and nationality

  • The Chirac government of 1986–88, with Charles Pasqua as interior minister, passed laws that tightened immigration controls, permitted identity checks and made deportation easier.
  • In 1986 the Chirac government proposed ending the automatic acquisition of French nationality at 18 by young people born in France to foreign parents, who would have to make a formal declaration; after mass protests, including from SOS Racisme, it dropped the plan in 1987 and set up the Long commission, and the Méhaignerie law of 1993 later introduced the declaration.
  • The Pasqua law of September 1986 made expulsion of illegal immigrants easier and required visas for most foreigners, and in October 1986 a charter flight deported about 100 Malians.
  • The Socialist government returned to power in 1988 and the Joxe law of 1989 eased some of the 1986 restrictions; the Rocard government created the Haut Conseil à l'intégration in December 1989 and favoured the word integration over assimilation.
  • In December 1986 the death of a student, Malik Oussekine, beaten by riot police during protests in Paris, caused outrage and led Chirac to drop an education bill.

The headscarf affair, 1989

  • In October 1989 the head of a collège in Creil, north of Paris, excluded three Muslim girls for wearing headscarves in class, which became a national controversy.
  • The Minister of Education, Lionel Jospin, referred the question to the Conseil d'État, which ruled on 27 November 1989 that headscarves were allowed unless they were ostentatious or used for propaganda.
  • The affair showed the tension between laïcité and Muslim identity in schools; it was debated in the press and by politicians of all parties, and the Conseil d'État's approach lasted until the law of 2004 banned conspicuous religious symbols in state schools.
  • The Jospin circular of December 1989 told schools to accept headscarves unless they were ostentatious or used for proselytising.

The colonial past and Algeria in French race relations

  • Algerian-origin families formed the largest group among the second generation, and the Algerian war, which ended in 1962, shaped how many French people saw North African migrants.
  • Many Algerian migrants and their children were linked by family and history to the war, while the harkis and their families remained a sensitive issue in French politics.
  • The FN drew support from some former settlers and army veterans of the Algerian war, and its rhetoric echoed arguments about French identity from the colonial period.
  • The government's policies on immigration in the 1980s were shaped by the memory of decolonisation, including the Évian Accords and the departure of between 800,000 and a million settlers in 1962.
  • Pieds-noirs settled in large numbers in southern France, a region where the FN later won much of its support, though the party's voters were not drawn only from former settlers.
  • Under the 'double jus soli' rule of the nationality code, children born in France to parents born in Algeria before 1962 were French from birth, which was one target of the right's proposed reforms.

Comparison with Britain

  • Like Britain, France had racial violence in urban areas in the early 1980s and a far-right party that gained votes from anti-immigration feelings.
  • Unlike Britain, France had no race relations act and did not record ethnic statistics, which made the measurement of discrimination more difficult.
  • France's assimilation model, which treated citizens as individuals, meant that anti-discrimination arguments were framed in terms of universal rights rather than ethnic groups.
  • Britain's Scarman Report of 1981 and the Race Relations Acts offered a framework for responding to racial disadvantage that France lacked in the 1980s.
  • Britain's Commonwealth migrants had the vote from arrival, whereas in France non-citizen residents did not, which made the unfulfilled promise of local voting rights a live grievance.
  • Both countries saw confrontations between police and young people in the early 1980s, but Britain answered with a public inquiry, the Scarman Report, while France's response was through urban regeneration programmes.

Assessment: how France became multi-racial and how race relations developed

  • Achievement: the second generation organised national movements such as the 1983 march and SOS Racisme, and they gained a public voice.
  • Limit: the government's responses in the 1980s focused on policing and immigration control, and social disadvantage in the banlieues remained a source of unrest.
  • Achievement: the Conseil d'État's 1989 headscarf ruling set a legal framework for religious expression in schools that lasted until the 2004 law.
  • Limit: the FN's electoral success showed that anti-immigrant politics could gain mainstream attention, and mainstream parties adopted some of its themes.
  • Contemporary view: supporters of assimilation saw the banlieue unrest as a failure of integration, while activists saw it as the result of racism and social exclusion.
  • Later view: historians stress that the banlieues were shaped by housing policy, deindustrialisation and the colonial past, not by immigration alone.
  • Contemporary government view: Socialist ministers presented integration, rather than assimilation, as the aim in 1989, while Le Pen's supporters said immigration was a threat to French identity.

Key terms

Racism
The belief that people can be ranked by race and treated differently on that basis, expressed in discrimination, violence and exclusion.
Assimilation
The expectation that migrants and their children adopt the language, values and civic culture of the French majority.
Identity
A person's sense of who they are, which for second-generation migrants could combine French citizenship with Algerian, Moroccan or Tunisian heritage.
Cultural diversity
The presence of many cultures, languages and religions within one society, which the banlieues and the second generation made visible in France.
Banlieue
The working-class suburbs and high-rise estates around French cities, where many migrant families lived from the 1960s.
Beurs
The name given to the second generation of North African origin in France, derived from a verlan term for Arab.
Front National
The far-right party founded in 1972 by Jean-Marie Le Pen, which campaigned on immigration restriction and national preference.
Headscarf affair
The 1989 controversy over Muslim girls wearing headscarves in French schools, which led to a Conseil d'État ruling on laïcité.
SOS Racisme
The anti-racist organisation founded in 1984 by Harlem Désir and others, whose badge showed an open hand and the slogan 'Touche pas à mon pote'.
Laïcité
The French principle of state secularism, based on the 1905 law, which was at stake in the 1989 headscarf affair.
Jus soli
The principle that a child born in a country acquires its nationality, which French law applied in a limited way and which the right wished to restrict in 1986.
