Zionism, the Balfour Declaration and the British Mandate, 1917–1939

Britain's wartime promise of a Jewish national home and its Mandate for Palestine created the conflicting claims that later drove the withdrawal.

  • Zionism was a movement that sought a Jewish national homeland; Theodor Herzl led its political organisation from 1897, when the first Zionist Congress was held at Basel.
  • The Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917 stated British support for ‘a national home for the Jewish people’ in Palestine, provided the rights of existing non-Jewish communities were not prejudiced.
  • Britain received the Mandate for Palestine from the League of Nations, which came into force in 1923; the Mandate also covered the territory east of the Jordan, which was separated as Transjordan in 1921.
  • Jewish immigration rose sharply in the 1930s, especially after the Nazis came to power in Germany in 1933, and the Jewish share of Palestine's population grew from about 11 per cent in 1922 to roughly 30 per cent by 1939.
  • The Arab Revolt of 1936–39 against Mandate rule and Jewish immigration was crushed by British forces, and the Peel Commission of 1937 proposed partitioning Palestine.
  • The British White Paper of 17 May 1939 limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years, after which Arab consent would be needed, restricted Jewish land purchases and promised an independent Palestine within ten years; the Zionist movement rejected it.
  • The Peel Commission of 1937 proposed a small Jewish state, an Arab state joined to Transjordan and a British-held enclave around Jerusalem; Arab leaders rejected it and the British dropped it in 1938.

Jewish armed groups and the Holocaust's effect, 1939–1947

  • The Holocaust killed about six million Jews in Europe between 1941 and 1945, and it intensified the demand for a Jewish state and for the admission of Jewish refugees to Palestine.
  • The Haganah, founded in 1920, was the main Jewish defence force in Palestine, and it was linked to the Jewish Agency under the leadership of David Ben-Gurion.
  • The Irgun, formed in 1931 by Revisionist Zionists and led from 1943 by Menachem Begin, and the smaller Lehi (Stern Gang) carried out attacks on British targets.
  • The Irgun bombed the south wing of the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, the headquarters of the British administration, on 22 July 1946, killing 91 Arabs, Britons and Jews.
  • From October 1945 to mid-1946 the Haganah, Irgun and Lehi co-operated in a Jewish Resistance Movement, but in 1944-45 the Haganah had handed Irgun members to the British in the 'Saison'; the Irgun hanged two British sergeants in July 1947 in reprisal for the execution of its members.
  • Britain's policy of restricting refugee ships led to the Exodus of 1947, when about 4,500 Holocaust survivors were sent back to Europe, a scene that drew world attention.
  • The Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry recommended in April 1946 that 100,000 Jewish refugees be admitted to Palestine; Britain, with about 100,000 troops in the country, refused without Arab agreement.

Britain refers Palestine to the United Nations and partition, 1947

Unable to reconcile Arab and Jewish claims, Britain handed the problem to the United Nations in 1947.

  • In February 1947 the British government announced that it would refer the Palestine question to the United Nations, and in September 1947 it said it would end the Mandate.
  • The UN Special Committee on Palestine (UNSCOP) recommended partition in 1947, and the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 181 on 29 November 1947, by 33 votes to 13 with 10 abstentions, to divide Palestine into an Arab state and a Jewish state, with Jerusalem under international control.
  • The partition plan gave the proposed Jewish state about 55 per cent of the territory, although the Jewish population was only about a third of the total.
  • The Arab Higher Committee and the Arab states rejected partition, and fighting between Arab and Jewish forces began in December 1947.
  • Britain refused to implement partition itself and announced that it would withdraw its forces by 15 May 1948, which left a gap in security control during the spring of 1948.
  • The Mandate expired on 14 May 1948, the same day that Israel was proclaimed, so British authority in Palestine ended as the Jewish state began.
  • Both the United States and the Soviet Union supported partition, an unusual agreement in the early Cold War; Britain abstained.
  • In the civil war that followed, Jewish forces under Plan Dalet seized Tiberias, Haifa and Jaffa in April-May 1948, and the Arab population of those cities fled in large numbers.

Israel's declaration and the 1948–49 war

The declaration of Israel on 14 May 1948 led to war with the neighbouring Arab states and the flight of many Palestinian Arabs.

  • David Ben-Gurion proclaimed the State of Israel in Tel Aviv on 14 May 1948 and became its first prime minister.
  • Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Iraq and Lebanon sent armies into former Mandate territory on 15 May 1948, starting the first Arab–Israeli war.
  • The attack on Deir Yassin on 9 April 1948 by Irgun and Lehi fighters killed roughly 100 to 120 villagers, though early reports said 254; it was used in propaganda by both sides and fuelled panic among Arabs. Four days later Arab forces killed about 78 Jews in a medical convoy near Jerusalem.
  • About 700,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled from territory that became Israel, mostly to the West Bank, Gaza, Lebanon, Syria and Jordan; UN General Assembly Resolution 194 of December 1948 said refugees who wished to return should be allowed to, a right Israel has not accepted.
  • The armistice agreements of 1949 left Israel with more territory than the UN plan had allotted it; Jordan held the West Bank and Egypt administered the Gaza Strip.
  • Jordan annexed the West Bank in 1950, a step recognised by only a few states, and the refugee question remained unresolved.
  • About 6,000 Israelis died in the war, roughly one per cent of the Jewish population; Israel's Law of Return of 1950 gave Jews the right to immigrate, and Jews also left or were pushed out of Arab states in the years after 1948.
  • The United States recognised Israel within minutes of the declaration and the Soviet Union recognised it three days later; Israel's declaration did not fix its borders.

Nasser, Arab nationalism and the road to Suez, 1952–1956

  • On 23 July 1952 the Free Officers movement overthrew King Farouk of Egypt; Gamal Abdul Nasser emerged as the leading figure and became prime minister in 1954.
  • The Anglo-Egyptian Agreement of October 1954 provided for the withdrawal of British troops from the Suez Canal base, which was completed in June 1956.
  • The Baghdad Pact of 1955 linked Britain, Iraq, Turkey, Iran and Pakistan in a defensive alliance that Nasser saw as an attempt to isolate Egypt.
  • In 1955 Nasser bought arms from the Soviet bloc through Czechoslovakia, a deal that made Egypt a point of Cold War rivalry.
  • Nasser's Arab nationalism was opposed to Western alliances and to the state of Israel; he attended the Bandung Conference in April 1955 and supported anti-colonial movements.
  • Nasser was elected president of Egypt by referendum in June 1956, and on 19 July 1956 the United States, followed by Britain, withdrew its offer to fund the Aswan High Dam, after Egypt recognised communist China and bought Soviet-bloc arms.
  • Egypt barred Israeli ships from the Suez Canal and, from 1955, from the Straits of Tiran, and fedayeen raids from Gaza led to Israeli reprisals such as the Gaza raid of February 1955, which killed 38 and pushed Nasser to seek arms.

The Suez Crisis, July–November 1956

Nasser's nationalisation of the Suez Canal provoked an Anglo-French and Israeli invasion that ended in political defeat.

  • The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, was run by the Suez Canal Company; Britain had bought the Khedive's 44 per cent of its shares in 1875, and about two-thirds of Western Europe's oil imports passed through it in the 1950s.
  • On 26 July 1956 Nasser announced the nationalisation of the Suez Canal Company, promising that canal revenues would fund the Aswan High Dam.
  • Britain and France planned military action with Israel; the secret Protocol of Sèvres, signed on 24 October 1956, set out an Israeli attack on Sinai followed by an Anglo-French ultimatum.
  • Israeli forces invaded the Sinai Peninsula on 29 October 1956, and Britain and France issued an ultimatum to both sides on 30 October; the Anglo-French bombing of Egyptian airfields followed on 31 October.
  • British and French paratroopers landed at Port Said and Port Fuad on 5 November 1956 and seaborne landings followed on 6 November; the operation was halted and a ceasefire took effect at midnight on 6-7 November.
  • The United States under Eisenhower opposed the invasion; Britain faced a run on the pound, and the US refused support from the IMF until Britain agreed to withdraw.
  • The Soviet Union threatened rocket attacks on London and Paris in a letter of 5 November 1956, and it supported Egypt in the UN.
  • The UN created the United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF) in November 1956 on a proposal by the Canadian foreign minister Lester Pearson, commanded by the Canadian general E. L. M. Burns, to supervise the withdrawal and the Sinai frontier.
  • The Hungarian rising had been crushed by Soviet troops on 4 November, which weakened Britain's and France's complaint against Soviet behaviour and made Eisenhower angrier at his allies.
  • A British-French force had advanced about 23 miles south of Port Said by the time of the ceasefire.

Results of the Suez Crisis

  • Anthony Eden, ill and discredited, resigned as British prime minister on 9 January 1957 and Harold Macmillan succeeded him; the crisis had also shown that Britain could not act against American wishes.
  • Israel withdrew from Sinai and Gaza in early 1957, after the United States gave assurances about the Straits of Tiran and the UNEF presence.
  • Egypt had sunk about fifty ships in the canal; a UN clearance operation reopened it in April 1957, under Egyptian control, and Egypt seized British and French property.
  • Nasser's prestige in the Arab world increased sharply because he had defied Britain, France and Israel and kept control of the canal.
  • Britain and France lost their position as the main Western powers in the Middle East, and the United States took a larger role there.
  • The crisis showed the limits of Anglo-French power after 1945 and led the United States to announce the Eisenhower Doctrine in January 1957 to fill the gap in the Middle East.
  • The Hashemite monarchy in Iraq, Britain's closest Arab ally, was overthrown in 1958, and Iraq left the Baghdad Pact in 1959.

Key personality: David Ben-Gurion

Ben-Gurion led the Jewish community in Palestine and was the first prime minister of Israel.

  • Born David Grün in 1886 in Płońsk in the Russian Empire, he emigrated to Ottoman Palestine in 1906 and worked as a labour activist.
  • He led the Jewish Agency's executive from 1935 and accepted the partition plan of 1947 as a first step towards a Jewish state.
  • As a participant he proclaimed Israel on 14 May 1948 and served as prime minister in 1948-54 and 1955-63, also holding the defence ministry; he resigned in 1953 and retired to the kibbutz of Sde Boker before returning.
  • He oversaw the creation of the Israel Defence Forces in May 1948, ordering the shelling of the Irgun ship Altalena in June 1948 to establish one army, and in October 1956 he attended the secret talks at Sèvres with Britain and France.
  • Arab opinion regarded him as the architect of a state that displaced Palestinians, while most Jews saw him as the founder of the state.
  • Historians of the 'new history' school such as Benny Morris argue that there was no overall expulsion plan in 1948 but that Ben-Gurion backed the expulsions from Lydda and Ramle in July 1948.

Key personality: Gamal Abdul Nasser

Nasser was the leader of Egypt from 1954 to 1970 and the most important figure of Arab nationalism in the Suez crisis.

  • Born 15 January 1918 in Alexandria, he trained as an army officer and was one of the Free Officers who overthrew King Farouk in 1952.
  • As prime minister from 1954 and president from 1956, he pursued land reform, industrialisation and the building of the Aswan High Dam.
  • He was a participant in the Suez Crisis, in which he nationalised the canal and, though his army was defeated in Sinai, survived the Anglo-French-Israeli attack with Egyptian control of the canal confirmed.
  • He formed the United Arab Republic with Syria in 1958, which lasted until 1961, and he supported anti-colonial movements in Algeria and elsewhere.
  • British ministers, including Eden, compared him with dictators of the 1930s, while Arab nationalists across the region saw him as a hero who had defied the West.
  • Historians credit him with land reform, the Aswan High Dam and Arab self-confidence, but criticise his one-party rule, the suppression of the Muslim Brotherhood and the military failure of 1967.
  • After 1956 he sent troops into the Yemeni civil war in 1962, which became a costly commitment, and in 1967 he blocked the Straits of Tiran; Egypt lost Sinai in the Six-Day War of June 1967.
  • He offered to resign after the 1967 defeat, withdrew his resignation after mass demonstrations, and died on 28 September 1970.

Assessment: British policy in Palestine and Suez

  • Achievement: Britain ended the Palestine Mandate and passed the question to the UN instead of fighting both communities, as the cost and the attacks on British forces had become too great.
  • Limit: the Mandate's conflicting promises to Arabs and Jews were not reconciled, and the 1948 war left a legacy of refugees and hostility.
  • Achievement: Israel overran Sinai in days and the Anglo-French force had advanced down the canal before the ceasefire, so the military operation was largely successful as far as it went.
  • Limit: the Suez operation failed politically; the ceasefire was forced by the United States, the Soviet threat and the UN, and the canal remained in Egyptian hands.
  • Contemporary view: Eden presented the action as police action to separate Israel and Egypt, while Labour leader Hugh Gaitskell and the junior minister Anthony Nutting, who resigned, condemned the secret collusion with Israel.
  • Later view: many historians treat Suez as the moment that exposed Britain's limits as a power; others argue that Britain's withdrawal from empire was already under way, and they debate how far Britain's Mandate policy, including the 1917 declaration and the 1939 White Paper, helped to create Middle East tensions.

Key terms

Zionism
The movement that sought a Jewish national homeland, which in the 1917–48 period centred on Palestine.
Terrorism
The use of violence against civilians or political targets to create fear and advance a political aim, used in Palestine by Jewish and Arab armed groups.
Balfour Declaration
The British statement of 2 November 1917 supporting a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine.
Mandate
The League of Nations system under which Britain administered Palestine from 1923 until 14 May 1948.
Arab nationalism
The movement for the political unity and independence of Arab peoples, which opposed both Western control and the state of Israel.
Nationalisation
The transfer of a privately owned company or asset to state ownership, as in Nasser's takeover of the Suez Canal Company in 1956.
Protocol of Sèvres
The secret agreement of October 1956 among Britain, France and Israel that set out the military plan for the invasion of Egypt.
UNEF
The United Nations Emergency Force, set up in November 1956 to supervise the withdrawal of the invading forces and the Sinai frontier.
Haganah
The main Jewish defence force in Mandate Palestine, founded in 1920 and under the Jewish Agency, which became the core of the Israel Defence Forces.
Irgun
The Revisionist Zionist armed group, led from 1943 by Menachem Begin, which attacked British and Arab targets including the King David Hotel.
White Paper of 1939
The British policy of May 1939 that limited Jewish immigration and land purchases and promised an independent Palestine in ten years.
Free Officers
The group of Egyptian army officers that overthrew King Farouk in July 1952 and brought Nasser to power.
