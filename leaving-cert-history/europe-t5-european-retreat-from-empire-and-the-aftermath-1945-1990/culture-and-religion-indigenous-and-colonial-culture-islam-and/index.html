Colonial education and the civilising mission

Colonial powers presented their rule as a civilising mission, and education was the main means of spreading European culture.

  • The French idea of a mission civilisatrice held that France had a duty to spread French language, law and values among its colonial subjects.
  • Most schools in British and Belgian Africa were run by Christian missions, which taught literacy, scripture and European history and often discouraged African languages and customs.
  • The French colonial system created a small educated elite trained in French and expected to adopt French culture, through schools such as the Lycée Faidherbe in Saint-Louis, Senegal, and the École William Ponty near Dakar, which trained future teachers and officials.
  • In British colonies, indirect rule and mission schools produced clerks, teachers and interpreters, and the education of girls lagged far behind that of boys.
  • Colonial curricula taught European rivers, kings and battles, and schoolbooks in French Africa began with 'our ancestors the Gauls'; African historical and literary traditions were largely absent from schools until after independence.

Négritude and cultural nationalism

  • The Négritude movement was founded in Paris in the 1930s by the poet Léopold Sédar Senghor of Senegal, the Martinican poet Aimé Césaire and the Guianese poet Léon-Gontran Damas.
  • The journal L'Étudiant noir, founded in 1934, was one of the first publications to bring together students from Africa, the Caribbean and Guiana around the idea of a shared black identity.
  • Négritude celebrated African and black cultural values, and it challenged the assumption that European culture was superior to African culture.
  • Senghor became the first president of Senegal in 1960 and held office until 1980, and in 1983 he became the first African elected to the Académie française; he advocated African socialism and kept close ties with France.
  • Critics such as Wole Soyinka and the South African writer Es'kia Mphahlele argued that Négritude was too closely tied to European intellectual traditions and romanticised African culture.
  • Césaire's poem Cahier d'un retour au pays natal (1939) gave the word négritude its first literary use, and Senghor's poetry celebrated an African sensibility based on rhythm and emotion.

Christianity, Islam and indigenous religion in Africa

Christianity and Islam spread in Africa through different routes, and both were reshaped by African societies and by colonial rule.

  • Christian missions, Catholic and Protestant, were the main agents of Christian expansion in colonial Africa, and they built schools, hospitals and churches across the continent.
  • African Independent Churches broke away from the mission churches; the Kimbanguist Church, founded by Simon Kimbangu in the Belgian Congo after his preaching began in 1921, became one of the largest, and the Aladura churches grew in Yorubaland in Nigeria.
  • Islam had spread across West Africa through trade and Sufi brotherhoods, and the Mouride brotherhood in Senegal, founded by Amadou Bamba and linked with the groundnut economy, became a major political and economic force despite his exile by the French.
  • On the East African coast Islam had been established for centuries through trade, and it shaped Swahili language and culture.
  • In northern Nigeria, Lugard's indirect rule worked through the Muslim emirs and barred most Christian missions from the emirates, so western education spread much faster in the south than in the north.
  • Many Africans combined Christian or Islamic belief with older religious practices, and the relationship between religion and political authority varied widely.
  • The White Fathers, a Catholic missionary order founded in Algiers in 1868 by Cardinal Lavigerie, became prominent in East and Central Africa.
  • The Church Missionary Society, an Anglican mission founded in 1799, was active across West and East Africa.
  • The Sokoto Caliphate, founded after the jihad led by Usman dan Fodio from 1804, spread Islamic law across much of northern Nigeria.
  • Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a freed slave from Yorubaland, became the first African Anglican bishop in 1864, an early sign of African leadership in mission churches.
  • Irish missionaries such as the Holy Ghost Fathers and the Society of African Missions worked in Nigeria and other British colonies, running schools and hospitals.

Islam in Europe

  • The Grand Mosque of Paris was inaugurated in 1926 to honour the Muslim soldiers who died for France in the First World War and as a symbol of the French state's relationship with Islam in its empire.
  • Muslim migration to Britain and France from the 1950s and 1960s led to the building of mosques in British cities, including Birmingham, Bradford and London, and to the establishment of Islamic cultural centres.
  • Salman Rushdie's novel The Satanic Verses was published in September 1988, and many Muslims in Britain objected to its portrayal of the Prophet Muhammad.
  • In January 1989 Muslims in Bradford burned copies of the book in a public protest, which drew national attention to the question of blasphemy in Britain.
  • On 14 February 1989 the Iranian leader Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa calling for Rushdie's death, which led to his going into hiding under British police protection.
  • The Rushdie affair raised questions about freedom of expression and religious identity in Britain, and it was debated alongside the headscarf question in France.
  • France had the largest Muslim population in Western Europe, estimated at between two and three million by the 1980s, mostly of Algerian, Moroccan and Tunisian origin, and Islam became France's second religion.
  • Britain's blasphemy law protected only the Christian faith, and the courts refused in 1991 to extend it to Islam, which Muslim campaigners cited after the Rushdie affair.

English as a world language

  • English spread across the world through the British Empire and the Commonwealth, and it became the official language of many former British colonies, including Nigeria and Kenya.
  • In Nigeria, English was the language of government, education and the courts, and it became a second language for many Nigerians alongside the major local languages.
  • Tanzania chose Swahili as its national language and language of instruction in primary schools, making it one of the few African states to adopt an indigenous language as its main national language.
  • After 1945 the spread of English was also driven by American economic and cultural power, through films, music, business and the new international organisations.
  • The use of English in African literature raised the question of whether colonial languages could carry African experience, and writers answered this question in different ways.
  • Pidgin English developed in West African trading ports and became a widely spoken lingua franca in Nigeria and Cameroon.
  • In India Hindi was declared the official language in 1950 but English remained an associate official language, and it was retained after anti-Hindi protests in Tamil Nadu in 1965.
  • France promoted its own language through La Francophonie, whose first institution, an agency for cultural and technical cooperation, was founded in 1970 by French-speaking states.

Key personality: Chinua Achebe

Achebe was the Nigerian novelist whose work changed how Africa was represented in world literature.

  • Born 16 November 1930 at Ogidi in eastern Nigeria, he studied at University College Ibadan and worked for the Nigerian Broadcasting Corporation before becoming a full-time writer.
  • Things Fall Apart, published in 1958 by Heinemann, portrayed an Igbo community before and during the arrival of British colonial rule through the story of Okonkwo; its title comes from a line in a poem by W. B. Yeats, and it became one of the most widely read African novels in English.
  • His later novels, including No Longer at Ease (1960), Arrow of God (1964) and A Man of the People (1966), examined corruption and the failures of the new independent states.
  • During the Biafran War he supported Biafra and travelled abroad as an advocate for its cause, and he wrote about the war in There Was a Country (2012).
  • Contemporaries praised his novels, while critics such as Obi Wali argued that African literature in European languages was a dead end; Achebe replied in his 1965 essay on the African writer and the English language that English could be remade to carry African experience.
  • In his 1975 lecture An Image of Africa he criticised Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness for portraying Africa as the opposite of Europe, and historians of literature still debate his argument.
  • He died in March 2013, and his work is now studied as a foundational text of post-colonial literature.
  • His novel Anthills of the Savannah (1987) was set in a fictional West African state and examined military rule and its corruption.
  • As the first editorial adviser of Heinemann's African Writers Series from 1962, he helped to publish many other African writers.

Key personality: Nadine Gordimer

Gordimer was a South African novelist whose writing examined white and black lives under apartheid and the limits of liberal opposition.

  • Born 20 November 1923 at Springs near Johannesburg, she was a white South African who wrote in English and lived most of her life in Johannesburg.
  • Her early novels, including The Lying Days (1953) and A World of Strangers (1958), explored the social divisions of white and black South Africans.
  • The Conservationist (1974) shared the Booker Prize, A World of Strangers (1958) was banned in South Africa for twelve years, and Burger's Daughter (1979), about the child of a communist activist, was banned for a time.
  • July's People (1981) imagined a South African revolution in which a white family takes refuge with their black servant in a village, and it was widely read internationally.
  • Contemporaries in South Africa saw her as a critic of apartheid whose work was subject to censorship; she was a supporter of the African National Congress and helped Nelson Mandela with his speech for the Rivonia trial in 1964, while some black writers argued that white liberal writers were limited in their perspective.
  • She was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1991, gave a lecture called Writing and Being, remained an opponent of censorship after apartheid ended, and died in July 2014.
  • My Son's Story (1990) explored the politics of the apartheid years through a family's divided loyalties.

Post-colonial literature beyond Achebe and Gordimer

  • Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o wrote his early novels in English but was held without trial in 1977-78 after staging a play in Gikuyu, and afterwards wrote in Gikuyu; his book Decolonising the Mind (1986) argued that writing in colonial languages restricted African culture.
  • The Nigerian playwright and poet Wole Soyinka won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1986, the first African to receive it; he had been detained in solitary confinement during the Nigerian civil war.
  • The African Writers Series, begun by Heinemann in 1962, made African novels widely available in schools and universities across the English-speaking world.
  • Post-colonial writers often explored the tension between traditional beliefs and the new Christian or Islamic faiths, and between village life and urban modernity.
  • Wole Soyinka's novel The Interpreters (1965) and Ngũgĩ's A Grain of Wheat (1967) examined the moral failures of the independence generation.
  • French-language writers included Cheikh Hamidou Kane, whose novel Ambiguous Adventure (1961) explored a Senegalese Muslim boy's education in France, and Mariama Bâ, whose So Long a Letter (1979) described the lives of Muslim women in Senegal.

Assessment: colonial culture, indigenous culture and identity

  • Achievement: African writers and religious movements created new forms of cultural expression that combined African traditions with European and Islamic influences.
  • Limit: colonial education and missions had reduced the teaching of African languages, and many African states inherited a dependence on English or French for higher education.
  • Achievement: Tanzania's choice of Swahili showed that an African language could be used as the language of government and education.
  • Limit: the debate over writing in English or in local languages showed the continuing tension between cultural identity and the legacy of colonial education.
  • Contemporary view: colonial officials and missionaries often saw African culture as backward, while African nationalists argued for the revival of African traditions.
  • Later view: Achebe argued that English could be reshaped to carry African experience, while Ngũgĩ argued that using European languages cut African writers off from the peasants and workers who spoke African languages.

Key terms

Identity
A person's or community's sense of who they are, shaped by language, religion, ethnicity and history, and contested under colonial rule.
Cultural diversity
The presence of many languages, religions and cultural traditions within one society, which post-colonial states had to manage.
Assimilation
The expectation that colonial subjects adopt the language and culture of the colonial power, which the French policy of assimilation pursued most fully.
Civilising mission
The European idea that colonial rule was justified by the duty to bring Christianity, education and European values to non-European peoples.
Négritude
A movement founded in Paris in the 1930s that celebrated black cultural identity and challenged European claims of cultural superiority.
Post-colonial literature
Writing from former colonies, in English, French or local languages, that examines colonial rule, identity and the aftermath of empire.
Sufi brotherhood
A mystical Islamic religious order, such as the Mouride or Tijaniyya brotherhoods in West Africa, which organised religious and economic life.
Censorship
The control of published or performed material by the state, which in South Africa banned many of Gordimer's novels.
African Independent Churches
Churches founded and led by Africans after breaking away from the mission churches, such as the Kimbanguist Church in the Congo.
Fatwa
A ruling by an Islamic authority; Ayatollah Khomeini's fatwa of 1989 called for the death of Salman Rushdie.
Lingua franca
A language used for communication between people with different mother tongues, such as English, Swahili or West African pidgin.
