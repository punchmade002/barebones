Background: Katanga's mineral wealth and its politics before 1960

Katanga was the richest mining region of the Belgian Congo, and its mining economy was controlled by a Belgian company.

  • Katanga, later called Shaba, was the copper-mining heart of the Congo, and it also produced cobalt, zinc, and uranium that was used in the first atomic bombs.
  • The Union Minière du Haut-Katanga, a company with Belgian shareholders, ran the mines and gave Katanga a far higher revenue base than other provinces.
  • Katanga's administration and its mining towns, such as Élisabethville (now Lubumbashi), were dominated by Europeans, and Congolese workers lived in segregated housing.
  • The Conakat party, founded in 1958 by Moïse Tshombe, represented the Lunda and related peoples of southern Katanga and campaigned for regional autonomy, arguing that Katanga should keep its own revenue; Tshombe was connected by marriage to the Lunda royal family.
  • The rival Balubakat party, led by Jason Sendwe, drew support from the Baluba of northern Katanga and Baluba migrants in the mining towns, and the rivalry between the two parties became a regional and ethnic conflict.
  • Belgian mining interests and officials supported Conakat's autonomy campaign, which made Katanga's secession more likely once independence came.
  • In the provincial elections of May 1960 Conakat won the most seats in the provincial assembly, though not a clear majority, and Tshombe became president of the province with the support of independents; in the national elections Lumumba's allies took many of Katanga's seats.

The secession and Belgian backing, July 1960

  • Tshombe proclaimed the secession of Katanga from the Congo on 11 July 1960, just eleven days after independence, and declared the Katangan State.
  • The secession was supported by Belgian officers, advisers and paid foreign mercenaries, who trained and led the Katanga Gendarmerie.
  • Lumumba's government saw the secession as a threat to national unity, and it asked the United Nations for help in restoring the Congo's territory.
  • Tshombe argued that Katanga was a separate state with its own revenue and that a central government in Léopoldville was controlled by outsiders and Communists.
  • The Katanga secession was not recognised by any state, and Belgium sent troops back to the Congo on 10 July 1960 and gave the Katangan authorities military and technical help, with Belgian officers commanding its gendarmerie.
  • The UN Security Council called on Belgium to withdraw from Katanga on 9 August 1960 and UN troops entered the province on 12 August; Belgian regular troops left by September, but Belgian officers and technicians stayed on in the Katangan forces.

Lumumba's overthrow and murder, 1960-1961

  • Patrice Lumumba, prime minister, asked the UN for help against Katanga but was dissatisfied that ONUC would not use force against Tshombe, and turned to the Soviet Union for aircraft and advisers in August 1960.
  • President Kasa-Vubu dismissed Lumumba on 5 September 1960, Lumumba declared Kasa-Vubu deposed, and Colonel Mobutu intervened on 14 September to neutralise both.
  • Lumumba, confined to his residence under UN guard, slipped away in late November 1960 and was arrested by Mobutu's troops in early December.
  • On 17 January 1961 he was flown to Katanga and shot, with Belgian officers present and Katangan leaders taking part; his death was announced on 13 February 1961.
  • The murder provoked Soviet and African protests, riots in several capitals, and the UN Security Council's Resolution 161 of February 1961, which authorised the use of force as a last resort.
  • Lumumba became a martyr of African nationalism; in Moscow the Friendship University was renamed after him in 1961.

The UN operation and the Irish troops, 1960–1961

  • The UN Security Council set up the ONUC operation in July 1960, and the UN declared that it would not intervene in internal disputes except to prevent civil war.
  • Security Council Resolution 161 of 21 February 1961 authorised the UN to use force as a last resort to prevent civil war and called for the withdrawal of foreign military personnel and mercenaries; Resolution 169 of 24 November 1961 explicitly authorised action against the Katangan secession and foreign mercenaries.
  • Irish troops, forming part of the UN force, suffered nine dead when a patrol was ambushed at Niemba on 8 November 1960 by Baluba tribesmen, and 155 Irish soldiers were besieged at Jadotville in September 1961.
  • At Jadotville, A Company of the 35th Battalion under Commandant Pat Quinlan was besieged by Katangan gendarmes and foreign mercenaries from 13 September 1961; with no relief, little water and low ammunition they surrendered on 17 September without an Irish death, and were held prisoner for about a month.
  • Conor Cruise O'Brien, an Irish diplomat serving as the UN representative in Katanga, pressed for a more forceful UN policy, and after Operation Rumpunch (28 August 1961) the UN launched Operation Morthor on 13 September 1961 to arrest the foreign officers and take control of Élisabethville, which failed.
  • In August 1960 Lumumba's government accepted Soviet transport aircraft and trucks, used in an offensive in South Kasai, which heightened Western concern about Cold War involvement in the Congo.
  • O'Brien later wrote of his Katanga experience in To Katanga and Back (1962); the UN's Congo operation killed about 250 personnel, among them 26 Irish soldiers.

The death of Hammarskjöld and the end of secession, 1961–1963

  • At the UN General Assembly in 1960 Nikita Khrushchev criticised Hammarskjöld's handling of the Congo and demanded replacing the Secretary-General with a three-person executive (a troika), a proposal that the West and most members rejected.
  • Dag Hammarskjöld, the UN Secretary-General, died in an air crash near Ndola in Northern Rhodesia on 18 September 1961 while travelling to negotiate a ceasefire with Tshombe.
  • Several inquiries examined Hammarskjöld's death, including a UN inquiry and a Rhodesian one in 1962 that favoured pilot error; later UN-appointed panels in 2013 and 2017 concluded that an external attack was possible and urged further investigation.
  • Fighting between UN and Katangan forces in Élisabethville in December 1961 ended with the Kitona Declaration of 21 December, in which Tshombe appeared to accept Katanga's place in the Congo but he later repudiated it.
  • The UN's operations in Katanga were continued under U Thant, and in December 1962 the UN launched Operation Grand Slam against the Katangan forces.
  • Tshombe announced the end of the secession on 14 January 1963 at Kolwezi, UN troops entered Kolwezi a week later, and Tshombe left for exile in 1963.
  • The secession had lasted about two and a half years, and its end removed the largest threat to the central government's authority.

Tshombe, Mobutu and the Congo's new order, 1963–1965

  • Tshombe became prime minister of the Congo in July 1964, a post he held until his dismissal by President Kasa-Vubu in October 1965.
  • The Simba rebellion of 1964, by supporters of Lumumba, spread through the east of the Congo; Stanleyville fell in August 1964 and the rebellion was crushed with Belgian paratroopers, American aircraft and mercenaries in 1964-65.
  • Mobutu, who had been army chief of staff since 1960, seized power on 24 November 1965, and Tshombe went into exile in Spain; Tshombe's plane was hijacked to Algeria in 1967 and he died in custody there in 1969.
  • The Katanga secession had shown the army's importance and the effect of foreign support, and Mobutu's coup used the army to end the factional fighting of the 1960s.
  • In 1977 and 1978 former Katangan gendarmes based in Angola invaded Shaba province; the 1977 invasion was repelled with Moroccan troops and French transport, and in 1978 French and Belgian paratroops retook Kolwezi.
  • The United States supported Mobutu's regime with military aid after 1965, which made the army a key instrument of Cold War policy in central Africa.

Key personality: Sese Seko Mobutu

Mobutu rose from the army to become the dictator of the Congo, which he renamed Zaire, and ruled it until 1997.

  • Born Joseph-Désiré Mobutu on 14 October 1930 at Lisala in the Congo, he served in the Force Publique and then worked as a journalist before independence.
  • Chief of staff of the Congolese army from July 1960, he led the coup of 14 September 1960 that 'neutralised' both Lumumba and Kasa-Vubu; his troops arrested Lumumba in December 1960, and he returned power to Kasa-Vubu in February 1961.
  • As a participant in the Congo crisis he worked with the CIA station chief Larry Devlin and Belgian advisers, and the United States saw him as the best anti-communist leader; the Church Committee of the US Senate in 1975 later revealed CIA plans against Lumumba.
  • He seized power a second time on 24 November 1965, had four political figures hanged in public in June 1966, founded his Popular Movement of the Revolution (MPR) in 1967 and ruled as president under a one-party system.
  • Contemporaries in the West, including the United States and Belgium, regarded him as a bulwark against communism in central Africa; many Congolese saw him as a corrupt ruler who enriched his circle.
  • Historians describe his rule as personal dictatorship and kleptocracy: he drew on mineral revenues and Western aid, rewarded loyalists, kept the state weak and left the economy in ruin by the 1990s, while some stress that his control of the army and his Cold War value to Washington kept him in power so long.
  • He renamed the Congo Zaire in 1971 and took the name Mobutu Sese Seko in 1972; he nationalised the Union Minière's Katanga assets in 1966-67, launched the policy of authenticité, and was overthrown in May 1997 by Laurent-Désiré Kabila's forces, dying in exile in Morocco in September 1997.
  • Mobutu's rule lasted from 1965 to 1997, so only the period to 1990 belongs to this topic; in April 1990 he announced the end of the one-party state, after which his support from the West weakened with the end of the Cold War.

Significance of the Katanga secession

  • Nationally, the secession tested whether the Congo could survive as one state, and the central government's victory preserved its territory.
  • Internationally, the crisis was a Cold War contest in which the United States and the Soviet Union, and the former colonial power Belgium, each supported different sides.
  • The Katanga case set a precedent for the UN's involvement in internal conflicts and for its peacekeeping role, which became a standard tool of the organisation.
  • Mobutu nationalised the Union Minière's Katangan assets in 1966-67 and set up the state company Gécamines, so the province's mineral wealth became the central state's main source of revenue.
  • African states split: the Casablanca group, including Ghana, Guinea and Egypt, backed Lumumba and the central government, while the more moderate Monrovia group was less hostile to Tshombe and the Western powers; the crisis was one of the first tests of African unity before the OAU was founded in 1963.
  • The cost of the operation, about 400 million dollars, caused a UN financial crisis when the Soviet Union and France refused to pay towards it.

Assessment: why Katanga seceded and why it drew international involvement

  • Cause: Katanga's mineral revenues gave its leaders a strong economic reason to keep control of their wealth, and Belgian companies and officials supported that aim.
  • Cause: Tshombe's Conakat party claimed that Katanga's peoples had a separate identity, and the fear of Lumumba's centralising government strengthened regional loyalties.
  • Result: the secession was defeated by the central government with UN help, but the crisis left the Congolese state dependent on foreign military support.
  • International involvement came from Belgian self-interest, Cold War rivalry and the UN's need to protect a new member state's territorial integrity.
  • Contemporary view: the Congolese government and many African states saw the secession as a neo-colonial plot, while Tshombe and his supporters saw it as a defence against centralised rule.
  • Later view: Ludo De Witte's book on Lumumba's murder prompted a Belgian parliamentary inquiry that reported in 2001 that Belgian ministers and officials bore moral responsibility for it, and historians stress the roles of Belgium and the mining company in sustaining Katanga.
  • Achievement: the central government's victory preserved the Congo's borders as they had been drawn at independence, in line with the African principle of accepting colonial frontiers.
  • Limit: the defeat of the secession did not end the instability in the Congo, and the Simba rebellion of 1964 showed how much of the country remained outside central control.
  • Contemporary view of the UN: the Soviet bloc and some African states accused the UN of failing to protect Lumumba and the central government, while Britain and France criticised it for acting against Katanga.

Key terms

Tribalism
A term used by colonial officials for loyalty to ethnic groups, which in Katanga shaped the rivalry between the Conakat and Balubakat parties.
Transnational economy
An economy in which companies and capital operate across national borders, as with the Union Minière's control of Katangan mining.
Secession
The withdrawal of a region from a state to form a separate state, as Katanga did from July 1960 to January 1963.
Union Minière du Haut-Katanga
The Belgian-controlled mining company that ran Katanga's copper, cobalt and uranium industries.
Mercenary
A foreign soldier paid to fight for a state or faction, who served in the Katanga Gendarmerie and against the UN forces.
Cold War
The rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union from 1945 to 1991, which shaped the Katanga crisis and the Congo's politics.
Authenticité
Mobutu's policy from 1971 to promote African culture and names, and to reject European influence.
Peacekeeping
The deployment of UN forces to keep order and separate combatants, as in ONUC in the Congo from 1960.
Gécamines
The state mining company created in 1967 out of the Union Minière's Katanga assets after Mobutu nationalised them.
Kolwezi
The mining town in Katanga where Tshombe announced the end of the secession in January 1963, and which French and Belgian paratroops recaptured in 1978.
Zaire
The name Mobutu gave the Congo in 1971, used until his overthrow in 1997.
