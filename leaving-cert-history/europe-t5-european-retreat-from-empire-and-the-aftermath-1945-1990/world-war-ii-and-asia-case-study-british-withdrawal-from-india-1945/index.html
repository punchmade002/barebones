Japanese conquests and the loss of European prestige in Asia, 1941–42

The defeat of the European colonial powers by Japan weakened their claim to rule in Asia and gave nationalists new arguments.

  • Japan attacked Pearl Harbor and invaded British Malaya on 7–8 December 1941; Hong Kong surrendered on 25 December 1941.
  • Singapore, Britain's main base in the East, surrendered on 15 February 1942; about 80,000 British, Indian and Australian troops became prisoners of war.
  • Japan overran Burma in 1942, closing the Burma Road that supplied China and threatening India's eastern frontier.
  • The Dutch East Indies fell to Japan in early 1942, ending Dutch rule there until the Japanese surrender in August 1945.
  • Japan moved troops into northern Indochina in 1940 and took full control from the French colonial administration on 9 March 1945.
  • Japanese propaganda promoted a ‘Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere’ and ‘Asia for the Asians’, which encouraged anti-colonial nationalists across the region.
  • About 2.5 million men served in the British Indian Army during the war, and the army expanded greatly as a result.
  • Subhas Chandra Bose, a former Congress president, raised the Indian National Army in 1942-43 from Indian prisoners captured at Singapore to fight alongside Japan; the British trials of INA officers at the Red Fort in Delhi in 1945-46 caused a public outcry and made it politically risky to punish them.
  • The Bengal famine of 1943, in which an estimated two to three million people died, damaged British prestige in India; wartime requisitioning and the loss of Burmese rice imports contributed to it.

Indian National Congress and Gandhi's mass campaigns, 1915–1942

The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, moved from petitioning to mass civil disobedience under Mohandas Gandhi.

  • Gandhi returned from South Africa in 1915 and became the dominant Congress leader after 1920.
  • Satyagraha, meaning ‘truth-force’, was non-violent civil disobedience; Gandhi first used it in peasant campaigns at Champaran (1917) and Kheda (1918).
  • The Amritsar massacre of 13 April 1919 at Jallianwala Bagh, when British-Indian troops fired on a crowd, killed hundreds and turned many Indians against British rule.
  • The Non-Cooperation movement of 1920–22 urged boycotts of British goods, courts, schools and councils; Gandhi called it off after the Chauri Chaura incident of February 1922.
  • The Salt March of 12 March to 6 April 1930 covered about 240 miles to the coast at Dandi, where Gandhi made salt in defiance of the British salt monopoly.
  • The Government of India Act 1935 gave Indians self-government in the provinces; in the 1937 elections Congress won outright majorities in five of the eleven provinces and went on to form governments in most of them.
  • In October–November 1939 Congress ministers resigned in protest at the Viceroy declaring war on India's behalf without consulting Indian leaders.
  • On 8 August 1942 Congress passed the Quit India resolution demanding British withdrawal; Gandhi and most leaders were arrested, and widespread unrest followed until Gandhi's release in May 1944.
  • The Cripps Mission of March 1942 offered India dominion status after the war, with the right to secede; Congress rejected it because it allowed provinces to opt out and gave no immediate control of defence, and it was withdrawn.

The Muslim League and the demand for Pakistan

Partition grew out of the Muslim League's campaign for separate Muslim states, which the Congress leadership would not accept for the whole of India.

  • The All-India Muslim League was founded at Dhaka in 1906 to protect Muslim political interests.
  • Muhammad Ali Jinnah, a barrister who had once been a Congress member, led the League from 1934 and was known as Quaid-i-Azam (great leader).
  • The Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940 called for independent states in the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and east of India.
  • Jinnah argued that Hindus and Muslims were two nations, and that Congress claimed to speak for all Indians while representing mainly Hindus.
  • In the 1945–46 elections the League won most of the seats reserved for Muslims in the central and provincial assemblies, which gave it a claim to speak for Muslim India.
  • Punjab and Bengal, each with a large Muslim population but a large non-Muslim minority, became the central problem in the partition settlement.
  • The name Pakistan was coined in 1933 by the student Choudhry Rahmat Ali; the League did poorly among Muslim voters in the 1937 elections but used the years of Congress rule to claim that Muslims could not be safe in a Hindu-majority state, and Jinnah called a Day of Deliverance on 22 December 1939 when the Congress ministries resigned.

Labour government and the decision to leave, 1945–1947

Britain's exhausted post-war economy and Labour's commitment to independence set the timing of withdrawal.

  • Clement Attlee's Labour Party won a landslide in the general election of July 1945, and Attlee became prime minister on 26 July 1945.
  • Britain had spent heavily on the war; it borrowed about 3.75 billion dollars from the United States in 1945-46, and its debts and the cost of occupation left it unable to hold a large army in India.
  • In February 1946 ratings of the Royal Indian Navy mutinied at Bombay over pay and conditions; the mutiny was suppressed, but it showed that the armed forces could no longer be relied on to support British rule.
  • The Cabinet Mission of March–June 1946 proposed a loose federal India with groups of provinces; it failed to win agreement between Congress and the League.
  • The League called Direct Action Day for 16 August 1946; communal killings in Calcutta left thousands dead and many more injured.
  • On 20 February 1947 Attlee announced that British rule would end by June 1948 at the latest, and Lord Wavell was replaced as Viceroy by Lord Mountbatten.
  • Mountbatten's plan of 3 June 1947 accepted partition, and the date was brought forward to 15 August 1947 once the principal Indian leaders agreed.
  • The Simla Conference of June-July 1945 broke down when Jinnah insisted that only the League could name the Muslim members of the Viceroy's council.
  • After the Cabinet Mission failed, an interim government headed by Jawaharlal Nehru took office in September 1946 and the League joined it in October; the two parties still could not work together.
  • Lord Mountbatten arrived as Viceroy on 22 March 1947; he judged that a united India was no longer possible and pressed for a quick transfer of power.
  • Under the 3 June 1947 plan the assemblies of Punjab and Bengal voted on dividing their provinces, and a referendum in the North-West Frontier Province and Sylhet decided their future.

Independence and partition, August 1947

Independence on 15 August 1947 created two dominions, India and Pakistan, and divided the Punjab and Bengal.

  • The Indian Independence Act, passed by the British Parliament in July 1947, ended British rule and made India and Pakistan independent dominions.
  • Pakistan was formed in two parts, West Pakistan and East Bengal, separated by about 1,600 km of Indian territory.
  • Cyril Radcliffe, a barrister who had never visited India, drew the Punjab and Bengal boundaries; the Radcliffe Line was announced on 17 August 1947, two days after independence.
  • Millions crossed the new border as Hindus and Sikhs moved from West Pakistan into India and Muslims moved into Pakistan; estimates of those displaced range from about 10 to 15 million.
  • Communal violence in the Punjab and Bengal killed many people; estimates of the death toll range from about 200,000 to about 2 million.
  • The princely states, over 500 in number, had to join India or Pakistan; the Nizam of Hyderabad and the Maharaja of Kashmir were the most contested cases.
  • The Maharaja of Kashmir signed an Instrument of Accession to India on 26 October 1947, and fighting over Kashmir between India and Pakistan began that year.

Aftermath: Gandhi's death and the Commonwealth settlement

Independence did not end the conflicts that partition created, and India chose to remain in the Commonwealth.

  • Gandhi had fasted in Calcutta and Delhi in 1947–48 to stop communal violence; he was shot dead in Delhi on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist.
  • The first Indo-Pakistani war over Kashmir ended with a ceasefire from 1 January 1949, leaving a line that became a long-term division of the state.
  • Jinnah became Governor-General of Pakistan and died in September 1948.
  • The London Declaration of April 1949 allowed India to become a republic while remaining a member of the Commonwealth, with the King recognised as head of the association.
  • India became a republic on 26 January 1950, with Jawaharlal Nehru as prime minister.
  • Independent India followed non-alignment under Nehru and remained a major trading partner of Britain, with the sterling area linking the two economies.

Significance of the withdrawal for Britain and India

  • India was the largest and most valuable of the British possessions; its loss ended the British Raj, which had ruled India directly since 1858.
  • Burma and Ceylon became independent in 1948, and the Gold Coast became independent as Ghana on 6 March 1957, the first British colony in tropical Africa to do so.
  • The partition of the Indian Army, the civil service and the railways between the two dominions had to be done in a few weeks, causing administrative strain.
  • For Britain, the loss of India reduced its role in Asia and made the Commonwealth its main continuing link with former colonies.
  • India's decision to remain in the Commonwealth as a republic established that membership did not depend on accepting the monarch as head of state.
  • The war with Japan had left the British Indian Army the largest volunteer force in history, but its loyalty could not be taken for granted after the INA trials and the 1946 naval mutiny.

Why Britain left India: explanations

  • Nationalist explanation: decades of Congress agitation, from non-cooperation to Quit India, made British rule impossible to maintain without a degree of repression the British public would not accept.
  • Economic explanation: By 1945 Britain owed India over one billion pounds in sterling balances for wartime supplies, which reversed the old financial relationship and reduced the economic value of the Raj.
  • Political explanation: Labour's 1945 government was committed to Indian self-rule, and Attlee argued that Britain could not hold India against the will of its people.
  • Security explanation: the INA trials and the 1946 naval mutiny led some British officials, including Viceroy Wavell, to doubt that the Indian Army and police could be relied on to suppress a mass rising.
  • Communal explanation: the failure of Congress and the League to agree, and the violence in Calcutta, Bihar and the Punjab, led Mountbatten to fear civil war if he delayed.
  • Mountbatten's critics argue that bringing the date forward by ten months left too little time to plan the boundary and policing; his defenders argue that delay would have meant more violence.

Key personality: Mohandas Gandhi

Gandhi was the leading figure of the Indian independence movement and the most influential Indian voice in the British withdrawal.

  • Born 2 October 1869 at Porbandar in Gujarat, he trained as a barrister in London and practised law in South Africa from 1893 to 1914.
  • In South Africa he led campaigns against discriminatory laws affecting Indians, and developed the method of non-violent resistance that he later called satyagraha.
  • After 1915 he built a mass movement among peasants and urban workers, wore simple dress and promoted hand-spinning (khadi) as a symbol of self-reliance.
  • At the London Round Table Conference of 1931 he was Congress's only representative; the talks failed over separate electorates for minorities, which B. R. Ambedkar demanded for Dalits.
  • He was a participant in every major Congress campaign and was imprisoned several times, including from August 1942 to May 1944.
  • Contemporaries saw him in very different ways: many Indians revered him as the Mahatma, British officials often treated him as an obstacle, and Jinnah accused him of speaking for Hindus.
  • Historians differ on his impact: Judith Brown and others stress his skill in turning Congress into a mass movement, while others argue that war exhaustion, the INA and naval unrest and Labour's policy did more to end the Raj; critics point to his limited success on caste and his failure to prevent partition, and B. R. Ambedkar was among his sharpest contemporary critics.
  • Gandhi's stated aims were swaraj (self-rule), Hindu-Muslim unity and the end of untouchability; in Hind Swaraj (1909) he rejected industrial civilisation, and he called the untouchables Harijans.
  • After Ambedkar won separate electorates for Dalits in the 1932 Communal Award, Gandhi fasted in Yerwada jail and the Poona Pact (September 1932) replaced them with reserved seats in general electorates.
  • In late 1946 he walked through villages in Noakhali in East Bengal to calm communal violence, and he was in Calcutta on 15 August 1947 fasting rather than celebrating independence.

Assessment: achievements and limits of the independence struggle

  • Achievement: non-violent mass protest became the central method of Indian politics, and campaigns such as the Salt March drew in very large numbers of people.
  • Limit: the Gandhian campaigns did not secure a united India, and partition and the violence that followed were not prevented.
  • Achievement: the Quit India movement and the 1942 unrest showed the British that their authority could not be maintained by force alone.
  • Limit: the decision to accept partition was taken by the Congress and League leadership in June 1947, not by Gandhi alone, and Gandhi publicly opposed partition.
  • Achievement: the transfer of power ended British rule without a colonial war of the kind France fought in Indochina and Algeria.
  • Limit: British withdrawal was rushed in 1947, and the boundary settlement was made within weeks, which left the population to face the consequences.
  • Limit: the Gandhian method relied on the moral restraint of the British government and on disciplined followers, and it did not stop the communal killings that followed Direct Action Day in 1946.
  • Later view: many historians judge the speed of withdrawal and the Radcliffe boundary commission's rushed work as major contributors to the scale of the violence.

Key terms

Colonial rule
The control of a territory by an external power that governed it for the benefit of the imperial state, often without the consent of its people.
De-colonisation
The process by which colonies gained independence from their European rulers, usually between 1945 and the 1960s in Asia and Africa.
British Commonwealth
The association of Britain and its former colonies and dominions, which by 1949 allowed republics such as India to remain members.
Satyagraha
Gandhi's method of non-violent civil disobedience, based on the idea of truth-force, used to challenge unjust laws.
Partition
The division of British India into the separate states of India and Pakistan at independence in August 1947.
Muslim League
The political party founded in 1906 that campaigned for Muslim political rights and, from 1940, for a separate Muslim state.
Cabinet Mission
The British delegation of 1946 that proposed a federal settlement for India, which failed to gain the agreement of the Congress and the League.
Dominion status
The status of a self-governing member of the British Commonwealth, with its own parliament, while the British monarch remained head of state.
Indian National Congress
The nationalist party founded in 1885 that under Gandhi became a mass movement for self-rule and led the independence struggle.
Quit India
The Congress resolution of 8 August 1942 that demanded immediate British withdrawal and led to mass arrests and unrest.
Raj
The period of direct British rule in India, from 1858 to 1947.
