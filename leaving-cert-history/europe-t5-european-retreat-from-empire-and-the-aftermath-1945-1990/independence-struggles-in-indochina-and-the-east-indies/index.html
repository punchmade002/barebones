French Indochina and the Japanese occupation, 1887–1945

France ruled Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos as French Indochina; the Japanese occupation of 1940–45 weakened French authority and gave the nationalists their chance.

  • French Indochina was formed as a federation in 1887 from Tonkin, Annam, Cochinchina and Cambodia, and Laos was added in 1893.
  • The French ruled through a colonial administration headed by a governor-general and kept the Vietnamese emperor in place as a figurehead at Hue; Cochinchina was a direct colony and Tonkin and Annam were protectorates.
  • Japan moved troops into northern Indochina in 1940 under an arrangement with the Vichy French administration, and Japanese forces took full control on 9 March 1945.
  • The Viet Minh (League for the Independence of Vietnam) was founded in May 1941 under Ho Chi Minh, combining communists and non-communist nationalists.
  • A famine in northern Vietnam in 1944–45 killed many people; estimates range from several hundred thousand to about two million.
  • The Viet Minh seized power in Hanoi and other towns in August 1945, and Emperor Bảo Đại abdicated on 25 August 1945.
  • Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh fought the Japanese from the hills of northern Vietnam and received some American supplies and training from the OSS in 1945.

Vietnam: declaration of independence and the return of France, 1945–1946

  • Ho Chi Minh proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Vietnam in Hanoi on 2 September 1945, quoting the United States Declaration of Independence.
  • Chinese Nationalist forces occupied the north and British forces the south after the Japanese surrender, and British troops helped the French return to Saigon in September 1945.
  • Under the Franco-Vietnamese agreement of 6 March 1946, France recognised the Democratic Republic as a free state within the French Union, but left the question of Cochinchina unresolved.
  • The French Union was set up under the French constitution of 1946 as a federation of France, its overseas territories and associated states.
  • Fighting broke out in Hanoi on 19 December 1946, starting the First Indochina War between France and the Viet Minh.
  • Ho Chi Minh went to France for the Fontainebleau conference in mid-1946 and signed a temporary agreement in September, but the talks failed; a French naval bombardment of Haiphong in November 1946 killed many civilians and made war likely.
  • Ho Chi Minh wrote several appeals to President Truman in 1945-46 asking for American support, and received no reply.

The First Indochina War and the Geneva settlement, 1946–1954

The French tried to defeat the Viet Minh with a French-backed Vietnamese state, but the war ended in defeat at Dien Bien Phu.

  • France set up a State of Vietnam under Bảo Đại in 1949 as an alternative to the Democratic Republic, and it was recognised by Western states.
  • After the Chinese communist victory of 1949, China supplied the Viet Minh with weapons and military advisers from 1950, while the United States financed much of France's war effort.
  • The French commander Henri Navarre set out to force a decisive battle in 1953, and he fortified the valley at Dien Bien Phu in north-west Vietnam.
  • The siege of Dien Bien Phu ran from 13 March to 7 May 1954; the Viet Minh commander Vo Nguyen Giap used artillery and supply routes to overrun the French garrison.
  • The Geneva Conference of April–July 1954 produced the Geneva Accords, signed on 21 July 1954, which divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel pending elections in 1956.
  • The planned 1956 elections were never held, and Ngô Đình Diem became premier of the South in 1954 and president in 1955 with United States backing.
  • Laos and Cambodia gained independence from France in 1953; in Cambodia King Norodom Sihanouk's 'royal crusade' pressed France for independence, and in Laos the communist Pathet Lao, allied to the Viet Minh, held the north-east and fought the royal government after 1954.
  • Dien Bien Phu ended with more than 10,000 French Union troops captured; the news reached the Geneva Conference the day after the surrender, and the new French prime minister Pierre Mendes France had promised to reach a settlement within a month of taking office in June 1954.
  • France withdrew its forces from the south by 1956, and the United States replaced it as the main outside power in South Vietnam.
  • Ho Chi Minh's government in the north was recognised by China and the Soviet Union; his land reform of 1953-56 was violent and was followed by a public admission of errors.

The Dutch East Indies: occupation and the independence proclamation, 1942–1945

The Japanese occupation ended Dutch control and allowed Indonesian nationalists to build organisations and armed forces.

  • The Japanese occupation of 1942-45 ended Dutch rule, drew heavily on Indonesian forced labourers known as romusha, and left Indonesians in senior positions that they had never held under the Dutch.
  • The Japanese trained Indonesian forces, such as the PETA volunteer army, and allowed nationalists such as Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta to speak publicly.
  • On 1 June 1945 Sukarno gave a speech setting out the Pancasila, five principles on which an independent Indonesia would be based.
  • Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed Indonesian independence in Jakarta on 17 August 1945, a few days after Japan announced its surrender.
  • Dutch forces returned with Allied support in late 1945; Indonesian and British troops fought in Surabaya in November 1945, and British forces withdrew in 1946.

The Dutch war and the transfer of sovereignty, 1946–1949

  • The Linggadjati Agreement of 15 November 1946 recognised the Republic's authority over Java, Madura and Sumatra and provided for a United States of Indonesia in union with the Netherlands; the Dutch soon reinterpreted it.
  • The first Dutch ‘police action’ began in July 1947 and occupied much of the Indonesian-held territory in Java and Sumatra.
  • The UN Security Council called for a ceasefire in August 1947 and set up a Good Offices Committee; the Renville Agreement of January 1948 was negotiated aboard the US ship Renville and left Dutch forces holding large parts of Java and Sumatra.
  • The second police action of 19 December 1948 captured Yogyakarta, the Republic's capital at the time, and imprisoned Sukarno and Hatta; a government in exile on Sumatra continued the resistance.
  • United States pressure on the Netherlands, which included threats over Marshall Plan aid, helped bring the Dutch to the negotiating table.
  • The Round Table Conference at The Hague in 1949 led to the transfer of sovereignty on 27 December 1949 to the United States of Indonesia.
  • The Dutch kept West New Guinea until 1962, when it passed to a UN administration, and it was transferred to Indonesia on 1 May 1963.
  • Sukarno's government had suppressed a communist rising at Madiun in September 1948, which helped to win American sympathy for the Republic before the second action.
  • At The Hague Indonesia agreed to take over about 4.3 billion guilders of Dutch East Indies debt, and the Netherlands retained West New Guinea for later negotiation.
  • The Netherlands-Indonesian Union created in 1949 was a weak association; Indonesia abolished it unilaterally in 1954.

Sukarno's Indonesia, 1950–1967

  • The Asian–African Conference at Bandung in April 1955 brought together 29 countries; it promoted non-alignment and anti-colonialism, and Sukarno was its most prominent host.
  • From 1957 Sukarno took over Dutch businesses in Indonesia, and the Dutch-owned enterprises were placed under Indonesian control.
  • Sukarno introduced ‘Guided Democracy’ by decree on 5 July 1959, returning to the 1945 constitution and ruling with a National Front and the army.
  • Konfrontasi (confrontation) from 1963 to 1966 was Indonesia's opposition to the new Federation of Malaysia, including armed raids into Malaysian Borneo.
  • A UN-supervised 1969 vote, the Act of Free Choice, confirmed Indonesian rule in West New Guinea, though it was held among about 1,000 chosen representatives and has been widely criticised.
  • Sukarno withdrew Indonesia from the United Nations in January 1965, and he moved towards closer relations with China.
  • After an attempted coup on 30 September 1965 in which six generals were killed, General Suharto took control of the army; anti-communist killings followed, with estimates ranging from about 500,000 to one million or more.
  • Sukarno was stripped of real power by the Supersemar order of 11 March 1966 and placed under house arrest; Suharto became acting president in 1967 and president in 1968, and Sukarno died in June 1970.
  • The first free national election, in September 1955, left four parties of similar size (PNI, Masjumi, Nahdlatul Ulama and the PKI), and unstable coalition cabinets followed.
  • Regional rebellions in 1957-58, the PRRI in Sumatra and Permesta in Sulawesi, protested against Java's domination and were put down by the army, strengthening the army's political role.
  • Sukarno had himself named president for life in 1963 and promoted 'Nasakom', the union of nationalism, religion and communism; the PKI, with about three million members by the mid-1960s, was the largest communist party outside the communist states, and the army distrusted it.
  • Economic decline in the early 1960s, with high inflation and heavy spending on Konfrontasi, weakened his position before 1965.

Key personality: Ho Chi Minh

Ho Chi Minh led the Vietnamese independence movement from the 1920s and was the head of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam.

  • Born Nguyen Sinh Cung in 1890 in central Vietnam, he left Vietnam in 1911 and spent about thirty years abroad, including in France, Britain, the Soviet Union and China, using the name Nguyen Ai Quoc before adopting Ho Chi Minh in 1942.
  • In 1919 he presented a petition for Vietnamese rights to the Versailles peace conference, which was ignored by the Allied powers.
  • He joined the French Communist Party in 1920 and worked with the Communist International, and in 1930 he helped found the Indochinese Communist Party.
  • He was a participant in the 1945 August Revolution and proclaimed the Democratic Republic on 2 September 1945, and he led the government through the war against France.
  • Contemporaries in France and the United States saw him as a communist agitator; many Vietnamese nationalists saw him as the leader who had made independence possible.
  • He died in September 1969, before the end of the Vietnam War, and his government took control of the whole country in 1975.
  • Historians debate whether his nationalism or his communism was the stronger force in his politics, and they also debate the role of the Soviet Union and China in supporting him.
  • He returned to Vietnam in 1941 and founded the Viet Minh; he was held by the Chinese Nationalists in 1942-43, and he led the First Indochina War as president, with Vo Nguyen Giap commanding the army.
  • He was known as Uncle Ho; after the 1954 division he led North Vietnam, and in the north the Lao Dong (Workers') Party ran the state with Soviet and Chinese aid.

Key personality: Achmad Sukarno

Sukarno was the first president of Indonesia and the central figure in the independence struggle against the Dutch.

  • Born 6 June 1901 in East Java, he trained as an engineer and founded the Indonesian Nationalist Party (PNI) in 1927.
  • The Dutch imprisoned him in 1929 and released him in 1931; they later exiled him to remote islands, where he remained until the Japanese occupation.
  • Under the Japanese occupation he worked with the Japanese administration and encouraged mass mobilisation, which some Dutch and later Indonesian critics saw as collaboration.
  • He was a participant in the proclamation of 17 August 1945 and became the first president of the Republic of Indonesia.
  • Contemporaries were divided: Indonesians called him Bung Karno (brother Karno) and the father of the nation, while Dutch officials treated him as a collaborator with Japan and the army and the Islamic parties came to see him as too close to the communists.
  • His later rule is judged harshly by many historians: Guided Democracy ended parliamentary government, and the economy and foreign policy of confrontation left Indonesia isolated by 1965; others credit him with giving a diverse archipelago a national language, Indonesian, and a unifying ideology in the Pancasila.
  • He was never a leader of the Indonesian Communist Party but worked closely with it in the early 1960s, which caused suspicion in the army.
  • He was a gifted orator who studied engineering at Bandung (graduating in 1926); his Pancasila speech of 1 June 1945 showed his aim of balancing nationalists, Muslims and communists in one state.

Assessment: achievements and limits of the Indochinese and Indonesian independence movements

  • Achievement: Vietnam and Indonesia both defeated attempts by returning colonial powers to restore their old authority, Indonesia in 1949 and the Viet Minh in 1954.
  • Limit: Vietnam was divided in 1954, and the division became the basis of a larger war between the two Vietnamese states from the late 1950s.
  • Achievement: Indonesia gained sovereignty in 1949 after a diplomatic campaign that involved the UN and the United States, not only military action.
  • Limit: the Dutch police actions, the French war and the Geneva settlement all showed that European powers could resist independence for years.
  • Achievement: Sukarno's diplomacy at Bandung gave newly independent states a voice in world politics.
  • Limit: Guided Democracy and the economic decline of the 1960s ended in the 1965 coup, the killings that followed, and Suharto's rise to power.
  • Later view: Benedict Anderson argued that the Indonesian revolution of 1945 was driven by young pemuda activists, trained under the Japanese, as much as by leaders such as Sukarno and Hatta.
  • Limit: independence did not bring stable government, as Vietnam divided in 1954 and Indonesia moved from parliamentary democracy to authoritarian rule within a decade.

Key terms

Colonial rule
The control of a territory by an external power that governed it for the benefit of the imperial state.
De-colonisation
The process by which colonies gained independence from their European rulers, most of it after 1945.
French Union
The federation set up by the French constitution of 1946, linking metropolitan France with its overseas territories and associated states.
Viet Minh
The League for the Independence of Vietnam, founded in 1941 under Ho Chi Minh, which fought the French and the Japanese.
Guided Democracy
Sukarno's system from 1959, in which parliamentary democracy was replaced by presidential rule with the army and a National Front.
Police actions
The Dutch term for their military operations in Indonesia in 1947 and 1948, which were not treated as a war.
Konfrontasi
Indonesia's campaign of opposition to the formation of Malaysia, from 1963 to 1966, including armed incursions into Borneo.
Pancasila
The five principles set out by Sukarno on 1 June 1945 as the basis of an independent Indonesian state.
Nasakom
Sukarno's formula for uniting nationalism (nasionalisme), religion (agama) and communism (komunisme) in his Guided Democracy.
Geneva Accords
The 1954 agreements that ended the First Indochina War, divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel and recognised the independence of Laos and Cambodia.
Bandung Conference
The April 1955 meeting of 29 Asian and African states in Indonesia that promoted non-alignment and anti-colonial co-operation.
Supersemar
The order of 11 March 1966 that gave General Suharto authority to restore order and in practice removed Sukarno's power.
