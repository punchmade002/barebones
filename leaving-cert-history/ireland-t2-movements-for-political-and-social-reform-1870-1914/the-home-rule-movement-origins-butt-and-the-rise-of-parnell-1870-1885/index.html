Background: why Home Rule emerged around 1870

By 1870 Irish nationalism had tried repeal, rebellion and Fenian conspiracy without success. Home Rule offered a constitutional, moderate alternative that could attract Protestants as well as Catholics.

  • The Act of Union (1801) abolished the Irish parliament; Daniel O’Connell’s Repeal campaign failed in the 1840s and the Young Ireland rising of 1848 collapsed.
  • The Fenian (IRB) rising of 1867 was crushed, but the arrest and trial of Fenians produced a public movement for an amnesty for political prisoners in 1869, and Isaac Butt was among its leaders.
  • Gladstone became Prime Minister in December 1868 declaring that his mission was to pacify Ireland; he disestablished the Church of Ireland (1869) and passed a first Land Act (1870).
  • Those reforms failed to satisfy nationalists: the 1870 Act left tenants without real security, and many Irish MPs were Liberals who did not press for self-government.
  • Political agitation (organised public pressure through meetings, newspapers and electoral campaigns) was the method by which Home Rulers hoped to build a mass base, as O’Connell had done.
  • The electorate was small and unevenly spread: most Catholic tenant farmers had no vote, so early Home Rulers depended on small towns and a wealthy, often Protestant, professional class.

Isaac Butt, the Home Government Association and the Home Rule League (1870–1874)

Butt, a Protestant barrister and former Conservative MP, founded the movement in Dublin in 1870 and made federal Home Rule a parliamentary question.

  • Isaac Butt (1813–1879) was born in Co. Donegal, the son of a Church of Ireland rector, and became a Dublin barrister and professor of political economy at Trinity College.
  • He began as a Conservative and unionist opponent of O’Connell, but his defence of the Young Irelanders in 1848 and of Fenian prisoners in the 1860s turned him towards nationalism.
  • The Home Government Association was founded in Dublin in May 1870, bringing together Protestant Conservatives, Liberals, Repealers and Catholic moderates.
  • Butt’s programme was federal: an Irish parliament in Dublin dealing with domestic affairs, with the imperial parliament at Westminster keeping control of the empire, defence and foreign policy.
  • Butt won the Limerick City by-election in 1871 as a Home Ruler, which showed that the new demand could win Catholic votes.
  • The Ballot Act (1872) introduced the secret ballot, so landlords and agents could no longer watch how tenants voted; the Galway election petition of 1872 had exposed clerical pressure on voters.
  • A national conference in Dublin in November 1873 replaced the Association with the Home Rule League and committed Home Rule MPs to sit as an independent party.
  • Butt’s method was constitutional: persuasion, parliamentary motions and public meetings, with a rejection of physical force.

The 1874 election and why Butt’s leadership failed

The 1874 election produced the first sizeable Home Rule party, but Butt could not make it effective.

  • In the 1874 general election about 60 of the 103 Irish seats were won by Home Rulers (sources give 59 or 60), making them a significant bloc at Westminster.
  • Many of those MPs were Liberals who had added the label for local reasons and did not vote consistently with the party; the League had no pledge or whip to bind them.
  • Butt’s Home Rule motions in the 1870s were rejected by overwhelming majorities, and neither Disraeli’s Conservatives nor the Liberals took them seriously.
  • Butt’s approach depended on winning English sympathy through argument, and the Commons treated Irish business as a nuisance rather than a priority.
  • The party had little money or local organisation, so the movement remained a body of MPs and lawyers with few roots among the tenant farmers.
  • Younger members, including Joseph Biggar and John O’Connor Power, believed that politeness had failed and demanded more aggressive tactics.
  • Butt’s health and finances (he was chronically in debt) weakened his authority; he died on 5 May 1879, by which time his control had already slipped to the obstructionists.

Obstruction at Westminster (1875–1881)

Obstruction was the first tactic that forced the House of Commons to pay attention to Irish MPs.

  • Obstruction meant prolonged speeches, endless amendments and procedural objections designed to jam Commons business until the government gave way.
  • Joseph Biggar, a Belfast-born Presbyterian provision merchant and member of the IRB, was MP for Cavan from 1874 and began the practice.
  • Charles Stewart Parnell was elected MP for Meath in a by-election in April 1875 and joined Biggar’s obstruction from 1877, a famous South Africa Bill debate being an early example.
  • In 1877 Parnell, speaking of the Manchester Martyrs executed in 1867, said he did not believe any murder had been committed at Manchester, a deliberate break with Butt’s caution that won Fenian respect.
  • In August 1877 Parnell replaced Butt as president of the Home Rule Confederation of Great Britain, the body that organised Irish voters in English cities.
  • In January–February 1881 Irish MPs obstructed the coercion bill in a sitting of about 41 hours; the Speaker, Henry Brand, ended the debate on his own authority and the Commons later adopted the closure rule (1882).
  • The Liberal and Conservative leaderships both condemned obstruction as an abuse of parliament, and Butt himself deplored it, but it showed that a small, disciplined group could make the House of Commons unworkable.

The New Departure (1878–1879)

The New Departure linked three forces that had previously worked apart: Fenian-minded nationalists, constitutional Home Rulers and the land-reform movement.

  • The agricultural depression from 1877 (bad weather, falling prices and cheap American grain) brought a threat of evictions and a crisis in the countryside.
  • Michael Davitt, released from Dartmoor Prison in December 1877 after serving seven years of a fifteen-year sentence for Fenian arms-dealing, travelled to the USA in 1878 and met John Devoy of the Clan na Gael.
  • In October 1878 Devoy sent Parnell a telegram offering Clan na Gael support if he adopted a more active programme: a general demand for self-government, a drive for peasant ownership of land, and independent action by Irish MPs.
  • Parnell never formally accepted the offer, but he moved towards it, and his public alliance with Davitt created the working partnership known as the New Departure.
  • The New Departure committed Fenians to co-operate with a parliamentary party, which was a break with the IRB’s refusal to accept constitutional politics.
  • It also tied Home Rule to the land question, so that a tenant-farmer movement could supply the votes and funds for national politics.
  • Many in the IRB, including the Supreme Council, opposed co-operation, and the alliance was always uneasy, with Davitt favouring land nationalisation and Parnell peasant proprietorship.

Parnell takes control and builds a party machine (1879–1885)

Between 1879 and 1885 Parnell fused a land movement, a parliamentary party and a national organisation into a disciplined machine.

  • The Land League of Mayo (Irishtown meeting, April 1879) was extended into the Irish National Land League, founded in Dublin in October 1879 with Parnell as president.
  • Parnell and John Dillon toured the United States from January to March 1880, raising funds and addressing the US House of Representatives, which gave the movement American money and publicity.
  • At the general election of April 1880 about 60 Home Rulers were returned; Parnell was elected for three constituencies and chose Cork City, which he held until his death.
  • In May 1880 Parnell was elected chairman of the Irish Parliamentary Party, defeating William Shaw (23 votes to 18), and pledge-bound unity became the aim.
  • United Ireland, founded in 1881 and edited by William O’Brien, became the party’s weekly newspaper.
  • The Irish National League, founded in October 1882, replaced the suppressed Land League; it put Home Rule, land reform and local self-government first, and Parnell kept personal control of it.
  • In 1884 the party required its candidates to sign a pledge to sit, act and vote with the party, and constituency conventions (usually with clergy in attendance) chose candidates, ending the independence of the old Home Rule MPs.
  • Funding came from the Irish diaspora and local collections, and the 1883 Parnell Tribute raised about £37,000 for Parnell’s personal use despite the disapproval of the Vatican.
  • Parish branches of the National League gave the party an organisation in nearly every county, with priests often serving as chairs or treasurers.

Kilmainham, the Phoenix Park murders and the National League

Between October 1881 and May 1882 Parnell went from prison to a deal with the government, and the murders in Phoenix Park nearly wrecked it.

  • Gladstone’s Chief Secretary, W.E. Forster, introduced coercion in 1881, allowing detention without trial; the Land Act of August 1881 followed (see the notes on land agitation).
  • Parnell was arrested on 13 October 1881 and imprisoned in Kilmainham Gaol; from there he and other leaders issued the No Rent Manifesto, and the Land League was suppressed a week later.
  • Captain William O’Shea acted as go-between, and in April–May 1882 a settlement (nicknamed the Kilmainham Treaty by its critics) was reached: Parnell was released on 2 May and the government promised to deal with rent arrears.
  • In return Parnell indicated that he would use his influence to discourage outrages and co-operate with the Liberals; it was an unwritten understanding, not a treaty.
  • Forster resigned in protest at the release, and Lord Frederick Cavendish was appointed Chief Secretary.
  • On 6 May 1882, the day Cavendish arrived in Dublin, he and the Under-Secretary Thomas Henry Burke were stabbed to death in Phoenix Park by the Invincibles, a small IRB splinter group.
  • Parnell denounced the murders and offered to resign his seat, and Gladstone refused the offer; the government nevertheless introduced the Prevention of Crimes Act (1882).
  • Five Invincibles were hanged in 1883 after the informer James Carey gave evidence.
  • The Arrears Act (August 1882) wrote off arrears for poorer tenants, and the National League’s foundation in October 1882 refocused the movement on Home Rule.

Key personality: Charles Stewart Parnell (part 1: background and rise)

Parnell’s career is covered in two parts; this part runs from his background to the eve of the 1885 election.

  • Parnell was born at Avondale, Co. Wicklow, in 1846 into a Protestant landowning family; his mother, Delia Stewart, was American.
  • He studied at Magdalene College, Cambridge, and was High Sheriff of Wicklow in 1874, hardly an obvious future rebel.
  • He was elected for Meath in 1875, joined Biggar’s obstruction, became president of the Land League in 1879 and chairman of the Irish Parliamentary Party in 1880.
  • He was a direct participant: he organised the party, led the Land League, and negotiated with Gladstone and the Conservatives.
  • His speeches urged tenants to keep a firm grip on their homesteads (Westport, June 1879) and to isolate those who took evicted farms (Ennis, September 1880), phrases that Unionists later quoted against him.
  • Contemporary supporters admired his aloof, disciplined manner and his ability to hold together constitutionalists and Fenians; critics, including The Times, described him as a revolutionary in disguise.
  • Historians such as F.S.L. Lyons stress his political skill and his reliance on a loose alliance that was bound to be tested; others emphasise that he was a pragmatic parliamentarian, not a radical, who steered the Land War into a political settlement.

Assessment: Butt and Parnell compared

Judgement turns on whether the movement’s success owed more to Butt’s foundation or to Parnell’s machine.

  • Butt’s achievement was to found a national, respectable and constitutional movement and win about 60 seats in 1874; his limits were the absence of discipline, money and mass support, and the failure to win any concession.
  • Parnell’s achievement was to give the party discipline (the pledge, conventions), a press, American funding and a mass base through the Land League; his limit was his dependence on the agrarian movement and the Fenians, whom he could not fully control.
  • Butt thought the land question a distraction and accepted the 1870 Act, whereas Parnell made land the engine of the national movement.
  • Butt appealed to English opinion by argument; Parnell appealed to the threat of disruption, and by 1885 his party held the balance of power in the Commons.
  • Butt was a Protestant gentleman who lacked contact with tenant farmers; Parnell was also a Protestant landowner, but built networks that reached every parish.
  • Contemporaries saw Butt as an honourable failure and Parnell as a dangerous but effective leader; later historians generally accept that Butt prepared the ground and Parnell harvested it.
  • Kilmainham is read by some as a sensible deal that brought land reform and steered Parnell towards parliamentary politics, and by critics as an opportunistic retreat from the Land League’s aims.

Key terms

Home Rule
The demand for a parliament in Dublin to manage Ireland’s domestic affairs, with Westminster keeping control of the empire, defence and foreign policy.
Democracy
Government by the people through elected representatives; in this period it meant widening the franchise and protecting voters through the secret ballot.
Political agitation
Organised public pressure for change using meetings, newspapers, petitions, boycotts and parliamentary tactics rather than violence.
Home Government Association
The body founded by Isaac Butt in Dublin in 1870 to campaign for federal Home Rule by constitutional means.
Home Rule League
The organisation founded in November 1873 to replace the Association and bind Home Rule MPs into an independent parliamentary party.
Obstruction
A parliamentary tactic of prolonged speeches and amendments used to stop Commons business until the government gave way.
New Departure
The 1878–79 working alliance of Davitt, Devoy and Parnell linking Fenians, land reformers and constitutional nationalists.
Irish Parliamentary Party
The party of Irish Home Rule MPs, led by Parnell from 1880 and bound by a pledge to act together.
Kilmainham Treaty
The unwritten 1882 understanding between Parnell and Gladstone under which Parnell was released and the arrears question was addressed.
Irish National League
The organisation founded in October 1882 that succeeded the Land League and became Parnell’s national party machine.
Federalism
Butt’s model of Home Rule, in which Ireland would have its own parliament for domestic matters while remaining within the United Kingdom and empire.
