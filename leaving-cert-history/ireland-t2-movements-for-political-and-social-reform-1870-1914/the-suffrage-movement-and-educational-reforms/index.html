Women’s rights and the early suffrage societies (1870–1900)

Women’s suffrage began in Ireland as a small, respectable campaign among middle-class Protestant and Quaker women, linked to property rights, education and local government.

  • Women had no parliamentary vote and, under common law, a married woman’s property belonged to her husband; Married Women’s Property Acts (1870 and 1882) improved this, and Isabella Tod campaigned for them.
  • Isabella Tod organised a suffrage society in Belfast in the early 1870s (accounts give 1871 to 1873), the first in Ulster.
  • Anna Haslam, a Quaker, and her husband Thomas founded the Dublin Women’s Suffrage Association in 1876; it later became the Irish Women’s Suffrage and Local Government Association.
  • The early societies petitioned MPs, held drawing-room meetings and sent speakers, and they worked alongside the campaign for girls’ education and against the Contagious Diseases Acts.
  • Women ratepayers won the municipal vote in 1887, and women were first elected as poor law guardians in 1896.
  • The Local Government (Ireland) Act of 1898 gave women the vote in county and district council elections and the right to stand for district councils and boards of guardians, though not for county councils (opened by an Act of 1911).
  • Several prominent Irish suffragists, such as Tod, were unionists, while others supported Home Rule, so the movement avoided taking sides on it for decades.
  • The King and Queen’s College of Physicians in Dublin began licensing women in 1877, the first body in the United Kingdom to do so, opening medicine to Irish women.
  • Anna Haslam (1829–1922) led the Dublin society for over forty years and lived to see women gain the vote.

Key personality: Isabella Tod

Tod was a Belfast Presbyterian reformer who campaigned on women’s education, property and votes within a unionist outlook.

  • Isabella Tod (1836–1896) was born in Edinburgh and settled in Belfast, where she lived until her death.
  • She was a participant in the campaigns that led to the Married Women’s Property Act (1882) and to the inclusion of girls in the Intermediate Education Act (1878) and the Royal University’s charter (1879).
  • She helped found the Ladies’ Institute in Belfast (1867), a society for girls’ education, and worked with Margaret Byers of the Ladies’ Collegiate School (later Victoria College).
  • She co-founded the Belfast Women’s Temperance Association (1874) and campaigned against the Contagious Diseases Acts.
  • As a Liberal Unionist she opposed Home Rule in 1886 and urged women to oppose it; she helped found the Irish Women’s Liberal Unionist Association.
  • Contemporaries respected her as a public speaker and organiser, though her unionism limited her influence among nationalists.
  • Historians present her as a pioneer who created the first sustained suffrage organisation in Ulster and broke the exclusion of women from Irish public debate.

Suffrage organisations and the Irish Party (1900–1912)

Suffrage groups multiplied after 1900, but the Irish Party refused to back the cause.

  • The Irish Women’s Suffrage Federation, founded in 1911 under Louie Bennett and others, linked local societies and used peaceful methods.
  • The Irish Women’s Franchise League (1908) was more radical, and argued that no Home Rule settlement was legitimate without votes for women.
  • In Westminster, a Conciliation Bill proposing a limited vote for women was defeated in March 1912, with the Irish Party mostly voting against.
  • Redmond and Dillon feared that a franchise bill would complicate the passage of Home Rule, and Redmond personally opposed votes for women.
  • Suffragists tried to have an amendment in favour of women’s votes added to the Third Home Rule Bill, which the Irish Party and the government opposed.
  • Suffragists generally split on the national question: some, like Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington, argued that independence would be incomplete without women’s votes; others, notably in Ulster, were unionists.

The Irish Women’s Franchise League, the Irish Citizen and militancy (1908–1914)

From 1908 the League brought militant methods to Irish politics, and its campaign peaked in 1912–13.

  • The Irish Women’s Franchise League was founded in Dublin in 1908 by Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington, Margaret Cousins, her husband James Cousins and Francis Sheehy-Skeffington; its aim was votes for women on the same terms as men.
  • The League published the weekly Irish Citizen from May 1912 (edited at first by Francis Sheehy-Skeffington and James Cousins), which ran until 1920.
  • In June 1912 eight League members, including Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington and Margaret Cousins, were arrested for breaking windows at government buildings in Dublin, and were imprisoned.
  • During Asquith’s visit to Dublin in July 1912, English suffragettes of the WSPU disrupted his visit, and Mary Leigh threw a hatchet into the carriage in which he and Redmond were travelling.
  • Imprisoned suffragettes went on hunger strike, and the English prisoners were force-fed in Mountjoy, which provoked public protest.
  • Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington was jailed again in 1913 and released after a hunger strike under the Cat and Mouse Act.
  • Hostile crowds attacked suffragettes at public meetings, and many nationalists and trade unionists considered the militant tactics counter-productive.

Key personality: Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington

Sheehy-Skeffington was the leading Irish militant suffragist, who tied women’s rights to nationalism and socialism.

  • Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington (1877–1946) was born in Kanturk, Co. Cork, the daughter of the nationalist MP David Sheehy, and took an MA from the Royal University in 1902.
  • She married the pacifist and feminist Francis Skeffington in 1903, and they each added the other’s surname.
  • She co-founded the Irish Women’s Franchise League in 1908, was imprisoned for window-breaking in 1912 and in 1913, and was a contributor and later editor of the Irish Citizen.
  • She was a participant, and her organisation shaped how the issue was seen in Ireland, but she was also a critic of the Irish Party and later of Cumann na mBan, which she saw as subordinating women to men.
  • Her husband was shot without trial by Captain Bowen-Colthurst in April 1916, and she campaigned for an inquiry and refused compensation.
  • Contemporaries admired her courage but many nationalists thought her tactics divisive; unionists and the Irish Party saw her as a nuisance.
  • Historians now see her as a key figure of Irish feminism who insisted that independence must include equality.

Suffrage, nationalism and unionism

The suffrage cause was entangled with the national question, and women in each camp chose their side.

  • Unionist women in Ulster signed their own declaration on Ulster Day in September 1912, around 234,000 of them, and had a public role in unionism; suffragists such as Tod had been unionists.
  • Nationalist women formed Cumann na mBan in April 1914 to support the Volunteers; suffragists such as Sheehy-Skeffington objected to its auxiliary status.
  • Many suffragists argued that women should not be asked to postpone their claim until Home Rule or the Union had been settled.
  • The Irish Citizen pursued both aims, and it attracted writers from different political groups, from the pacifist movement to the trade unions.
  • Votes for women were granted in 1918 (women over thirty with property qualifications), after the First World War, and the Irish Party that had opposed the cause had by then been destroyed.

Intermediate and primary education reforms

State funding of secondary schools began in 1878 and used examinations to decide payment; primary education was reformed in 1900.

  • The Intermediate Education (Ireland) Act (1878) set up an Intermediate Board that held public examinations (junior, middle and senior grades) for boys and girls from 1879.
  • Schools were paid results fees according to how many pupils passed, a system known as payment by results; prizes and exhibitions rewarded top candidates.
  • The system financed denominational schools without interfering in their management, so Catholic, Protestant and convent schools all grew.
  • Girls’ schools used the examinations to compete with boys; Alexandra College (Dublin, 1866), Victoria College (Belfast) and the Loreto and Dominican convent schools all prepared girls.
  • Critics said payment by results encouraged cramming, narrowed the curriculum to examinable subjects and neglected Irish and science.
  • National school teachers had been paid by results since the 1870s; the Revised Programme of 1900 ended this, broadening the curriculum to include drawing, practical work, nature study and Irish as an ordinary subject within school hours.
  • The bilingual programme of 1904 encouraged teaching through Irish in Gaeltacht schools.
  • Victoria College in Belfast, founded by Margaret Byers in 1859 as the Ladies’ Collegiate School, and Alexandra College, founded in 1866, were pioneers of girls’ secondary education.

The Royal University and the Irish Universities Act (1879–1913)

The Royal University opened degrees to women and Catholics; the 1908 Act settled the university question with two new universities.

  • The Royal University of Ireland (1879) was an examining body that awarded degrees to students of any college or none; it replaced the old Queen’s University.
  • It admitted women to its examinations and degrees on equal terms, and the first women graduated in the early 1880s; Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington was among its later graduates.
  • Catholics complained that no university gave them a real Catholic institution; Trinity College Dublin was Protestant in ethos and the Catholic bishops forbade Catholics to attend it.
  • The Irish Universities Act (1908), prepared by Chief Secretary Augustine Birrell, dissolved the Royal University and created the National University of Ireland (constituent colleges at Dublin, Cork and Galway) and Queen’s University Belfast.
  • The NUI had a Catholic ethos, with Archbishop Walsh as first chancellor, and was open to women on equal terms.
  • The Gaelic League campaigned for compulsory Irish for matriculation: the NUI Senate decided in 1910 to require it, applied from 1913.

Assessment: the suffrage and education record

Reforms opened up public life to women and widened access to education, but the parliamentary vote came late and by a different route.

  • Achievement: by 1914 women could vote and stand in local elections, sit on boards of guardians, take degrees and run campaigns and newspapers.
  • Limit: Irish women had no parliamentary vote before 1918, none of the three Home Rule Bills gave them one, and militancy produced little change.
  • Achievement: the Intermediate system and the 1908 Act widened access to secondary and university education, especially for Catholics and girls.
  • Limit: payment by results encouraged cramming and the narrow curriculum, and denominational divisions over universities lasted for decades.
  • Contemporary Irish Party leaders saw suffrage as a distraction from Home Rule; suffragists saw it as a democratic right.
  • Historians stress that the movement was divided by nationalism and unionism, which weakened it, but that its organisation gave women experience used in later politics.

Key terms

Democracy
Government through elected representatives; suffragists argued that excluding women from the vote made Irish democracy incomplete.
Feminism
The belief in women’s equal rights and public roles, expressed here through the suffrage and education campaigns.
Suffragist
A campaigner for women’s votes who used peaceful, constitutional methods such as petitions and meetings.
Suffragette
A militant campaigner for women’s votes who used direct action such as window-breaking and hunger strikes; the term was coined by the British press.
Irish Women’s Franchise League
The militant suffrage society founded in Dublin in 1908 by Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington and Margaret Cousins.
Irish Citizen
The weekly suffrage newspaper founded in 1912 that ran until 1920.
Intermediate Education Act 1878
The Act that created a state-funded secondary system based on public examinations.
Payment by results
A system of funding in which schools were paid according to the number of pupils who passed examinations.
Royal University of Ireland
The examining and degree-awarding university of 1879 that opened its degrees to women and to students of any college.
National University of Ireland
The university created in 1908 with colleges at Dublin, Cork and Galway, regarded as acceptable to Catholics.
