Tenant grievances and the limits of the 1870 Land Act

Most Irish farmers were tenants at will or on short leases, and the law gave them little protection against eviction or rent increases.

  • Land was owned by a small landlord class, often Protestant and often absentee; most farmers rented their holdings, usually without a lease.
  • The tenants’ three demands became known as the Three Fs: fair rent, fixity of tenure and free sale of the tenant’s interest in the holding.
  • Ulster had the Ulster Custom, a tradition of tenant right that let outgoing tenants sell their interest; the 1870 Act gave it legal force there, though tenants elsewhere had no such protection.
  • Gladstone’s Land Act of 1870 offered compensation for improvements and for disturbance (eviction other than for non-payment of rent), but it did not fix rents.
  • Landlords could still raise rents beyond what tenants could pay and then evict for non-payment, which the Act did not forbid.
  • The purchase clauses (the Bright clauses) offered loans to tenants who wished to buy, but little land was sold under them.
  • The Act therefore left tenants without security of tenure, the main reason for the later land agitation (see also the earlier land legislation in the notes on Ireland before 1870).

The agricultural depression and the founding of the Land League (1877–1879)

A farming crisis turned long-standing grievances into a mass movement led by Davitt and Parnell.

  • From 1877 bad weather, poor harvests and cheap American grain brought falling prices; the wet summer of 1879 produced a partial potato failure.
  • Rent arrears mounted and evictions rose sharply: about 460 families were evicted in 1877 and more than 2,000 in 1880.
  • Michael Davitt, released from prison in 1877 and linked to Devoy through the New Departure, organised a meeting at Irishtown, Co. Mayo, on 20 April 1879 against a landlord’s high rents.
  • At Westport in June 1879 Parnell told tenants to keep a firm grip of their homesteads, his first major public identification with the agitation.
  • The Land League of Mayo was founded in August 1879, and the Irish National Land League was founded in Dublin in October 1879 with Parnell as president and Davitt as a leading organiser.
  • The League’s programme was to reduce rents and then to make tenants owners of their land (peasant proprietorship); Davitt personally favoured land nationalisation.
  • Funds came from Irish America, and Land League branches multiplied across the south and west in 1880.

The Land War: boycotting, the Ladies’ Land League and coercion (1880–1881)

The League fought evictions with collective action, and the government answered with coercion.

  • At Ennis in September 1880 Parnell urged that anyone who took a farm from which a tenant had been evicted should be shunned by the whole community.
  • The first victim was Captain Charles Boycott, agent for Lord Erne at Lough Mask, Co. Mayo, whose workers and tradesmen left him in 1880.
  • In November 1880 about fifty Ulster Orange labourers were brought in to save Boycott’s harvest, protected by around 1,000 soldiers and police; the cost of the operation became a national joke.
  • Moonlighting, the night-time maiming of cattle and attacks on property, accompanied the campaign, and Parnell’s opponents blamed the League.
  • The Ladies’ Land League, led in Dublin by Anna Parnell and formed in 1881, kept the campaign going when the men were jailed and helped evicted families with huts and relief.
  • Gladstone’s Chief Secretary, W.E. Forster, secured the Protection of Person and Property Act in March 1881, allowing the imprisonment of suspects without trial; hundreds of local leaders were jailed.
  • Parnell’s obstruction of the coercion bill in the Commons in early 1881 led to the introduction of closure.

The 1881 Land Act, the No Rent Manifesto and the Arrears Act

The 1881 Act met most of the League’s demands, but Parnell would not let the League accept it without testing it.

  • The Land Law (Ireland) Act (August 1881) granted the Three Fs: fair rents set by land courts (Land Commission), fixity of tenure while the tenant paid the rent, and free sale.
  • Fair rents were fixed by the courts for fifteen years, and the first rounds of judicial rents reduced rents by about one fifth on average.
  • The landlord kept ownership but shared control of the holding with the tenant, a system known as dual ownership.
  • Leaseholders and tenants in arrears were excluded at first, which left about a third of tenants outside its benefits until the Arrears Act of 1882.
  • Parnell adopted a policy of testing the Act by sending selected test cases to the land courts, in effect telling tenants to wait.
  • Gladstone denounced Parnell at Leeds on 7 October 1881; Parnell was arrested on 13 October and the League issued the No Rent Manifesto five days later.
  • The manifesto flopped (the Catholic hierarchy and many tenants rejected it) and the League was suppressed on 20 October 1881.
  • The Arrears Act (August 1882) cleared arrears of rent for tenants rated under £30, with the state paying part, as part of the Kilmainham settlement.

The Plan of Campaign (1886–1891)

When prices fell again in 1885–86, a new campaign sought rent reductions estate by estate.

  • The Plan of Campaign was launched in October 1886 in United Ireland by William O’Brien, John Dillon and Timothy Harrington.
  • Tenants on an estate demanded a reduction of rent; if the landlord refused, they paid the reduced amount into a fund controlled by trustees, which paid for the defence of evicted tenants.
  • It was applied on about 100 estates, including those of Lord Clanricarde in Co. Galway and Lord Massereene in Co. Louth.
  • Arthur Balfour, Chief Secretary from 1887, answered with the Criminal Law and Procedure (Ireland) Act (1887), allowing trial by magistrates without a jury for certain offences and the banning of organisations.
  • On 9 September 1887 police fired on a crowd at Mitchelstown, Co. Cork, killing three; nationalists called it a massacre and Balfour was nicknamed Bloody Balfour.
  • The Holy Office in Rome condemned the Plan of Campaign and boycotting in a decree of April 1888, an unwelcome intervention for nationalists.
  • Parnell was never close to the Plan: the Liberal alliance mattered more to him, and the movement was weakened by the Parnell split in 1890–91.

Constructive unionism: land purchase and the Congested Districts Board

Conservative governments tried to defuse the land question by funding tenants’ purchase of their farms, a policy called constructive unionism or killing Home Rule with kindness.

  • The Ashbourne Act (1885), passed by Salisbury’s government, advanced loans of the full purchase price to tenants who bought from willing landlords, repayable by annuities.
  • Balfour’s Land Purchase Act (1891) extended the funds for purchase and aimed to make sales faster.
  • The Congested Districts Board (1891) targeted poor areas of the west, buying and dividing estates, improving fisheries and promoting crafts and agriculture.
  • Constructive unionism assumed that economic reform would reduce nationalist feeling; critics have argued it made landlords richer while the problem of poor western farms remained.
  • Gerald Balfour’s Land Act (1896) made small changes to the purchase terms, though little changed in practice.

The Land Conference, the Wyndham Act and the Birrell Act (1902–1914)

A negotiated settlement between landlords and tenant leaders brought mass tenant purchase.

  • The Land Conference met in Dublin in December 1902, with Lord Dunraven (chair), Captain John Shawe-Taylor, William O’Brien and John Redmond, among others, and agreed a purchase scheme.
  • The Wyndham Land Act (1903), introduced by the Chief Secretary George Wyndham, offered tenants loans of the full price repayable over about 68 years at low interest.
  • Landlords were given a bonus of 12 per cent of the price as an inducement to sell.
  • More than 200,000 tenants bought farms under the Act within a few years, and the Treasury found the bonus costly; the terms were then revised.
  • The Evicted Tenants Act (1907) enabled reinstatement of tenants evicted in the Land War.
  • The Labourers (Ireland) Act (1906), a Liberal measure, provided for new cottages for farm labourers, with tens of thousands built by 1914.
  • The Birrell Land Act (1909), named for the Chief Secretary Augustine Birrell, allowed compulsory purchase where landlords would not sell and increased the powers of the Estates Commissioners.
  • By 1914 most tenants had bought, or were buying, their holdings, and landlordism had effectively ended as a political and social force.

Key personality: Michael Davitt

Davitt was the son of an evicted Mayo tenant who became a Fenian, then the organiser of the Land League and later a Labour voice.

  • Born in 1846 at Straide, Co. Mayo, he was evicted with his family in 1850 and grew up in Haslingden, Lancashire.
  • He lost his right arm in a cotton-mill accident at the age of eleven, and educated himself while working.
  • He joined the IRB, was arrested for arms trafficking in 1870 and sentenced to fifteen years; he was released on licence in December 1877 after about seven years, mostly at Dartmoor.
  • He founded the Land League in Mayo in 1879, travelled to the USA, and was the main organiser behind Parnell’s public leadership.
  • He was re-arrested in February 1881 and held at Portland until May 1882; his prison experience produced Leaves from a Prison Diary (1885).
  • He argued for land nationalisation and was dismayed that the League accepted peasant proprietorship; he rejected the use of violence against individuals.
  • In 1890 he opposed Parnell’s continued leadership after the O’Shea divorce, and he founded the newspaper Labour World the same year.
  • He resigned his seat in 1899 in protest against the Boer War and published The Fall of Feudalism in Ireland in 1904; he died in 1906.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a dangerous agitator (unionists) or a hero of the tenants (nationalists); historians now stress his social radicalism and his role in joining Fenians and constitutionalists.

Assessment: land agitation and land reform

The land campaign changed who owned Irish land, but it was less successful in solving rural poverty and in delivering Home Rule.

  • Achievement: agitation forced the state to concede the Three Fs in 1881 and then to finance the transfer of land from landlords to tenants by 1914.
  • Limit: the settlement was reached by state funding and compromise, not by land nationalisation; labourers and small western farmers gained least.
  • Achievement: the Land League mobilised a mass base and made Parnell’s party a national force.
  • Limit: the agitation depended on Parnell’s control, and violence, divisions over the Plan of Campaign and the papal condemnation weakened it.
  • Contemporary unionists claimed concessions were extracted by intimidation, while nationalists said that only pressure had moved Westminster.
  • Historians differ on whether constructive unionism worked: it removed a major grievance, but did not stop the growth of nationalism.
  • Davitt’s social vision (land nationalisation, labour rights) was more radical than Parnell’s, a contrast examiners ask students to draw.

Key terms

Political agitation
Organised public pressure for change, such as meetings, boycotts, rent campaigns and parliamentary tactics, used here to press the land question.
Three Fs
The tenants’ demands for fair rent, fixity of tenure and free sale of the tenant’s interest.
Land War
The period of mass agitation from 1879 to 1882, marked by rent strikes, boycotting, evictions and agrarian crime.
Boycotting
The social and economic isolation of a person who offended the tenants’ cause, named after Captain Charles Boycott in 1880.
Moonlighting
Night-time agrarian violence such as cattle maiming and attacks on property carried out in the Land War.
Dual ownership
The arrangement under the 1881 Act in which landlord and tenant both had legal interests in the same holding.
Plan of Campaign
The 1886 strategy by which tenants on an estate offered a reduced rent collectively and paid it into a fund if the landlord refused.
Constructive unionism
The Conservative policy of combining coercion with land purchase and economic reform to weaken support for Home Rule.
Tenant proprietorship
The system, reached by 1914, in which tenants became owner-occupiers by buying their land with state loans.
Congested Districts Board
The body set up in 1891 to relieve poverty in overcrowded western districts by improving land, fisheries and industries.
