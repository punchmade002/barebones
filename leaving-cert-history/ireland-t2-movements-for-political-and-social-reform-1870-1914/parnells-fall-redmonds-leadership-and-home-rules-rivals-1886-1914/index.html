The Plan of Campaign, Pigott and the Special Commission (1886–1890)

After 1886 Parnell kept the Liberal alliance and kept his distance from agrarian violence; his enemies tried to destroy him with forged evidence, and failed.

  • The Plan of Campaign (October 1886) was launched by O’Brien, Dillon and Harrington; Parnell did not endorse it and feared that evictions and violence would alarm British Liberals.
  • Parnell’s main concern was the Liberal alliance, and he spent much of 1887–89 away from Ireland and the daily running of the party.
  • In March 1887 The Times began a series called Parnellism and Crime, and on 18 April 1887 it printed a letter, supposedly signed by Parnell, that excused the Phoenix Park murders.
  • The letter was one of several forged by Richard Pigott, a discredited Dublin journalist who sold them to The Times.
  • The government set up a Special Commission of three judges in 1888 to examine the charges against Parnell and sixty-odd Irish MPs.
  • Under cross-examination in February 1889 Pigott collapsed; he had misspelled the word hesitancy in the same way as in the forged letter, and he fled to Madrid, where he shot himself on 1 March 1889.
  • Parnell was cheered in the Commons, visited Gladstone at Hawarden in December 1889, and won £5,000 in damages from The Times in 1890.
  • The Commission’s report (February 1890) rejected the forged letters, though it criticised the Land League’s methods; Parnell stood at the height of his reputation.

The O’Shea divorce and the split (1890–1891)

The divorce case of November 1890 turned the Liberal alliance and the Catholic hierarchy against Parnell and split the party.

  • Parnell had lived with Katharine O’Shea for years; her husband Captain William O’Shea, a former Irish Party MP, filed for divorce in December 1889 and named Parnell as co-respondent.
  • The case was heard on 15–17 November 1890; Parnell did not contest it and a decree nisi was granted.
  • On 25 November the Irish Party re-elected Parnell as chairman, before Gladstone’s letter to John Morley (saying Parnell’s leadership would make his own position untenable) was published.
  • Non-conformist Liberals threatened to desert Gladstone, and Gladstone told the Irish Party that he would not lead the Liberals if Parnell stayed.
  • On 3 December 1890 the Catholic bishops, led by Archbishop Walsh, condemned Parnell as unfit to lead.
  • Parnell issued a manifesto To the People of Ireland (29 November) which revealed details of private talks with Gladstone and attacked the Liberals, and it deepened the bitterness.
  • The debate in Committee Room 15 at Westminster lasted from 1 to 6 December 1890; on 6 December about 45 MPs led by Justin McCarthy walked out, leaving about 28 with Parnell.
  • The anti-Parnellites, later organised as the Irish National Federation, included Dillon, William O’Brien and the fierce critic Tim Healy; the Parnellites kept the National League.
  • Parnell lost by-elections in North Kilkenny (December 1890), North Sligo (April 1891) and Carlow (July 1891), campaigning in vicious conditions.
  • He married Katharine on 25 June 1891 and died at Brighton on 6 October 1891, aged 45; about 200,000 people are said to have followed his funeral to Glasnevin.

Key personality: Charles Stewart Parnell (part 2: 1886–1891 and overall evaluation)

This part covers his years of peak influence, his fall and the overall judgement; the strengths and weaknesses below are the usual basis for essays.

  • Between 1886 and 1890 he combined an alliance with Gladstone with firm control over a disciplined party of 85 MPs.
  • He was a participant in the Pigott and O’Shea affairs and lost control of events after the divorce, when his refusal to step aside split the movement.
  • Contemporary nationalists were divided: the Parnellites saw him as a leader betrayed by Liberals and bishops, and the anti-Parnellites as a leader who put himself above the cause.
  • Contemporary unionists saw him as a dangerous agitator, until the Pigott collapse made them look foolish.
  • Strengths: party discipline, tactical skill, ability to unite Fenians, land reformers and constitutionalists, and a clear command of Commons procedure.
  • Weaknesses: aloofness and autocratic style, dependence on the Liberals, ambiguity about violence in the Land War, and a private life that he did not manage.
  • Historians differ: F.S.L. Lyons in his 1977 biography stresses the tragic collision of ambition and scandal; Paul Bew in 1980 stresses Parnell as a conservative landlord politician whose strategy had limits.
  • His legacy was the idea of an independent Irish party; he also left the mythology of a martyr, which later separatists, including Yeats and Joyce, drew on.

The Second Home Rule Bill and the divided party (1891–1900)

Gladstone’s last attempt at Home Rule was killed by the House of Lords, and the Irish Party spent the 1890s in feud.

  • The Liberals returned to office in 1892 with a small majority that depended on the Irish MPs; Gladstone, aged 83, introduced the Second Home Rule Bill on 13 February 1893.
  • The Bill proposed a legislature of two houses, and it kept a reduced number of Irish MPs at Westminster, to meet the objection that they would be excluded.
  • The Commons passed the bill in September 1893 by 301 votes to 267, after eighty-two sittings.
  • The House of Lords threw it out on 8 September 1893 by 419 votes to 41, and Gladstone retired in March 1894.
  • Unionists had mobilised against it, notably at the Ulster Convention in Belfast in June 1892 (see the notes on unionism).
  • The Conservatives under Salisbury won the 1895 election, and the Unionists governed for most of the next ten years, trying to kill Home Rule with kindness.
  • In the 1890s the Irish National Federation (Dillon and McCarthy), Healy’s followers and Redmond’s small Parnellite group fought each other, and voters became disillusioned.
  • The United Irish League was founded by William O’Brien at Westport, Co. Mayo, in January 1898 to revive the land agitation in the west.
  • The parties reunited in January 1900, and Redmond was elected chairman in February 1900; Healy was excluded.

The Irish Party under Redmond, 1900–1914

Redmond rebuilt the party, won land reform and then used the Liberals’ dependence on his votes to put Home Rule on the statute book.

  • Redmond and William O’Brien took part in the Land Conference of 1902–03, which led to the Wyndham Act (see the notes on land reform).
  • In May 1907 a national convention of the party rejected the Irish Council Bill, a limited devolution scheme introduced by Chief Secretary Augustine Birrell.
  • In 1910 two elections left the Liberals dependent on the Irish Party: in January the Irish had 82 seats, and in December 84.
  • After the Lords rejected the People’s Budget (1909), Redmond’s party backed the Liberals and the Parliament Act (1911) removed the Lords’ veto, leaving only a two-year delay.
  • Asquith introduced the Third Home Rule Bill in April 1912; it passed the Commons three times and became law on 18 September 1914 (see the notes on the Third Home Rule crisis).
  • The Act was suspended by a Suspensory Act for the duration of the war, which Redmond accepted.
  • Under pressure from the unionist threat, in March 1914 Redmond accepted a proposal that Ulster counties could opt out for six years, a concession he had previously refused.
  • In June 1914 Redmond forced the Irish Volunteers to accept 25 of his nominees on their provisional committee.

Key personality: John Redmond

Redmond led the reunited party for eighteen years and believed that Home Rule could be won by constitutional methods and kept by loyalty to the empire.

  • John Redmond (1856–1918) was born at Ballytrent, Co. Wexford, into a Catholic gentry family, educated at Clongowes, and became a barrister and MP in 1881.
  • He stayed loyal to Parnell in 1890 and led the small Parnellite group after Parnell died in 1891; he sat for Waterford City from 1891 until his death.
  • He was a participant who negotiated the Land Conference, the 1911 Parliament Act and the 1912 Bill, relying on his control of the party’s votes.
  • Contemporary nationalists generally admired him as the leader who delivered Home Rule; critics such as Griffith and the IRB attacked his reliance on Liberal goodwill.
  • His pledge in August 1914 that the Volunteers should defend Ireland, and his Woodenbridge speech of 20 September 1914 urging them to fight in the war, split the Irish Volunteers.
  • About 170,000 followed him into the National Volunteers and about 11,000 stayed with Eoin MacNeill’s Irish Volunteers (estimates vary).
  • Strengths: party management, parliamentary skill, a genuine achievement in land and the Home Rule Act, and personal integrity.
  • Weaknesses: dependence on the Liberals, failure to resolve the Ulster problem, acceptance of partition in principle in 1914 and poor judgement of the new separatism.
  • Historians differ: older accounts such as Denis Gwynn’s sympathetic 1932 biography and Dermot Meleady’s more recent work present a statesman wrecked by war; others see a leader out of touch with the new generation.

The first Sinn Féin party (1899–1910)

Arthur Griffith proposed a separatist policy of abstention and economic self-reliance as an alternative to Home Rule.

  • Arthur Griffith, a Dublin printer and journalist, founded the newspaper The United Irishman in 1899 with William Rooney.
  • Cumann na nGaedheal was founded in 1900 to unite groups promoting Irish language, industry and the national cause.
  • In The Resurrection of Hungary (1904) Griffith argued that Ireland should follow Hungary, which had won its own parliament within a dual monarchy in 1867 by abstaining from the imperial parliament.
  • At a convention in November 1905 Griffith’s National Council adopted what became the Sinn Féin policy: abstention from Westminster, a national assembly in Dublin and protective tariffs for Irish industry.
  • Cumann na nGaedheal merged with the Dungannon Clubs of Bulmer Hobson in 1907 to form the Sinn Féin League; Griffith’s weekly Sinn Féin followed in 1906.
  • In the North Leitrim by-election of February 1908 the Sinn Féin candidate C.J. Dolan, a former Irish Party MP, was beaten by roughly three to one.
  • Sinn Féin lost support after 1908 and the party remained small, but its ideas about abstention and self-reliance were revived in 1917.

The IRB revival and the Irish Volunteers (1907–1914)

A younger generation of separatists revived the IRB and used the Ulster Volunteers as a model for armed nationalism.

  • Tom Clarke, a Fenian who had served fifteen years in prison, returned from the USA in December 1907 and opened a tobacconist’s shop in Dublin.
  • With Bulmer Hobson and Seán Mac Diarmada, Clarke took control of the IRB and founded the paper Irish Freedom (1910).
  • Eoin MacNeill’s article The North Began, published on 1 November 1913, argued that the Ulster Volunteers had shown that Irishmen could organise to defend their rights.
  • The Irish Volunteers were founded on 25 November 1913 at the Rotunda Rink in Dublin; MacNeill became chairman and the IRB had a hidden influence.
  • Cumann na mBan, a women’s organisation to support the Volunteers, was founded in April 1914.
  • The Howth gun-running on 26 July 1914 landed about 900 rifles from Erskine Childers’s yacht Asgard; British soldiers then fired on a crowd at Bachelor’s Walk in Dublin, killing four.
  • After Redmond’s Woodenbridge speech the Volunteers split in September 1914: the majority became the National Volunteers and a minority kept the name Irish Volunteers.

Assessment: Parnell, Redmond and the alternatives to Home Rule

The period shows constitutional nationalism at its strongest and the first serious challenge to it.

  • Achievements of the Home Rule movement: a united parliamentary party, land reform and, by 1914, a Home Rule Act on the statute book.
  • Limits: Parnell’s fall wasted a decade, the Lords killed the 1893 Bill, and the Ulster question remained unsolved in 1914.
  • Sinn Féin and the IRB offered a separatist alternative, but before 1914 they were small and divided; their growth after 1914 owed more to war and the 1916 Rising than to their own efforts.
  • Contemporary unionists judged Redmond a threat to the Union; separatists judged him too moderate; most nationalists in 1914 supported him.
  • Historians now often argue that Home Rule was not doomed before 1914 and that its collapse came from the war, the Rising and the conscription crisis.

Key terms

Home Rule
The demand for an Irish parliament to manage domestic affairs, pursued by Parnell and Redmond through constitutional means.
Separatism
The aim of full independence from the United Kingdom, pursued in this period by the IRB and, in a different way, by Sinn Féin.
Militarism
The use of armed volunteer forces as a political instrument, as the Ulster Volunteers and the Irish Volunteers did from 1913.
Pigott forgeries
Letters forged by Richard Pigott and published by The Times in 1887 to link Parnell to the Phoenix Park murders.
Special Commission
The three-judge inquiry of 1888–90 that examined The Times charges against Parnell and his party.
Committee Room 15
The room at Westminster where the Irish Party debated Parnell’s leadership from 1 to 6 December 1890 and split.
Anti-Parnellites
The majority of the Irish Party who rejected Parnell’s leadership in December 1890 and later formed the Irish National Federation.
United Irish League
The land and political organisation founded by William O’Brien in 1898, which became Redmond’s grassroots machine.
Sinn Féin
Griffith’s party, adopted in 1905, based on abstention from Westminster, a Dublin assembly, dual monarchy and economic self-sufficiency.
Irish Volunteers
The nationalist force founded in November 1913 in response to the Ulster Volunteers, which split in September 1914.
Dual monarchy
The Austro-Hungarian model, in which Hungary had its own parliament under a shared monarch, proposed by Griffith for Ireland.
