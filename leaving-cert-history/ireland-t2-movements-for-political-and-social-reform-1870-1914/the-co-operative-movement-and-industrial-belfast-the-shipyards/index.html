Horace Plunkett and the origins of the co-operative movement

The co-operative movement tried to improve farming by self-help at a time when the land question was moving towards settlement.

  • Horace Plunkett (1854–1932), the son of Lord Dunsany, was an Anglo-Irish landowner who ranched in Wyoming in the 1880s and returned to Ireland convinced that farmers needed better organisation.
  • He was a Unionist MP for South Dublin from 1892 to 1900, though he worked with nationalists in his reform schemes.
  • Irish farmers suffered from poor marketing, dependence on local shopkeepers and moneylenders, and competition from Danish butter, which was better made and graded.
  • Plunkett took the Danish model of farmer-owned co-operative creameries, where farmers pooled milk and used a mechanical separator, giving higher quality at lower cost.
  • The first co-operative creamery is usually dated to 1889 at Dromcollogher, Co. Limerick, and the model then spread in Munster and elsewhere.
  • Plunkett’s supporters included Fr Tom Finlay, a Jesuit, and the poet George Russell (AE), who later edited the movement’s paper, the Irish Homestead.
  • The Irish Agricultural Wholesale Society was founded in 1897 and the Irish Homestead began in 1895 as the movement’s weekly paper.

The IAOS, credit societies and the growth of co-operation

In 1894 Plunkett founded a central body to promote co-operative societies across the country.

  • The Irish Agricultural Organisation Society (IAOS) was founded in 1894 to organise, advise and inspect co-operative societies.
  • Its motto was better farming, better business, better living, a statement of its self-help philosophy.
  • Co-operative creameries multiplied in the 1890s, giving small dairy farmers a market and a share of profits.
  • Credit societies, modelled on German Raiffeisen banks, lent small amounts to members on the guarantee of their neighbours, reducing dependence on gombeen men (local moneylenders and shopkeepers).
  • Other societies supplied seeds and fertiliser and sold eggs and poultry; the Irish Agricultural Wholesale Society was founded in 1897.
  • By the First World War there were close to 1,000 societies with roughly 100,000 members, though they were strongest in Munster and weakest in Ulster (estimates vary).
  • Co-operative creameries introduced mechanical cream separators and bought milk by its fat content, which rewarded quality and raised the standard of Irish butter.

The Recess Committee and the Department of Agriculture (1895–1899)

Plunkett brought nationalists and unionists together in 1895 to call for state help for farming and industry.

  • The Recess Committee met during the parliamentary recess of 1895, and brought together unionist and nationalist members.
  • Its report in 1896 studied Danish, French and German methods and recommended a government department with a minister responsible for agriculture and technical education.
  • The Department of Agriculture and Technical Instruction (DATI) was established in 1899, with Plunkett as its first vice-president (the effective head).
  • DATI supported agricultural education, instructors, research, technical schools and fisheries, and the Chief Secretary was its president.
  • Plunkett lost the vice-presidency in 1907 after hostility from the Irish Party.
  • The Department was financed by the Treasury and the Chief Secretary was its nominal president, with T.W. Russell succeeding Plunkett as vice-president in 1907.

Suspicion and results of the movement

Co-operation brought clear economic gains, but political suspicion limited it.

  • Many nationalists suspected Plunkett, a unionist, of trying to turn farmers away from the land and national questions; others, like AE, saw the movement as national self-help.
  • Many unionists, especially Ulster farmers, were slow to join, partly through suspicion of Plunkett’s dealings with nationalists.
  • The Catholic clergy often supported local creameries, but Plunkett’s book Ireland in the New Century (1904) criticised the Church’s influence on Irish initiative, and a clerical campaign contributed to his defeat in 1900 and his later problems.
  • Private creamery owners and shopkeepers resented the competition from co-operatives.
  • Results: the co-operatives improved the quality of Irish butter, raised farm incomes, spread the idea of self-help and trained a generation of local leaders.
  • The movement did not solve the problems of poor western farmers, who were helped more by the Congested Districts Board (see the notes on land reform).

Belfast’s growth and industries

Belfast grew from a town of about 20,000 around 1800 into Ireland’s industrial capital.

  • Belfast’s population grew fast: about 255,000 at the 1891 census (when it passed Dublin city), 349,000 in 1901 and 387,000 in 1911.
  • Linen, spun and woven in mills, was the first great industry; Belfast was nicknamed Linenopolis and employed tens of thousands, most of them women and girls.
  • The Belfast Ropeworks at Connswater was among the largest ropeworks in the world.
  • Tobacco manufacture grew with Gallaher and other firms, and engineering firms such as Sirocco Works produced machinery for export.
  • Other trades included whiskey distilling (Dunville) and aerated waters.
  • Catholic workers from the countryside moved into west Belfast, around the Falls Road, so that by 1911 about a quarter of the city was Catholic.
  • The American Civil War cut cotton supplies in the 1860s and boosted linen, and power-loom mills replaced handloom weaving.
  • Belfast’s population was only about 20,000 around 1800, so the later growth was rapid.
  • The Belfast Ropeworks, founded in 1873, supplied rope to shipping across the world.

Harland and Wolff, Workman Clark and the great liners

Shipbuilding on Queen’s Island made Belfast famous and tied its prosperity to the empire’s shipping trade.

  • Edward Harland, an English engineer, bought a small yard on Queen’s Island in 1858 and went into partnership in 1861 with Gustav Wolff, a German-born engineer from Hamburg.
  • William Pirrie, born in Quebec to Ulster parents, joined the yard as an apprentice, became a partner in 1874 and chairman after Harland’s death in 1895; he was made Lord Pirrie in 1906.
  • The firm built ships for the White Star Line, including the Olympic-class liners Olympic, Titanic and Britannic, the biggest ships in the world when launched.
  • Titanic was launched on 31 May 1911 and sank on her maiden voyage on 15 April 1912 with the loss of about 1,500 lives.
  • Workman Clark, founded in 1880 by Frank Workman and George Clark, was the second Belfast yard and built cargo and passenger ships.
  • Together the yards employed tens of thousands, with Harland and Wolff alone employing about 15,000 men by 1912.
  • The Arrol Gantry, built over the slipways in 1908–09, allowed Olympic and Titanic to be built side by side.

Working conditions and sectarian employment

Industrial Belfast offered work but also hard conditions and a religious divide that fed unionism.

  • Mill workers, mostly women and girls, worked long hours in hot, damp rooms full of flax dust, which caused lung disease, and wages were low.
  • Shipyard work was dangerous and casual labourers were laid off between contracts.
  • Skilled jobs in the shipyards and engineering were mainly held by Protestants, often through family and Orange Order connections, while Catholics tended to be labourers.
  • Sectarian riots occurred in 1857, 1864 and 1886, and the riots of 1886 involved attacks on Catholic workers at the shipyards (see the notes on the 1885–86 elections).
  • In July 1912, during the Home Rule crisis, Protestant workers drove Catholic workers and some Protestant labour sympathisers out of Workman Clark and Harland and Wolff, thousands in all by some estimates.
  • Larkin’s 1907 strike briefly united Catholic and Protestant workers but did not last.
  • Many Protestant workers feared that Home Rule would bring tariffs and Catholic dominance, and this linked industrial interests to unionism.
  • Many mill women were the main earners in their families, and half-time child workers also worked in the mills until reforms limited this.

Belfast and Dublin compared

The two cities had different economies, which shaped their politics.

  • Belfast depended on export manufacturing, mainly linen and shipbuilding, linked to the British and imperial markets; Dublin depended on administration, trade, brewing and distilling.
  • Dublin had large employers such as Guinness at St James’s Gate and Jacob’s, but many of its workers were casual and poorly paid.
  • Belfast wages for skilled men were higher than in Dublin, and its tenements were less bad (Belfast had back-to-back terraces), though disease and low wages still afflicted the mill districts.
  • Belfast was strongly Protestant and unionist; Dublin was nationalist, with a strong labour movement.
  • Belfast’s industrial development depended on its link with Britain, which was a major reason for unionist opposition to Home Rule.
  • Belfast’s Catholic minority and Protestant majority lived in separate districts, such as the Falls and the Shankill, so the city’s politics were territorial as well as economic.

Assessment: co-operation and industrial Belfast

Both developments brought prosperity to part of Ireland, but each left problems unsolved.

  • Achievement: the co-operatives improved farmers’ incomes and quality of produce and encouraged self-reliance; Belfast became an industrial city of international importance.
  • Limit: the co-operatives hardly reached the poorest western farmers and Ulster, and Belfast’s prosperity relied on cheap female labour and sectarian divisions.
  • Contemporary nationalists were suspicious of Plunkett as a unionist, while unionists admired Belfast as proof of the Union’s benefits.
  • Historians see Plunkett’s movement as a significant success of self-help, though it is often judged less influential than land reform in modernising Irish farming.

Key terms

Co-operative movement
The organisation of farmers into societies for creameries, credit and supplies, promoted by Plunkett from the 1890s.
Co-operative creamery
A dairy owned by local farmers who pooled their milk to make butter and shared the profits.
Irish Agricultural Organisation Society
The central body founded by Plunkett in 1894 to organise and inspect co-operative societies.
Credit society
A small co-operative bank, modelled on the German Raiffeisen banks, that lent to members on the security of their neighbours.
Recess Committee
The committee of 1895 that studied foreign methods and recommended a government department for Irish agriculture.
Department of Agriculture and Technical Instruction
The state department established in 1899 to promote farming, fisheries and technical education.
Harland and Wolff
The Belfast shipbuilders that built the Olympic-class liners, including Titanic.
Workman Clark
The second Belfast shipbuilder, founded in 1880 by Frank Workman and George Clark.
Linenopolis
A nickname for Belfast, reflecting its status as the centre of the linen industry.
