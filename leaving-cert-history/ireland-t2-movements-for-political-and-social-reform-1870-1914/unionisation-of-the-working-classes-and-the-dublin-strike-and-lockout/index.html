Dublin’s urban poverty before 1913

Dublin had few factories and a large pool of unskilled labour, and its working class lived in some of the worst housing in the United Kingdom.

  • Dublin’s tenements were old Georgian townhouses on the north side and in the Liberties, divided into single rooms; more than 20,000 families lived in one room each (1911 census).
  • Many houses were owned by landlords who collected high rents for rooms with no running water and a shared yard toilet, and some landlords sat on Dublin Corporation.
  • Dublin had a very high death rate, among the highest of any city in the United Kingdom, with tuberculosis and infant mortality especially severe.
  • Most unskilled men were casual labourers at the docks, in carting and building, hired by the day at the stand with no security and very low pay.
  • Women worked in laundries, textile mills, biscuit factories and as domestic servants for low wages.
  • On 2 September 1913 two tenement houses in Church Street collapsed, killing seven people and exposing the state of Dublin housing in the middle of the lockout.
  • A Local Government Board housing inquiry in 1913–14 reported on the conditions of tenement life.

Trade unions before 1913: craft unions and new unionism

Irish unions began as craft bodies for skilled workers; from the 1890s a new unionism reached unskilled workers.

  • Craft unions, mainly branches of British unions, organised skilled tradesmen such as printers, carpenters and engineers, and had little interest in the unskilled.
  • The Irish Trades Union Congress was founded in 1894 to give Irish unions a common voice, though most unions remained British-based.
  • New unionism, which spread from Britain in the 1880s, organised unskilled workers into general unions, and used the strike, including the sympathetic strike.
  • James Connolly founded the Irish Socialist Republican Party in Dublin in 1896, arguing that socialism and national independence were linked.
  • In 1907 James Larkin, then an organiser for the National Union of Dock Labourers, led a strike of Belfast dockers and carters that united Catholic and Protestant workers for a time; riots in August left two dead.
  • Larkin broke with the British union and founded the Irish Transport and General Workers’ Union in Dublin in 1909, with Liberty Hall as its headquarters.
  • The ITGWU won disputes in Dublin and Cork and fought a lockout in Wexford in 1911; Larkin founded the weekly Irish Worker in 1911.
  • The Irish Labour Party was founded in 1912, at the Irish TUC meeting in Clonmel, to represent workers in parliament.

Key personality: James Larkin

Larkin was the charismatic organiser who built the ITGWU and led the Dublin workers in 1913.

  • James Larkin was born in Liverpool in the 1870s (he gave 1876; some sources say 1874) to Irish parents, and worked as a docker before becoming a union organiser; he died in 1947.
  • He was a participant: he led the Belfast strike of 1907, founded the ITGWU in 1909 and was the main figure in the Dublin events of 1913.
  • His tactics, known as Larkinism, included the sympathetic strike and the refusal of union members to handle goods from firms in dispute (tainted goods).
  • He was a powerful speaker; he appeared in disguise on the balcony of the Imperial Hotel, Murphy’s property, on 31 August 1913, to address the banned meeting.
  • He was sentenced to seven months for sedition in October 1913 but released on 13 November after a public campaign.
  • He left Ireland for the United States in October 1914 and was imprisoned there in 1920 and deported in 1923; he later returned to Irish politics and died in 1947.
  • Contemporary employers, the conservative press and Archbishop Walsh’s supporters condemned him as an agitator, while Dublin workers revered him as a champion.
  • Historians credit him with the first mass organisation of unskilled Irish workers, though some argue that his temper and tactics made compromise harder in 1913.

Key personality: James Connolly

Connolly was the thinker of the Irish labour movement, who argued that socialism and national freedom were inseparable.

  • James Connolly (1868–1916) was born in Edinburgh to Irish parents, served in the British army, and became a socialist organiser in Edinburgh before moving to Dublin in 1896.
  • He founded the Irish Socialist Republican Party in 1896 and edited its paper, the Workers’ Republic.
  • He lived in the USA from 1903 to 1910, organising for socialist bodies and the Industrial Workers of the World, and wrote Labour in Irish History (1910).
  • He returned in 1910, became Ulster organiser of the ITGWU and helped found the Labour Party in 1912.
  • During the lockout he was Larkin’s lieutenant; when Larkin was jailed and then left for the USA, Connolly led the union and the Irish Citizen Army, becoming its commander in 1914.
  • Contemporaries on the left admired him, while the Church and employers distrusted his socialism; many nationalists saw him as a distraction from the national cause until 1916.
  • Historians judge him the leading Irish socialist thinker, with debate over whether his turn to the Rising in 1916 showed that he put nation before class.

Background to the lockout: Murphy and the employers

William Martin Murphy led the employers’ attempt to destroy the ITGWU before it could win recognition.

  • William Martin Murphy (1844–1919) was Dublin’s leading capitalist: chairman of the Dublin United Tramways Company, owner of the Irish Independent, and an Irish Party supporter turned critic.
  • He was president of the Dublin Chamber of Commerce and the leading figure of the Dublin Employers’ Federation, which had hundreds of members.
  • Murphy argued that the ITGWU’s sympathetic strikes and boycotts were tyranny over business and workers, and that employers had the right to manage without interference.
  • In July and August 1913 Larkin’s union had won gains from several Dublin firms, and Murphy decided to challenge it on the trams and in the Independent’s despatch department.
  • In mid-August 1913 Murphy dismissed workers at the Independent and DUTC who were members of the ITGWU.

The strike and lockout begin (August–October 1913)

A tram strike on the first day of Horse Show week grew into the lockout of thousands of workers.

  • On Tuesday 26 August 1913, the first day of Horse Show week, tram drivers and conductors walked off their cars, causing chaos in the city.
  • Murphy replaced strikers with other workers and the police protected them; the ITGWU responded by calling out other workers who handled tram-related goods.
  • Larkin burned a police proclamation banning a meeting and promised to speak in Sackville Street (now O’Connell Street) on Sunday 31 August 1913.
  • On 30 August baton charges in Ringsend and the city killed two men; on 31 August police baton-charged the crowd in O’Connell Street, injuring hundreds in what was called Bloody Sunday.
  • On 3 September about 400 employers agreed that all workers must sign the Document, a pledge to leave the ITGWU and not support it, and many locked out those who refused.
  • About 20,000 workers were out of work by autumn (estimates range up to 25,000), which meant many thousands more dependants were affected.
  • The Board of Trade’s Askwith inquiry (late September–October 1913) condemned the Document, but the employers refused its proposals.
  • George Russell (AE) published an Open Letter to the Masters of Dublin in the Irish Times on 7 October 1913, attacking the employers for starving workers into submission.

Relief, the Citizen Army and the end of the lockout (October 1913–January 1914)

Help from British unions kept the workers going, but the British TUC refused to take the sympathetic action Larkin wanted.

  • British trade unions sent food: the first food ship, the Hare, arrived in Dublin on 27 September 1913, and money and supplies continued to arrive from British unions.
  • Strike pay and relief were distributed from Liberty Hall, with soup kitchens and food parcels.
  • The Save the Kiddies scheme, promoted by Dora Montefiore and others in October 1913, planned to send strikers’ children to homes in Britain; Archbishop Walsh condemned it as a threat to the children’s faith, and priests and the Ancient Order of Hibernians prevented children leaving.
  • On 23 November 1913 the Irish Citizen Army was founded at Liberty Hall to protect pickets from police, drilled by Captain Jack White.
  • A special British TUC congress in December 1913 rejected calls for a sympathetic strike or a boycott of Dublin goods, a heavy blow to the ITGWU.
  • By January 1914 hunger was driving workers back, and the ITGWU advised them to return; the dispute formally ended around 18 January 1914, many men returning without signing the Document.
  • Employers kept the right to bar ITGWU members in some firms and some workers were blacklisted, so the outcome favoured the employers.

Outcomes and significance

The union lost the lockout but survived, and the dispute left a lasting mark on Irish labour and politics.

  • Many strikers returned without recognition of the union, and the ITGWU lost members, but it survived and grew during the First World War.
  • The Irish Citizen Army, born of the lockout, took part in the Easter Rising of 1916 under Connolly.
  • The dispute made labour a political force in Dublin and began the alliance of labour and nationalism that Connolly favoured.
  • It split nationalist opinion: Griffith’s Sinn Féin and the Irish Party were largely unsympathetic to the strikers, while Connolly, Pearse’s circle, Yeats, AE and Constance Markievicz expressed support.
  • The Church sympathised with the poor but condemned the tactics and the Save the Kiddies scheme, and opposed socialism.
  • The housing inquiry and public sympathy increased pressure for slum clearance in Dublin.

Documents and the views of each side

Documents on 1913 include employer statements, newspaper reports, Church statements, poems and cartoons.

  • Murphy’s Irish Independent portrayed Larkin as a dangerous agitator and the strike as an attack on trade and liberty.
  • The Irish Worker and Connolly’s writings portrayed the employers as aggressors starving workers into submission.
  • Archbishop Walsh criticised the housing conditions but warned against socialist ideas and condemned the removal of children.
  • Yeats’s poem September 1913 (published 8 September) was written in the dispute over Hugh Lane’s gallery, in which Murphy’s Independent opposed funding, and it attacks the narrow, commercial Dublin middle class.
  • AE’s Open Letter to the employers is a strong middle-class voice for the workers, written by a poet, not a trade unionist.
  • Cartoons in the Irish Worker and in Dublin papers show Murphy and the police as oppressors, or Larkin as a demagogue, depending on the paper.
  • To place a document, identify the stage (tram strike, Bloody Sunday, the Document, the food ships, the end), the author’s interest and the audience.

Assessment: the 1913 strike and lockout

Judgements on 1913 compare the strength of organised labour with the cost of defeat.

  • Achievement: the ITGWU organised thousands of unskilled workers, kept a mass movement alive for five months and won widespread sympathy.
  • Limit: it failed to defeat the employers, lacked British union support for sympathetic action and ended with hunger forcing a return.
  • Larkin’s strengths were inspiration and organising ability; his weaknesses were intransigence and the extension of the dispute into a general conflict.
  • Murphy’s strengths were unity and resources; his weaknesses were hardness that cost him public sympathy and drew the attention of the British government.
  • Contemporaries disagreed: employers called it a defence of liberty, workers a defence of the right to organise.
  • Historians see 1913 as a defeat that strengthened the labour movement, and as a source of the Citizen Army and the radicalisation that fed 1916.

Key terms

Socialism
The belief that wealth and the means of production should be owned in common; Connolly argued it was inseparable from Irish national freedom.
Lockout
An employers’ shutting out of workers until they accept terms; used against ITGWU members in 1913.
Sympathetic strike
A strike by workers in support of others in dispute, the tactic known as Larkinism.
Larkinism
Larkin’s militant methods, including sympathetic strikes and refusing to handle tainted goods.
New unionism
The organising of unskilled workers into general unions, in contrast to craft unions of skilled trades.
Craft union
A union of skilled tradesmen such as printers or carpenters, often reluctant to admit unskilled workers.
ITGWU
The Irish Transport and General Workers’ Union, founded by Larkin in Dublin in 1909.
The Document
The pledge employers required in 1913, to give up the ITGWU and not support it.
Irish Citizen Army
The workers’ defence force founded at Liberty Hall in November 1913.
Irish Labour Party
The political wing of the trade union movement, formed in 1912.
