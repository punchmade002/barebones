Douglas Hyde and the de-anglicisation argument (1892)

Hyde’s lecture of 1892 gave the cultural revival its programme: Irish people should stop imitating England and revive their own language and customs.

  • Douglas Hyde delivered The Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland to the National Literary Society in Dublin on 25 November 1892.
  • Hyde argued that Ireland had been anglicised in language, names, dress, games and ideas, and that the Irish were failing both to be English and to be Irish.
  • He insisted the aim was to preserve what was distinctive about Ireland, and that this was compatible with a political view that was not separatist.
  • He pointed to the decline of Irish as a spoken language, which had fallen sharply since the Famine, and asked for it to be cultivated and used in schools.
  • The lecture influenced the GAA’s earlier example, and led within a year to the founding of the Gaelic League.

The Gaelic League (1893–1914)

The Gaelic League, founded in 1893, built a mass movement to revive the Irish language.

  • The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge) was founded in Dublin on 31 July 1893 by Douglas Hyde, Eoin MacNeill and others, with Hyde as president.
  • It was officially non-political and non-sectarian, and it welcomed Catholics, Protestants, nationalists and unionists.
  • Branches ran Irish classes, and travelling teachers (timirí) were sent to rural areas; there were several hundred branches by 1904.
  • The League organised feiseanna (festivals of music, dance and language competitions) and, from 1897, the annual Oireachtas.
  • Its weekly paper An Claidheamh Soluis began in 1899; Patrick Pearse edited it from 1903 to 1909.
  • Successes: Irish was allowed in primary schools by the Revised Programme of 1900, and the League won a campaign to make Irish compulsory for matriculation to the National University, decided in 1910 and applied from 1913.
  • St Patrick’s Day became a national holiday for the League, with processions from 1903, and people also took Irish names and used Irish-language signs.
  • From about 1910 a growing number of IRB members joined the League and pushed it towards politics; Hyde resigned as president in 1915 when the League declared for Irish independence.

Key personality: Douglas Hyde

Hyde was the scholar who launched the language revival and gave it a non-sectarian, non-political character.

  • Douglas Hyde (1860–1949) was born in Castlerea, Co. Roscommon, the son of a Church of Ireland rector, and learned Irish from the people around him in Frenchpark.
  • He studied at Trinity College Dublin, published collections of Gaelic songs and folktales, including Love Songs of Connacht (1893), and wrote under the name An Craoibhín Aoibhinn.
  • He was a participant: he gave the 1892 lecture, co-founded the League in 1893 and led it for twenty-two years.
  • Contemporaries admired his scholarship and his appeal across religious lines, but Moran and other Irish Irelanders thought him too genteel and too tolerant of Anglo-Irish culture.
  • Hyde resigned as president in 1915 when the League adopted an explicitly political aim; he later became a senator and the first President of Ireland (1938–45).
  • Historians credit him with launching the revival and mass interest in the language, while recognising that its political use after 1900 went beyond his intentions.

Irish Ireland and D.P. Moran

Moran gave the revival a sharper edge by defining the Irish nation as Gaelic and Catholic.

  • David Patrick Moran founded the newspaper The Leader in 1900 and used it to argue for an Irish Ireland.
  • He urged support for Irish language, industry and games, and attacked the Anglo-Irish literary movement and the Irish Party as West Britons.
  • In his view the nation was Gaelic and Catholic, and Protestants could be accepted only if they adopted this identity.
  • Moran’s book The Philosophy of Irish Ireland (1905) gathered his articles and set out his creed.
  • The Leader promoted boycotts of English goods and entertainment, and attacked music-hall and English newspapers.
  • Moran’s influence divided the revival between Hyde’s inclusive approach and a narrower Catholic and Gaelic nationalism.

The Anglo-Irish Literary Revival

A group of mainly Protestant writers in English drew on Irish myth, folklore and peasant speech to create a national literature and theatre.

  • The National Literary Society was founded in Dublin in 1892 with Hyde and Yeats among its leaders.
  • The Irish Literary Theatre was founded in 1899 by W.B. Yeats, Lady Gregory and Edward Martyn; its first production, Yeats’s The Countess Cathleen, was in May 1899.
  • Yeats and Lady Gregory’s Cathleen ni Houlihan was first staged on 2 April 1902, with Maud Gonne in the title role.
  • The Abbey Theatre opened on 27 December 1904, funded by Annie Horniman, with Yeats, Lady Gregory and J.M. Synge as directors.
  • Lady Gregory (1852–1932) wrote plays and collected folklore; Synge (1871–1909) used the speech of the Aran Islands and the west of Ireland.
  • Synge’s The Playboy of the Western World provoked riots at the Abbey in January 1907, as nationalist audiences objected to its picture of rural Irish life as immoral.

Key personality: W.B. Yeats

Yeats, a Protestant poet and dramatist, tried to give Ireland a literature that would shape a national identity.

  • William Butler Yeats (1865–1939) was born in Sandymount, Dublin, the son of the painter John Butler Yeats, and spent much of his childhood in Sligo and London.
  • He was a participant who co-founded the National Literary Society, the Irish Literary Theatre and the Abbey, and who campaigned for a national theatre.
  • His early poetry, such as The Wanderings of Oisin (1889), drew on Irish mythology, and his friendship with the nationalist Maud Gonne linked him to political circles.
  • Contemporary nationalists admired his work but distrusted his Anglo-Irish background; Moran mocked his Celtic note, while the Catholic Church attacked The Countess Cathleen in 1899.
  • September 1913, published in the Irish Times on 8 September 1913, grew out of the Hugh Lane gallery dispute and condemned the narrow Catholic middle class for lacking the generosity of earlier Irish rebels.
  • He later served as a senator (1922–28) and won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1923.
  • Historians such as R.F. Foster see him at the centre of Irish cultural life, while critics find an elitist attitude to democracy in his politics.

Controversies of the revival

The revival was marked by public disputes about who defined Irish identity.

  • The Countess Cathleen (1899) was attacked as heretical by Catholic critics, and Cardinal Logue condemned it without seeing it.
  • The Playboy riots of January 1907 showed the clash between the Abbey’s artistic freedom and the nationalist demand for flattering images of Irish life.
  • The Hugh Lane dispute began in 1912, when Dublin Corporation hesitated to fund a gallery for Lane’s collection of French paintings; Lane withdrew the offer and lent the pictures to the National Gallery in London in 1913.
  • After Lane died on the Lusitania in May 1915, a codicil left the pictures to Dublin, leading to a long dispute that was only settled in 1959.
  • Nationalists argued that Irish culture should be Gaelic and Catholic, while the Anglo-Irish writers claimed a place for English-language literature and for Protestant traditions.
  • Tension between the two visions, Anglo-Irish and Irish Ireland, ran through the revival and remained after independence.

The consolidation of Catholic identity

After the Famine the Catholic Church tightened its hold on Irish life, and from the 1880s Catholic and nationalist identity increasingly overlapped.

  • Historians speak of a devotional revolution: from the 1850s Mass attendance rose, with parish missions, retreats, confraternities and devotions such as the Rosary and the Sacred Heart.
  • New churches and cathedrals were built across the country, funded by a more prosperous farming and trading middle class.
  • The Church controlled most national schools through clerical managers and ran secondary schools through religious orders such as the Christian Brothers, Jesuits and convent teaching orders.
  • Maynooth trained a large clergy, which held a high social position in rural communities and was often involved in politics and the GAA.
  • Sodalities and confraternities organised lay devotion, and the Pioneer Total Abstinence Association was founded by Fr James Cullen, a Jesuit, in 1898 to fight drunkenness.
  • The Ne Temere decree, in force from Easter 1908, required the children of mixed marriages to be raised as Catholics and angered Protestants.
  • The land reforms and expanding education produced a Catholic middle class, which provided the personnel of the nationalist movement.
  • The bishops generally supported Home Rule after 1885, which tied Catholic identity to nationalism, and the Irish Party was largely Catholic.
  • Unionists reacted by presenting Home Rule as Rome Rule; the Church’s role in the Parnell split and Ne Temere reinforced the fear.

The GAA after 1891 and comparison with the Gaelic League

The GAA recovered in the 1890s and the two bodies became partners and rivals.

  • The GAA recovered slowly after 1891, reorganised its county boards and resumed competitions; it bought grounds at Jones’s Road in Dublin in 1908, later Croke Park.
  • The IRB remained influential in its leadership, and in the early 1900s it formalised a ban on foreign games.
  • The GAA was mass, rural, male and sport-based; the League drew women, teachers and the urban middle class, and its work was language and education.
  • The GAA was politically committed from the start; the League was non-political until 1915.
  • Both were created by cultural nationalists and shared the aim of de-anglicisation and many members.
  • Fr Michael O’Hickey, professor of Irish at Maynooth, was dismissed by the bishops in 1909 after campaigning for compulsory Irish, showing limits to Church support for the League.

Assessment: the revival and Catholic identity

The revival gave Ireland a cultural identity but left argument about who belonged to it.

  • Achievement: the League, GAA and theatre created lasting institutions and symbols, and gave self-confidence to the nationalist movement.
  • Limit: the language did not revive as an everyday tongue, and the Catholic and Gaelic emphasis excluded many Protestants and unionists.
  • Contemporary unionists saw the revival as a disguise for separatism; Irish Irelanders such as Moran saw the Literary Revival as too Anglo-Irish.
  • Historians differ on whether the revival caused the revolution of 1916, as Pearse and the IRB claimed, or merely provided a language for politics that had other causes.

Key terms

Anglicisation
The adoption of English language, dress, games and manners in Ireland, which the revivals set out to reverse.
De-anglicisation
The reversal of anglicisation, as proposed by Douglas Hyde in 1892, through the revival of Irish language, games and customs.
Irish Ireland
D.P. Moran’s idea that the nation should be Gaelic and Catholic, built on Irish language, industry and games.
Anglo-Irish
The mainly Protestant landed and professional class with English ties, many of whose writers led the Literary Revival.
Gaelic League
The Conradh na Gaeilge, founded in 1893 to revive the Irish language through classes, festivals and publications.
Literary Revival
The movement of Yeats, Lady Gregory, Synge and others that drew on Irish myth and speech to create a national literature and theatre.
Abbey Theatre
The national theatre opened in Dublin in December 1904, which staged the plays of the revival.
Devotional revolution
The post-Famine growth in Catholic practice, church building and organisations that strengthened Catholic identity.
Ne Temere decree
The 1908 papal decree requiring the children of mixed marriages to be raised as Catholics.
Timirí
The Gaelic League’s travelling teachers, who set up Irish classes in rural districts.
