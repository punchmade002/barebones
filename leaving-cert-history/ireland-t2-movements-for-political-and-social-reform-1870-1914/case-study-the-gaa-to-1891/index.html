Background: Irish sport before 1884

In the early 1880s organised sport in Ireland followed English rules and was dominated by the gentry and middle class, while the native game of hurling had declined.

  • Athletics was organised by bodies that followed English amateur rules; these rules excluded artisans and labourers, so most working people and farmers could not compete.
  • Rugby, cricket and later soccer were played in schools, garrison towns and among the gentry; the Irish Rugby Football Union was founded in 1879 and the Irish Football Association in 1880.
  • Hurling had declined since the Famine, as landlord patronage ended and rural communities shrank, though it survived in parts of Galway, Clare, Tipperary, Kilkenny, Cork and Limerick, played under varying local rules.
  • Michael Cusack founded the Metropolitan Hurling Club in Dublin in 1882 and tried to standardise rules, while Trinity College students also played hurling.
  • The cultural background included the Gaelic Union (1880), formed to preserve the Irish language, and a growing sense that English habits were replacing Irish ones, which later writers called anglicisation.
  • The political background was the Land War, the rise of the Irish Party and Parnell, and a Fenian revival; the new association emerged in a period of intense national feeling.
  • Cusack’s Metropolitan Hurling Club in Dublin held a match in 1883 under agreed rules and used the Irish Times to argue for a native game.

The founding: Cusack, Davin and Hayes’ Hotel (1884)

The Gaelic Athletic Association was founded at Thurles on 1 November 1884 by a small group led by Michael Cusack and Maurice Davin.

  • Michael Cusack (1847–1906) was born in Co. Clare, trained as a teacher and ran a cramming academy in Dublin; he was a forceful, abrasive enthusiast, later the model for the Citizen in James Joyce’s Ulysses.
  • Maurice Davin (1842–1927) was a champion weight-thrower and rower from Carrick-on-Suir, Co. Tipperary, who was respected for his fairness and became the first president.
  • In October 1884 Cusack published an article, A Word about Irish Athletics, in United Ireland, calling for a national body to revive Irish sports.
  • Cusack and Davin sent invitations to a meeting at Hayes’ Hotel in Thurles, Co. Tipperary, on 1 November 1884.
  • Seven men attended, including Cusack, Davin, John Wyse Power, a journalist, and others; Davin was elected president and Cusack, Wyse Power and J.K. Bracken were elected secretaries.
  • The Association aimed to revive Irish games, to open athletics to all classes, and to promote physical fitness and national pride.
  • The Association’s full title was the Gaelic Athletic Association for the Preservation and Cultivation of National Pastimes.

The patrons and Archbishop Croke’s letter

Cusack won the support of the most powerful figures in nationalist Ireland, who gave the GAA legitimacy and publicity.

  • Archbishop Thomas Croke of Cashel, a nationalist and supporter of the land movement, agreed to be patron in a letter of December 1884.
  • Croke attacked the imitation of English games and manners, and urged the revival of Irish sports as an act of national self-respect.
  • Charles Stewart Parnell and Michael Davitt also agreed to be patrons, which linked the GAA to the Home Rule and land movements.
  • Croke’s letter was widely printed and made the GAA respectable, and many priests became club presidents and organisers.
  • Davitt saw the GAA as a way of drawing working people and farmers into the national movement, and he wrote and spoke in support.
  • Croke, Archbishop of Cashel from 1875, had supported the tenants during the Land War and was a known nationalist, so his name carried weight with farmers.
  • John Wyse Power, a journalist and IRB man, was one of the first secretaries, showing that Fenians were involved from the start.

Rules, bans and organisation

The GAA set rules that separated Irish sport from English sport and built a structure on the parish.

  • Davin drew up the first rules for hurling and Gaelic football in 1885, reducing the old local variations to a national code.
  • Competition was open to all classes; the old amateur rules that excluded manual workers were rejected.
  • In the early years the GAA banned members from playing or attending foreign games such as cricket, rugby and soccer, the so-called foreign games ban.
  • Members of the Royal Irish Constabulary and the British armed forces were also barred, because they represented British rule.
  • Clubs were organised by parish, with county committees and a Central Council above them, and an annual convention that was usually held in Thurles.
  • The parish structure suited the Catholic Church’s organisation, which made it easy for priests to take part.
  • The GAA was strictly amateur in principle, and it set out to keep money and professionalism out of sport.

Growth and the first championships (1885–1888)

The GAA spread quickly and in 1887 held the first All-Ireland championships.

  • The GAA spread to the south and west and then to the midlands and Leinster; within a few years hundreds of clubs had affiliated.
  • Hurling and football matches drew large crowds, often with bands and processions, and club matches became local occasions.
  • The first All-Ireland championships began in 1887, with open draws and no provincial stage.
  • The first hurling final was played at Birr, Co. Offaly, on 1 April 1888, when Thurles (Tipperary) beat Meelick (Galway); the score is variously reported.
  • The first football final was played in April 1888 and was won by Limerick (represented by the Commercials club) against Louth.
  • Cusack founded the weekly The Celtic Times in 1887 to promote Gaelic games.

The IRB, the clerical reaction and the crisis (1886–1889)

The GAA’s success made it a target for Fenians and a worry for the Church, and its leaders fell out.

  • Members of the Irish Republican Brotherhood joined the GAA in large numbers, using clubs and county boards to recruit and to gain influence.
  • Dublin Castle watched the GAA closely, and RIC reports described it as a front for Fenianism.
  • Cusack, who disliked the secret society, quarrelled with Davin and other officers, and was removed as secretary in 1886.
  • The annual conventions of 1886 and 1887, held in Thurles, were contests between moderates and IRB-linked delegates for control of the Central Council.
  • Archbishop Croke and other bishops grew uneasy, and clergy withdrew from clubs in some places; Davin stepped down as president in 1889.
  • The IRB hold on the Central Council was strong by the later 1880s, and the more it grew the more the Church held back.

The American tour and the Parnell split (1888–1891)

Two blows in quick succession, an expensive overseas tour and the split in Irish nationalism, almost destroyed the association.

  • In autumn 1888 a party of about fifty hurlers and athletes, known as the American Invasion, toured the United States, with Irish-American support in cities such as New York and Boston.
  • The tour lost money, left debts and disrupted the 1888 championships, which were left unfinished; some of the athletes stayed in America.
  • The O’Shea divorce and the split in the Irish Party in December 1890 divided GAA members, with the IRB generally favouring Parnell and Church-minded members opposing him.
  • Parnell’s death in October 1891 left the association demoralised.
  • By 1891 membership and the number of clubs had collapsed, and a few county boards were all that remained in many areas.
  • The GAA recovered slowly in the 1890s, with a new generation of leaders and the support of the Gaelic revival.

Why the GAA survived and how it dealt with its difficulties

Examiners ask how the GAA dealt with its difficulties to 1891; the main answers are organisation, patronage and compromise.

  • The parish-based club system meant that local clubs could continue even when the Central Council quarrelled.
  • The patronage of Croke and the clergy gave the association moral authority, and priests helped to restore order in clubs.
  • The appeal of hurling and football and of local rivalry was strong in itself and did not depend on politics.
  • The removal of Cusack and the departure of Davin showed an attempt to settle disputes by changing leaders rather than breaking up the body.
  • Connections with the nationalist movement, through Parnell, Davitt and the IRB, gave it energy but also exposed it to the Parnell split.
  • The GAA’s identity as an Irish-only, amateur body made it a symbol of de-anglicisation, a core part of the Irish Ireland ideas later developed by the Gaelic League.

Documents and the views of each side

Documents on the GAA are letters, speeches and reports, and they reflect the aims and tensions of the movement.

  • Cusack’s article of October 1884 and his invitations are founding documents, expressing the aim of opening athletics to all classes.
  • Croke’s letter of December 1884 is the best-known statement of the GAA’s national purpose, and it should be read as a clerical and nationalist appeal against English fashions.
  • Davitt’s letters and speeches of support show the GAA’s links with the land movement; Parnell’s patronage brought political prestige.
  • Unionist newspapers and the Dublin Castle reports describe the GAA as seditious or as a cover for the IRB.
  • Reports of conventions and county board meetings are evidence of the dispute between moderates and the IRB, though they are written by partisans.
  • Historians such as Marcus de Búrca (The GAA: A History) and W.F. Mandle (The Gaelic Athletic Association and Irish Nationalist Politics) emphasise the connection between the GAA and nationalism, with Mandle stressing political motives and de Búrca the sporting ones.

Assessment: the GAA to 1891

The GAA’s early years show both a cultural achievement and the cost of political entanglement.

  • Achievement: it created a national organisation open to all classes, standardised the rules of hurling and football and built a parish network.
  • Limit: IRB infiltration, the quarrel with the Church, the American tour and the Parnell split almost destroyed it by 1891.
  • Contemporary nationalists saw it as a school for national feeling; unionists and the authorities saw it as Fenian; bishops saw it as useful but dangerous.
  • Historians now see the GAA as a central part of the cultural nationalism of the 1880s, which anticipated the Gaelic League of 1893.

Key terms

Anglicisation
The process by which English language, dress, games and customs replaced Irish ones, which the GAA and later the Gaelic League opposed.
De-anglicisation
The effort to reverse anglicisation by reviving the Irish language, games and customs; Hyde used the word in 1892.
Irish Ireland
The cultural nationalist idea that the nation should be built on Irish language, games and Catholic identity, rather than English models.
Gaelic Athletic Association
The body founded at Thurles on 1 November 1884 to promote Irish games and athletics.
Foreign games ban
The GAA rule banning members from playing or attending games such as cricket, rugby and soccer.
Parish club
A GAA club based on a parish, the local unit of the association.
Central Council
The GAA’s governing body, whose control was contested between moderates and the IRB in the later 1880s.
American Invasion
The 1888 tour of the United States by Irish hurlers and athletes, which lost money and disrupted the association.
Amateurism
The principle that sportsmen play without payment; the GAA kept it but opened sport to all classes.
