What history is, kinds of evidence and where they are kept

History is the past itself, the evidence that survives of it, and the written accounts historians build from that evidence.

  • The past itself is gone; no one can examine the Dublin lockout of 1913 directly, only what it left behind.
  • Evidence is whatever survives: documents, objects, images, buildings and recorded memories.
  • A written history is a historian’s account, shaped by the questions asked and the evidence found, so two historians can offer different histories of the same events.
  • Historical knowledge is provisional: new evidence or new questions can revise an account, as when the Pigott forgeries collapsed in 1889 and showed the letters against Parnell to be false.
  • A source is the item itself, such as a speech by Gladstone; it becomes evidence only when a historian uses it to answer a particular question, such as why Liberals split in 1886.
  • Public records (census returns, parliamentary papers, police reports) show official policy and statistics; private institutions’ records (union minute books, company papers, the GAA’s convention reports) show the interests of their members.
  • Letters, memoirs and eyewitness accounts give personal views, for example Davitt’s Leaves from a Prison Diary or accounts by participants in Bloody Sunday.
  • Newspapers, speeches and pamphlets show how events were presented to the public at the time.
  • Maps, photographs and political cartoons are visual evidence; oral testimony and radio or television transcripts are usually recorded long after events.
  • Repositories include archives (state records and the papers of organisations), libraries (printed books, newspapers and pamphlets) and museums (objects, banners and photographs), and each holds different kinds of source.

Interrogating a source and the stages of research

A historian cross-examines every source with the same set of questions before using it.

  • Who made it: a nationalist MP, a unionist editor, a bishop or a trade unionist each saw events from a position that shaped what they recorded.
  • Why it was made: to inform, persuade, defend an interest, record an event or claim credit afterwards.
  • When it was made: a report written during the election campaign of 1885 differs from a memoir written decades later.
  • In what circumstances: a statement made under pressure, in secret or before an inquiry may differ from a public speech.
  • For what audience: a speech to supporters aims to rally them; a letter to an opponent aims to persuade or warn.
  • Stages of research: choose a question, locate sources, evaluate them, compare them with each other, form an interpretation and test it against the evidence.
  • Primary sources were produced at the time of the events (a speech, a letter, a newspaper report, a cartoon); secondary sources are later accounts based on them (a modern book on Home Rule).
  • Primary does not mean reliable: a participant’s account is close to events but may be biased, and a secondary source may be more balanced but further from the facts.
  • A source can be both: a historian’s book is secondary for the period it describes but primary evidence for the thinking of the time it was written.

Fact, opinion, bias, propaganda and objectivity

Documents mix checkable facts with opinion and persuasion, and the historian has to separate them.

  • A fact can be checked against other evidence, for example that Lord Frederick Cavendish and Thomas Burke were killed in Phoenix Park on 6 May 1882.
  • An opinion is a judgement that evidence cannot settle, for example that the Land Act of 1881 was a betrayal of the tenants.
  • Bias is a consistent tilt in how a source presents events, shown by loaded words, selective detail and omission, for example a unionist newspaper describing a nationalist rally as a mob.
  • Propaganda is material designed to persuade a mass audience, using emotion, slogans and selected facts; Home Rule is Rome Rule is a unionist slogan that works this way.
  • A biased source is not worthless: it is good evidence of what its author and audience believed, even when poor evidence of what happened.
  • Objectivity is the aim of fair, evidence-based judgement, although no historian is free from all assumptions.
  • Strengths of a source include closeness to events, first-hand knowledge, precision and corroboration by others; limitations include bias, a narrow viewpoint, gaps, later revision and the survival of only some records.
  • Revising a view: historians change their accounts when new sources appear, when old ones are tested harder, or when new questions are asked, such as women’s roles in the suffrage movement.

The documents-based question and part (a): comprehension

Question 1 on the paper is the documents-based question on the prescribed topic, with four parts: comprehension, comparison, criticism and contextualisation.

  • Question 1 is worth 100 marks of the 400: part (a) comprehension 20, part (b) comparison 20, part (c) criticism 20 and part (d) contextualisation 40.
  • The documents, written or visual, come from one of the topic’s three case studies: the elections of 1885–86, Dublin 1913 or the GAA to 1891.
  • With four equal questions in about 170 minutes, plan roughly 40 minutes for Question 1: a few minutes each for parts (a) to (c) and the biggest share for part (d).
  • Part (a) asks what a document says or shows; the answer must come from the named document alone.
  • Step 1: read the source note giving the author, date and publication, then the document.
  • Step 2: underline the exact points the question asks for, and find the line for each.
  • Step 3: put each point in your own words with a detail from the document (a name, date, place or figure).
  • Step 4: do not add outside knowledge; part (a) is marked on what the document tells you, and extra background earns nothing.
  • Example approach: for a speech on Home Rule, list each argument the speaker gives, in order, with a short reference to each.

Part (b): comparison

Comparison asks how two documents are alike or different on a stated point, and every point needs evidence from both.

  • Choose the exact point the question names, such as attitude to Home Rule, to the strikers or to the GAA’s bans.
  • State what the first document says on that point, quoting or referring to a line.
  • State what the second says, then say whether they agree, differ or agree only in part.
  • A specific similarity or difference in both documents earns marks; a description of each document separately does not.
  • Explain the difference where you can, using the authors’ positions: for example a nationalist paper and a unionist paper describing the same crowd would use different words and numbers.
  • Compare like with like: a private letter and a public speech can differ because of audience rather than disagreement.
  • Use linking words such as whereas, both and unlike to keep every sentence a comparison.

Part (c): criticism and usefulness

Criticism asks how far a document can be trusted or how useful it is, and the answer tests provenance, purpose, viewpoint and reliability.

  • Provenance: who produced the document, when and where; a name and date alone are not an evaluation.
  • Purpose: why it was produced and for whom; a speech meant to rally supporters will present facts in their favour.
  • Viewpoint: the author’s party, religion, class or nationality, for example a landlord, a tenant, a priest or an employer.
  • Reliability: whether the claims are specific and supported by other evidence, or general and unsupported.
  • Cite a line from the document for each judgement, for example an emotional phrase as evidence of bias.
  • Balance the answer: say what the document is good evidence for as well as what it cannot show; a biased source can still be useful evidence of attitudes.
  • Usefulness questions ask how useful a document is for a stated enquiry, so measure it against that enquiry.
  • Questions on fact and opinion ask you to pick out one example of each, and questions on propaganda ask how the language and imagery persuade the audience.

Reading cartoons and photographs

Visual sources are read with the same questions as written ones, with extra attention to symbols and captions.

  • Identify every figure and what it stands for: Hibernia or Erin for Ireland, John Bull or Britannia for Britain, a statesman drawn from his known appearance.
  • Read the caption, labels and dates, because the meaning often rests on the words.
  • Note the exaggeration: caricature of faces, size, dress or animal imagery shows the artist’s attitude to a person or group.
  • Identify the message and the target audience: a cartoon in Punch addressed British readers, while one in a nationalist or unionist paper addressed supporters.
  • A cartoon is opinion, not record: use it as evidence of attitudes and propaganda, not of the facts of an event.
  • Ask whether a cartoon is hostile to Irish people or to a politician, and support the answer with a detail such as the way a figure is drawn.
  • A photograph shows what stood in front the camera, but the choice of subject, framing, staging and caption can mislead.
  • Example: a photograph of the Church Street ruins of September 1913 is good evidence of the state of Dublin housing, but does not show who was to blame.

Part (d): contextualisation

Part (d) is a short essay on the subject of the case study, using your own knowledge; it is not a retelling of the documents.

  • Part (d) carries 40 marks, twice as much as any other part, and is marked like a short essay on cumulative relevant points.
  • It asks about the case study’s subject, such as a leader’s strengths and weaknesses, how an organisation dealt with difficulties, or the causes and outcome of a dispute.
  • Open with the background and context, then deal with events in order, with dates, names and numbers, then outcomes and significance.
  • Use the documents sparingly as supporting evidence, referring to them by letter, but do not summarise them.
  • Cover both sides: for example the arguments of nationalists and unionists in 1885–86, employers and workers in 1913, or the Church, the IRB and the founders in the GAA.
  • Finish with a judgement that answers the question set, such as how far a leader succeeded or how significant an event was.
  • Draw on the rest of the topic for context: Home Rule and land for the elections, labour and Dublin’s poverty for 1913, and cultural revival and the Parnell split for the GAA.

Placing a document in its case study

Every document can be dated by the people, events and language it contains, and dating it is the first step in using it.

  • Elections of 1885–86: mention of the Third Reform Act or the new voters points to 1884–85; the balance of power, the Hawarden Kite or Salisbury points to late 1885; a bill, Chamberlain or the Liberal Unionists points to April–June 1886; the Orange card, Belfast rioting or the July poll points to mid-1886.
  • Typical voices for 1885–86: Parnell and Irish Party speakers, Gladstone, Chamberlain, Hartington, Lord Randolph Churchill, the Freeman’s Journal, the Belfast News-Letter, and Punch.
  • Dublin 1913: trams and Horse Show week point to late August; O’Connell Street, batons and a banned meeting point to 31 August; the Document, Askwith or AE’s letter to September–October; food ships, Save the Kiddies and the Citizen Army to October–November; return to work to January 1914.
  • Typical voices for 1913: Larkin, Connolly, William Martin Murphy, Archbishop Walsh, the Irish Worker, the Irish Independent, the TUC and the Dublin Metropolitan Police.
  • The GAA to 1891: Thurles and Hayes’ Hotel point to November 1884; Croke, Parnell and Davitt as patrons to 1884–85; Cusack’s removal and IRB influence to 1886–87; the first championships to 1887–88; the American tour to 1888; the Parnell split and collapsing membership to 1890–91.
  • Typical voices for the GAA: Cusack, Davin, Croke, Davitt, Parnell, the nationalist press, the RIC and Dublin Castle.
  • Once dated, ask who gains from the document’s version of events, and what it leaves out.

Assessment: strong and weak documents-based answers

Marks separate answers that use the evidence precisely from those that retell it or ignore it.

  • Strong comprehension answers use only the document and give precise details; weak ones add outside facts or copy whole passages.
  • Strong comparison answers name a point and give a line from each document; weak ones describe the two documents one after the other.
  • Strong criticism answers judge provenance, purpose and viewpoint with cited lines and reach a balanced verdict; weak ones say only that the source is biased.
  • Strong part (d) answers combine dated detail, two or more viewpoints and a clear judgement; weak ones paraphrase the documents or offer a general essay.
  • A strength of the format is that it rewards close reading; a limit is that it rewards answers that are tied closely to the case study and its documents.
  • Contemporary views and later historians’ views are both useful in part (d), for example how contemporaries saw Parnell or Larkin and how historians now judge them.

Key terms

Primary source
A source produced at or near the time of the events it describes, such as a speech, letter, newspaper report or cartoon.
Secondary source
A later account or interpretation based on primary sources, such as a modern history book.
Evidence
A source used to answer a particular historical question; the same source can be evidence for different questions.
Provenance
The origin of a document: who produced it, when, where and for what audience.
Bias
A consistent tilt in a source’s account arising from the author’s position, shown by loaded language and selective detail.
Propaganda
Material designed to persuade a mass audience by using emotion, slogans and selected facts.
Reliability
How far a source can be trusted as an accurate account, judged by provenance, purpose and corroboration.
Fact
A statement that can be checked against other evidence.
Opinion
A judgement or belief that cannot be settled by checking evidence.
Objectivity
The aim of fair, evidence-based judgement, recognising the historian’s own assumptions.
Contextualisation
Placing a document or event within the political, social and cultural circumstances of its time.
Corroboration
Testing a source’s claims against other independent evidence.
