Background: the new electorate and constituencies (1884–1885)

Two Acts rewrote the electoral map just before the 1885 election, and Parnell’s organised party was the main beneficiary.

  • The Representation of the People Act 1884 (the Third Reform Act) gave the vote to male householders and lodgers in the counties on the same terms as the boroughs, ending the old gap between town and country.
  • In Ireland the electorate rose from roughly 220,000 to roughly 740,000, more than tripling; the new voters were mostly small farmers and agricultural labourers (estimates vary).
  • The Redistribution of Seats Act 1885 abolished many small boroughs and divided the counties into single-member constituencies, though Ireland kept its 103 seats.
  • Single-member rural seats favoured a party organised in every parish, and the Irish National League had branches across the country, often led by priests.
  • Candidates were chosen at constituency conventions, and from 1884 each had to pledge to sit, act and vote with the Irish Party.
  • Voting remained in secret under the 1872 Ballot Act, so tenants could vote against their landlords without fear.
  • The Catholic hierarchy, led by Archbishop Walsh of Dublin and Archbishop Croke of Cashel, supported the party on the understanding that it would press for denominational education.

Parnell’s balance-of-power strategy (1884–1885)

Parnell’s aim was to bargain with both British parties and to give Home Rule to whichever offered more.

  • At Cork in January 1885 Parnell declared that no man had the right to fix the boundary to the march of a nation, a demand for more than a limited local parliament.
  • In 1884 Conservatives such as Lord Randolph Churchill joined the Irish MPs in attacking the Liberal Lord Lieutenant, Earl Spencer, over the Maamtrasna murders (August 1882), where Myles Joyce was hanged despite doubts about his guilt.
  • That common cause is sometimes called the Maamtrasna alliance, and it opened the way to co-operation between Parnell and the Conservatives.
  • On 9 June 1885 the Irish Party voted with the Conservatives against Gladstone’s budget and brought down his government; Lord Salisbury formed a minority Conservative ministry.
  • Salisbury’s Lord Lieutenant, Lord Carnarvon, met Parnell secretly in London in August 1885; the Crimes Act was allowed to lapse and the Ashbourne Act offered loans for tenant purchase.
  • Parnell’s manifesto of November 1885 told Irish voters in Britain to vote against Liberal candidates, because of Liberal coercion in 1881–82.
  • Joseph Chamberlain’s plan for an elected Central Board for Ireland, discussed in 1885 through Captain O’Shea, was rejected by Parnell and by the Cabinet and kept Home Rule on the table.
  • At Arklow in October 1885 Parnell set out a demand for a legislature with control of Irish domestic affairs, and he stressed that Ireland would not be satisfied with less.

The general election of November–December 1885

The election produced a hung Parliament in which the Irish Party held the balance, and it split Ireland along religious and regional lines.

  • The Irish Party won 85 of the 103 Irish seats, with one more at Liverpool Scotland, giving 86 MPs in all.
  • It won every seat outside Ulster except the two for Dublin University and a few in Dublin suburbs, and it swept the south and west.
  • In Ulster the party won 17 of 33 seats, in the Catholic-majority counties of the south and west of the province, while unionists held the Belfast seats and most of east Ulster.
  • In Great Britain the Liberals won about 335 seats (counts vary by method), the Conservatives about 249, and the Irish Party 86, so the Liberals’ lead over the Conservatives was almost exactly the Irish total.
  • That gave Parnell the decisive vote: the Irish Party plus the Conservatives could equal the Liberals, and the Irish Party could keep either in or out of office.
  • The Irish vote in British cities, especially Liverpool, helped the Conservatives in some constituencies, though its effect is hard to measure.
  • Parnell chose the moment well: he could now say that Ireland’s demand was clear, and that Home Rule could not be refused to a nation that had voted for it in this way.
  • Contemporary Liberals saw the results as proof that Ireland, minus Protestant Ulster, wanted Home Rule; contemporary Conservatives and unionists replied that two Irelands had voted.

The Hawarden Kite, Gladstone’s conversion and the First Home Rule Bill

Gladstone moved to Home Rule within weeks of the election, took office again in February 1886 and introduced a bill in April.

  • On 17 December 1885 newspapers published the Hawarden Kite: reports, leaked by Gladstone’s son Herbert, that Gladstone (at Hawarden in Flintshire) favoured Home Rule.
  • Salisbury’s ministry was defeated in January 1886 on an amendment about allotments for labourers (Jesse Collings), with the Irish voting against it, and Gladstone formed his third ministry in February.
  • Joseph Chamberlain, who joined the cabinet, resigned in March 1886 along with Sir George Trevelyan, and Lord Hartington declined to serve.
  • Gladstone introduced the Government of Ireland Bill on 8 April 1886.
  • The bill would have set up an Irish legislature in Dublin with an executive responsible to it; the Crown, defence, foreign policy, trade and the customs remained with Westminster.
  • Ireland was to pay a fixed share (one fifteenth) of imperial expenditure, and the Irish members would no longer sit at Westminster.
  • The Irish legislature was to have two orders, sitting together, designed to reassure Protestants and property owners, and was forbidden to establish or endow any religion.
  • A companion Land Purchase Bill would have offered loans to buy out the landlords; it was unpopular in Britain and was dropped, and it weakened the main bill.
  • Parnell accepted the bill as a basis for a settlement, though he objected to its financial terms and its control of the police.

The defeat of the bill (June 1886)

The Liberal split, not the Conservatives alone, defeated the First Home Rule Bill.

  • The bill was debated for several weeks in April–June 1886 and the vote came early on 8 June at second reading.
  • It was defeated by 341 votes to 311 (some sources give 343 to 313), a majority of 30.
  • About 93 Liberals, led by Hartington and Chamberlain, voted with the Conservatives against the bill; they became the Liberal Unionists.
  • Chamberlain argued that the bill would break up the empire and treat Ulster Protestants unfairly; the veteran radical John Bright also opposed it.
  • Conservatives such as Lord Randolph Churchill argued that Home Rule meant separation and the loss of the Protestant minority’s rights.
  • Gladstone’s supporters argued that coercion had failed and that Home Rule would give Ireland a settled government and reconcile it to the Crown.
  • After the defeat Gladstone dissolved Parliament and called a general election.

Unionist mobilisation: the Orange card and the Belfast riots

The crisis of 1886 created organised unionism in Ulster and the south.

  • Lord Randolph Churchill wrote in February 1886 that the Orange card was the one to play against Home Rule, and he spoke in the Ulster Hall, Belfast, on 22 February 1886.
  • A later letter of May 1886 gave him the phrase that Ulster would fight and Ulster would be right, a slogan unionists used for decades.
  • The Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union had been formed in 1885 by southern unionists, mainly landlords and professionals, to organise opposition to Home Rule.
  • Ulster Protestants established Loyalist Anti-Repeal organisations in 1886, and the Orange Order grew sharply; unionists feared that an Irish parliament would be a Catholic parliament.
  • The Belfast riots began on 4 June 1886 after the bill’s defeat was in prospect and continued into September.
  • At least 30 people were killed, and hundreds were injured; estimates of the death toll vary.
  • The Belfast violence deepened sectarian division, and both sides used it as evidence: nationalists blamed unionist mobs and the Orange Order, unionists blamed Home Rule agitation.

The July 1886 election and its outcomes

The voters rejected Gladstone, and the Conservatives governed for most of the next twenty years.

  • The July 1886 general election gave the Conservatives and Liberal Unionists a combined majority of more than 100 over the Gladstonian Liberals and the Irish Party.
  • Salisbury formed his second government, supported by the Liberal Unionists, who joined the Conservatives in coalition in 1895.
  • The Irish Party again won about 85 seats in Ireland, so the nationalist case was undiminished.
  • Home Rule was now the official policy of the Liberal Party, and the Liberal–Nationalist alliance was the central fact of Irish parliamentary politics until 1914.
  • Parnell was at the height of his power, with a disciplined party and a Liberal ally, until the Pigott forgeries and the O’Shea divorce.
  • The Liberal split turned Home Rule into a cause that could pass only when the Liberals had a majority independent of the Lords, which did not happen until the Parliament Act of 1911.
  • Unionism became a lasting organised force in Ireland and Britain, and the Conservative–Unionist alliance shaped policy up to the Ulster crisis.

Key personality: Charles Stewart Parnell (role in the 1885–1886 elections)

Parnell was the central figure in the case study: he made the Irish Party decisive and tied Home Rule to the Liberals.

  • He turned the National League into an election machine and imposed the pledge, so the party could deliver its votes as a bloc.
  • He chose to keep both British parties guessing, bargaining with Carnarvon and then supporting Gladstone, an example of his tactical skill.
  • He imposed Captain O’Shea as candidate in the Galway by-election of February 1886 against Tim Healy’s objections, which showed his control but sowed resentment.
  • He was a participant who led the campaign, and nationalist contemporaries saw the 85 seats as a personal triumph and a national mandate.
  • Unionists and many Conservatives saw him as the leader of a movement linked to violence and the Land War, and The Times later published forged letters aimed at this reputation.
  • Strengths examiners ask about include his discipline, his tactical sense, his appeal to both Fenians and constitutionalists and his skill in the Commons; weaknesses include his reliance on the Liberal alliance, his aloofness and the secrecy of his private life.
  • Historians such as F.S.L. Lyons describe 1885–86 as Parnell’s zenith; others note that his strategy depended on Gladstone surviving the Liberal split.

Views of each side and the kinds of documents

Documents on this case study are speeches, newspaper reports and cartoons, and each can be placed by who produced it and when.

  • Nationalists (Freeman’s Journal, United Ireland) presented the 1885 result as a national verdict and the First Home Rule Bill as a just but limited settlement.
  • Unionists (Belfast News-Letter, the Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union) presented Home Rule as a threat to the empire, to property and to Protestants.
  • Gladstone’s speeches argued that Home Rule was a moral duty after the failure of coercion; his critics called it a surrender to agitators.
  • Liberal Unionist speeches, by Chamberlain and Hartington, argued that Irish self-government would endanger the union of the two islands.
  • Punch, drawn by John Tenniel and others, usually depicted Irish agitators as violent or ape-like, as in the Irish Frankenstein of 1882, and took an anti-Home Rule line; some Irish and radical papers published pro-Home Rule cartoons.
  • A cartoon can be placed by its date and symbols: Gladstone as the old man with an axe, Parnell as a tempter or bully, Hibernia as a woman, John Bull as a stout man.
  • Historians differ on why Gladstone took up Home Rule: genuine conviction after the 1885 result, or tactical manoeuvre to keep the Liberals together, as argued by A.B. Cooke and John Vincent in The Governing Passion (1974).

Assessment: the 1885–1886 elections

The elections were a triumph for the Irish Party and a defeat for Home Rule.

  • Achievement: the Irish Party won a national mandate, forced Home Rule onto the British agenda and won over a major British party.
  • Limit: the party could not pass a bill without a British majority, and Gladstone’s conversion split the Liberals and left the Conservatives in power.
  • Achievement: the bill’s defeat was narrow and the Liberal alliance gave nationalists a realistic route to Home Rule.
  • Limit: the elections showed that Ulster Protestants were opposed, a problem that Parnell’s party never solved.
  • Contemporary nationalists blamed the Liberal Unionists and the House of Commons; contemporary unionists said the vote showed the Union was safe.
  • Later historians see 1886 as the origin of the Ulster question as well as of the Liberal–Nationalist alliance, which makes it a turning point whatever its immediate failure.

Key terms

Home Rule
The demand for an Irish parliament in Dublin to manage Irish domestic affairs, the central question of the 1885–86 elections.
Democracy
Government through elected representatives; the 1884 Act widened it by extending the vote to county householders.
Third Reform Act
The Representation of the People Act 1884, which gave county householders the vote and more than tripled the Irish electorate.
Redistribution of Seats Act 1885
The Act that abolished many small boroughs and created mostly single-member constituencies.
Balance of power
The position of a smaller party whose votes decide which of two larger parties can govern.
Hung Parliament
A Parliament in which no party has an overall majority, as after the December 1885 election.
Hawarden Kite
The December 1885 press leak, via Herbert Gladstone, that Gladstone had become a convert to Home Rule.
Liberal Unionists
The Liberals led by Hartington and Chamberlain who voted against the First Home Rule Bill and allied with the Conservatives.
Orange card
Lord Randolph Churchill’s term for the appeal to Ulster Protestant fears as a weapon against Home Rule.
Pledge
The undertaking, required of Irish Party candidates from 1884, to sit, act and vote with the party.
