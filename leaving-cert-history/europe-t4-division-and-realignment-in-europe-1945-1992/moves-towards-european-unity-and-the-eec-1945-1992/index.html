Key personality: Jean Monnet

Monnet was a French planner and diplomat who designed the first European communities and is widely called a founding father of European integration.

  • Born in Cognac in 1888, he worked in the family brandy business and then in international finance and diplomacy, including as deputy secretary-general of the League of Nations from 1919 to 1923.
  • During the Second World War he coordinated Anglo-French supply and proposed a union between France and Britain in June 1940, which was rejected.
  • As head of the French planning commission from 1946 he produced the Monnet Plan for modernising French industry, which relied on state investment.
  • He drafted the Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950, which proposed pooling French and West German coal and steel production under a joint authority.
  • He was the first president of the High Authority of the European Coal and Steel Community from 1952 to 1955, and in 1955 he founded the Action Committee for the United States of Europe.
  • Contemporaries in France were divided: supporters praised him as a builder of peace, while Gaullists opposed the supranational powers he wanted.
  • Historians present him as an enthusiast for supranationalism who designed a method of gradual integration, though some stress that his role was as a planner rather than a politician.

Key personality: Jacques Delors

Delors was the French socialist who led the European Commission from 1985 and relaunched integration through the single market and monetary union.

  • Born in Paris in 1925, he worked in the French planning and social service systems, and he was French finance minister in the Mitterrand government from 1981 to 1984.
  • He became president of the European Commission in January 1985, a post he held until 1995, the longest tenure in the Commission's history at that time.
  • He drove the Single European Act of 1986, which set a deadline of 1992 for completing a single market without internal border controls.
  • He chaired the committee whose 1989 report set out a three-stage route to economic and monetary union, which became the basis of the Maastricht Treaty.
  • He was a participant in the Maastricht negotiations of 1991, which created the European Union and committed member states to a single currency.
  • Margaret Thatcher opposed his federalist ideas and his social policy, and she said the Commission was overreaching; supporters across the EEC credited him with reviving the Community.
  • Historians credit him with the relaunch of the 1980s, though they debate how far his federalist vision was shared by member states.

Reasons for unity and the Council of Europe

After 1945 Western Europeans sought unity to prevent a return of war, to contain Soviet power and to rebuild their economies.

  • Reasons for unity after 1945: the wish to end Franco-German rivalry, the Soviet threat and the need for economic recovery, helped by American support for integration through Marshall Aid.
  • The Council of Europe was founded in London on 5 May 1949 by ten states, with its seat at Strasbourg, and it was a forum for debate rather than a source of binding law.
  • Its consultative assembly in Strasbourg lacked the power to make laws, which disappointed federalists who had wanted a European parliament with real authority.
  • The European Convention on Human Rights, drafted within the Council of Europe, was opened for signature in 1950 and entered into force in 1953.
  • Winston Churchill called in a speech at Zurich in September 1946 for a kind of United States of Europe, built on reconciliation between France and Germany, though he expected Britain to stay outside.
  • The Hague Congress of May 1948 brought together several hundred European political figures, with Churchill presiding, and led to the creation of the Council of Europe the following year.
  • Federalists such as Altiero Spinelli and Paul-Henri Spaak wanted a political federation, while the British and Scandinavian governments favoured cooperation between states.
  • The Benelux customs union of Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg came into effect in 1948 and served as a model for later integration.

The Schuman Plan and the European Coal and Steel Community

The first supranational community was created to pool the coal and steel industries of France and West Germany.

  • The Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950 proposed a joint high authority for French and West German coal and steel, open to other European states.
  • The Treaty of Paris, signed on 18 April 1951 by France, West Germany, Italy and the Benelux countries, created the European Coal and Steel Community, which came into force in July 1952.
  • The ECSC's High Authority had powers that were supranational: it could set prices and quotas and impose fines on firms, without needing the approval of member governments.
  • Coal and steel were the raw materials of the wartime arms economies, and pooling them under a common authority made a new war between France and Germany harder to imagine; the Saar and the Ruhr had been sources of Franco-German friction.
  • The Schuman Plan's economic logic was that a common market in coal and steel would give each state access to more resources at lower cost.
  • Robert Schuman, the French foreign minister, announced the plan on 9 May 1950; Monnet and Schuman had agreed that a joint authority for coal and steel would make war between France and Germany materially impossible.
  • Konrad Adenauer welcomed the plan because it treated West Germany as an equal and ended the international control of the Ruhr; the British Labour government declined to take part.
  • The ECSC also had a Common Assembly, a Council of Ministers and a Court of Justice, which became the model for the later institutions.

The failure of the European Defence Community

The attempt to create a European army under supranational control failed, and it pushed integration towards economic rather than military union.

  • The European Defence Community was proposed in 1950 by the French prime minister René Pleven, as an answer to West German rearmament within a supranational army.
  • The treaty establishing the EDC was signed in May 1952, but the French National Assembly rejected it on 30 August 1954.
  • The rejection was driven by fears of German rearmament, Gaullist and communist opposition, and the view of some that national sovereignty over the army must be kept; Britain had refused to join.
  • After the EDC failed, West Germany joined NATO in 1955, and the Western European Union was created to include West Germany in defence arrangements.

The Treaties of Rome and the European Economic Community

The Treaties of Rome created a customs union and a common market, and the EEC became the core of European integration.

  • The Messina Conference of June 1955 relaunched the project, and the Spaak Report of 1956 proposed a common market and atomic energy community.
  • The Treaties of Rome were signed on 25 March 1957 by the six ECSC members and came into force on 1 January 1958.
  • The EEC aimed at a customs union, with internal tariffs removed and a common external tariff, and at free movement of goods, services, capital and workers.
  • Euratom was created by the second treaty to develop nuclear energy for civil use across the six states.
  • The Common Agricultural Policy began in 1962 and set common prices for farm products, and it quickly became the largest item of the EEC's budget.
  • The customs union was completed in July 1968, eighteen months ahead of the original timetable.
  • The European Free Trade Association, formed in 1960 by Britain, Austria, Denmark, Norway, Portugal, Sweden and Switzerland, was a looser alternative for states that rejected supranationalism.
  • The EEC's institutions were the Commission (which proposed legislation, led at first by Walter Hallstein), the Council of Ministers, the European Parliament (then an appointed assembly) and the Court of Justice; the three communities' executives were merged in 1967.
  • The first president of the Commission, Walter Hallstein of West Germany, served from 1958 to 1967 and was a leading federalist.
  • Britain applied to join in 1961 under Harold Macmillan after the success of the EEC, and Ireland applied in 1961 and 1967 alongside Britain, but both applications were halted by de Gaulle's vetoes.

De Gaulle, the veto and the empty chair crisis

Charles de Gaulle opposed supranational institutions and used vetoes and a boycott to block British membership and to assert national sovereignty.

  • De Gaulle vetoed British membership of the EEC on 14 January 1963 and again on 27 November 1967, on the grounds that Britain was too closely tied to the USA.
  • The empty chair crisis began in June 1965 when France withdrew its representatives from the Council of Ministers over the financing of the CAP and the powers of the Commission.
  • The Luxembourg Compromise of January 1966 gave each member state a veto over matters it regarded as vital national interests, which weakened majority voting.
  • De Gaulle's vision was a Europe of nations, cooperating on intergovernmental lines, which contrasted with the federalist and supranational model of Monnet.

Enlargement, the EMS and the relaunch under Delors

The EEC grew from six to twelve members, and the 1980s relaunch aimed at completing the single market and creating monetary union.

  • Britain, Ireland and Denmark joined the EEC on 1 January 1973, and Greece joined in 1981.
  • Spain and Portugal joined on 1 January 1986, which brought the membership to twelve.
  • The European Monetary System was launched on 13 March 1979, and its Exchange Rate Mechanism linked member currencies within fixed bands.
  • Britain's budget rebate, agreed at Fontainebleau in June 1984, reduced its net contribution, and it eased the dispute over the budget that had dogged the Community.
  • The Single European Act, signed in February 1986 and in force from July 1987, extended qualified majority voting and set the goal of a single market by 1992.
  • The Maastricht Treaty, signed on 7 February 1992, created the European Union, set out a timetable for economic and monetary union and introduced European citizenship.
  • The Hague summit of December 1969 agreed to enlarge the Community and to work towards monetary union, and membership talks with Britain, Ireland, Denmark and Norway followed; Norway's voters rejected membership in 1972.
  • Ireland joined after a referendum in May 1972 in which about 83 percent voted in favour; Britain confirmed its membership in a referendum in June 1975.
  • The first direct elections to the European Parliament were held in June 1979.
  • The Delors Commission published a White Paper in 1985 listing nearly 300 measures to remove physical, technical and fiscal barriers, and in 1989 the Social Charter set out workers' rights.
  • Maastricht set the aim of a single currency by 1999 at the latest, set up a common foreign and security policy and cooperation on justice and home affairs, and allowed Britain to opt out of the single currency and the social chapter.

Assessment: federal Europe or a Europe of nations?

Historians and politicians disagree about whether integration was a steady advance or a series of compromises between federalists and nationalists.

  • Achievements: peace between France and Germany, the creation of a single market and the growth of membership from six to twelve.
  • Limits: the CAP's costs, the British budget dispute, de Gaulle's vetoes and the failure of the EDC, which showed that defence remained a national matter.
  • Federalists regarded the Maastricht Treaty as the beginning of a political union, while intergovernmentalists insisted that member states remained the decision-makers.
  • Later historians stress that the EEC's success rested on the compromises reached between national governments, not on the supranational method alone.
  • Effects of unity: trade between members grew rapidly, farmers had guaranteed prices under the CAP, poorer regions received money from the structural funds, and citizens gained rights to live and work across the Community.
  • Thatcher's Bruges speech of 1988 (see the notes on Thatcher) was the sharpest statement of the intergovernmental position, and she opposed Delors's drive towards a social dimension and a single currency.

Key terms

Common market
A trading area in which goods, services, capital and workers move freely between member states, with common external tariffs.
Federal Europe
The vision of a united Europe with supranational institutions and a central authority, opposed by intergovernmentalists who wanted national governments to decide.
European Coal and Steel Community
The 1951 community that pooled French, West German, Italian and Benelux coal and steel production under a supranational High Authority.
Empty chair crisis
The French boycott of the EEC Council of Ministers from June 1965 over the CAP and the powers of the Commission.
Luxembourg Compromise
The January 1966 agreement that let member states use a veto on matters they regarded as vital national interests.
Single European Act
The 1986 treaty that set the goal of a single market by 1992 and extended majority voting in the EEC.
European Monetary System
The 1979 arrangement that linked member currencies through fixed exchange rate bands, forerunner of the single currency.
Supranational
Describing institutions, like the ECSC High Authority and the Commission, whose decisions bind member states and which stand above national governments.
Intergovernmentalism
The view that European cooperation should be decided by national governments, which keep their veto, rather than by central institutions.
Common Agricultural Policy
The EEC's system, begun in 1962, of common farm prices and support for farmers, financed from a common budget.
Customs union
A group of states that remove tariffs between themselves and impose a common tariff on imports from outside.
