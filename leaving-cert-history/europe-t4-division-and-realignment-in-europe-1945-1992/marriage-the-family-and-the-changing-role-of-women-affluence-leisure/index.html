Key personality: Simone de Beauvoir (The Second Sex and feminism)

De Beauvoir was a French philosopher and writer whose 1949 study The Second Sex became a founding text of modern feminism.

  • Born in Paris in 1908, she taught philosophy and became the partner of Jean-Paul Sartre, with whom she shared a close intellectual life for decades.
  • The Second Sex was published in two volumes in 1949, and it argued that women were defined as the 'Other' by a male-centred society.
  • Her best-known line, from The Second Sex, is that one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman, a statement that separated biological sex from social gender.
  • She was a witness to the Second World War and the Occupation of Paris, and her later activism made her a participant in the feminist movement of the 1970s.
  • In April 1971 she signed the Manifesto of the 343, in which women declared that they had had abortions and called for their legalisation.
  • Contemporaries were divided: many male intellectuals and conservative commentators mocked the book, while younger women read it as a guide to their own position.
  • Historians now treat the book as a foundational text of second-wave feminism, though later critics questioned its assumptions about universal experience.
  • She passed the highly competitive agrégation in philosophy in 1929, where she came second to Sartre, and she was among the first women to teach philosophy in French lycées.
  • She argued in the book that women's inferior position was created by history, law, religion and education, not by biology, and that women should win economic independence.
  • The Catholic Church put The Second Sex on its Index of Forbidden Books in 1956, and some on the left attacked it too; it sold very widely and was translated into English in 1953.
  • In the 1970s she joined the Women's Liberation Movement in France, campaigned for the legalisation of abortion and became a leading voice of second-wave feminism; she died in 1986.

Post-war family life and the baby boom

The years after 1945 saw marriage and childbirth rise, and a more domestic model of family life became the norm in many Western European countries.

  • The baby boom in Western Europe ran roughly from 1946 to the mid-1960s, and birth rates were higher than in the 1930s.
  • Marriage was earlier and more common than before the war, and the nuclear family of two parents and their children became the ideal.
  • Housing shortages after the war meant that many young couples lived with relatives, and public housing programmes were used to increase family accommodation.
  • The post-war family was supported by family allowances, which were extended in France in 1945–46 and paid in other countries through social insurance schemes.
  • The new emphasis on the housewife and mother reflected the wish to restore stability after the war, and conservative parties and churches promoted the family as the basis of society.
  • In West Germany the law until 1977 allowed a husband to decide whether his wife could take a job, and in France married women won the right to work and open a bank account without their husband's consent only in 1965.
  • Cinema, magazines and television presented the ideal of the happy housewife with labour-saving appliances.

Women in the workforce and the feminist challenge

Women's employment grew after the 1950s, and women campaigned for equal pay, legal rights and control over their bodies.

  • The number of married women in paid employment rose steadily from the 1950s, especially in service and clerical work.
  • Article 119 of the Treaty of Rome required equal pay for equal work between men and women, a provision that the European Court of Justice enforced in the Defrenne case of 1976.
  • The Equal Pay Act of 1970 in Britain required equal pay for men and women doing work of equal value, and it took full effect in 1975 alongside the Sex Discrimination Act.
  • The contraceptive pill was approved in the USA in 1960 and became widely used in Western Europe in the 1960s, giving women control over childbearing.
  • France legalised contraception with the Neuwirth Law of 1967, and the Veil Law of 17 January 1975 legalised abortion in its first ten weeks, sponsored by the health minister Simone Veil.
  • The Manifesto of the 343 in 1971 publicly called for abortion to be legalised, and it was followed by campaigns by groups such as Choisir.
  • Women's work was mostly in low-paid jobs such as teaching, nursing, secretarial work and factory assembly, and women were paid less than men and were barred from many professions.
  • Second-wave feminism arose in the 1960s, influenced by Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963), and it demanded equal pay, nurseries and reproductive freedom; the Mouvement de libération des femmes (MLF) was formed in France in 1970.
  • Britain's Abortion Act of 1967 legalised abortion on medical grounds, West Germany reformed its abortion law in 1974–76, and Italy legalised abortion in 1978.
  • The Catholic Church opposed contraception and abortion, and Pope Paul VI's encyclical Humanae Vitae (1968) reaffirmed the ban on artificial birth control (see the notes on the Second Vatican Council).

Divorce and family law reform

Divorce became easier in most Western European countries after 1969, and this changed the legal basis of marriage.

  • The Divorce Reform Act of 1969 in Britain allowed divorce on the grounds of irretrievable breakdown, taking effect in 1971.
  • West Germany introduced no-fault divorce in its reform of marriage law in 1977, which replaced the requirement to prove the other party's fault.
  • In Italy, a referendum of 12 May 1974 upheld the divorce law of 1970 against an attempt to repeal it.
  • Divorce rates rose across the region in the 1970s, and single-parent families became a growing category of households.
  • Sweden and Denmark had liberal divorce laws earlier; Italy legalised divorce in 1970 and Spain in 1981, while Ireland, whose constitution banned it, legalised divorce only after a referendum in 1995.
  • Family law reform in West Germany and in Italy (1975) replaced the idea of the husband as head of household with equal rights for spouses.
  • The rise in divorce and in working mothers led to debate about the future of the family, with conservatives blaming the changes on permissiveness.

Affluence and the consumer society

Rising incomes in the 1950s and 1960s allowed families to buy consumer durables, cars and holidays, which transformed daily life.

  • Households acquired refrigerators, washing machines, televisions and cars, and the Volkswagen Beetle became one of the most common cars in West Germany.
  • Self-service supermarkets spread across Western Europe in the 1960s, and they changed how families shopped and the goods available to them.
  • Hire purchase and consumer credit made it possible for families to buy goods before they could afford them outright.
  • Advertising and television encouraged consumption, and critics such as Vance Packard in The Hidden Persuaders (1957) argued that advertising manipulated consumers.
  • Car ownership expanded sharply: in the 1950s the Fiat 500, the Citroën 2CV and the Mini made cars affordable for ordinary families.
  • Television spread rapidly in the 1950s and 1960s, becoming the centre of the home and the main form of evening leisure.
  • Critics included John Kenneth Galbraith, whose The Affluent Society (1958) argued that private affluence went with public squalor, and the French Situationists, whose Guy Debord wrote The Society of the Spectacle (1967).

Leisure time and mass tourism

Paid holidays and rising incomes created mass tourism, and the Mediterranean coast became a destination for northern Europeans.

  • Paid annual holidays became standard in Western Europe in the 1950s and 1960s, extending the time that workers could spend away from work.
  • Package holidays to Spain, Italy and the Greek islands grew rapidly in the 1960s and 1970s, and the Costa Brava became a symbol of the trend.
  • Car ownership made domestic holidays and weekend trips possible, and camping and caravanning became popular in Britain and Scandinavia.
  • Herbert Marcuse, in One-Dimensional Man (1964), argued that consumer society encouraged conformity and suppressed critical thought.
  • Working hours fell, with the five-day week and the forty-hour week spreading in the 1960s, and paid holidays were extended to three or four weeks.
  • Charter flights and package tours brought the Spanish Costa del Sol and the Balearic islands within reach of workers; Club Méditerranée, founded in 1950, offered all-inclusive holidays.
  • Leisure included spectator sport, cinema, television, record-buying and home improvement, which show the rise of mass leisure industries.

Differences between East and West

The communist states offered women employment and childcare but not the same consumer choice, and their feminist movements were limited.

  • In East Germany a far higher proportion of women went out to work than in West Germany, supported by state childcare and the expectation that women would work.
  • Communist states promoted equal rights in law, but domestic responsibilities for housework and childcare remained largely with women.
  • Consumer goods in the East were scarcer and of lower quality, so the consumer society was far less developed than in the West.
  • In the Soviet Union abortion was legalised again in 1955, women formed a large part of the workforce including doctors and engineers, and Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space in 1963, but women still did most of the housework, the so-called double burden.
  • East Germany offered paid maternity leave, a baby year from 1976 and state nurseries, and its divorce rate was high.
  • East German holidays were organised by the trade union federation in union holiday homes and Baltic resorts, and Black Sea resorts served the Soviet bloc, while travel to the West was restricted.
  • East Germans watched West German television and were conscious of the gap in goods; in the East cars such as the Trabant and Wartburg required waiting lists of years.

Assessment: liberation or consumerism?

Historians debate whether the changes of the period liberated women and families or extended consumer pressures into private life.

  • Achievements: legal equality in employment, access to contraception and abortion in many states, and more choice in marriage and divorce.
  • Limits: women still bore most domestic work, pay gaps persisted, and the consumer society tied family life to paid work and credit.
  • Contemporaries disagreed about the family: conservative churches and politicians defended the traditional family, while feminists demanded new rights.
  • Later historians see the 1960s and 1970s as a turning point, but they stress that the gains were uneven across social classes and between countries.

Key terms

Feminism
A movement and set of ideas that argue for equal rights and opportunities for women, and that challenge the social roles assigned to them.
Consumer society
A society in which the purchase of goods and services plays a central role in economic life and in people's identities.
Affluence
A condition of widespread rising incomes that allowed many households to buy consumer goods and enjoy leisure time.
Baby boom
The sharp rise in birth rates in Western countries from about 1946 to the mid-1960s.
Equal pay
The principle that men and women should receive the same pay for work of equal value, enshrined in Article 119 of the Treaty of Rome.
Consumer credit
Loans and hire purchase arrangements that allowed households to buy goods before paying for them in full.
Second-wave feminism
The women's movement of the 1960s and 1970s that campaigned for equal pay, reproductive rights and an end to the social roles that confined women to the home.
Package holiday
A holiday sold as a single price covering travel, accommodation and sometimes meals, which brought foreign travel within reach of workers from the 1960s.
The Second Sex
Simone de Beauvoir's 1949 book arguing that women were defined as the Other by a male-centred society.
