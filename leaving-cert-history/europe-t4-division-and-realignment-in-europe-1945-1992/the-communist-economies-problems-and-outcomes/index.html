The Soviet planning model and its export

Communist economies were run by central planning, in which the state set production targets and allocated resources, and this model was copied across eastern Europe.

  • Gosplan, the state planning committee, drew up five-year plans that set output targets for industry, agriculture and trade.
  • Priority was given to heavy industry, steel, coal, machinery and armaments, while consumer goods and housing were neglected.
  • Collectivisation of agriculture was carried through in the Soviet Union in the early 1930s, and was imposed on most eastern European farms after 1945, with the exception of Poland, where most farms stayed private.
  • Comecon, founded in January 1949, coordinated trade and planning among the Soviet bloc, and trade was largely conducted in non-convertible currencies at prices set by planners.
  • Stalin's five-year plans from 1928 built heavy industry and cities at great human cost, and collectivisation of farms in the early 1930s, together with the famine of 1932–33, killed millions.
  • In eastern Europe, industry, banks and large estates were nationalised in 1945–48, and Six-Year or Five-Year Plans on the Soviet pattern were introduced from 1949–51, with new steel towns such as Nowa Huta in Poland and Stalinstadt in East Germany.
  • Comecon was set up in response to the Marshall Plan; in practice it became a system of trade with the Soviet Union, which supplied cheap raw materials and energy and took manufactured goods, so the east European economies became dependent on Soviet supplies.
  • In 1949–53 eastern Europe's trade shifted from the West to the Soviet Union, and reparations and joint companies in Hungary, Romania and East Germany took resources for the Soviet economy.
  • Output was driven by pressing workers to exceed their norms, as in the Stakhanovite campaigns, and consumer goods were rationed in the early 1950s.

Problems: shortages, inefficiency and low innovation

Central planning produced chronic shortages of goods, poor quality and slow adoption of new technology, which undermined the economies over time.

  • Planners set targets in gross output terms, which encouraged factories to produce heavy and low-quality goods rather than goods that consumers wanted.
  • Shortages of consumer goods, meat, housing and cars were common, and long queues and black markets were a permanent feature of daily life.
  • Innovation was slow because managers had little incentive to adopt new technology, and the state discouraged risk-taking.
  • The arms race and the cost of maintaining an army and military industries absorbed a large share of national output, especially in the Soviet Union.
  • Soviet agriculture remained unproductive, with a large share of the labour force employed on farms, and the Soviet Union imported grain from the 1960s onwards.
  • Prices were fixed by the state rather than by supply and demand, so shortages in shops coexisted with surpluses of unwanted goods in warehouses.
  • Housing was built in standard blocks and was often overcrowded; in East Germany the wait for a car such as the Trabant could be many years.
  • Environmental damage was severe, as industrial regions such as northern Bohemia, Upper Silesia and the Aral Sea basin were polluted by heavy industry and irrigation.
  • The economies relied on extensive growth, adding labour and raw materials, and could not move to intensive growth based on technology and productivity once the supply of new workers dried up in the 1970s.

Reform attempts: Khrushchev, Kosygin and the Hungarian model

Several reforms tried to introduce market elements or decentralisation, but most were partial and some were reversed.

  • Khrushchev's 1957 decentralisation of industry replaced ministries with regional economic councils, a reform that was later reversed.
  • Alexei Kosygin's reform of September 1965 gave enterprises more control over profits and pricing, but it did not end central control of investment.
  • Hungary's New Economic Mechanism of 1968 allowed limited market prices and private enterprise in services, and it became a model of partial reform.
  • Hungary's policy under János Kádár, later called goulash communism, tolerated small private plots and enterprises and gave citizens more consumer goods than in other bloc states.
  • Czechoslovakia's New Economic Model of the 1960s, developed by the economist Ota Šik, proposed market mechanisms that the 1968 invasion ended.
  • Khrushchev tried to lift the production of consumer goods and housing, launched a mass housebuilding programme, and promised to overtake the USA in per capita output, but the targets were not met.
  • The 1965 Kosygin reforms were abandoned within a few years because ministries and the party feared the loss of control, and the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968 discouraged further experiment.
  • Yugoslavia, outside Comecon after 1948, developed a system of worker self-management and market socialism, and allowed its citizens to travel and work abroad; but it ran up heavy foreign debt and suffered high inflation in the 1980s.
  • Hungary's reforms gave managers more freedom and allowed a second economy of private work after hours, but the state kept control of the main industries and prices.

The Brezhnev era and economic stagnation

Under Leonid Brezhnev from 1964 to 1982 the Soviet economy grew more slowly, and the period became known as the era of stagnation.

  • Growth in the Soviet economy slowed in the 1970s, as the labour force grew more slowly and the rise in productivity fell.
  • The Soviet Union earned large hard currency revenues from oil and gas exports after the price rises of 1973 and 1979, which let the leadership buy Western grain and technology and postpone reform; when oil prices fell in the mid-1980s this support vanished.
  • The Soviet leadership avoided reform, and living standards for most people improved only slowly during the 1970s.
  • Under Brezhnev the Soviet Union reached rough nuclear parity with the USA and increased its military spending, which took a large share of national output at the cost of consumer goods and investment.
  • The war in Afghanistan from 1979 added to the burden, and the system of rewarding party loyalty rather than performance led to corruption and an ageing leadership.
  • Consumers experienced shortages of basic goods, and the black market, bribery and informal networks (blat) became part of ordinary life.

Poland: price rises, protest and Western debt

Poland's attempts to raise living standards by borrowing from the West ended in debt and repeated waves of protest.

  • Protests in Gdańsk and other Baltic ports in December 1970, against food price rises, led to the fall of Władysław Gomułka and his replacement by Edward Gierek.
  • Gierek borrowed heavily from Western banks to finance imports of machinery and consumer goods, which were intended to modernise industry and win public support.
  • The June 1976 price rises led to strikes and protests, and the government withdrew most of the increases within days.
  • By 1980 Poland's foreign debt had become unsustainable, and the strikes of that summer at the Gdańsk shipyard led to the founding of Solidarity.
  • Poland's Western debt rose to more than 20 billion dollars by 1980, and a large share of its export earnings went on servicing it.
  • Price rises announced on 1 July 1980 sparked strikes that spread to Gdańsk in August; the economy had shrunk and shops were bare of meat.
  • Gierek's policy of importing Western technology failed because the new factories produced goods that were not competitive in Western markets, and agricultural output suffered from a lack of investment.
  • Poland's economic crisis was a cause of Solidarity, and the economic collapse in the 1980s helped to bring the communists to the Round Table talks of 1989.

Gorbachev, the East–West gap and the end of the system

By the late 1980s the economic gap between the communist states and the West had widened, and the reform attempts came too late.

  • Mikhail Gorbachev's perestroika aimed to restructure the economy, but it coincided with falling oil prices in 1986, which cut the Soviet Union's hard currency earnings.
  • Romania under Nicolae Ceaușescu repaid its foreign debt in the late 1980s by cutting imports and imposing severe austerity, and the population suffered shortages of food, heating and electricity.
  • East Germany was the most productive economy of the bloc, but it too depended on West German credit and trade concessions by the late 1980s.
  • The end of the Soviet bloc's economic system left a legacy of obsolete industry, environmental damage and unemployment that the successor states had to address.
  • Gorbachev's economic reforms included the Law on State Enterprise (1987), which gave factories more freedom, and the 1988 law on cooperatives, which allowed private business; they weakened central control without creating a functioning market.
  • By 1989–90 the Soviet economy was suffering from shortages, a growing budget deficit, rationing in some cities and falling output.
  • The gap with the West was visible by 1989: East German and Czechoslovak citizens could see how much better West Germans and Austrians lived on television and through travel, and living standards in the Soviet Union lagged far behind.
  • East Germany's economy had been propped up by loans from West Germany, including a billion-mark credit in 1983, and the German monetary union of July 1990 exposed its industries to competition and led to mass closures.

The satellite economies compared

The bloc was not uniform, and examiners reward comparison of different states.

  • Czechoslovakia had been the most industrialised state in the region before the war, and its living standards were the highest in the bloc in the 1950s, but it stagnated under rigid planning in the 1960s and was the home of Ota Šik's reform.
  • East Germany was the most advanced economy in the bloc and the showcase of socialism, but it lost skilled workers to the West until the Wall was built in 1961.
  • Bulgaria and Romania were still largely agricultural in 1945 and were industrialised rapidly, Romania developing heavy industry, oil and chemicals and pursuing an independent foreign policy under Ceaușescu.
  • Ceaușescu's Romania exported food and fuel to pay its debts, rationed food and electricity in the 1980s, and planned to demolish thousands of villages in his systematisation programme.
  • Hungary's partial reforms and Poland's private farming made those states more flexible than the Soviet Union, but their dependence on Western credit made them vulnerable when interest rates and prices rose around 1980.

Assessment: achievements and failures of communist economies

Communist economies achieved rapid industrialisation and social welfare, but they failed to sustain growth or satisfy consumers.

  • Achievements: rapid industrialisation, near-universal literacy, free health care and education, and full employment, including high employment of women.
  • Limits: chronic shortages, low quality, poor housing, environmental damage and a standard of living that fell further behind the West.
  • Contemporary Western accounts often stressed the failure of the planned economy, while Soviet and bloc officials emphasised the achievements of industrialisation and social welfare.
  • Later historians stress both the system's real successes in mobilising resources and the structural limits that made it impossible to sustain growth without political reform.
  • Eastern Europe's communist states did expand education, cheap housing and health care, but their living standards fell behind West European levels by the 1970s.
  • The final judgement of historians is that the planned economy could build basic industry, but could not adapt to the information-based economies of the 1980s.

Key terms

Communism
A political and economic system in which a single communist party directs the state and the economy is publicly owned and centrally planned.
Sovietisation
The process by which communist parties backed by the Soviet Union took control of eastern European states and remodelled their economies on the Soviet pattern.
Central planning
The system in which the state sets production targets and allocates resources through plans, rather than through market prices.
Comecon
The Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, founded in January 1949, which coordinated trade and planning among the Soviet bloc states.
Five-year plan
A plan setting output targets for the economy over five years, the central instrument of Soviet-style planning.
Goulash communism
The informal name for the more relaxed, consumer-oriented policies of Kádár's Hungary, which allowed limited private enterprise.
Collectivisation
The merging of private farms into large state or collective farms run under central plans.
Stagnation
The name given to the Brezhnev years, when Soviet economic growth slowed and reform was avoided.
Perestroika
Gorbachev's programme of restructuring the Soviet economy, begun in 1986, which introduced limited private enterprise and enterprise freedom.
Second economy
The unofficial private work, trade and services that grew up alongside the planned economy in communist states, especially in Hungary.
