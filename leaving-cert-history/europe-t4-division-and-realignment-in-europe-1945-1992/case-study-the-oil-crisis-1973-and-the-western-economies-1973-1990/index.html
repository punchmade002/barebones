Background: the Western economy before 1973

The oil crisis struck an economy that had grown rapidly for almost thirty years on cheap energy and stable exchange rates.

  • Western European oil consumption had risen sharply since the 1950s, and most of the imported oil came from the Middle East.
  • The Arab members of OPEC had long sought higher prices, and the Organisation of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries had been formed in 1968.
  • Inflation had begun to rise in the late 1960s, and the collapse of the Bretton Woods system in August 1971 ended fixed exchange rates.
  • The Yom Kippur War, which began on 6 October 1973 when Egypt and Syria attacked Israel, provided the occasion for the Arab producers to act.
  • Oil had become Western Europe's main energy source during the 1950s and 1960s, supplying around half or more of its energy by 1973, and the price had stayed low, at about three dollars a barrel in 1973.
  • The Arab–Israeli conflict was the trigger: the USA's decision to resupply Israel with arms during the war led the Arab states to use oil as a weapon.
  • The Netherlands was targeted because its government supported Israel and because Rotterdam was Europe's main oil port.

The oil embargo and the price rises, October 1973

Arab oil producers used a supply embargo and a sharp price increase to pressure states that supported Israel.

  • On 16 October 1973 OPEC's Gulf members raised the posted price of oil by about 70 per cent, and in the following months prices roughly quadrupled.
  • On 17 October 1973 the Arab oil-producing states (OAPEC) announced cuts in output of 5 per cent a month until Israel withdrew from occupied territory, and an embargo on oil exports to the USA followed from 19–20 October and on the Netherlands from 21 October, as supporters of Israel.
  • The embargo was lifted in March 1974, but the new higher prices remained in place.
  • The Yom Kippur War ended with a ceasefire in late October 1973 (UN Security Council Resolution 338 of 22 October), after which Western governments reassessed their dependence on imported energy.

Effects on Western Europe

The oil shock brought fuel rationing, falling production and rising inflation, and it ended the long period of steady growth.

  • Several countries introduced measures to save fuel, including car-free Sundays in the Netherlands and West Germany in the winter of 1973–74.
  • Britain's government introduced a three-day working week for industry from January to March 1974, when a miners' dispute and power shortage had cut electricity supplies.
  • Industrial production fell, and unemployment rose from the low levels of the 1960s, with the sharpest increases in heavy industry.
  • Inflation rose sharply, producing stagflation, a combination of stagnation and rising prices that conventional Keynesian policies struggled to address.
  • The second oil shock of 1979, after the Iranian revolution, raised prices again and pushed many economies back into recession in the early 1980s.
  • Prices were raised again at the end of 1973: in December the OPEC states agreed to double the posted price, and by early 1974 it was about four times the pre-war level.
  • Western governments reduced speed limits, introduced fuel rationing in some countries, banned Sunday driving in West Germany and the Netherlands, and asked households to turn down heating.
  • The balance of payments of oil-importing states went into deficit, as they had to pay far more for imports, and the Arab producers gained huge revenues (petrodollars) that were recycled through Western banks.
  • Unemployment across the EEC rose from low levels in the early 1970s to above 10 percent by the mid-1980s, and Spain and Ireland suffered even higher rates.
  • The second shock, after the Iranian revolution of 1979, led to a doubling of oil prices between 1978 and 1980 and a second recession in 1980–82.
  • Governments abandoned the post-war commitment to full employment; their main aim became the defeat of inflation.

Responses: energy policy and industry

Governments responded by seeking new energy sources, and by restructuring industries that could no longer compete.

  • France announced a nuclear power programme in 1974, the Messmer Plan, which made nuclear electricity the core of its energy supply in the 1980s.
  • North Sea oil began to flow in the mid-1970s, and by 1980 Britain was self-sufficient in oil and Norway a major exporter, which cushioned both economies against the second oil shock.
  • Heavy industries such as shipbuilding, steel and coal declined sharply in Britain, and deindustrialisation changed the regional economies of the north and Scotland.
  • Energy conservation measures and the development of coal and nuclear power were promoted as ways to reduce dependence on imported oil.
  • The International Energy Agency was set up in 1974 under the OECD to coordinate oil stocks and policy among importing states.
  • France relied on nuclear power for about 70 percent of its electricity by the late 1980s, with plants built under the Messmer Plan.
  • West Germany's industry moved up-market into machinery and cars, while older industries such as coal, steel and shipbuilding were cut back across Europe; the EEC introduced steel quotas in 1980 under the Davignon Plan.

Key personality: Margaret Thatcher

Thatcher led the Conservative Party from 1975 and was Prime Minister from 1979 to 1990, pursuing monetarist and free-market policies.

  • Born in Grantham in 1925, she studied chemistry at Oxford and trained as a barrister before entering parliament in 1959.
  • She was elected Conservative leader on 11 February 1975, defeating Edward Heath, and became Prime Minister on 4 May 1979.
  • Her government pursued monetarism, cut public spending, reduced the power of the trade unions through the Employment Acts of 1980 and 1982 and the Trade Union Act of 1984, and sold state assets including British Telecom.
  • She was a participant in the Falklands War, after Argentina invaded the islands on 2 April 1982, and the British victory on 14 June 1982 helped her re-election in 1983.
  • The miners' strike of March 1984 to March 1985 was a major confrontation with the National Union of Mineworkers, which ended with the miners' defeat.
  • Contemporaries were divided: supporters admired her resolve, while critics pointed to unemployment passing three million in 1982 and to deindustrialisation.
  • Historians debate whether her policies were a decisive break with the post-war settlement or the continuation of trends already under way.
  • She was Secretary of State for Education in Heath's government (1970–74), and her break with Heath came after his defeats in 1974, with Keith Joseph's policy ideas (the Centre for Policy Studies) behind monetarism.
  • She won the general elections of 1979, 1983 and 1987, becoming the longest-serving British prime minister of the twentieth century.
  • Her government allowed tenants to buy council houses (the right to buy of 1980), removed exchange controls in 1979 and deregulated the City in 1986.
  • Unemployment reached about three million in 1982, and the early 1980s recession hit manufacturing in the north, Scotland and Wales hardest, with riots in English cities in 1981.

Thatcher and Europe, the USA and the USSR

Thatcher's foreign policy combined close links with the USA, a hard line on the EEC budget and a cautious approach to the Soviet Union.

  • Her relations with President Ronald Reagan were close, and the two leaders coordinated the Western response to Soviet policy in the early 1980s.
  • She negotiated the Fontainebleau rebate of June 1984, which reduced Britain's net contribution to the EEC budget.
  • In her Bruges speech of 20 September 1988 she criticised plans for a federal Europe while supporting a single market.
  • The Soviet press nicknamed her the Iron Lady in 1976, a name she later adopted with some pride.
  • She resigned as Prime Minister on 22 November 1990 after a challenge within her party over Europe and the poll tax, and was succeeded by John Major.
  • She approved the Single European Act in 1986 because it supported the single market, but opposed moves to economic and monetary union and the Social Charter, and Britain joined the ERM only in October 1990.
  • She signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement with Garret FitzGerald on 15 November 1985, giving the Irish government a consultative role in Northern Ireland.
  • She met Gorbachev in December 1984 before he took power and said he was someone she could do business with; she then helped to persuade Reagan that he was sincere.
  • She allowed American aircraft to use British bases in the 1986 raid on Libya, and backed the American deployment of cruise missiles at Greenham Common.
  • Her fall: Geoffrey Howe resigned in November 1990 over her European policy, Michael Heseltine challenged her, and she withdrew after the first ballot gave her too narrow a lead.

Significance of the Oil Crisis and differing views

Examiners ask how lasting the impact was, so weigh the oil shock against other causes.

  • The crisis ended the long boom and exposed the dependence of Western prosperity on cheap energy, and it shifted political attention from growth to inflation and unemployment.
  • It strengthened the case for the EEC to act together, leading to the European Monetary System in 1979 (see the notes on European unity).
  • Contemporaries blamed the Arab embargo and OPEC; many economists later argued that inflation, the end of Bretton Woods and falling productivity were already weakening the boom, so the oil shock hastened rather than caused the end of the boom.
  • The embargo also gave oil-producing states a new role in world politics and led Western states to look for energy independence, through nuclear power, North Sea oil and conservation.

Thatcher and the miners' strike, 1984–85

The strike was the most severe test of Thatcher's trade union policy.

  • The strike began in March 1984 when the National Coal Board under Ian MacGregor announced pit closures, and Arthur Scargill's National Union of Mineworkers called a strike without a national ballot.
  • The government had built up coal stocks and used the police; violent clashes occurred at Orgreave in June 1984.
  • The strike ended in March 1985 without a settlement, and the defeat weakened the union movement and speeded up the closure of coal mines.

Assessment: successes and failures of the 1970s and 1980s

The record of the Western economies after 1973 is judged on whether they restored growth and whether the costs of adjustment were fair.

  • Achievements: lower inflation by the mid-1980s, a more flexible labour market in Britain, and a deeper single market in the EEC after 1986.
  • Limits: high unemployment for much of the decade, the decline of older industries and the widening of regional inequality.
  • Supporters of Thatcher regard her as having reversed Britain's relative decline, while critics regard the decline of manufacturing as a heavy and lasting cost.
  • Historians point out that the oil shock was a cause of recession but not the sole cause, since inflation and industrial change were already under way.

Key terms

Capitalism
An economic system in which private owners run businesses for profit in competitive markets, which Thatcher's government sought to strengthen.
Welfare state
A system in which the state provides health care, pensions, unemployment benefit and education to citizens, funded mainly through taxation and insurance.
Stagflation
A combination of stagnant output, high unemployment and rising prices, which challenged conventional economic policy in the 1970s.
Monetarism
The economic doctrine that controlling the money supply is the key to controlling inflation, which Thatcher's government adopted.
Privatisation
The sale of state-owned companies to private owners, such as British Telecom in 1984.
Deindustrialisation
The decline of manufacturing and heavy industry as a share of an economy's output and employment.
OPEC
The Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, formed in 1960, whose members set oil prices and output; its price rises of 1973 and 1979 caused the oil shocks.
Oil shock
A sudden and large rise in the price of oil, as in 1973–74 and 1979, which raised costs and inflation in importing countries.
Trade union laws
Thatcher's Employment Acts of 1980 and 1982 and Trade Union Act of 1984, which restricted secondary picketing and the closed shop and required ballots.
