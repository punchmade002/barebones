Post-war devastation and the need for recovery

Western Europe emerged from the war with damaged cities, disrupted trade and food shortages, which made reconstruction the first priority.

  • Much of the industrial capacity of Germany, France and Italy was damaged or destroyed, and transport networks, bridges and ports had to be rebuilt.
  • Coal shortages in the harsh winter of 1946–47 slowed factories across Western Europe, and rationing of food and fuel continued in several countries into the late 1940s.
  • Britain borrowed heavily from the USA after the war, including an Anglo-American loan of about 3.75 billion dollars agreed in 1945.
  • Western Europe had a large trade deficit with the USA, which meant that it could buy American goods only with dollars it did not have.
  • About 12 million German refugees and expellees arrived in West Germany by the 1950s; they added to housing shortages but also supplied labour for industry.
  • Britain remained dependent on dollars and devalued the pound from 4.03 dollars to 2.80 dollars in September 1949.
  • The Cold War shaped recovery: the Marshall Plan was designed to prevent economic collapse from strengthening communist parties, notably in France and Italy.

Marshall Aid and the institutions of recovery

US aid under the Marshall Plan supplied dollars and goods, and it encouraged the Western European states to cooperate on trade and payments.

  • The European Recovery Programme ran from 1948 to 1952 and provided about 13 billion dollars of aid, mostly in the form of grants rather than loans.
  • Recipients had to draw up plans for recovery, and the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation, set up on 16 April 1948, coordinated the allocation of aid.
  • The OEEC later became the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development in 1961, after the USA and Canada joined.
  • Marshall Aid paid for imports of raw materials and machinery, and some of its counterpart funds were used for investment in industry and housing.
  • The historian Alan Milward argued in The Reconstruction of Western Europe (1984) that Marshall Aid was a modest share of total investment, and that domestic policy did much of the work of recovery.
  • Sixteen European states received Marshall Aid; Britain, France and West Germany received the largest sums, and conditions encouraged states to remove trade barriers and to reduce inflation.
  • The European Payments Union of 1950 allowed Western European currencies to be settled through a clearing system and helped to revive trade between members.
  • Marshall Aid also gave the USA leverage to shape European politics: it supported the Christian Democratic and social democratic parties against the communists and promoted American-style productivity methods.

Bretton Woods and free trade

The international monetary and trading order was rebuilt through the Bretton Woods system and the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, which encouraged freer trade.

  • The Bretton Woods conference of July 1944 created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, and it fixed currencies to the US dollar, which was convertible into gold at 35 dollars an ounce.
  • The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade was signed at Geneva in October 1947 and came into force in 1948, providing a framework for reducing tariffs through successive rounds.
  • The Benelux customs union between Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg was agreed in 1944 and came into effect in 1948.
  • The European Coal and Steel Community of 1952, the EEC of 1958 and the European Free Trade Association of 1960 each advanced freer trade in different ways.
  • The fixed exchange rate system gave Western economies stable conditions for trade and investment until the strains of the 1960s undermined it.
  • The GATT rounds, including the Kennedy Round of 1964–67, reduced tariffs on industrial goods across the Western world.
  • The EEC removed internal tariffs on industrial goods in July 1968 and introduced the Common Agricultural Policy; EFTA's members similarly removed tariffs on industrial goods but not on farm produce.
  • By the end of 1958 the main Western European currencies had become convertible, ending the controls on trade payments that had survived from the war.

Growth in West Germany, France and Italy

The economic growth of 1945–1973 was fastest in West Germany, France and Italy, and it was driven by reconstruction, planning and cheap energy.

  • West Germany's currency reform of 20 June 1948 and the abolition of most price controls, carried out by Ludwig Erhard (economics minister from 1949 to 1963), created the basis of the economic miracle (Wirtschaftswunder).
  • Erhard's social market economy combined free markets with social insurance and trade union rights, and it gave West Germany a stable framework.
  • In France the state planning system, begun under the Monnet Plan, directed investment into coal, electricity, steel and transport, and the period was later called the trente glorieuses.
  • Italy experienced rapid industrial growth in the 1950s and 1960s, and the north-west attracted migrants from the rural south, which strained housing and services.
  • Cheap oil from the Middle East fuelled industrial expansion in Western Europe, and its relative cheapness was a significant factor in the growth of the 1950s and 1960s.
  • Skilled labour, the adoption of American production methods and the reintegration of trade all helped sustain productivity growth.
  • West Germany's economy grew at more than 7 percent a year on average in the 1950s, and its unemployment fell to very low levels by 1960.
  • Italy's economic miracle, strongest between 1958 and 1963, was built on exports of cars (Fiat), appliances and textiles, but the south stayed poor and large numbers moved north or abroad.
  • France's planning was run by the Commissariat général du Plan through four-year plans that set targets for key industries; the French economy grew strongly through the 1950s and 1960s.

Labour shortages and immigration

Western European economies recruited large numbers of workers from abroad to meet labour shortages, which changed the social make-up of the continent.

  • West Germany recruited guest workers, the Gastarbeiter, under agreements with Italy from 1955, Spain and Greece from 1960, Turkey from 1961 and Yugoslavia from 1968; recruitment was halted in 1973, by which time there were over two million foreign workers.
  • France drew workers from its former colonies in North Africa and from Portugal, and Belgium from Italy and Spain.
  • Britain admitted workers from the Commonwealth, and the arrival of the Empire Windrush at Tilbury on 22 June 1948 with migrants from the Caribbean marked a major stage in Commonwealth migration.
  • The British Nationality Act of 1948 gave Commonwealth citizens the right to enter and settle in Britain, and this right was later restricted in 1962 and 1968.
  • Immigration brought social tensions, and the Race Relations Act of 1965 was Britain's first law against racial discrimination in public places.
  • Many guest workers were expected to return home, but many stayed and their families joined them, which made Western Europe multicultural in the later decades.
  • France received over 800,000 settlers (pieds-noirs) from Algeria around its independence in 1962, and Algerian, Moroccan and Portuguese workers filled jobs in construction and car factories.
  • Britain's Commonwealth migrants, from the Caribbean, India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, worked in the NHS, transport and textile factories, and they faced discrimination in housing and employment.
  • The Netherlands received migrants from its former colonies of Indonesia and Surinam, and Italy and Spain exported workers to northern Europe.

The welfare state

The welfare state gave citizens protection against sickness, unemployment and old age, and it was an important part of the post-war settlement.

  • The Beveridge Report of December 1942 identified five giant evils: want, disease, ignorance, squalor and idleness, and proposed a system of national insurance from the cradle to the grave.
  • Britain's National Health Service was established on 5 July 1948 and provided free treatment at the point of use.
  • France's social security system, set up by ordinances of October 1945, combined health insurance, family allowances and pensions for workers.
  • The Scandinavian countries developed universal welfare systems, and Sweden's model combined social security with active labour market policies.
  • West Germany's welfare system was closely tied to the social market economy, and it provided compulsory social insurance funded by employers and employees.
  • Welfare spending rose steadily across Western Europe in the 1950s and 1960s, and it became a permanent feature of the political consensus.
  • Britain's Labour government of 1945–51 under Clement Attlee passed the National Insurance Act (1946), set up the NHS and nationalised coal, the railways and the Bank of England.
  • Education was expanded: the 1944 Education Act in Britain made secondary education free, and universities grew in the 1960s.
  • Christian Democratic governments in West Germany, Italy and Belgium also supported social insurance, which showed that the welfare state was favoured by parties of both left and right.

Factors that helped and hindered growth

Examiners ask what helped and what hindered growth, so group the factors.

  • Helped: high investment in industry; the move of workers from farms into factories in Italy, France and Germany; the supply of labour from refugees and later guest workers; access to cheap oil; the catching up with American technology; and growing export markets.
  • Helped: governments accepted responsibility for full employment, using the ideas of John Maynard Keynes to manage demand, and political stability supported long-term investment.
  • Helped: the Korean War boom of 1950–53 raised demand for European exports of steel and machinery.
  • Hindered: war damage and the shortage of dollars in the late 1940s; the cost of rearmament and the Cold War; and weak productivity and frequent strikes in Britain.
  • Hindered: inflation and the pressure on currencies as wages rose in the late 1960s, and the strain of the Vietnam War on American finances, which weakened the dollar.
  • Britain grew more slowly than France, Italy and West Germany, with balance-of-payments crises that led governments to alternate between expansion and restraint, known as stop-go policies.

Assessment: why did the boom end?

The boom of 1945–1973 is judged as the most prosperous period in Western Europe's modern history, but its causes and its end are debated.

  • Achievements: rising living standards, full employment in many countries, the spread of home ownership and a large expansion of education.
  • Limits: dependence on cheap oil, the inflationary pressures of the late 1960s and the strain on the dollar-based system, which collapsed in 1971.
  • The Bretton Woods system broke down after the US president Richard Nixon suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold on 15 August 1971; by 1973 the major currencies were floating.
  • Historians disagree on how far Marshall Aid caused growth: some stress American aid and pressure for cooperation, while Milward and others stress domestic policy and economic structure.
  • Western Europe's growth rate between 1950 and 1973 was far higher than in any earlier period, but it slowed sharply after 1973 (see the notes on the Oil Crisis).
  • Contemporary opinion saw the boom as the result of good management; later historians stress that it was partly a catching-up with the American economy that could not continue indefinitely.

Key terms

Welfare state
A system in which the state provides health care, pensions, unemployment benefit and education to citizens, funded mainly through taxation and insurance.
Capitalism
An economic system in which private owners run businesses for profit in competitive markets, which the Western economies relied on after 1945.
Common market
A trading area in which goods, services, capital and workers move freely between member states, with common external tariffs.
Marshall Aid
The US aid programme of 1948 to 1952 that provided about 13 billion dollars to help Western European recovery.
Bretton Woods system
The 1944 monetary order that fixed currencies to the US dollar, which was convertible into gold, and created the IMF and the World Bank.
Guest worker
A foreign worker recruited under an agreement for temporary work in a Western European economy, often in West Germany.
Social market economy
Ludwig Erhard's model for West Germany, combining free markets with social insurance and trade union rights.
Wirtschaftswunder
The West German economic miracle of the 1950s, when rapid growth followed the 1948 currency reform and Erhard's market policies.
Trente glorieuses
The thirty years of rapid growth in France between 1945 and 1975.
General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade
The 1947 agreement under which states met in rounds of talks to reduce tariffs and barriers to trade.
