Advances in medicine and antibiotics

Scientific advances after 1945 transformed medicine, and the new drugs and vaccines changed the health of Europeans.

  • Penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928, was developed into a mass-produced drug during the Second World War by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at Oxford.
  • Antibiotics were used widely in the 1950s, which cut deaths from bacterial infections such as pneumonia and tuberculosis in Western Europe.
  • Jonas Salk's polio vaccine was announced on 12 April 1955, and Albert Sabin's oral vaccine was widely used from the early 1960s.
  • The oral contraceptive pill, approved in the USA in 1960, spread through Western Europe in the 1960s and changed reproductive choice (see the notes on women and the family).
  • The first successful kidney transplant between identical twins took place in Boston in 1954, and the first human heart transplant was performed by Christiaan Barnard in Cape Town on 3 December 1967.
  • Streptomycin, discovered in 1943 by Selman Waksman, was the first effective drug against tuberculosis, and TB deaths fell sharply in Europe in the 1950s.
  • Vaccination campaigns against diphtheria, tuberculosis (BCG), polio and measles reduced child deaths, and smallpox was declared eradicated worldwide by the World Health Organization in 1980.
  • The welfare state made new drugs and hospital treatment available to all, and life expectancy in Western Europe rose by over a decade between 1945 and 1990.
  • Surgery advanced with the heart pacemaker, kidney dialysis and hip replacements (developed by John Charnley in England in the 1960s), and the CT scanner developed by Godfrey Hounsfield in 1971.

DNA and genetic science

The discovery of the structure of DNA opened the way to genetic engineering and new understanding of heredity.

  • James Watson and Francis Crick published the double-helix structure of DNA in April 1953, using X-ray crystallography data gathered by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins.
  • Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1962, and Franklin, who had died in 1958, was not included.
  • In 1973 Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer demonstrated recombinant DNA techniques, which allowed genes to be moved between organisms.
  • The first in-vitro fertilisation baby, Louise Brown, was born in Oldham in England on 25 July 1978, after work by Robert Edwards and Patrick Steptoe.
  • Rosalind Franklin's X-ray photograph of DNA, Photo 51 (taken in 1952 at King's College London), gave key evidence for the helical structure, and Watson and Crick worked at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge.
  • The genetic code was cracked in the 1960s, and in 1977 Frederick Sanger at Cambridge developed a method of sequencing DNA.
  • Recombinant DNA techniques led to the first genetically engineered drugs, such as human insulin produced by bacteria in 1978, and to the founding of the biotechnology industry in the 1980s.

Ethics, risks and regulation in the biological sciences

Advances in biology brought benefits and new moral questions.

  • The thalidomide scandal of 1961 showed the risks: the drug, sold in West Germany from 1957 as a sedative and for morning sickness, caused birth defects in thousands of children and led to stricter drug testing.
  • The ethics of IVF, embryo research and genetic engineering were debated; the Warnock Report in Britain in 1984 recommended regulation, and Pope John Paul II's Church opposed IVF.
  • DNA fingerprinting, developed by Alec Jeffreys at Leicester in 1984, was first used in a criminal case in Britain in 1986.
  • Debates about genetic engineering, embryo research and drug safety led governments to set up regulatory bodies and ethics committees in the 1980s.

Nuclear power: development and the first plants

Nuclear power was developed for both weapons and electricity, and in the 1950s and 1960s governments promoted it as a source of cheap energy.

  • The Soviet Union's Obninsk reactor, which began supplying electricity to the grid in June 1954, was the first nuclear power station connected to a public electricity network.
  • Calder Hall in Cumbria, England, opened in October 1956 and was the world's first nuclear power station to supply electricity on a commercial scale; it also produced plutonium for weapons.
  • France developed a national nuclear programme, and in 1974 the Messmer Plan committed the country to building a large number of reactors, which by the 1980s provided most of its electricity.
  • CERN, the European Organisation for Nuclear Research, was founded in Geneva in 1954 by twelve European states to promote cooperative research in physics.
  • Atoms for Peace, an address by US President Eisenhower to the UN in December 1953, promoted civil nuclear power and led to the International Atomic Energy Agency in 1957.
  • The Treaty of Rome of 1957 created Euratom to coordinate nuclear energy in the six EEC states.
  • The Windscale fire in Cumbria in October 1957 was Britain's worst nuclear accident and released radioactive iodine over the countryside.
  • After the oil crisis of 1973 many governments, including France, West Germany, Sweden and the USSR, expanded nuclear programmes, and nuclear power came to supply around three quarters of French electricity by the 1990s.
  • The USSR built reactors across the bloc, including the RBMK reactors at Chernobyl, and nuclear weapons stockpiles grew on both sides.

The anti-nuclear movement and nuclear accidents

The growth of nuclear power produced a strong opposition movement, and serious accidents in 1979 and 1986 strengthened it.

  • Greenpeace was founded in 1971 in Vancouver, Canada, and it campaigned against nuclear testing and nuclear power at sea.
  • The accident at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania on 28 March 1979 was the most serious in the USA, and it halted new nuclear orders there for many years.
  • The explosion at the Chernobyl reactor in Soviet Ukraine on 26 April 1986 released radioactive material across Europe, and the nearby town of Pripyat was evacuated.
  • Chernobyl led to the creation of a 30-kilometre exclusion zone around the plant, and its effects on health and the environment were the subject of long disputes.
  • Anti-nuclear protests in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands grew in the early 1980s, alongside protests against NATO missile deployments.
  • The Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND) was founded in Britain in 1958 and organised marches to Aldermaston, using the peace symbol designed in 1958.
  • In Ireland a plan to build a nuclear plant at Carnsore Point, Co. Wexford, was dropped after large protests in 1978–79 and was never built.
  • Sweden held a referendum in 1980 on the future of nuclear power, and Austria's Zwentendorf plant, completed in 1978, never operated after a referendum rejected it.
  • Chernobyl's explosion followed a safety test and design flaws in the RBMK reactor; the Soviet authorities were slow to admit it, and Swedish monitors detected the radioactive cloud, which forced an announcement on 28 April.
  • many hundreds of thousands of people were eventually resettled from the affected areas.
  • Chernobyl led to greater international safety cooperation and strengthened the case for glasnost; in Western Europe it prompted a re-think in Italy, which voted to end nuclear power in 1987.

The development of the computer

Computers moved from wartime code-breaking and large mainframes to personal machines in the 1980s, changing work and daily life.

  • Colossus, built at Bletchley Park in 1943–44 by Tommy Flowers' team, was used to break German teleprinter codes, and it was one of the first programmable electronic computers.
  • ENIAC, completed in the USA in 1945, was one of the first general-purpose electronic digital computers.
  • The transistor was invented at Bell Laboratories in the USA in December 1947, and it replaced vacuum tubes in computers.
  • The integrated circuit, or microchip, was developed by Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce around 1958 and 1959, and the Intel 4004 microprocessor followed in 1971.
  • IBM's System/360 mainframe, launched in 1964, set the standard for business computing in Europe and the USA.
  • The IBM Personal Computer, launched on 12 August 1981, and the Apple II of 1977 spread personal computing into offices and homes in the 1980s.
  • The ARPANET, a US network, was first connected in 1969, and it became the basis of the internet.
  • Alan Turing's theoretical work in the 1930s and wartime code-breaking laid the foundations of computing, and the Manchester Baby of 1948 was the first stored-program computer to run.
  • The first commercial computer for business use was LEO, built for the Lyons tea shops in Britain in 1951, and IBM dominated the market for mainframes.
  • Minicomputers and microprocessors from the 1970s made computing cheaper; personal computers such as the Sinclair ZX Spectrum (1982) and the Commodore 64 were bought for homes in Britain and Europe.
  • In France the Minitel online terminal was launched in 1982 and was given free to telephone subscribers.
  • Tim Berners-Lee, working at CERN, proposed the World Wide Web in 1989, and it was launched publicly in 1991.

Effects on work, society and the Cold War

Advances in science and computing changed working life and the balance between East and West, as technology became central to economic power.

  • Computers automated routine office and factory work, and they reduced demand for some clerical and manufacturing jobs in Western Europe.
  • Western export controls, coordinated through COCOM from 1949, restricted the sale of advanced computing technology to the Soviet bloc.
  • The Soviet bloc fell behind the West in computing and electronics, a gap that Soviet planners tried to close through copying and espionage.
  • Information technology became a key concept in the economy, and the pace of change in the 1980s prepared the ground for the global networks that followed.
  • Banks introduced cash machines from 1967, supermarkets adopted bar codes in the 1970s, and word processors and spreadsheets transformed office work in the 1980s.
  • Automation reduced employment in manufacturing, while service jobs in banking, insurance and administration grew, contributing to deindustrialisation.
  • By the 1980s Soviet leaders recognised that the Soviet Union lagged badly in computing and microelectronics, which was one of the reasons for the push for economic reform.
  • Europe lagged behind the USA and Japan in computing and electronics, and the EEC launched programmes such as ESPRIT in 1984 to support research.

Assessment: benefit or risk?

Historians and contemporaries have judged these advances by the benefits they brought and the new dangers and inequalities they created.

  • Achievements: longer life expectancy, the control of infectious disease, cheap electricity from nuclear plants and the transformation of communication and work.
  • Limits: nuclear accidents, the risks of radioactive waste, the ethical questions raised by reproductive and genetic technology, and job losses from automation.
  • Contemporaries in the 1950s often celebrated science as the key to progress, while the anti-nuclear and environmental movements of the 1970s and 1980s challenged that view.
  • Later historians stress that the impact of these technologies depended on political decisions about how they were used, not only on their scientific potential.

Key terms

Information technology
The use of computers and electronic systems to store, process and transmit information, which transformed work and communication after 1945.
Nuclear deterrence
The strategy of preventing attack by possessing nuclear weapons able to inflict unacceptable damage on an aggressor.
Genetic engineering
The technique of altering the genetic material of an organism, first demonstrated in 1973 through recombinant DNA methods.
Microchip
A small integrated circuit that combines many electronic components on one piece of semiconductor material, enabling the modern computer.
Mainframe computer
A large computer used by businesses and governments for data processing from the 1950s to the 1970s.
Anti-nuclear movement
The campaign against nuclear weapons and nuclear power, which grew in the 1970s and 1980s.
Recombinant DNA
DNA made by joining genetic material from different organisms, first achieved in the early 1970s, the basis of genetic engineering.
Chernobyl
The nuclear reactor in Soviet Ukraine that exploded on 26 April 1986, causing the worst nuclear accident in history.
Personal computer
A computer small and cheap enough to be bought by individuals, spreading from the late 1970s.
