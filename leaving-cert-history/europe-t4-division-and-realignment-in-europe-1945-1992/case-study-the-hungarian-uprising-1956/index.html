Key personality: Imre Nagy

Nagy was a Hungarian communist who became the symbol of reform inside the party and was executed after the uprising.

  • Born in 1896, Nagy was a communist who spent much of the 1930s and early 1940s in the Soviet Union and returned to Hungary with the Red Army in 1944, serving as minister of agriculture in 1945 when land was distributed to peasants.
  • He was prime minister from 1953 to 1955, pursuing a New Course of consumer goods, relief for farmers and looser controls against the Stalinist policies of Mátyás Rákosi.
  • Rákosi had him removed in April 1955 and expelled from the party later that year, after Nagy had criticised collectivisation and heavy industry.
  • During the uprising he was a participant: he was reappointed prime minister on 24 October 1956, announced Hungary's neutrality and its withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact on 1 November, and appealed to the West for help.
  • Contemporaries in the Soviet bloc saw him as a traitor; Kádár's government branded him a counter-revolutionary, and he was tried in secret.
  • Nagy was executed on 16 June 1958, together with Pál Maléter and others, and buried in an unmarked grave; historians now treat him as a national martyr and a reformer who was the uprising's political leader.
  • He was reburied with a state ceremony on 16 June 1989, the thirty-first anniversary of his execution, as the regime was collapsing.

Background: Stalinism and the reform crisis in Hungary

Hungary under Rákosi was one of the most rigidly Stalinist states of the Soviet bloc, and events in the Soviet Union and Poland in 1956 weakened its leadership.

  • Mátyás Rákosi led the Hungarian communists from the late 1940s and ran a one-party state with show trials, a powerful secret police (the AVH) and forced collectivisation of farms.
  • The Hungarian economy was run on Soviet lines, with heavy industry favoured and living standards falling, which fuelled resentment among workers and peasants.
  • Khrushchev's secret speech of 25 February 1956 exposed the crimes of Stalin's era and gave Hungarian reformers a precedent for criticising their own leaders.
  • Rákosi was forced to resign in July 1956 and was succeeded by Ernő Gerő, a Stalinist who lacked popular support.
  • The Polish October of 1956, when Władysław Gomułka returned to power after pressure from Poland's workers and party, encouraged Hungarian intellectuals in the Petőfi Circle and the Writers' Union to demand change.
  • Cardinal József Mindszenty, the head of the Hungarian Catholic Church, was jailed in 1949 in a show trial; the regime's attack on the Church added to resentment.
  • Nagy's New Course of 1953 followed Soviet pressure on the Hungarian leaders after Stalin's death; Rákosi stayed as party leader and used his control of the party to undermine Nagy, which divided the communists.
  • The AVH secret police, which spied on and arrested ordinary citizens, was the best-known instrument of Rákosi's rule and the main target of popular hatred.
  • Hungary also had large economic burdens: Soviet reparations in the late 1940s and obligations to supply the Soviet Union, which kept living standards low.

The outbreak: 23 October 1956

A student demonstration in Budapest turned into a national uprising within a day.

  • On 22 October students in Budapest drew up a list of sixteen demands, including free elections, the withdrawal of Soviet troops and the return of Imre Nagy as prime minister.
  • On 23 October a large crowd gathered at the statue of Stalin, which was pulled down, and a delegation tried to broadcast the demands on Radio Budapest.
  • The AVH secret police opened fire on the crowd outside the radio building, which triggered fighting across Budapest.
  • Soviet troops entered the city, and the Gerő government asked Moscow for help, which it received on the same night.
  • Armed civilians and army units joined the fighting, and the uprising spread to other towns in the following days.

The revolution and the Soviet withdrawal, 24 October–3 November

For about a week Hungary had a reformed communist government and Soviet troops were pulling back, before the second intervention.

  • Nagy was reinstated as prime minister on 24 October 1956 and sought a ceasefire; he ordered Soviet troops out of Budapest, and the Soviet government announced on 30 October that it was prepared to negotiate the withdrawal of its forces.
  • On 30 October Nagy formed a coalition with non-communist parties, including the Smallholders and the Social Democrats, and the Communist Party was reduced to a minority role.
  • Workers' councils formed in factories and in Budapest, and some of them organised strikes and demanded genuine workers' control.
  • On 1 November Nagy declared Hungary neutral and announced its withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact, and he appealed to the United Nations.
  • Kádár, who had joined Nagy's government and become party leader on 25 October, left Budapest on 1 November, held talks with the Soviet leaders, and with their backing formed a rival government that was proclaimed on 4 November and based at Szolnok in central Hungary.
  • The Soviet leadership decided on 31 October to intervene again; fresh Soviet troops crossed the border from 1 November, and the main attack, Operation Whirlwind, began on 4 November, even as Soviet delegates were negotiating a withdrawal.
  • On 30 October a crowd attacked the party headquarters at Republic Square in Budapest and lynched several AVH men and party officials; the revolution's violence was used later by Soviet propaganda.
  • Cardinal Mindszenty was freed from house arrest on 30 October and returned to Budapest, and political prisoners were released.
  • Khrushchev and the Soviet Presidium at first leaned towards a negotiated settlement, but the declaration of neutrality, the end of one-party rule and the Suez crisis helped to convince them to intervene.

The second Soviet intervention and the fall of Nagy

On 4 November 1956 Soviet forces returned in strength, crushed the fighting in Budapest and removed Nagy's government.

  • The second intervention began on 4 November 1956 with a large Soviet force that attacked Budapest and the provincial centres.
  • Nagy broadcast an appeal to the nation and the world that Soviet troops were attacking Hungary; he then took refuge in the Yugoslav embassy in Budapest.
  • Pál Maléter, the Hungarian minister of defence, was arrested while negotiating with the Soviet commanders and later executed alongside Nagy.
  • Kádár became prime minister on 4 November and formed a government that was backed by Soviet troops.
  • Nagy left the Yugoslav embassy on 22 November after a Soviet promise of safe passage, and he was taken away by Soviet forces that same day.
  • Fighting in Budapest continued until mid-November, with workers' councils calling a general strike that paralysed the country for weeks.

Outcomes: casualties, refugees and the trial of Nagy

The uprising ended with a Soviet victory and a long repression, and the scale of the casualties and flight is the most widely quoted measure of its cost.

  • Commonly cited estimates put the Hungarian dead at about 2,500 to 3,000 and the Soviet dead at several hundred (often given as about 700); figures vary between sources and are approximate.
  • About 200,000 Hungarians fled to Austria and beyond, among them many young and skilled people; sources give varying figures, so the number is approximate.
  • Many thousands were arrested in the months after the uprising, and hundreds were executed in the years that followed under Kádár's government.
  • Nagy was lured from the Yugoslav embassy, taken to Romania, then returned to Hungary and tried in secret with Maléter and others; he was executed on 16 June 1958, with the verdict decided in advance.
  • Kádár's regime later moved to a policy of consolidation and limited reform, which in time became known as goulash communism.

The West's response and the role of the Cold War

The uprising coincided with the Suez crisis, and the Western powers did not intervene, which shaped Soviet calculations.

  • Israel invaded Egypt on 29 October 1956, and Britain and France bombed Egypt from 31 October and landed at Port Said on 5–6 November, drawing American and international attention away from Hungary at the crucial moment.
  • Radio Free Europe broadcast messages of encouragement to Hungarians, and Western politicians used the language of liberation, but no Western government intervened militarily.
  • The UN Security Council debated the Hungarian question but the Soviet Union vetoed action; the General Assembly condemned the Soviet intervention, but no UN force was sent.
  • Eisenhower publicly ruled out military intervention, reflecting the risk of a wider war and US caution over Soviet nuclear power.
  • The refugees who reached Austria were welcomed in the West, and their arrival was a propaganda victory for the West, though it did not change the outcome in Hungary.
  • Radio Free Europe's broadcasts were widely taken by Hungarians as promises of Western help, and the station was criticised afterwards for raising false hopes.
  • Western communist parties lost members and intellectuals after Hungary, and the uprising damaged the Soviet Union's reputation among the left.

Why the uprising failed, and its significance

The uprising was crushed because the Soviet Union was willing to use overwhelming force and the West would not act, but it left a lasting legacy in Hungary.

  • Soviet willingness to use force was decisive, because Moscow regarded Hungary's departure from the Warsaw Pact as unacceptable to its security.
  • The uprising had no organised armed leadership beyond local groups, and the negotiations with Soviet commanders failed to produce a lasting arrangement.
  • Its significance was that it confirmed the limits of de-Stalinisation in eastern Europe and showed that Soviet control would be defended by force.
  • Kádár's later policy of compromise (summed up in his 1961 saying that whoever is not against us is with us) and the New Economic Mechanism of 1968 allowed Hungarians more consumer goods and some market elements, as the price of political obedience.
  • The reburial of Nagy on 16 June 1989 and the later recognition of 23 October as Hungary's national day showed the collapse of communist authority in Hungary.
  • Contemporary views differed: Soviet and Kádár propaganda described a counter-revolution led by fascist elements, while Western reporting presented a national and largely anti-communist rising.
  • Hungary's declaration of neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact crossed the line that the Soviet leaders would tolerate; Poland's Gomułka had stayed within the alliance and was allowed to survive.
  • Tito, who was consulted by Khrushchev before the second intervention, accepted the need to defeat the uprising but disliked Kádár's methods.
  • The memory of 1956 shaped later events: Czechoslovak reformers in 1968 promised to stay in the Warsaw Pact to avoid Hungary's fate, and Soviet leaders drew the lesson that force might be needed again.

Assessment: was the uprising a revolution or a counter-revolution?

Historians disagree about the uprising's character, and the answer shapes judgement of Nagy and of the West.

  • Achievements: within days the uprising forced Soviet withdrawal, a multi-party government and a declaration of neutrality, which no other satellite had achieved.
  • Limits: the uprising relied on the goodwill of Soviet commanders, and it had no means of defending itself once Moscow chose to use force.
  • Later historians differ between those who see a popular anti-communist revolution and those who see a reformist communist revolt that grew beyond its leaders.
  • Nagy's reputation was rehabilitated after 1989, but judgements of his communism, his tactics and his naivety about Soviet intentions remain debated.

Key terms

Satellite state
A state formally independent but dominated politically and militarily by the Soviet Union, as Hungary was after 1945.
Sovietisation
The process by which communist parties backed by the Soviet Union took control of eastern European states and remodelled their institutions on the Soviet pattern.
New Course
Imre Nagy's programme of 1953 to 1955, which shifted Hungarian policy towards consumer goods, farm relief and looser political controls.
Warsaw Pact withdrawal
Hungary's announcement on 1 November 1956 that it was leaving the Warsaw Pact and declaring neutrality.
Petőfi Circle
A discussion group of reform-minded Hungarian intellectuals and party members formed in 1955, which helped prepare the uprising.
Workers' councils
Elected bodies set up in Hungarian factories and towns during the uprising that demanded workers' control and organised strikes.
Goulash communism
The informal name for the more relaxed, consumer-oriented policies of Kádár's Hungary after the uprising.
AVH
The Hungarian State Security Authority, the secret police of Rákosi's regime and the main target of popular anger in 1956.
Polish October
The change of leadership in Poland in October 1956, when Gomułka returned to power and Soviet intervention was avoided; it encouraged Hungarian reformers.
Radio Free Europe
A US-funded radio station broadcasting to eastern Europe, accused of raising false hopes of Western help in Hungary in 1956.
