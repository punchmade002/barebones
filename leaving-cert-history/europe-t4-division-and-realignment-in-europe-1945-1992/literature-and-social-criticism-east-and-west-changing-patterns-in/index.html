Key personality: Alexander Solzhenitsyn

Solzhenitsyn was the Soviet novelist whose writing on the Gulag made the camp system known to the world and made him the most famous dissident in the Soviet Union.

  • Born in Kislovodsk in 1918, he served as an artillery officer in the Second World War and was arrested in 1945 for criticising Stalin in private letters.
  • He was sentenced to eight years in labour camps and then to internal exile, and his experience of the Gulag became the basis of his later writing.
  • One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich was published in the Soviet journal Novy Mir in 1962 with Khrushchev's approval, and it brought the camps into Soviet public discussion.
  • The Gulag Archipelago, published in Paris in 1973, documented the Soviet camp system through testimony and history, and it had a major impact in the West.
  • He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1970, was expelled from the Soviet Union in February 1974, lived first in Switzerland and from 1976 in Vermont in the USA, and returned to Russia in 1994.
  • Contemporaries in the Soviet leadership treated him as a traitor, while many Western intellectuals admired him, though some later disliked his nationalist and religious views.
  • Historians regard his work as central to Western understanding of Soviet terror, although some question the reliability of his testimony and his later political stances.
  • He spent eight years in camps and three years in internal exile in Kazakhstan from 1953; he was cured of cancer in a Tashkent hospital in 1954, an episode behind his novel Cancer Ward.
  • After Khrushchev fell, the authorities tightened censorship; his novels The First Circle and Cancer Ward could be published only abroad (1968), and he was expelled from the Writers' Union in 1969.
  • The KGB seized a copy of The Gulag Archipelago manuscript in 1973, which led him to authorise publication in Paris; he was arrested on 12 February 1974 and flown the next day to West Germany.
  • His Harvard address in 1978 criticised Western materialism as well as communism; from exile in Vermont he continued to write The Red Wheel, a historical cycle on the Russian Revolution.
  • In Russia after 1994 his reputation was divided between admirers who saw him as the nation's conscience and critics who disliked his Russian nationalism and his religious conservatism.

Key personality: Simone de Beauvoir (writer and critic)

Beauvoir's later work was not only a feminist treatise but also a set of novels and memoirs that criticised Western society and its values.

  • Her novel The Mandarins portrayed the Parisian intellectual left after the Second World War and won the Prix Goncourt in 1954.
  • Her memoirs, including Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter (1958), described her youth and her relationship with Jean-Paul Sartre.
  • She was a participant in the political debates of the 1960s and 1970s, supporting the left and speaking out for the rights of women and of prisoners.
  • Contemporaries praised her as a writer and public intellectual, while conservative critics attacked her for challenging traditional morality.
  • Historians now judge her as one of the most influential critics of the twentieth century, though her later political views are contested.
  • She joined Sartre in founding the review Les Temps modernes in 1945, which became the leading journal of left-wing intellectuals in France.
  • In 1960 she signed the Manifesto of the 121 supporting French conscripts who refused to fight in Algeria, and she campaigned against torture by the French army, championing the case of Djamila Boupacha in 1962.
  • Her essay on old age, La vieillesse (The Coming of Age, 1970), attacked the way society treated the elderly.
  • She and Sartre visited the Soviet Union, China and Cuba in the 1950s and 1960s, where she observed communism, and her views on it changed over time.
  • Her relationship with Sartre, which was a partnership by choice without marriage, was presented by admirers as a model of equality and by critics as a privilege of the elite; she wrote about his death in 1980 in Adieux.

Eastern dissident writing and samizdat

Writers in the communist states could not publish freely, so they circulated their work in unofficial copies and published abroad.

  • Samizdat, meaning self-published, was the system by which typed and copied manuscripts were passed from hand to hand in the Soviet Union and Czechoslovakia.
  • Boris Pasternak's Doctor Zhivago was published in Italy in 1957, and he was pressured to decline the Nobel Prize in 1958.
  • Václav Havel, a Czech playwright, wrote The Power of the Powerless in 1978, arguing that ordinary people could resist by living within the truth.
  • Milan Kundera's novel The Joke (1967) and the Czech writers' congress of 1967 were part of the intellectual ferment that led to the Prague Spring.
  • Dissident writers were often expelled, imprisoned or forced into exile, and Solzhenitsyn's expulsion in 1974 was the most famous example.
  • Andrei Sakharov, the physicist and father of the Soviet hydrogen bomb, became a human rights campaigner and was exiled to Gorky in 1980 until Gorbachev released him in 1986.
  • In 1966 the writers Andrei Sinyavsky and Yuli Daniel were sentenced to labour camps for publishing satires abroad, and in 1964 the poet Joseph Brodsky was tried for social parasitism; both cases drew international protest.
  • Havel was imprisoned for his part in Charter 77 and spent most of 1979–83 in jail, before he became president of Czechoslovakia in December 1989.
  • Polish writers also took part: Czesław Miłosz, who defected in 1951, won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1980, the same year as Solidarity's rise.
  • Western radio stations such as Radio Free Europe and the BBC broadcast banned works back to the East, and the Helsinki Final Act of 1975 gave dissidents a standard of rights to appeal to.

Western social criticism

Western writers criticised totalitarianism, conformity and the moral condition of their societies, and their work reached a wide public.

  • George Orwell's Animal Farm (1945) and Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949) criticised Stalinism and the abuse of power, and both became standard texts in the Cold War.
  • Albert Camus's The Plague (1947) used an epidemic as an allegory for resistance to tyranny, and he received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1957.
  • Jean-Paul Sartre refused the Nobel Prize in 1964, and his plays and essays criticised bourgeois society and colonialism.
  • Günter Grass's The Tin Drum (1959) examined Germany's wartime past and the moral compromises of ordinary people, and he received the Nobel Prize in 1999.
  • Heinrich Böll's novels criticised the restoration of West German society after 1945, and he received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1972.
  • Sartre's existentialism, expressed in Being and Nothingness (1943) and in his plays, argued that people are free and responsible for their choices, and it dominated French thought in the late 1940s and 1950s.
  • Camus broke with Sartre in 1952 over the question of communism and violence, and he died in a car crash in 1960.
  • In Britain, John Osborne's play Look Back in Anger (1956) and the writers known as the Angry Young Men attacked class and complacency in post-war society.
  • Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932) and Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four were read in the 1950s as warnings against conformity and totalitarian control, whether communist or consumerist.

Secularisation and church attendance in the West

Religious observance fell in much of Western Europe from the 1960s, and the authority of the churches over public life declined.

  • Attendance at Mass and other religious services fell in most Western European countries from the 1960s, and the fall was sharpest among young people.
  • The Second Vatican Council's reforms and the controversy over contraception in Humanae Vitae (1968) created divisions within the Catholic Church.
  • Secularisation meant that the state, education and family life increasingly ran without reference to religious authority.
  • Muslim communities grew in Western Europe through migration from North Africa, Turkey and South Asia, and from the 1970s prayer rooms and mosques were set up in cities such as Paris, Cologne and Birmingham, adding a visible new religion to societies that were becoming more secular.
  • Church attendance fell sharply in Britain, the Netherlands, France and Scandinavia from the 1960s, while in Ireland, Poland, Spain and Italy religious practice remained high for longer.
  • In the Netherlands the proportion of Catholics attending Mass fell dramatically between the 1960s and 1980s, and many left the Church.
  • Causes of secularisation included rising affluence, education, the growth of television, urban life, the welfare state taking over church services and the questioning of authority in the 1960s.
  • The decline of clergy vocations was visible by the 1970s, when seminaries and religious orders in many countries recruited far fewer men and women.

Religion under communism

Churches in communist states were persecuted or controlled, though some kept real influence.

  • The Catholic Church in Czechoslovakia was suppressed after 1948, and bishops were imprisoned, while the Church in Poland retained real independence and mass support.
  • The Evangelical Church in East Germany adopted a policy of coexistence with the state, while maintaining its own institutions and social work.
  • In Poland, the primate Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński was interned from 1953 to 1956, but the Church retained its influence, and its authority grew after the election of the Polish pope in 1978.
  • In Hungary, Cardinal Mindszenty was jailed in 1949, freed during the 1956 uprising, and spent the next 15 years in the American legation in Budapest.
  • The Russian Orthodox Church was permitted to operate under tight state control and with KGB infiltration, and thousands of churches were closed under Khrushchev's anti-religious campaign of 1959–64.
  • In East Germany, Protestant churches provided shelter for peace and environmental groups, and church-led prayers for peace in Leipzig in 1989 became the starting point for mass protests.

The ecumenical movement

After 1945 Christians of different denominations cooperated more closely than at any time since the Reformation.

  • The World Council of Churches was founded at Amsterdam in August 1948 by Protestant, Anglican and Orthodox churches; the Roman Catholic Church did not join, and the Russian Orthodox Church joined in 1961.
  • The ecumenical community of Taizé in Burgundy, founded in 1940 by Brother Roger, drew young people from many countries in the 1960s and 1970s.
  • Ecumenism made progress after Vatican II: in January 1964 Pope Paul VI met Patriarch Athenagoras in Jerusalem, and in 1965 the mutual excommunications of 1054 were lifted.
  • The Anglican–Roman Catholic International Commission was established in 1969 to discuss differences between the churches.

Assessment: did literature change politics?

Historians debate how far writers and religious movements shaped political change, and how far they reflected social trends.

  • Achievements: dissident writing kept alive the memory of Stalin's crimes, and it helped to create an intellectual opposition in the communist states.
  • Limits: the readership of samizdat was small, and most people in the communist states were not directly influenced by dissident writing.
  • Contemporaries in the West often praised dissident writers as heroes of freedom, while Soviet officials dismissed them as enemies of the state.
  • Later historians stress that secularisation in the West was a broad social trend, not the result of a single movement or event.

Key terms

Dissident writer
A writer who openly opposed the official policies or ideology of a communist state, often facing censorship, imprisonment or exile.
Ecumenical movement
The movement among Christian churches to cooperate and reunite across denominational divisions, formally organised in the World Council of Churches.
Samizdat
The unofficial, self-published and hand-copied literature circulated in the Soviet Union and other communist states.
Secularisation
The decline of religious belief, practice and influence in public life as society becomes less religious.
Gulag
The Soviet system of labour camps and prisons in which millions were held under Stalin, and the subject of Solzhenitsyn's work.
Social criticism
Writing that examines and condemns the moral and political condition of a society, as the work of Orwell and Camus did.
Existentialism
The philosophy, associated with Sartre and de Beauvoir, that people are free and must take responsibility for their choices.
Angry Young Men
British writers of the 1950s, including John Osborne, who attacked class privilege and complacency in post-war society.
Totalitarianism
A system in which the state controls all aspects of life, the target of Orwell's novels.
