Background: why the Council was called

Pope John XXIII called the Council to renew the Catholic Church and bring it into closer contact with the modern world.

  • Angelo Roncalli, aged 76, was elected Pope John XXIII on 28 October 1958, and he was regarded as a caretaker pope when he was chosen.
  • He announced the Council on 25 January 1959, and he used the term aggiornamento, meaning updating, to describe its aim of renewal.
  • The Church faced declining practice in Western Europe, the spread of secularisation and the division between Catholics and other Christians.
  • The Council was intended to deal with the relationship between the Church and the modern world, the liturgy, and Christian unity.
  • Pius XII (pope 1939–58) had centralised authority in Rome, and by the 1950s the Church faced pressure from secularisation, the loss of the working class in industrial Europe and the demands of theologians for renewal.
  • John XXIII wanted a pastoral Council, to renew the Church's teaching and practice rather than to condemn errors.
  • He also hoped the Council would help the Church speak to ordinary people in a world of nuclear weapons, television and rapid social change, and bring non-Catholic Christians closer to Rome.
  • Preparations from 1960 were carried out by commissions dominated by Roman Curia officials, including Cardinal Ottaviani, who expected a short Council to approve prepared drafts.

The sessions, 1962–1965

The Council met in four sessions in Rome, and its work was carried on after John XXIII's death in June 1963 by Pope Paul VI.

  • The Council opened on 11 October 1962 in St Peter's Basilica, with about 2,500 bishops attending, along with observers from other Christian churches.
  • John XXIII died on 3 June 1963, and Cardinal Giovanni Battista Montini was elected Pope Paul VI on 21 June 1963.
  • Paul VI kept the Council open and oversaw its completion, and it closed on 8 December 1965.
  • Joseph Ratzinger, later Pope Benedict XVI, attended as a theological adviser, and the Council drew on the expertise of many theologians.
  • At the first session (October to December 1962) the bishops rejected the prepared drafts and elected their own commission members, led by progressive cardinals such as Suenens of Belgium, Frings of Germany and Léger of Canada; this gave the Council its own direction.
  • The second session (September to December 1963) debated the nature of the Church and collegiality, the idea that the bishops share authority with the pope.
  • The third session (autumn 1964) approved Lumen Gentium and the decree on ecumenism, though a conservative minority and last-minute papal changes caused tension in a week known as Black Week.
  • The fourth session (autumn 1965) approved the declaration on religious liberty, Nostra Aetate and Gaudium et Spes; Paul VI also set up the Synod of Bishops in 1965 to continue consultation.
  • Observers from Orthodox, Anglican and Protestant churches attended the sessions, and the proceedings were widely covered by the press, radio and television.

The Council's main documents

The Council issued four constitutions and several declarations and decrees, and the constitutions set out its main teaching.

  • Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963) reformed the liturgy and allowed the use of local languages in the Mass.
  • Lumen Gentium (1964) described the Church as the people of God, and it gave a larger role to the laity and to bishops collectively.
  • Dei Verbum (1965) addressed revelation and Scripture, and it encouraged the study of the Bible by Catholics.
  • Gaudium et Spes (1965) addressed the Church in the modern world, including economic life, marriage, peace and social justice.
  • Unitatis Redintegratio (1964) committed the Church to ecumenism, the search for unity with other Christian churches.
  • Nostra Aetate (1965) addressed non-Christian religions and rejected the charge of collective Jewish guilt for the death of Christ.
  • Dignitatis Humanae (1965) affirmed religious liberty, the right of people to follow their conscience in religion.

Changes in worship, relations and the Church

The Council changed Catholic worship and relations with other faiths, and these changes were the most visible results.

  • Changes to the Mass began in 1964, when local languages were allowed in parts of it, and the new Roman Missal of 1969–70 completed the reform, so that Mass was said in the vernacular and the priest faced the congregation.
  • Lay Catholics were encouraged to take a more active part in worship, reading the Scriptures and serving as ministers.
  • The Church began formal dialogue with other Christian churches, and observers from other churches attended the sessions.
  • Relations with Judaism changed, and Nostra Aetate became the basis for improved Catholic–Jewish dialogue.
  • Pope John Paul II visited the Great Synagogue of Rome on 13 April 1986, the first pope in modern times to enter a synagogue, and he called Jews the elder brothers of Catholics.
  • Priests and religious orders were asked to return to their founders' ideals and to adapt their dress and rules, and many nuns changed their habits.
  • In December 1965 Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras of Constantinople lifted the mutual excommunications of 1054 between the Catholic and Orthodox churches (see the notes on the ecumenical movement).
  • The Council also accepted the principle of religious liberty, that the state should not coerce people in matters of faith, which reversed the earlier teaching and ended the ideal of the Catholic confessional state.

Reactions: traditionalists, progressives and Humanae Vitae

The Council divided Catholics, and its reforms produced a traditionalist reaction as well as progressive demands for further change.

  • Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre, a French bishop who had led opposition at the Council, founded the Society of St Pius X in 1970, which rejected the Council's teaching on religious liberty and the new liturgy; he was excommunicated in 1988 after consecrating four bishops without papal approval.
  • Progressive Catholics argued that the Council had not gone far enough on the role of women, on collegiality and on sexual ethics.
  • Paul VI's encyclical Humanae Vitae of 25 July 1968 reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception, and it was widely rejected by Catholics in the West.
  • Many Catholics in Western Europe continued to attend Mass less frequently after the 1960s, a trend that historians link to the wider secularisation of society.
  • A papal commission on birth control reported in 1966 with a majority favouring a relaxation of the ban after the introduction of the pill, so the encyclical's confirmation of the ban in 1968 came as a shock to many Catholics.
  • Many theologians and bishops' conferences in the Netherlands, Belgium, West Germany and elsewhere publicly voiced dissent, and in practice many Catholic couples used contraception.
  • Humanae Vitae is often seen as a turning point because it opened a gap between the Church's teaching and the practice of lay Catholics, and weakened the authority of the hierarchy.
  • The Dutch Church, which held a pastoral council in 1968–70, was seen as the most progressive in Europe, and Rome moved to restore control under Paul VI and John Paul II.

Key personality: Pope John Paul II (religious role)

Karol Wojtyła, a Polish bishop who had taken part in the Council, became pope in 1978 and led the Church for almost three decades.

  • Born in Wadowice in 1920, Wojtyła was made a bishop in Kraków in 1958 and archbishop in 1964, and he took part in the Second Vatican Council, where he contributed to the drafting of Gaudium et Spes.
  • He was elected pope on 16 October 1978, and he was the first non-Italian pope since 1523.
  • He travelled widely as pope, and his visits to Poland in 1979 and later were occasions when the Church and the national identity of Poland were linked.
  • He was shot on 13 May 1981 in St Peter's Square by Mehmet Ali Ağca and visited his attacker in prison in December 1983.
  • He reaffirmed traditional Catholic teaching on contraception, abortion and the priesthood, which contemporary critics and supporters saw as a conservative turn after the Council.
  • Historians disagree about his legacy: supporters see him as a defender of human dignity, while critics argue that he resisted reform and handled abuse scandals badly.
  • Contemporaries in the communist states saw him as a challenge to their authority, and his support for Solidarity made him a political figure.
  • He travelled more than any previous pope, making over a hundred foreign journeys, and drew huge crowds in Poland, Latin America, Africa and Europe.
  • He visited Ireland from 29 September to 1 October 1979, the first visit by a pope, saying Mass before about a million people in the Phoenix Park, Dublin, and visiting Drogheda, Galway, Knock and Limerick; at Drogheda he appealed to paramilitaries to end violence in Northern Ireland.
  • He opposed the ordination of women, liberation theology, and a change to the discipline of clerical celibacy, and under his authority the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, led by Cardinal Ratzinger from 1981, disciplined theologians.
  • He launched World Youth Days in the mid-1980s and promoted new movements such as Opus Dei and the Neocatechumenal Way, and he apologised for historical wrongs of Christians.
  • He met Gorbachev at the Vatican on 1 December 1989, a sign that the Church had outlasted the communist regimes; he died in April 2005.

Influence on religious practice

The effects of the Council on practice are debated because it coincided with wider social change.

  • Influence on religious practice: Mass attendance fell steeply in the 1960s and 1970s in Western Europe, though the same decline occurred among Protestants who had no Council, which suggests social causes.
  • In Ireland practice stayed high into the 1980s, but the number of vocations and Mass attendance began to fall, and the authority of the Church was shaken later.
  • Vocations to the priesthood and religious life fell in the 1970s, as many priests and nuns left their orders in the years after the Council and fewer young people entered.
  • Weekly Mass attendance in Western Europe's Catholic countries fell: in France and the Netherlands it dropped by half or more by the 1980s, while Poland and Ireland remained exceptions.
  • The Council did lead to increased lay involvement, in parish councils, readers and Eucharistic ministers, and to a stronger Catholic interest in social justice and in peace.

Assessment: did the Council renew the Church?

Historians differ on whether Vatican II was a successful renewal or a cause of decline, and the judgement depends on which measure is used.

  • Achievements: the vernacular liturgy, the new relations with other faiths, and a more open style of Church leadership.
  • Limits: falling practice, declining vocations to the priesthood and the conflict between traditionalists and progressives that continued after 1965.
  • Contemporaries in the Church were divided, with some seeing the Council as a new beginning and others as a betrayal of tradition.
  • Later historians stress that the changes in religious practice were part of wider social trends, and that the Council's effects differed between countries.
  • Traditionalists blame the Council for decline; progressives argue that the Church failed to implement its reforms, especially on the role of women and collegiality.

Key terms

Ecumenical movement
The movement among Christian churches to cooperate and reunite across denominational divisions, which the Council endorsed through Unitatis Redintegratio.
Aggiornamento
John XXIII's term for updating the Church's life and teaching to meet the modern world, the purpose behind the Council.
Vernacular liturgy
The celebration of the Mass in local languages rather than Latin, introduced by the Council's reform of worship.
Nostra Aetate
The Council's 1965 declaration on non-Christian religions, which rejected collective Jewish guilt for the death of Christ.
Humanae Vitae
Paul VI's 1968 encyclical reaffirming the Catholic ban on artificial contraception.
Religious liberty
The right of individuals to follow their conscience in religious matters without coercion, affirmed by Dignitatis Humanae.
Collegiality
The principle affirmed at Vatican II that the bishops share responsibility for governing the Church with the pope.
Constitution
The most authoritative kind of Council document; Vatican II issued four constitutions, on the liturgy, the Church, revelation and the Church in the modern world.
Laity
Ordinary church members who are not ordained, whose role in worship and Church life Vatican II enlarged.
