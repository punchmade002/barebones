Key personality: Nikita Khrushchev

Khrushchev led the Soviet Union from Stalin's death in 1953 to 1964 and set the terms of the Cold War between de-Stalinisation and nuclear confrontation.

  • Born in 1894 in Kalinovka, a village in Kursk province, he rose through the Communist Party under Stalin, becoming first secretary of the Moscow party in 1935 and of the Ukrainian party in 1938.
  • He was a participant in the Great Purge in Ukraine and Moscow in the late 1930s, which later made his attacks on Stalin's terror open to challenge.
  • After Stalin's death in March 1953 he became First Secretary of the Central Committee in September 1953, and he defeated rivals such as Georgy Malenkov and Lavrentiy Beria in power struggles.
  • In his secret speech to a closed session of the 20th Party Congress on 25 February 1956 he denounced Stalin's cult of personality and terror; the text was published in the West within months.
  • He argued at the same congress that war with the capitalist states was not inevitable, which became known as peaceful coexistence.
  • Contemporaries in the West saw him as unpredictable and aggressive, while Mao Zedong's China criticised him as a revisionist who had abandoned Marxist-Leninist purity.
  • Historians such as William Taubman, in Khrushchev: The Man and His Era (2003), describe a reformer whose policies were improvised and often failed, but whose de-Stalinisation opened Soviet society.
  • Rise to power, 1953–57: Beria was arrested in June 1953 and executed that December; Malenkov was forced to resign as premier in 1955; in June 1957 Khrushchev defeated the 'anti-party group' of Molotov, Malenkov and Kaganovich with help from Marshal Zhukov and the Central Committee, and in March 1958 he added the premiership to his party post.
  • Foreign initiatives of the thaw: he agreed to the Austrian State Treaty and withdrew Soviet troops from Austria in 1955, visited Tito's Yugoslavia in 1955 to mend the 1948 split, and attended the Geneva summit of July 1955.
  • At the 1959 American National Exhibition in Moscow Khrushchev and Vice-President Nixon held the 'kitchen debate' on the merits of communism and capitalism.
  • On 14 October 1964 the Central Committee removed him from the posts of First Secretary and chairman of the Council of Ministers, while he was on holiday in the Black Sea.
  • Leonid Brezhnev became First Secretary of the party and Alexei Kosygin chairman of the Council of Ministers.
  • His critics cited the Cuban humiliation, the failed agricultural and economic reforms, and his erratic style of leadership.
  • He was pensioned off and lived in retirement, largely at a dacha outside Moscow, until his death in 1971.
  • His fall confirmed that the Soviet leadership would not tolerate a single figure dominating policy, which shaped the more collective style of the Brezhnev years.

De-Stalinisation and the Virgin Lands campaign

Khrushchev's domestic programme loosened Stalinist controls but its economic experiments produced mixed results.

  • De-Stalinisation released many political prisoners, reduced the role of the secret police and allowed more open discussion in literature.
  • The Virgin Lands campaign from 1954 ploughed up grassland in Kazakhstan and Siberia to grow grain, and it produced good harvests in its early years.
  • Droughts and poor management later brought failures, most seriously in 1963, when the harvest fell and grain had to be imported.
  • In 1957 industry was decentralised into regional economic councils, a reform that was later reversed by his successors.
  • The Novocherkassk strike of June 1962 over food prices and wages was suppressed by troops who fired on the crowd, a sign of the limits of his reforms.
  • Space achievements boosted prestige: Sputnik 1 was launched on 4 October 1957, and Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on 12 April 1961.

Early Cold War crises before 1953

The crises before Khrushchev set the pattern of confrontation without direct war.

  • Berlin Blockade (1948–49): the first major crisis, ended by the airlift and followed by the founding of NATO.
  • Korean War (1950–53): the North Korean invasion of the South in June 1950 persuaded Western governments that communism was expansionist, and led to the rearmament of NATO, the creation of a unified NATO command under Eisenhower and the push to rearm West Germany.
  • Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 opened a more flexible phase; an armistice in Korea followed in July 1953.

The Berlin crises and the Berlin Wall

Berlin was the most dangerous flashpoint in Europe, because West Berlin was an enclave inside East Germany and a window to the West.

  • On 27 November 1958 Khrushchev issued an ultimatum giving the Western powers six months to agree to a settlement that would end their occupation of West Berlin.
  • Khrushchev's visit to the USA in September 1959, including talks with Eisenhower at Camp David, produced a brief thaw that the U-2 incident later ended.
  • The Paris summit of May 1960 collapsed after the U-2 spy plane piloted by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union on 1 May 1960.
  • At the Vienna summit in June 1961 Khrushchev pressed the new US president John F. Kennedy on Berlin, and the crisis deepened over the summer.
  • East German forces began building the Berlin Wall on 13 August 1961, closing the border between the two halves of the city.
  • The Western powers did not use force against the Wall, accepting it as the price of avoiding war; Kennedy is reported to have said that a wall was better than a war.
  • Between 1961 and 1989 the Wall and the border fortifications around it were guarded, and people who tried to cross were shot or arrested.

The Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962

The Cuban Missile Crisis was the closest the Cold War came to nuclear war, and it was resolved by a compromise that Europeans watched closely.

  • In October 1962 US reconnaissance photographs showed Soviet medium-range missiles being installed in Cuba, within range of much of the USA.
  • President Kennedy announced a naval quarantine of Cuba on 22 October 1962 and demanded the removal of the missiles.
  • For about a week the two superpowers were on the brink of war; Kennedy sent former secretary of state Dean Acheson to brief the French president de Gaulle, who gave firm support, while most European governments learned of the crisis only as it unfolded.
  • On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed to withdraw the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
  • The secret part of the deal was a US promise to remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey, which was not made public at the time.
  • The crisis shaped later arms control: a Moscow–Washington hotline was set up in 1963, and the Partial Test Ban Treaty followed in August 1963.
  • Khrushchev's motives were to defend Castro's Cuba after the Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961, to offset US missiles in Turkey and Italy, and to narrow the Soviet Union's weakness in long-range missiles.
  • The crisis began on 16 October 1962 when Kennedy was shown U-2 photographs; Kennedy's advisers (ExComm) weighed an air strike against a naval blockade and chose the blockade, described publicly as a quarantine.
  • Seen from Europe, the crisis showed that Berlin and Cuba were linked, and made West European leaders more aware that decisions on nuclear war would be taken in Washington and Moscow.

Nuclear deterrence, arms control and the Sino-Soviet split

Each superpower relied on nuclear weapons to deter attack, and limited agreements grew out of the fear that deterrence could fail.

  • Nuclear deterrence meant that each side kept enough weapons to destroy the other after a first strike, a condition later called mutually assured destruction.
  • The Soviet Union tested the 50-megaton Tsar Bomba on 30 October 1961, the largest nuclear explosion ever set off.
  • The Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed in Moscow on 5 August 1963 by the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union, banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
  • The Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968 committed non-nuclear states not to acquire nuclear weapons, while the nuclear powers agreed to pursue disarmament.
  • Western Europe depended on the US nuclear umbrella under NATO, and debates in France and Britain over independent deterrents reflected doubts about that protection.
  • France tested its first atomic bomb in 1960 and developed its own deterrent, which it kept outside NATO's military command from 1966.
  • The ideological dispute with Mao's China grew from the late 1950s and became open in 1960, when Soviet advisers were withdrawn from China.
  • The Sino-Soviet split meant the Soviet Union faced two rival communist powers and could not count on Chinese support in Europe.
  • Albania sided with China and in 1968 became the only member state to leave the Warsaw Pact.
  • Soviet leaders regarded the Berlin Wall as a means of stabilising the German Democratic Republic, which had lost about 2.7 million people to the West since 1945.

Détente: Ostpolitik and the Helsinki Final Act

Détente was a period of easing tension, from about 1969 to the late 1970s, in which the superpowers and European states signed agreements on arms and borders.

  • Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik sought better relations with Eastern Europe; the Treaty of Moscow of 12 August 1970 renounced the use of force between the Federal Republic and the Soviet Union.
  • The Four Power Agreement on Berlin of 3 September 1971 guaranteed access between West Berlin and West Germany and confirmed the Western presence in the city.
  • The Basic Treaty of 1972 established relations between the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic, which led to both joining the United Nations in 1973.
  • SALT I, signed on 26 May 1972, limited anti-ballistic missile systems and placed interim limits on strategic offensive missiles.
  • The Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe produced the Helsinki Final Act on 1 August 1975, signed by 35 states including the USA, Canada and the Soviet Union.
  • The Final Act recognised existing borders in Europe and committed signatories to respect human rights, a commitment that later dissidents used to press their governments.

The second Cold War and the Euromissiles

Détente broke down in the late 1970s, and a new arms race in Europe led to the deployment of intermediate-range missiles in the early 1980s.

  • On 24 December 1979 Soviet forces invaded Afghanistan, and the USA responded with a grain embargo and a boycott of the Moscow Olympics in 1980.
  • NATO's double-track decision of 12 December 1979 agreed to deploy Pershing II and cruise missiles in Western Europe unless arms talks with the Soviet Union succeeded.
  • The Soviet Union had deployed SS-20 intermediate-range missiles in the late 1970s, which threatened Western Europe.
  • Large peace demonstrations took place in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands in 1981–83 against the deployment of the new missiles.
  • The Soviet leadership's fear of a Western first strike led to a serious misreading during the NATO exercise Able Archer 83 in November 1983.
  • The crisis was defused by arms negotiations, and the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty was signed in December 1987 under Gorbachev.

The turning points of the Cold War to 1989

Examiners ask which events changed the course of the Cold War, so know the order and what each changed.

  • Berlin Blockade, 1948–49: confirmed the division of Germany and led to NATO.
  • Hungary, 1956: showed that the Soviet Union would use force to keep its bloc together.
  • Berlin Wall, 1961 and Cuba, 1962: the high points of confrontation, after which both sides sought safeguards such as the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty.
  • Prague Spring, 1968: the invasion of Czechoslovakia confirmed the Brezhnev Doctrine but did not end détente.
  • Helsinki, 1975: the high point of détente, which gave dissidents in the East a human rights standard.
  • Afghanistan and the Euromissiles, 1979: ended détente and began the second Cold War.
  • Reagan's election in 1980 brought a hard line: in March 1983 he called the Soviet Union an evil empire, and announced the Strategic Defense Initiative.
  • Gorbachev from 1985, the Reykjavik summit of October 1986 and the INF Treaty of 1987 reversed the arms race, and 1989 ended Soviet control of eastern Europe (see the notes on reform movements and the collapse of communism).

Assessment: Khrushchev's achievements and failures

Khrushchev's record is judged by contrasting his bold moves with the setbacks that ended his rule.

  • Achievements: de-Stalinisation, the Partial Test Ban Treaty, the hotline, the Austrian State Treaty of 1955 and the space successes.
  • Failures: the Virgin Lands harvest crises, the reversal of his administrative reforms, and the Cuban settlement, which Soviet leaders saw as a retreat.
  • Contemporary Western views often focused on his unpredictability, while his Soviet colleagues attacked his rash 'hare-brained schemes' and his boasting.
  • Later historians have judged him as a reformer who opened the system but never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning.
  • The Berlin Wall, which his policy helped to produce, was for many observers the most lasting symbol of his era, and later assessments weigh it heavily against his reform record.

Key terms

Nuclear deterrence
The strategy of preventing attack by possessing nuclear weapons able to inflict unacceptable damage on an aggressor after a first strike.
Détente
The easing of Cold War tension from about 1969, marked by arms control agreements and improved contact between East and West.
Cold War
The rivalry from 1945 to 1991 between the USA and its allies and the Soviet Union and its eastern European bloc, short of direct war between them.
De-Stalinisation
Khrushchev's policy after 1956 of condemning Stalin's terror and personality cult, and loosening political controls.
Peaceful coexistence
The Soviet doctrine announced in 1956 that the communist and capitalist systems could compete without war between them.
Ostpolitik
West German chancellor Willy Brandt's policy of normalising relations with the Soviet Union and eastern European states, begun around 1969.
Mutually assured destruction
The condition in which each superpower could destroy the other with nuclear weapons even after absorbing a first strike, which discouraged war.
Partial Test Ban Treaty
The 1963 agreement by the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
Virgin Lands campaign
Khrushchev's programme from 1954 to plough up grassland in Kazakhstan and Siberia to grow grain.
Berlin Wall
The fortified barrier built by East Germany from 13 August 1961 to stop people leaving East Berlin for the West.
Helsinki Final Act
The 1975 agreement of 35 states that recognised Europe's post-war borders and pledged respect for human rights.
Euromissiles
The Soviet SS-20 and the NATO Pershing II and cruise missiles of the late 1970s and 1980s, whose deployment caused the missile crisis in Europe.
