Wartime agreements and the strain between the Allies

The wartime alliance of the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union held while Germany was defeated, but the aims of its members diverged once the war ended.

  • Yalta Conference (4–11 February 1945): Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin agreed that Germany would be divided into occupation zones and that Poland would have a government broadened to include non-communists, with free elections promised.
  • The occupation zones had been agreed by the Allies in 1944. The Soviet Union took the east, the USA the south and centre-west, Britain the north-west and France the south-west.
  • Berlin lay deep inside the Soviet zone and was itself divided into four sectors, one for each occupying power.
  • Potsdam Conference (17 July–2 August 1945): Truman, Stalin and, after the British election, Attlee, disputed reparations and the German–Polish border.
  • Stalin's security aim was a buffer of friendly governments along the Soviet western frontier, after invasions from the west in 1914 and 1941.
  • The USA tested its first atomic bomb on 16 July 1945 and used atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August; the Soviet Union tested its own bomb in August 1949, ending the American monopoly.
  • At Yalta the Allies also agreed that the Soviet Union would join the war against Japan, and the Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections in liberated states; Stalin's later disregard of it fed Western distrust.
  • In October 1944 Churchill and Stalin had informally split influence in the Balkans (the percentages agreement), giving the Soviet Union the lead in Romania and Bulgaria and Britain in Greece.
  • President Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945, and Harry Truman, who took a harder line towards Moscow, became president.
  • The Red Army occupied Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, eastern Germany and much of Czechoslovakia by May 1945, so Soviet power on the ground shaped what the West could demand.
  • Poland was the first flashpoint: the Soviet Union backed the communist-dominated Lublin government, while Britain and the USA backed the London-based government-in-exile.

Potsdam's aims for Germany and the reparations dispute

The Allies agreed broad aims for a defeated Germany, but each occupying power ran its own zone, so the aims were never fully put into practice.

  • Potsdam set four aims for Germany: demilitarisation, denazification, democratisation and decentralisation, with the economy to be run as a single unit.
  • Each power took reparations from its own zone, and the Soviet Union also dismantled factories and shipped machinery east from its zone.
  • The Soviets wanted reparations from the industrial Ruhr in the west; the Western powers refused, because they wanted the western zones to recover.
  • The territory east of the Oder and Neisse rivers was placed under Polish and Soviet administration until a peace treaty, and German inhabitants were expelled from it.
  • Without a peace treaty, Germany remained under the control of the four occupying powers, which met as the Allied Control Council in Berlin.
  • By 1947 the zones were drifting apart economically, and the Western powers concluded that the single economy the Allies had agreed on could not be restored.

The Iron Curtain speech and the Truman Doctrine

From 1946 the Western powers publicly named the Soviet Union as the threat and began to offer aid and military guarantees to Europe.

  • George Kennan, a US diplomat in Moscow, sent his 'Long Telegram' (22 February 1946) arguing that Soviet expansion would have to be contained by firm resistance at points of pressure.
  • Churchill's speech at Fulton, Missouri (5 March 1946) said that from Stettin on the Baltic to Trieste on the Adriatic an iron curtain had descended across the Continent.
  • Stalin answered in an interview in Pravda, presenting Churchill's speech as a call for war against the Soviet Union.
  • In February 1947 Britain told Washington that it could no longer afford to support Greece and Turkey, which created the opening for the USA to take on that role.
  • Truman Doctrine: on 12 March 1947 President Truman asked Congress for about 400 million dollars of aid to Greece and Turkey, which faced communist insurgency (Greece) and Soviet pressure over the Turkish straits; Congress approved it in May 1947.
  • Marshall Plan: announced by Secretary of State George Marshall in a speech at Harvard on 5 June 1947, it provided about 13 billion dollars of US aid through the European Recovery Programme (1948–52).
  • The Soviet Union and its eastern allies were pressed to refuse Marshall aid; Czechoslovakia and Poland had shown interest before withdrawing in July 1947.
  • The Cominform, founded in September 1947, linked the communist parties of Europe under Soviet direction.
  • The Greek Civil War (1946–49) between the royal government and communist guerrillas was the first test of the Truman Doctrine; the guerrillas were defeated in 1949 and Greece stayed in the Western camp.
  • Marshall's offer was open to all of Europe, but Stalin forbade eastern states to take part, and the Soviet delegation walked out of the Paris conference in July 1947.
  • Western Europe received Marshall aid as grants and loans, with 16 countries sharing it through the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation, set up in 1948.

Sovietisation of Eastern Europe: from coalition to one-party rule

Sovietisation was the process by which communist parties, backed by the Red Army and Soviet security police, took full control of states that had previously had mixed or non-communist governments.

  • In 1945–47 communist parties usually governed through coalitions with peasant, socialist and liberal parties, while controlling key posts such as the interior ministry and the police.
  • The Hungarian communist leader Mátyás Rákosi later boasted of slicing away his opponents one thin piece at a time; the phrase salami tactics is usually attributed to him and describes the method used across the region.
  • Poland: sixteen leaders of the Polish underground were arrested and tried in Moscow in 1945, and the peasant leader Stanisław Mikołajczyk left Poland in October 1947 after rigged elections.
  • Romania: King Michael was forced to abdicate on 30 December 1947 and a people's republic was proclaimed.
  • Czechoslovakia: in February 1948 non-communist ministers resigned over police control, and Klement Gottwald's communists took power without a general election.
  • Show trials and purges eliminated rivals, including the Hungarian foreign minister László Rajk, executed in 1949 after a trial in Budapest.
  • Land reform in 1945–47 broke up large estates in Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and the Soviet zone of Germany, and gave land to small farmers, which helped the communists win support.
  • Soviet-style institutions followed: single-party rule, secret police, central planning, and collectivisation of farms in most states.
  • Yugoslavia under Tito was expelled from the Cominform in June 1948, showing that communist states could break from Moscow without ceasing to be communist.
  • Hungary: in the free election of November 1945 the communists won only about 17 percent against about 57 percent for the Smallholders; Rákosi then used the police and ministry of the interior to break the Smallholders, and fraud in the 1947 election gave the communists the largest share.
  • Bulgaria: the Fatherland Front government, dominated by communists, abolished the monarchy after a referendum in 1946, and the opposition leader Nikola Petkov was executed in 1947.
  • Czechoslovakia was the only eastern state to remain democratic until 1948; the death of the foreign minister Jan Masaryk on 10 March 1948, in a fall from a window, shocked Western opinion.
  • Poland: the Polish Workers' Party and Polish Socialist Party were merged into the Polish United Workers' Party in December 1948, ending independent socialist opposition.
  • Albania under Enver Hoxha and Yugoslavia under Tito had communist governments that came to power largely through their own wartime resistance rather than the Red Army.

Soviet-led economic alternatives to Marshall aid

The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and built its own trade system, which later became the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance.

  • Eastern European states were bound to the Soviet Union by bilateral trade agreements in which their trade was directed towards Moscow.
  • A Soviet-led aid and trade offer to eastern Europe in 1947, often called the Molotov Plan, was the alternative to Marshall aid.
  • Comecon, the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, was set up in January 1949 to coordinate trade and planning among the Soviet bloc states.
  • Eastern bloc states were not allowed to take part in the Marshall Plan, which divided Europe into two economic systems.
  • Soviet reparations and dismantling in eastern Germany slowed recovery there and made the Soviet zone economically dependent on Moscow.

The division of Germany and the Berlin Blockade

The Western zones were merged and rebuilt, which the Soviet Union opposed, and the split between the two halves of Germany became permanent by 1949.

  • The American and British zones were merged into Bizonia on 1 January 1947, and the French zone was added in 1948–49 after the London talks, creating a single western area.
  • A currency reform in the Western zones on 20 June 1948 introduced the Deutsche Mark, which the Soviet authorities saw as a threat to their zone.
  • The Soviet Union blockaded road, rail and canal access to West Berlin from June 1948, cutting the Western sectors off from supplies.
  • The Western powers organised an airlift that flew in food, fuel and other supplies; it delivered over two million tons before the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949.
  • The Federal Republic of Germany was founded on 23 May 1949 with a Basic Law drafted by a parliamentary council; Konrad Adenauer became its first chancellor in September 1949.
  • The German Democratic Republic was proclaimed on 7 October 1949 in the Soviet zone, where the Socialist Unity Party had been formed in April 1946 by merging the communist and social democratic parties.
  • Berlin stayed divided: West Berlin was run by the Western powers and closely tied to the Federal Republic, while East Berlin was declared the capital of the German Democratic Republic.
  • Stalin's aim in the blockade was to force the Western powers to abandon the new currency and their plans for a West German state, or to drive them out of Berlin.
  • General Lucius Clay, the American military governor, and the Berlin mayor Ernst Reuter led the Western and Berlin response; Truman refused to withdraw.
  • The airlift (Operation Vittles) flew into the Tempelhof, Gatow and Tegel airfields, with planes landing at times about every minute.

Military alliances: NATO and the Warsaw Pact

Each bloc formed an alliance for collective defence, and the Warsaw Pact was created largely in response to West German membership of NATO.

  • The Brussels Treaty of March 1948 linked Britain, France and the Benelux countries in a defensive alliance that became the basis for NATO.
  • The North Atlantic Treaty was signed in Washington on 4 April 1949 by twelve states, including the USA, Canada and most West European countries; its Article 5 treats an attack on one member as an attack on all.
  • West Germany joined NATO in May 1955, after its rearmament had been agreed.
  • The Warsaw Pact was signed in Warsaw on 14 May 1955 by the Soviet Union and seven eastern European states, and Soviet forces were stationed in member states.
  • The Warsaw Pact's founding members were the Soviet Union, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, East Germany and Albania; Albania stopped taking part in 1961 and formally withdrew in 1968.
  • NATO's first Secretary General was Lord Ismay, and the alliance's purpose was commonly summed up as keeping the Americans in, the Russians out and the Germans down.

Assessment: who caused the Cold War?

Historians have disagreed about responsibility, and the opening of Soviet archives after 1991 changed the terms of the debate.

  • Traditional Western accounts blamed Stalin's ideological drive for expansion and the threat it posed to Western Europe.
  • Revisionist historians from the late 1950s, such as William Appleman Williams in The Tragedy of American Diplomacy (1959), argued that US economic expansion and the atomic monopoly provoked Soviet hostility.
  • Stalin's control of eastern Europe went far beyond a defensive buffer, through rigged elections, purges and one-party rule.
  • Western decisions such as the currency reform of June 1948 deepened the division of Germany, which Soviet leaders could regard as a threat.
  • Soviet archives opened after 1991 gave historians more evidence of Stalin's aims, but debate continues over how far he planned expansion beyond eastern Europe.
  • Historians who stress the defensive side point to the wartime invasions of Russia; those who stress the offensive side point to the speed with which communist rule was imposed in 1947–48.

Key terms

Cold War
The rivalry from 1945 to 1991 between the USA and its allies and the Soviet Union and its eastern European bloc, short of direct war between them.
Iron Curtain
Churchill's term for the division of Europe between the Soviet-controlled east and the democracies of the west, and the physical border that later enclosed the Soviet bloc.
Sovietisation
The process by which communist parties backed by the Soviet Union took control of eastern European states and remodelled their politics, economies and police on the Soviet pattern.
Satellite state
A state formally independent but dominated politically and militarily by the Soviet Union, as the eastern European states were after 1945.
Capitalism
An economic system in which private owners run businesses for profit in competitive markets, which the Western powers defended against communism.
Communism
A political and economic system in which a single communist party directs the state and the economy is publicly owned and centrally planned.
Truman Doctrine
The 1947 US policy of giving economic and military aid to countries threatened by communism, beginning with Greece and Turkey.
Cominform
The Communist Information Bureau founded in 1947 to coordinate the European communist parties under Soviet direction.
Berlin Blockade
The Soviet cut-off of land access to West Berlin from June 1948 to May 1949, which the Western powers countered with an airlift.
Salami tactics
The step-by-step removal of non-communist rivals, slice by slice, by which communists took power in eastern Europe; usually attributed to Mátyás Rákosi.
Marshall Plan
The American programme of economic aid to Western Europe (the European Recovery Programme, 1948–52), offered in June 1947 to rebuild economies and weaken the appeal of communism.
Comecon
The Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, set up in 1949 to coordinate trade and economic planning among the Soviet bloc states.
