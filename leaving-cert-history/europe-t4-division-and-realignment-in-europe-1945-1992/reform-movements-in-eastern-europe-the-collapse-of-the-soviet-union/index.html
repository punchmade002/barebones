Key personality: Mikhail Gorbachev

Gorbachev was the Soviet leader whose reforms opened the way to the end of communist rule in eastern Europe and to the break-up of the Soviet Union.

  • Born in 1931 in Privolnoye in the Stavropol region, he studied law at Moscow State University, rose through the Communist Party in Stavropol, and became a full member of the Politburo in 1980.
  • He became General Secretary of the Communist Party on 11 March 1985, and in 1988 he became chairman of the Supreme Soviet, and in 1990 the first executive president of the USSR.
  • He pursued glasnost, which meant greater openness and criticism of the past, and perestroika, a restructuring of the economy that tried to combine market elements with planning.
  • He was a participant who chose not to use force in eastern Europe, and he withdrew Soviet troops from Afghanistan by February 1989.
  • Contemporaries in the West welcomed him: Margaret Thatcher, before he came to power, judged that he was a man she could do business with, and Reagan negotiated arms agreements with him.
  • Conservatives at home regarded him as having destroyed the Soviet system, while many Russians later blamed him for economic collapse.
  • Historians differ on whether he intended to end communism or to save it, and most agree that his reforms had consequences he did not foresee.
  • Gorbachev rose under the patronage of Yuri Andropov, and as General Secretary he drew on a generation of younger reformers, including Alexander Yakovlev and Eduard Shevardnadze (foreign minister from 1985).
  • His early slogan was uskorenie, acceleration of economic growth; when it failed he moved to perestroika and, at the 27th Party Congress in February 1986, to openness.
  • Democratisation: in 1989 the first partly free elections to the Congress of People's Deputies were held in March, and in 1990 the party gave up its constitutional monopoly of power.
  • He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1990 for his role in ending the Cold War.

Key personality: Pope John Paul II (political role)

Karol Wojtyła, elected pope in 1978, supported the Polish opposition to communism and became a political figure as well as a religious leader.

  • Born in Wadowice in 1920, he was Archbishop of Kraków and became a cardinal in 1967 before being elected pope on 16 October 1978, the first non-Italian pope since 1523.
  • His visit to Poland from 2 to 10 June 1979 drew very large crowds, and it encouraged Poles to organise independently of the state.
  • He met Lech Wałęsa during his 1983 visit to Poland and gave moral support to Solidarity, which the Polish authorities tried to suppress.
  • He was shot in St Peter's Square on 13 May 1981 by Mehmet Ali Ağca, and he attributed his survival to divine intervention.
  • Contemporaries in the Polish and Soviet governments saw him as a threat, and the Polish state security services monitored the Church closely.
  • Historians disagree about how far he caused the 1989 revolutions; many see him as a moral leader who strengthened Polish civil society, while others stress the economic and political factors that made communism unsustainable.
  • During the 1979 visit he spoke in Warsaw's Victory Square in a Mass attended by hundreds of thousands, and many Poles took his call for the renewal of their land as encouragement to demand change.
  • Gdańsk strikers in August 1980 displayed the pope's portrait at the shipyard gate, and many Poles saw his election and visit as having given them the confidence to organise.
  • After martial law he visited Poland in 1983 and 1987 and used sermons to defend workers' rights without calling for revolt; in 1989 he received Gorbachev in the Vatican on 1 December.

Reform movements before 1968

Reform in eastern Europe repeatedly appeared and was suppressed, which showed the limits of Soviet tolerance.

  • East German workers rose on 17 June 1953 against increased work norms and shortages, and Soviet tanks suppressed the uprising in East Berlin and other towns; it spread to hundreds of towns before it was crushed.
  • Poznań workers protested in June 1956, and Władysław Gomułka returned to power in October 1956 after a confrontation with Moscow that was resolved without Soviet intervention.
  • Poland's October 1956 settlement allowed limited autonomy for the Church and for intellectuals, but the Polish party maintained one-party rule.
  • Czechoslovakia's Alexander Dubček became First Secretary of the Communist Party in January 1968, and he launched the Prague Spring reforms.
  • The Soviet response in each case combined concession and threat: Poland in 1956 won a new leadership, Hungary in 1956 was crushed, and Czechoslovakia in 1968 was invaded.
  • Poland's Gomułka ended forced collectivisation in 1956 and released Cardinal Wyszyński from internment, but by the 1960s he had reimposed tight controls.

The Prague Spring and the Brezhnev Doctrine

The Prague Spring of 1968 tried to create socialism with a human face, and Soviet and Warsaw Pact forces ended it by force.

  • The Action Programme of April 1968 proposed freedom of expression, a looser press, the rehabilitation of victims of the 1950s and limits on the secret police.
  • Dubček's government also planned economic reforms that allowed more market mechanisms, and it promised a federal settlement between Czechs and Slovaks.
  • On 20–21 August 1968 Warsaw Pact forces from the Soviet Union, Poland, Hungary and Bulgaria invaded Czechoslovakia (East German units were mobilised but did not cross), and Dubček was arrested and taken to Moscow; Romania refused to take part.
  • Albania withdrew from the Warsaw Pact in protest against the invasion.
  • The Brezhnev Doctrine, set out in a Pravda article in September 1968 and in Brezhnev's speech to the Polish party congress that November, held that socialist states had a duty to intervene when socialism was threatened in another socialist state.
  • Gustáv Husák replaced Dubček and presided over a period of normalisation, in which many reformers were removed from office and purged from the party.
  • Charter 77, a document of Czechoslovak dissidents issued in January 1977 and led by Václav Havel, demanded that the state observe the human rights it had accepted.

Solidarity and the Polish crisis, 1980–1981

Solidarity was the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, and its rise forced the communist state to recognise an opposition.

  • Strikes at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk in August 1980 led to the Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980, which recognised the right to form independent trade unions.
  • Solidarity was led by Lech Wałęsa, an electrician at the shipyard, and at its peak in 1981 it had about ten million members.
  • Its demands included free trade unions, the right to strike, better food supplies and access to the media.
  • On 13 December 1981 General Wojciech Jaruzelski declared martial law, arrested Solidarity leaders and banned the union.
  • The Polish authorities argued that martial law prevented a Soviet invasion; historians accept that Moscow had pressed for firm action but that Jaruzelski also acted to keep communist control, and Soviet archives show the Soviet leaders did not plan to send troops in.
  • Solidarity was legalised again in April 1989 after the Round Table talks between the government and the opposition from February to April 1989.

The revolutions of 1989

In 1989 communist rule collapsed across eastern Europe in a series of peaceful transfers and one violent revolution, largely without Soviet intervention.

  • In Poland, Solidarity won almost all of the seats it contested in the June 1989 elections, and in August 1989 Tadeusz Mazowiecki became prime minister of the first non-communist government in the Soviet bloc since 1948.
  • In Hungary the government began dismantling the border fence with Austria in May 1989, allowed East Germans to cross to Austria from September 1989, and Imre Nagy was reburied as a national hero in June 1989, while in the autumn the party accepted multi-party elections.
  • East German citizens fled through Hungary and Czechoslovakia, and mass demonstrations in Leipzig and East Berlin forced Erich Honecker's resignation on 18 October 1989.
  • The Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November 1989 after an announcement that was misread as permitting unrestricted crossings.
  • In Czechoslovakia the Velvet Revolution began with a student demonstration on 17 November 1989, and Václav Havel was elected president on 29 December 1989.
  • In Bulgaria Todor Zhivkov was removed on 10 November 1989, and in Romania Nicolae Ceaușescu was executed on 25 December 1989 after a violent uprising.

Gorbachev's reforms, the Sinatra Doctrine and the collapse of the USSR

Gorbachev's reforms at home and his non-intervention abroad removed the props that had kept communist states in power.

  • Chernobyl exploded on 26 April 1986 in Soviet Ukraine, and the official response was criticised for concealment, which strengthened the case for glasnost.
  • The INF Treaty of 8 December 1987 eliminated US and Soviet intermediate-range missiles, a landmark in arms control.
  • Gennady Gerasimov's remark in October 1989 that the Brezhnev Doctrine had been replaced by the Sinatra Doctrine meant that Eastern European states could choose their own path.
  • Lithuania declared independence on 11 March 1990, and other Soviet republics followed in demanding sovereignty.
  • The Warsaw Pact was dissolved on 1 July 1991 and Comecon was dissolved in June 1991.
  • A conservative coup against Gorbachev on 19–21 August 1991 failed after Boris Yeltsin rallied opposition in Moscow, and it accelerated the break-up of the union.
  • The Soviet Union was dissolved on 26 December 1991, a day after Gorbachev resigned as its president.
  • Gorbachev and Reagan met at Geneva (November 1985) and Reykjavik (October 1986), where they discussed deep cuts in nuclear weapons, and signed the INF Treaty at Washington in December 1987.
  • Gorbachev told the United Nations in December 1988 that the Soviet Union would cut its armed forces by 500,000 men and withdraw divisions from eastern Europe.
  • Boris Yeltsin was elected president of the Russian republic in June 1991 and became the main rival to Gorbachev's authority.
  • The eight leaders who staged the coup in August 1991 included the vice-president, the head of the KGB and the defence minister; it left Gorbachev weakened and the republics moved to leave the union.

Fragmentation and realignment after 1989

The end of communism led to the reunification of Germany, the break-up of Yugoslavia and the new alignments of central Europe.

  • The German Democratic Republic and the Federal Republic were unified on 3 October 1990, under the Two Plus Four settlement signed in September 1990.
  • The Visegrád group of Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary was formed in February 1991 to coordinate their approach to integration with the West; its members later joined NATO (1999 for Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic) and the EU (2004).
  • Slovenia and Croatia declared independence on 25 June 1991, and fighting followed in Slovenia and Croatia, while Bosnia and Herzegovina's independence led to war from 1992.
  • Czechoslovakia divided peacefully into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on 1 January 1993, after the elections of 1992.
  • The Baltic states of Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania regained independence in 1991 and sought membership of Western institutions.
  • Germany: the chancellor Helmut Kohl pushed for rapid unity after the Wall fell; a currency union came into force on 1 July 1990, and the Two Plus Four Treaty let a united Germany remain in NATO.
  • Yugoslavia: after Tito's death in 1980 a federal presidency governed; from 1991 Serbia under Slobodan Milošević opposed the secession of Slovenia and Croatia, and the war in Bosnia (1992–95) was the most savage conflict in Europe since 1945.
  • The European Community recognised Slovenia and Croatia in January 1992, and the Baltic states were admitted to the United Nations in September 1991, as Western institutions began to expand eastwards.

Assessment: why did communism collapse?

Historians explain the collapse through economic failure, the loss of legitimacy, and the decision not to use force.

  • Achievements of Gorbachev: arms control, the withdrawal from Afghanistan, freedom of speech and the peaceful transfer of power across eastern Europe.
  • Failures of Gorbachev: a worsening economy, the rise of nationalism in the republics and the failure to reform the party's monopoly fast enough.
  • Some historians stress the structural weakness of the command economy and the cost of the arms race, which the West pressed on through defence spending.
  • Others stress the agency of opposition movements such as Solidarity and Charter 77, which kept reform alive through repression.
  • Contemporary Western opinion often credited Reagan's firm policy, while later historians give more weight to the Soviet system's internal crisis.

Key terms

Glasnost
Gorbachev's policy of greater openness in public life, including freer discussion of the Soviet past and criticism of officials.
Communism
A political and economic system in which a single communist party directs the state and the economy is publicly owned and centrally planned.
Perestroika
Gorbachev's programme to restructure the Soviet economy, introducing limited market mechanisms within a planned system.
Prague Spring
The 1968 reform movement in Czechoslovakia under Alexander Dubček, which was ended by a Warsaw Pact invasion on 20–21 August 1968.
Brezhnev Doctrine
The Soviet claim, set out in 1968, that socialist states had the right to intervene when socialism was threatened in another socialist state.
Solidarity
The independent trade union founded at the Gdańsk shipyard in 1980, which became a mass opposition movement in Poland.
Sinatra Doctrine
The informal name for Gorbachev's policy of not intervening in eastern Europe, so that each state could choose its own path.
Velvet Revolution
The peaceful overthrow of communist rule in Czechoslovakia in November and December 1989.
Normalisation
The policy in Czechoslovakia after 1968, under Husák, of reversing the Prague Spring reforms and purging reformers.
Charter 77
A declaration by Czechoslovak dissidents in January 1977 that demanded respect for the human rights guaranteed in the Helsinki Final Act.
Round Table talks
The talks between the Polish government and Solidarity from February to April 1989 that led to semi-free elections.
Dissolution of the USSR
The end of the Soviet Union on 26 December 1991, after the August coup, leaving fifteen independent republics.
