Key personality: John Lennon

Lennon was a member of The Beatles who became one of the most influential figures of 1960s youth culture and an advocate of peace.

  • Born in Liverpool on 9 October 1940, he formed the Quarrymen in 1957, which later developed into The Beatles with Paul McCartney, George Harrison and Ringo Starr.
  • The Beatles played long residencies in Hamburg from 1960 to 1962 and at the Cavern Club in Liverpool, and their first UK number one, From Me to You, followed in April 1963, with Please Please Me reaching the top of some charts in early 1963.
  • Their appearance on The Ed Sullivan Show on 9 February 1964 gave them a mass audience in the USA, and Beatlemania spread across Western Europe.
  • During the Amsterdam Hilton 'bed-in' of March 1969 with Yoko Ono he promoted peace, and his song Give Peace a Chance became an anthem of the anti-war movement.
  • His 1966 remark that the Beatles were more popular than Jesus provoked protests and record burnings in parts of the USA, and it showed the controversy his fame attracted.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a symbol of youthful rebellion; the American authorities tried to deport him in 1972 because of his anti-war activity, and he won the right to stay and was granted a green card in 1976.
  • Historians present him as a symbol of the 1960s counterculture, and his post-Beatles work, including Imagine (1971), is judged as both radical and commercially successful.
  • He was shot dead in New York on 8 December 1980, and his death was widely mourned across Europe and America.
  • The Beatles' manager from 1962, Brian Epstein, polished their image, and George Martin produced their records; their albums such as Rubber Soul (1965) and Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967) showed pop could be an art form.
  • Lennon and McCartney wrote most of the Beatles' songs, and the group broke up in 1970 after the death of Epstein in 1967 and disputes about business and music.
  • In June 1967 the Beatles performed All You Need Is Love on Our World, the first live global television broadcast by satellite, watched by hundreds of millions.
  • He returned his MBE in November 1969 in protest at British support for the US in Vietnam and the Nigerian civil war, and he and Yoko Ono ran peace campaigns including the War Is Over billboard campaign of December 1969.
  • His solo career produced the Plastic Ono Band records and Imagine (1971); after 1975 he spent five years as a househusband caring for his son Sean before the album Double Fantasy (1980).
  • Historians treat the Beatles as the first global pop stars and the model for later artists writing their own songs; critics note that Lennon's radicalism was sometimes self-contradictory.

The teenager and the rise of youth culture

After 1945 young people became a distinct market with their own spending, music and style, which reshaped popular culture.

  • The term teenager spread in the USA in the 1940s and 1950s, and by the 1950s young people had money to spend on records, clothes and cinema.
  • Rock 'n' roll arrived in Europe in the mid-1950s, with Bill Haley's Rock Around the Clock reaching the UK singles chart in 1955 and Elvis Presley's first hits in 1956.
  • Youth rebellion was partly driven by the expansion of secondary education, which kept young people in school longer and gave them a shared identity.
  • Teenage magazines, dances and radio stations such as Radio Luxembourg created a youth media; record sales increased with the cheap 45 rpm single.
  • Live Aid, organised by the Irish singer Bob Geldof and held at Wembley and Philadelphia on 13 July 1985, raised funds for famine in Ethiopia and was watched by a global television audience.

Fashion and subcultures

Dress and music were how young people marked themselves out from their elders.

  • The Mods and Rockers of Britain in the early 1960s, and the later punk subculture of 1976–77, were examples of youth styles that sparked public debate.
  • Teddy boys in Britain in the 1950s wore Edwardian-style jackets and became associated with delinquency; their critics saw rock 'n' roll as a threat, and the films Rebel Without a Cause and The Wild One (1953–55) fed that fear.
  • Fashion expressed the new youth market: Mary Quant popularised the miniskirt in London in the 1960s, Carnaby Street became a centre of fashion, and hippies in the late 1960s adopted long hair, flowers and communal living.
  • Jeans, which symbolised America, were worn by young people across Europe and highly valued in the East, where they were scarce.
  • Mods and Rockers clashed on the beaches of Brighton, Margate and Clacton at Easter 1964, and the press coverage created a moral panic about youth.
  • Punk arose in 1976–77 with the Sex Pistols and The Clash, expressing the anger of unemployed young people in the recession, and the Sex Pistols' God Save the Queen was released in the Silver Jubilee week of 1977.
  • Skinheads and later the new romantics, mostly in Britain, were other styles that fused fashion, music and class identity.

1968 and student protest

The student protests of 1968 in France and elsewhere combined political dissent with the cultural revolt of the youth movement.

  • Student protests began at Nanterre in March 1968 and moved to the Sorbonne in May 1968, and they spread into a general strike involving about ten million workers in France.
  • The 'Night of the Barricades' of 10–11 May 1968 saw fighting in the Latin Quarter of Paris, and the government of Charles de Gaulle faced a severe crisis.
  • Students in West Germany, led by figures such as Rudi Dutschke, protested against the Vietnam War and the legacy of the Nazi past.
  • The 1968 protests did not overthrow governments, but they changed attitudes to authority, sexuality and the role of universities.
  • The French protests began as a demand for university reform and a protest against restrictions at Nanterre, and widened into attacks on de Gaulle's government and on consumer society.
  • De Gaulle left France briefly on 29 May, then called an election in which his supporters won a large majority in June 1968, and he resigned in April 1969 after losing a referendum.
  • In Czechoslovakia, students supported the Prague Spring, and in Poland student protests in March 1968 were suppressed; unrest also hit Italy, Britain and Spain.
  • The 1968 movement influenced the women's, environmental and peace movements of the 1970s.

Sport and the Cold War

Sport became a field of prestige and competition, with national success and the Olympic Games used as displays of political systems.

  • The European Champion Clubs' Cup began in the 1955–56 season, and it became the leading club competition in Western Europe.
  • England won the World Cup on 30 July 1966 at Wembley, and the BBC's television coverage made the tournament a mass event across Europe.
  • The 1972 Munich Olympics were marred by the killing of eleven Israeli athletes and coaches by Palestinian militants on 5–6 September 1972.
  • The United States and some of its allies boycotted the Moscow Olympics of 1980 after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, and the Soviet Union and most of its allies boycotted the Los Angeles Olympics of 1984.
  • East Germany ran a state-sponsored doping programme in the 1970s and 1980s, which was exposed after 1990.
  • The Soviet Union entered the Olympic Games for the first time at Helsinki in 1952 and made the medal table a measure of the rival systems; East Germany, with a population of 17 million, finished near the top in the 1970s and 1980s.
  • At the 1956 Melbourne Olympics the Hungary–USSR water polo match, played soon after the Soviet invasion, was known as the blood in the water match.
  • West Germany's win in the 1954 World Cup final, the Miracle of Bern, against Hungary was a boost to national morale, and Real Madrid won the first five European Cups from 1956 to 1960.
  • Television transformed football and money flowed in through broadcasting rights, sponsorship and advertising, making stars such as Pelé, Johan Cruyff and George Best celebrities.

Television and the mass media

Television spread rapidly across Western Europe in the 1950s and 1960s, and it changed how people received news, entertainment and advertising.

  • The Eurovision Song Contest began in May 1956 at Lugano, and it became a shared event across Western Europe in the 1960s.
  • Commercial television began in Britain with ITV in September 1955, challenging the BBC's monopoly of public broadcasting.
  • Public broadcasting in Western Europe, including the BBC, RAI in Italy and ARD and ZDF in West Germany, was funded by licence fees and governed by public-service duties.
  • Pirate radio stations such as Radio Caroline, which began broadcasting in 1964, played pop music that the official BBC radio stations did not provide.
  • Television's spread changed advertising, politics and sport, and political leaders increasingly had to present themselves on screen.
  • Television spread from a few sets in 1950 to being owned by most households in Western Europe by the 1970s, and the Coronation of Elizabeth II in June 1953 persuaded many British families to buy sets.
  • The European Broadcasting Union's Eurovision network, founded in 1954, linked national broadcasters and carried the Eurovision Song Contest and live coverage of major sporting events across borders.
  • Commercial and public broadcasting differed: ITV and radio stations such as Radio Luxembourg depended on advertising, while the BBC, funded by the licence fee, was bound to inform, educate and entertain.
  • In Italy and France, state control of television was used by governments, and in the East television was used by the party for propaganda, though East Germans could often receive West German broadcasts.
  • Newspapers remained influential: the mass-circulation tabloids such as Bild in West Germany and the Daily Mirror in Britain reached millions of readers.

Youth culture in the East

  • Young people in the East listened to Western music on Radio Luxembourg, Radio Free Europe and tapes copied by hand, and the authorities in the bloc tried to control rock music.
  • In Czechoslovakia, the arrest and trial of members of the rock band the Plastic People of the Universe in 1976 helped to inspire Charter 77.
  • In East Germany the state sponsored the Free German Youth, and a ban on Western beat music in 1965 was followed by a limited tolerance in the 1970s.
  • In the Soviet Union, the Komsomol organised youth, and the stilyagi of the 1950s were young people who copied Western fashions and jazz.

Assessment: liberation or commercial manipulation?

Historians debate whether youth culture and the mass media were a form of liberation or a commercial system that absorbed rebellion.

  • Achievements: youth culture broadened tastes, challenged the authority of schools and churches, and gave young people a voice in politics and art.
  • Limits: much youth culture was commercially packaged, and the record companies and advertisers shaped what young people bought.
  • Contemporaries saw youth culture as a moral danger, with newspapers and politicians warning about delinquency, while young people saw it as freedom.
  • Later historians see the period as a major turning point in the cultural life of Western Europe, but they stress that its effects varied by class, country and gender.

Key terms

Teenager
A young person between the ages of thirteen and nineteen, treated after the 1940s as a distinct group with its own spending power and culture.
Pop star
A performer of popular music whose fame is spread through records, television and media coverage to a mass audience.
Mass media
The means of communication, such as television, radio, cinema and the press, that reach large audiences.
Counterculture
A culture that rejects the dominant values of a society, as the youth movements of the 1960s did.
Beatlemania
The mass enthusiasm for The Beatles in the early 1960s, marked by crowds and media attention.
Public broadcasting
Broadcasting funded by licence fees or the state and governed by public-service duties, as the BBC was.
Subculture
A youth group with its own style, music and values, such as teddy boys, mods, rockers, hippies and punks.
Eurovision
The network of European television broadcasters, and its annual Song Contest, which began in 1956.
Commercial broadcasting
Radio or television funded by advertising, such as ITV in Britain, as opposed to public service broadcasting funded by the licence fee.
