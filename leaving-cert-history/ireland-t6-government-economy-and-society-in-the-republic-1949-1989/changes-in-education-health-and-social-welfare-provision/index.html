Education before the reforms and Investment in Education

Until the 1960s the state left most second-level education to the churches and to parents who could pay.

  • Before 1967 most children left school at 14 with only a primary education; secondary schools charged fees and were mostly owned by religious orders, while vocational schools, set up under the 1930 Vocational Education Act, were run by local committees and had lower status.
  • Ireland had no state policy for the expansion of education before the 1960s, and the Department of Education was seen as a passive administrator.
  • Planning for economic growth after 1958 raised the question of whether the country was educating enough people with the skills it needed.
  • Patrick Hillery, Minister for Education from 1959 to 1965, commissioned a survey with the help of the OECD.
  • Investment in Education, published in 1965, showed wide differences in participation by social class and region and a shortage of trained teachers and technical skills.
  • The report argued that education was an investment in growth and not only a cultural or religious matter, and it recommended expanding second-level and technical education.

Free secondary education and comprehensive schools

Two ministers, Hillery and then Donogh O'Malley, turned the report's findings into reforms.

  • In 1963 Hillery announced a new type of second-level school, the comprehensive school, which would offer both academic and practical subjects to pupils of all abilities and be funded by the state.
  • The first comprehensive schools opened in 1966, among them schools at Carraroe, Co. Galway, and Cootehill, Co. Cavan, in areas that lacked second-level schools.
  • Donogh O'Malley became Minister for Education in July 1966 and announced on 10 September 1966 that free second-level education would be introduced the following year.
  • O'Malley is remembered for announcing the scheme boldly, and it is often said that he did so without having settled the cost with the Department of Finance.
  • The scheme began in September 1967 and abolished fees in schools that joined it, and it was accompanied by free school transport, which allowed rural pupils to reach a school.
  • Most secondary schools joined, and enrolment at second level rose steeply in the years that followed.
  • O'Malley died in March 1968 aged 47, and Brian Lenihan and then Pádraig Faulkner as Ministers for Education continued the work.
  • The Primary Certificate examination was abolished in 1967, and the school-leaving age was raised from 14 to 15 in 1972.

Community schools, technical colleges and third-level access

  • Community schools were introduced from the early 1970s, usually by merging a local secondary school and a vocational school, so as to offer a full range of subjects to all pupils and to serve adults in the area.
  • Regional Technical Colleges, which offered certificate and diploma courses in technology and business, opened from 1970 in towns such as Carlow, Dundalk, Athlone and Waterford.
  • The Higher Education Authority was set up in 1968 to advise the government on university funding and to plan expansion.
  • A higher education grants scheme was introduced in 1968 to help students from poorer homes to attend university or college.
  • The National Institute for Higher Education in Limerick opened in 1972 with an emphasis on technology and business.
  • The number of students in third-level education grew strongly through the 1970s, and more women and more students from rural and working-class homes reached university than before.

The new primary curriculum, 1971

  • A new curriculum for primary schools was introduced in 1971 after years of planning by a committee of teachers, inspectors and others.
  • It replaced a curriculum built on memorisation and examination with one that emphasised the needs and interests of the individual child.
  • It gave more attention to activity-based learning, to art, music and drama, and to the teaching of Irish through speaking rather than drill.
  • Teachers needed retraining, and some, along with some parents, found the change difficult in large classes and poor buildings.

Health services: the Health Act 1953, the VHI and the health boards

Health policy moved gradually from a means-tested service towards wider public provision, with continuing disputes over who should provide it.

  • The Health Act 1953 replaced the Mother and Child Scheme with means-tested maternity and child services and set out eligibility for public hospital care according to income.
  • Before the health boards, rural primary care was provided mainly through the dispensary system run by the county authorities, with dispensary doctors treating medical card holders.
  • The Voluntary Health Insurance Board was set up in 1957 to offer private health insurance, mainly to people who earned too much to qualify for free hospital care.
  • The Fitzgerald Report of 1968 on hospital services recommended fewer, larger regional hospitals, a change that proved controversial in local communities.
  • The Health Act 1970 created eight regional health boards, which began work in 1971 and took over the functions of the old county health authorities.
  • Comhairle na nOspidéal, set up in 1972, regulated the appointment of consultants and the structure of hospital services.
  • Medical cards, which gave lower-income families free GP care and medicines, remained the main way of providing free primary care, while better-off patients paid for visits.
  • Hospital building was funded partly by the Hospitals Sweepstake, and large projects such as Beaumont Hospital in Dublin were delayed for years by cost and planning disputes.

Social welfare provision

Welfare payments grew in range and level, especially after 1965, as the economy grew and the state's role in family support expanded.

  • Children's allowance, first introduced in 1944, was paid to families and its value and coverage were raised over the following decades.
  • The Social Welfare Act 1952 gathered the existing social insurance schemes into a single system covering unemployment, illness, widowhood and old age.
  • Contributory old age pensions were introduced in 1961 for insured workers, in addition to the non-contributory pension.
  • Free travel for old age pensioners was introduced in 1967 and an electricity allowance followed in 1968.
  • Deserted wife's benefit was introduced in 1970, and an unmarried mother's allowance followed in 1973, recognising mothers who had children outside marriage as claimants in their own right.
  • Pay-related benefit for unemployed and sick workers was introduced in 1974, tying payments to previous earnings.
  • Welfare spending rose as a share of national income in the 1970s, partly because unemployment grew after the oil crises (see the notes on the economic challenges of the 1970s and 1980s).

Housing, suburbs and amenities

  • Local authorities built large numbers of houses and flats from the 1950s onwards, replacing city-centre tenements and slums and housing families on new estates at the edge of the cities.
  • The Housing Act 1966 allowed tenants to buy their local authority houses, which increased home ownership.
  • Ballymun, in north Dublin, was built by Dublin Corporation between 1966 and 1969 as a large estate of flats, including seven fifteen-storey towers named after the leaders of 1916; poor facilities, unemployment and decay later gave it a bad name, and it was demolished and rebuilt from the late 1990s.
  • Planners in the late 1960s recommended new towns at Tallaght, Clondalkin and Blanchardstown around Dublin, and suburban growth transformed the edges of Dublin, Cork and Limerick.
  • The ESB's rural electrification scheme, begun in 1946, had connected nearly all rural homes by the mid-1970s, bringing lighting, radio, television and appliances to farms.
  • Group water schemes, supported by state grants from the 1960s, brought piped water to rural homes that had relied on wells and pumps.
  • Electricity and running water changed daily life, especially for women, and they made it easier to keep young people on the land or in rural towns.

Key figures in education reform: Patrick Hillery and Donogh O'Malley

  • Patrick Hillery, from Miltown Malbay, Co. Clare, was Minister for Education from 1959 to 1965, then Minister for Industry and Commerce, for Labour and from 1969 to 1973 for External Affairs, before becoming President in 1976.
  • Hillery's contribution was the planning: he commissioned the survey that became Investment in Education and announced the comprehensive schools in 1963.
  • Donogh O'Malley, a Limerick TD, was Minister for Health in 1965 and 1966 and Minister for Education from 1966 until his death in March 1968.
  • O'Malley's contribution was the political decision: his announcement of free second-level education turned a long-term plan into an immediate promise.
  • Historians generally credit Hillery with the groundwork and O'Malley with the public breakthrough, and note that the headline of 'free education' was a simplification because some costs remained.

Pressures and limits in education

  • The churches kept control of management in most primary and secondary schools, and the hierarchy was wary of comprehensive and community schools, so compromises gave the bishops representation on boards of management.
  • Many schools were small, overcrowded and short of qualified teachers when enrolment rose after 1967.
  • Tension between the academic secondary tradition and the practical vocational tradition continued, since the Leaving Certificate points for college places favoured academic subjects.
  • Free fees did not remove other costs such as books, uniforms and travel, so poorer families still faced barriers.
  • Rural areas, especially in the west, still had fewer second-level and third-level places than the east.

Assessment: equality of opportunity and welfare provision

  • Achievements: free post-primary education and transport widened access, a state-wide hospital and health board structure was created, and social welfare protected more families, including lone mothers.
  • Limits: class differences in educational attainment persisted, the VHI and the dual system left health care unequal, and some large housing schemes, notably Ballymun, created new social problems.
  • Contemporary view: parents, teachers and unions welcomed the reforms, while the churches accepted the expansion but were concerned to keep control of their schools.
  • Later view: historians stress that the reforms were driven as much by the economic need for skills as by social ideals, and that the benefits went first to the middle classes, but they accept that they were a major change of direction.

Key terms

Equality of opportunity
The principle that access to education, health care and welfare should not depend on a person's wealth, class or place of residence.
Investment in Education
The 1965 survey, commissioned by Patrick Hillery, that reported on participation and needs in Irish education and recommended expansion.
Comprehensive school
A state-funded second-level school offering both academic and practical subjects to pupils of all abilities, first opened in 1966.
Community school
A second-level school, usually formed by merging a secondary and a vocational school, offering a full range of subjects and adult education.
Regional Technical College
A college offering technical and business courses at certificate and diploma level, opened from 1970 in regional towns.
Free secondary education
The scheme announced by Donogh O'Malley in 1966 and begun in 1967 that ended fees in participating post-primary schools.
Voluntary Health Insurance Board
The body set up in 1957 to provide private health insurance to those not entitled to free hospital care.
Health board
One of eight regional bodies created by the Health Act 1970 to run health and hospital services in their areas.
Children's allowance
A regular state payment to families with children, introduced in 1944 and extended over the following decades.
Rural electrification
The ESB scheme begun in 1946 to connect farms and villages to the electricity supply, completed in the 1970s.
