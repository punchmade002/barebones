Population change and emigration, 1949–1989

A century of decline ended in the 1960s, and the population then grew faster than at any time since the Famine.

  • The census recorded 2,960,593 people in 1951 and 2,818,341 in 1961, a fall of about 140,000 caused by emigration.
  • The population rose to about 2.88 million in 1966 and about 2.98 million in 1971, as net emigration fell and the birth rate remained high.
  • It then grew rapidly, to about 3.44 million in 1981 and about 3.54 million in 1986, the highest figure since the 1860s.
  • Births reached a peak of about 74,000 around 1980, and the population was young, with a large proportion under 25.
  • The increase slowed after 1986, when heavy emigration returned, and the 1991 census recorded a small fall.
  • Net emigration was about 400,000 in the 1950s, and most emigrants were young people going to Britain for work.
  • Economic expansion in the 1960s cut net emigration to about 100,000 over the decade.
  • In the 1970s the flow reversed: about 100,000 more people arrived than left, many of them Irish emigrants returning home as Britain suffered recession and Ireland joined the EEC.
  • Emigration returned in the 1980s, with around 200,000 net emigrants over the decade, as unemployment passed 15 per cent; these emigrants were often well educated and went to Britain, the United States, Australia and continental Europe.
  • Emigration was a continuing feature of Irish society, and it shaped family life, rural depopulation and attitudes to politics (see the notes on the economic challenges of the 1970s and 1980s).

Urbanisation, marriage and family size

  • The urban population passed half the total around 1971, and more than a quarter of the people lived in the Dublin area by 1981.
  • New suburbs and towns such as Tallaght, Clondalkin and Blanchardstown absorbed people moving from the countryside, while small farms and rural villages lost population.
  • Marriage rates rose in the 1960s and 1970s, and people married at a younger age than in the 1950s, when late marriage and non-marriage were common.
  • Large families were the norm in the 1960s, but the fertility rate fell steadily afterwards, to close to replacement level by the late 1980s.
  • Falling family size was linked to more use of contraception, which was illegal to sell until the late 1970s, and to women's rising education and employment.

The marriage bar and the Commission on the Status of Women

  • The marriage bar required women in the civil service and other public posts to resign when they married; it was removed for primary teachers in 1958 and for the civil service in 1973.
  • Women were also paid less than men for similar work, and many employers, including banks, imposed their own bars on married women.
  • The Commission on the Status of Women, chaired by Thekla Beere, was set up by the Lynch government in 1970 after pressure from women's organisations.
  • Its interim report in 1971 recommended equal pay, and its final report in December 1972 made 49 recommendations on equal pay, employment, education, family law and social welfare.
  • The report provided a programme for reform, and many of its recommendations were implemented during the 1970s and 1980s.

The women's movement and the contraceptive train

  • The Irish Women's Liberation Movement was formed in 1970 by journalists and activists including Mary Kenny, Nell McCafferty, June Levine and Nuala Fennell, and its manifesto Chains or Change (1971) demanded equal pay, equal education, contraception and legal changes for unmarried mothers and deserted wives.
  • On 22 May 1971 dozens of members travelled by train from Dublin to Belfast, bought contraceptives that were illegal to import into the Republic and returned openly to Connolly Station, challenging customs officers to stop them.
  • The action attracted wide media attention, including coverage on The Late Late Show, and made contraception a public issue.
  • Later groups such as Women's Aid, founded in 1974 to help victims of domestic violence, and the Well Woman Centre, opened in 1977, provided services that the state did not.
  • Women's organisations also used the trade unions and the Council for the Status of Women to lobby ministers.

Contraception: the McGee case and the Health (Family Planning) Act

The law on contraception was the most controversial issue in the status of women in the 1970s.

  • Mary McGee, a mother of four who was medically advised against another pregnancy, took a case against the State after customs seized contraceptive jelly sent to her from abroad.
  • In McGee v Attorney General, decided in December 1973, the Supreme Court found by four to one that the ban on importing contraceptives was unconstitutional as it infringed the right to marital privacy.
  • The Fine Gael-Labour government introduced a bill to regulate contraceptives in 1974, but it was defeated when Taoiseach Liam Cosgrave himself voted against it.
  • The Health (Family Planning) Act 1979, introduced by Haughey as Minister for Health, allowed contraceptives to be sold on prescription to people with a genuine need for family planning, described by Haughey as an Irish solution to an Irish problem.
  • The Act took effect in 1980; in 1985 sale to people over 18 without prescription was allowed (see the notes on Lynch, Haughey and FitzGerald).
  • The Catholic Church officially opposed artificial contraception, a position reaffirmed by Pope Paul VI in 1968, but surveys suggested that many Irish married Catholics ignored the rule.

Equality legislation, 1973–1987

  • Equal pay for the public service was introduced in 1973, shortly after the removal of the marriage bar.
  • The Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 required equal pay for men and women doing the same or similar work, with the principle taking effect from the end of 1975 in line with the EEC directive.
  • The Employment Equality Act 1977 made it unlawful to discriminate on grounds of sex or marital status in recruitment, training and promotion.
  • The Employment Equality Agency was set up under the 1977 Act to promote equality, investigate complaints and advise the government.
  • Other measures included the Juries Act 1976, which ended the exclusion of women from jury service, and the Family Home Protection Act 1976, which stopped a husband selling the family home without his wife's consent.
  • The Maternity Protection of Employees Act 1981 gave women the right to leave and to return to their jobs, and social welfare was reformed in the 1980s to give married women equal treatment, under pressure from EEC law.

Women in politics and public life

  • Women remained a small minority in the Dáil: only a handful sat in the 1970s, six after the 1977 election, and the number reached 14 by 1987.
  • Eileen Desmond of Labour became Minister for Health and Social Welfare in 1981, the first woman in a cabinet since Constance Markievicz.
  • Gemma Hussey of Fine Gael was Minister for Education from 1982 to 1987, and Nuala Fennell was a Minister of State for women's affairs from 1982.
  • More women entered the workforce, particularly married women, whose participation rate rose from about 5 per cent in 1961 to about 17 per cent in 1981.
  • Mary Robinson, a senator from 1969, was the most prominent woman in public life outside government.

Key personality: Sylvia Meehan

Meehan was a trade unionist and campaigner who became the first head of the Employment Equality Agency.

  • Sylvia Meehan was born in Dublin in 1929, worked as a teacher, and was vice-president of the Association of Secondary Teachers, Ireland and chairperson of the women's committee of the Irish Congress of Trade Unions.
  • She was appointed in 1977 as the first chairperson of the new Employment Equality Agency, a post she held until 1992.
  • She was a participant in the enforcement of the equal pay and equal treatment laws, supervising research, investigations and advice on discrimination at work.
  • The agency's research into the position of girls and women in education, pay, maternity leave and childcare helped to shape later legislation.
  • Contemporaries regarded her as an effective and determined advocate, though employers and some politicians resisted the agency's findings.
  • Historians see her as a key figure in turning the 1970s equality laws into practice, though they stress that the agency's powers were limited; she died in 2018.

Key personality: Mary Robinson

Robinson was a lawyer and senator who used the courts and the Oireachtas to challenge discriminatory law.

  • Mary Robinson was born in Ballina, Co. Mayo, in 1944, graduated from Trinity College Dublin, was called to the bar in 1967, studied at Harvard and became Reid Professor of Law at Trinity at the age of 25.
  • She was elected to the Seanad for the University of Dublin in 1969 and served until 1989, as an independent.
  • She introduced private members' bills in the early 1970s to liberalise the law on contraception, which the Seanad refused to debate, and later sought reform of the law on homosexuality; neither became law at the time.
  • She campaigned for women to serve on juries, a change enacted in the Juries Act 1976.
  • As a barrister she took cases on behalf of individuals against the State, including a tax case brought by a married couple, which the Supreme Court decided in their favour in 1980, and the long-running case of the campaigner David Norris against the law criminalising homosexual acts.
  • She joined the Labour Party in 1976 and left it in 1985 in protest at the Anglo-Irish Agreement, and she was a Dublin city councillor from 1979 to 1983.
  • Contemporaries saw her as a liberal reformer and an able lawyer; she was attacked by conservatives and the church for her views, but respected for her persistence.
  • Historians credit her with creating a legal and political basis for reform in the 1970s and 1980s that prepared the way for the changes of the 1990s and for her election as President in 1990.

Assessment: the extent of social change

  • Achievements: the marriage bar ended, equal pay and employment equality were legislated, contraception became legally available and women gained new organisations, rights and visibility.
  • Limits: women remained scarce in politics and senior jobs, pay gaps persisted, divorce and abortion remained prohibited and the church kept its influence.
  • Contemporary view: conservatives argued that the reforms threatened the family and Catholic values; reformers saw the changes as too little and too slow.
  • Later view: historians see the period as the beginning of social liberalisation, driven by economic change, EEC law and campaigning, but argue that change was uneven and that Ireland remained more conservative than many European states in the 1980s.

Key terms

Discrimination
Unequal treatment of people on grounds such as sex or marital status, made unlawful in pay and employment by the 1974 and 1977 Acts.
Marriage bar
The rule that forced women to resign from public service jobs on marriage, removed for the civil service in 1973.
Equality of opportunity
The principle that men and women should have the same access to education, jobs and promotion.
Liberalisation
The relaxation of legal or social restrictions, for example on contraception, homosexuality or censorship.
Employment Equality Agency
The body set up under the Employment Equality Act 1977 to promote equality and investigate discrimination at work.
Commission on the Status of Women
The commission set up in 1970 under Thekla Beere, which reported in 1972 with 49 recommendations.
Irish Women's Liberation Movement
The group formed in 1970 that campaigned for equal pay, contraception and legal change, and organised the contraceptive train.
Contraceptive train
The journey of 22 May 1971 in which women travelled to Belfast and brought contraceptives back to Dublin.
McGee v Attorney General
The 1973 Supreme Court case that recognised married couples' right to privacy and struck down the ban on importing contraceptives.
Net emigration
The number of people leaving a country minus the number arriving to live there.
