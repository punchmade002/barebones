Background: from radio to television

Irish television began late, after a long debate about whether and how the state should run it.

  • Radio had been run by the state since 2RN opened in Dublin in 1926, and by the 1950s it was called Radio Éireann and was part of the Department of Posts and Telegraphs.
  • Before 1961 viewers in the east, the north and along the coast could pick up BBC and Ulster Television signals from Britain and Northern Ireland, and these showed that Irish demand for television was growing.
  • The government decided to set up a national service, financed by licence fees and advertising, and the Broadcasting Authority Act 1960 created the Radio Éireann Authority, soon known as the Radio Telefís Éireann (RTÉ) Authority, to run radio and television.
  • The members of the Authority were appointed by the government, which also set the framework in law.
  • Telefís Éireann began broadcasting on 31 December 1961; President de Valera spoke at the opening and warned that television had a great power for good and evil.
  • Television reached most of the country within a few years as transmitters were built, and by the early 1970s most households had a set.

Control, policy and the Lemass view

  • The Minister for Posts and Telegraphs could direct the Authority in certain matters under the 1960 Act, and Section 31 allowed him to require it to refrain from broadcasting specified material.
  • RTÉ was a statutory body, not a government department, and the Act required it to be impartial in news and current affairs; how far it was independent in practice was a source of conflict.
  • Seán Lemass saw television as a means of national development and public information, and he said that RTÉ was an instrument of public policy, which meant it should support the national goals of the government without being its mouthpiece.
  • Critics, including many in RTÉ, held that a broadcaster could serve the public only if it was free to criticise the government.
  • The tension between the two views shaped the history of RTÉ in the 1960s and the crisis of 1971 and 1972.

Programmes: imports and home-made output

  • Much early output was imported from Britain and the United States, including westerns, comedies and films, and imported programmes gave Irish viewers a view of life elsewhere.
  • Home-produced programmes covered news, sport, current affairs, drama, light entertainment and Irish-language material; coverage of Gaelic games drew large audiences.
  • The Riordans, a drama about a farming family that began in 1965, was a hit with viewers, especially in rural Ireland, and it dealt with real issues of farming life and change.
  • Telefís Scoile, which began on 4 February 1964, broadcast lessons for secondary school pupils and was one of the earliest schools television services in Europe.
  • Irish-language programmes and news bulletins in Irish were broadcast from the start, though they took a small share of airtime and often had a small audience.
  • RTÉ also raised standards for Irish drama and music, and helped writers, actors and entertainers to reach a national audience.

The Late Late Show and Gay Byrne

The Late Late Show, first broadcast in July 1962, became the best-known programme on Irish television and a forum for national debate.

  • The programme was a live Friday night talk show with guests, music and audience participation, and it started as a summer filler before it became a fixture.
  • Gay Byrne presented it from 1962 until 1999, and his skills as a chat-show host brought viewers an open-minded, humorous but probing style.
  • The show discussed subjects that were rarely talked of in public, including sex, marriage, contraception, unmarried mothers and the church.
  • In 1966 a quiz item for married couples led to the episode known as the Bishop and the Nightie: when Gay Byrne asked a wife, Eileen Fox, what she had worn on her honeymoon night, she said she had not worn a nightdress; the remark caused uproar.
  • Bishop Thomas Ryan of Clonfert condemned the programme, and other clergy and some viewers complained, while many viewers defended Byrne and the show.
  • The incident showed that television could provoke public arguments about morality and the limits of church authority, and it brought the Late Late Show to national attention.

Current affairs and clashes with government

  • Seven Days, a current affairs series launched in 1966, used young producers and reporters to investigate housing, poverty, emigration and other social issues.
  • Ministers resented programmes that criticised policy, and complaints were made to the RTÉ Authority, which was under pressure to restrain current affairs.
  • A Seven Days programme on illegal moneylending in 1969 led to a government-ordered inquiry and a row about RTÉ's independence, after which current affairs coverage was reorganised.
  • From the end of the 1960s the Troubles made coverage of Northern Ireland a major source of conflict between RTÉ and the government, which feared that broadcasts would encourage violence.
  • The government held financial and legal power over the broadcaster, as it appointed the Authority and could change the law.

Effects on rural life, religion and politics

  • Television altered how evenings were spent, since people stayed at home to watch rather than go to dances or visit neighbours, and it reduced the isolation of rural communities.
  • It brought national and international news and sport into every home, and gave people in the countryside access to the same culture and entertainment as city people.
  • Programmes on social issues exposed viewers to arguments about contraception, divorce and the church that censorship had kept out of print.
  • Church leaders such as McQuaid were concerned about the effects of television on faith and morals, but the church also used television to broadcast Mass, the Angelus and religious programmes.
  • Politicians had to learn to use the medium: party leaders appeared on television to speak directly to voters, and campaigns began to focus on television appearances and image.
  • Viewers' reactions varied: many welcomed the new window on the world, while others, including some clergy and traditionalists, saw it as a threat to Irish values.

The crisis of 1971 to 1972: Section 31 and the Authority

Coverage of the Northern conflict led to the first serious use of the government's powers over broadcasting.

  • On 1 October 1971 the Minister for Posts and Telegraphs, Gerry Collins, issued the first order under Section 31, directing RTÉ not to broadcast material that could promote or incite to crime or tend to undermine the authority of the State.
  • RTÉ journalists protested that the order restricted reporting of the Troubles and argued that the public had a right to hear different views.
  • On 19 November 1972 the radio programme This Week broadcast an interview by Kevin O'Kelly with Seán Mac Stíofáin, chief of staff of the Provisional IRA, who was arrested the same day.
  • The government regarded the broadcast as a breach of the Section 31 order, and in late November 1972 Collins dismissed the whole RTÉ Authority and appointed a new one.
  • O'Kelly was later jailed for refusing to identify Mac Stíofáin's voice in court.
  • The sacking caused protests by journalists and in the Oireachtas, and it raised the question of whether a state broadcaster could report freely.
  • Section 31 restrictions were extended and widened in the 1970s and 1980s, and they were not ended until 1994.

Key personality: Gay Byrne

Byrne was the presenter of The Late Late Show and the best-known broadcaster in Ireland for decades.

  • Gay Byrne was born in Dublin in 1934, worked in Britain in the 1950s and returned to join Radio Éireann in the late 1950s, becoming a presenter on Telefís Éireann in its first years.
  • He presented The Late Late Show from 1962 to 1999 and a morning radio programme from 1973, which gave him a wide audience in every part of the country.
  • He was a participant in the change of the 1960s: his questions to guests and audience on sex, marriage and religion set the agenda for debate.
  • He generally avoided taking sides, presenting himself as a neutral host, but his willingness to air subjects that the church or the government preferred to avoid had a liberalising effect.
  • Contemporaries were divided, with many welcoming him as a modern voice and others, including some clergy, seeing his programme as an attack on morals.
  • Historians and commentators regard him as a major influence on how Irish people talked about sexuality, the church and social issues, while noting that television reflected changes that were already under way; he died in 2019.

Differing contemporary views of RTÉ's role

  • The government and Lemass: RTÉ should help to modernise the country, explain official policy and support national aims, and ministers expected a degree of cooperation from its Authority.
  • RTÉ journalists and producers: a national broadcaster should be free to investigate and criticise, and many saw ministerial pressure as censorship.
  • The Catholic Church: television was a powerful influence on the young that needed to be watched, and bishops and priests objected to some programmes, though the church also valued the airtime given to religion.
  • Language activists: Irish-language output was too small a share of the schedule, and imported English-language programmes, mostly from Britain and the United States, risked weakening the language further.
  • Rural viewers: programmes such as The Riordans showed their own lives, while others thought the city-based producers misrepresented the countryside.
  • Politicians outside government: opposition parties complained of bias in favour of the government, and some felt that the Authority was too ready to give way to ministers.
  • Historians such as Robert Savage, in his study of television and Irish society in these years, show that RTÉ both reflected changes already under way and helped to speed them, and that its relationship with the state was complicated rather than simply one of control.

Assessment: did RTÉ entertain, challenge and change its viewers?

  • Entertained: it brought sport, drama, light entertainment and music to a national audience, and many programmes, such as The Riordans and The Late Late Show, were hugely popular.
  • Challenged: current affairs and chat shows questioned authority and exposed issues that were taboo, such as contraception, poverty and the Northern conflict.
  • Changed: viewers had a wider view of the world, attitudes to church and state became more questioning, and the habit of national debate grew.
  • Limits: the government held strong powers, Section 31 and the dismissal of the Authority showed that independence was fragile, and Irish-language programmes struggled for audiences.
  • Contemporary view: viewers welcomed the entertainment and new voices, while the church and government saw risks in the programmes.
  • Later view: historians argue that television both entertained and challenged, and that it speeded up changes in attitudes to religion, sexuality and authority, while acknowledging that other factors such as education, emigration and EEC entry mattered too.

Key terms

Censorship
The official restriction or banning of broadcasts, books, films and other material considered harmful or unacceptable.
Liberalisation
The relaxation of legal or social controls, such as those on what could be discussed in public.
Secularisation
The decline in the influence of religion over public life, institutions and personal behaviour.
Section 31
The provision of the Broadcasting Authority Act 1960 under which the minister could direct RTÉ not to broadcast specified material; first used in October 1971.
Broadcasting Authority Act 1960
The law that set up the RTÉ Authority, appointed by the government, to run radio and the new television service.
RTÉ Authority
The body appointed by the government to oversee RTÉ; dismissed by Gerry Collins in November 1972.
Telefís Scoile
The educational television service for schools, which began on 4 February 1964.
The Late Late Show
The live Friday night talk show first broadcast in July 1962, presented by Gay Byrne until 1999.
Seven Days
The current affairs series launched in 1966 that investigated social and political issues and clashed with the government.
Instrument of public policy
Lemass's description of RTÉ's role, which meant that it should serve national goals; critics saw it as a threat to independence.
