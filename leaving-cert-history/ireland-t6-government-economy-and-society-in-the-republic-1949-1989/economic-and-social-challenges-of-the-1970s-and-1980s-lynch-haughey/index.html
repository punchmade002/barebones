Economic shocks of the 1970s

The decade that began with EEC entry brought a series of external shocks that exposed the weaknesses of the Irish economy.

  • Ireland imported almost all of its oil, so it was badly exposed when the Arab members of OPEC cut supplies and raised prices after the Yom Kippur War of October 1973.
  • The oil price rise of 1973 pushed up inflation in the mid-1970s and ended the long post-war boom in Europe, reducing demand for Irish exports.
  • The Fine Gael-Labour coalition of 1973 to 1977 under Liam Cosgrave faced recession, rising unemployment and a large balance of payments deficit.
  • The Iranian Revolution of 1979 caused a second oil shock, which raised fuel costs and prices again, and inflation reached about 20 per cent in 1981.
  • Ireland joined the European Monetary System in March 1979 and broke its link with sterling for the first time since the 1820s; the link had been a feature of the Irish pound since the 1920s.
  • Governments responded to rising unemployment by raising public spending and borrowing, since the state hoped that growth would return, and the national debt rose rapidly.
  • By the early 1980s a large share of tax revenue was being spent on interest on the national debt (see the notes on unemployment and emigration in the 1980s).

Key personality: Jack Lynch (Taoiseach 1966–1973 and 1977–1979)

Lynch succeeded Lemass and led Fianna Fáil through the first years of the Troubles, then returned to power in 1977.

  • John Mary Lynch was born in Cork in 1917 and became a famous hurler, winning six All-Ireland medals in a row; he qualified as a barrister before entering the Dáil in 1948.
  • He was Minister for Education from 1957, for Industry and Commerce from 1959 and for Finance from 1965, and became Taoiseach on 10 November 1966 as a compromise choice between rival candidates.
  • He won the 1969 general election with a majority of seats but lost power in 1973 to the National Coalition of Fine Gael and Labour.
  • In August 1969, after violence in Derry and Belfast, he said in a broadcast that the state could not stand by, and the army set up field hospitals near the border, but he ruled out military intervention.
  • His policy on Northern Ireland was to seek unity by consent and to oppose violence; he condemned internment in August 1971 and recalled the Irish ambassador from London after Bloody Sunday in January 1972.
  • In December 1972, after bombs in Dublin, his government secured passage of amendments to the Offences Against the State Act to deal with the IRA.
  • Strengths: he was popular and conciliatory, kept the party together and stopped the state from being drawn into the conflict in the North.
  • Weaknesses: critics said he was indecisive, that he was slow to remove Haughey and Blaney from his cabinet, and that the 1977 programme he led was financially reckless.
  • Contemporaries admired his decency and moderate approach but doubted his firmness; historians assess him as a skilful manager of the party, whose time was dominated by the crisis in the North and the Arms Crisis.

The Arms Crisis, 1970

The Arms Crisis was the most serious split in Fianna Fáil in the Troubles and tested Lynch's authority.

  • In April 1970 the Special Branch told Lynch that senior ministers might be involved in a plan to import arms for Northern nationalists.
  • On 6 May 1970 Lynch dismissed Charles Haughey, Minister for Finance, and Neil Blaney, Minister for Agriculture, and Kevin Boland resigned from the cabinet in protest.
  • Haughey, Blaney, Captain James Kelly of army intelligence, John Kelly from Belfast and the Belgian businessman Albert Luykx were charged with conspiracy to import arms illegally.
  • The charge against Blaney was dropped before the trial, and Haughey and the other accused were acquitted in October 1970.
  • Haughey always maintained that the government had known about the plan; Lynch's version was that he acted as soon as he had evidence; the question of who knew what is still disputed by historians.
  • The crisis left a lasting division between Lynch's supporters and Haughey's in Fianna Fáil, which continued into the 1980s.

The 1977 election and Lynch's second government

  • Fianna Fáil's manifesto for the June 1977 general election promised to abolish domestic rates and the tax on cars and to create jobs by increasing spending.
  • The party won 84 of the 148 seats, the largest majority in the history of the state.
  • The new government cut taxes and increased public spending, financed by borrowing, in the hope of lifting growth; the current budget deficit and the national debt rose sharply.
  • Growth was strong at first, but inflation, a second oil crisis and the cost of the manifesto turned the position into a crisis by 1979.
  • Fianna Fáil lost two by-elections in Cork in November 1979, which weakened Lynch's authority, and he announced his resignation in December 1979.
  • Haughey defeated George Colley for the leadership of the party by 44 votes to 38 and became Taoiseach on 11 December 1979.

Key personality: Charles Haughey (Taoiseach 1979–1981, 1982 and 1987–1992)

Haughey dominated Irish politics in the 1980s and divided opinion more than any other leader.

  • Charles Haughey was born in 1925 in Castlebar, Co. Mayo, trained as an accountant, married Maureen Lemass, a daughter of Seán Lemass, and became very wealthy; he was Minister for Justice, for Agriculture and, from 1966 to 1970, for Finance.
  • He was dismissed in the Arms Crisis, spent the 1970s rebuilding his support in the party and returned as Minister for Health and Social Welfare in 1977.
  • In a television address on 9 January 1980 he told the country that it was living beyond its means and called for sacrifice, but his government's spending and borrowing continued to rise.
  • His first government lasted until June 1981; he returned briefly in March 1982 and held office again from March 1987.
  • In 1987 he led a minority government that, with the support of Fine Gael under Alan Dukes (the Tallaght Strategy), cut public spending sharply under his Minister for Finance Ray MacSharry.
  • The Programme for National Recovery, agreed in October 1987 with the unions, employers and farmers, held pay increases to low levels in return for a commitment to reduce the deficit.
  • Strengths: charisma, drive, loyalty from followers and a talent for tactics; weaknesses: secrecy, extravagance and the use of power for personal advantage.
  • Contemporaries divided between those who saw him as a bold nationalist who got things done and those who saw him as a corrupting influence; the tribunals of the 1990s and 2000s later found that he had received large sums from businessmen.
  • Historians now generally judge him a gifted but flawed leader whose record on the public finances was poor before 1987, and whose later cuts, along with the PNR, helped to prepare recovery in the 1990s.

Political instability, 1981–1982

Three general elections in eighteen months produced weak governments and a sense of crisis.

  • The June 1981 election produced a Fine Gael-Labour coalition under Garret FitzGerald, which depended on independent TDs.
  • That government fell in January 1982 when its budget, which proposed VAT on children's clothes and shoes, was defeated, and a new election followed in February 1982.
  • Haughey returned to office in March 1982 with the support of the Workers' Party, some independents and Tony Gregory, who secured investment for inner-city Dublin in return.
  • In 1982 a series of scandals followed: phone-tapping of journalists, a leadership challenge against Haughey, and the arrest in August of the murderer Malcolm MacArthur in the apartment of the Attorney General, Patrick Connolly.
  • A description of these events as grotesque, unbelievable, bizarre and unprecedented was turned into the acronym GUBU by Conor Cruise O'Brien, and it became shorthand for the political culture of the time.
  • Haughey's government fell in November 1982 when the Workers' Party and Tony Gregory withdrew their support, and the third election in eighteen months brought FitzGerald back to power.

Key personality: Garret FitzGerald (Taoiseach 1981–1982 and 1982–1987)

FitzGerald, an economist and Fine Gael leader, headed two coalitions with Labour and tried to modernise the state.

  • Garret FitzGerald was born in 1926, the son of Desmond FitzGerald, a minister in the first Free State government; he worked as an economist and lecturer before becoming Minister for Foreign Affairs from 1973 to 1977.
  • He became leader of Fine Gael in 1977 and was Taoiseach from June 1981 to March 1982 and from December 1982 to March 1987.
  • In 1981 he announced a constitutional crusade to make the Republic more pluralist and thereby more attractive to Northern unionists, with divorce reform and the removal of Catholic influences from the law.
  • The crusade had limited results: the 1983 referendum inserted a constitutional ban on abortion, the Eighth Amendment, which FitzGerald had not wished to see written in that form, and the 1986 divorce referendum was defeated.
  • In 1985 his government liberalised the law on contraceptives, allowing sale without prescription to people over 18.
  • He established the New Ireland Forum in 1983, and negotiated the Anglo-Irish Agreement with Margaret Thatcher in 1985 (see the notes on Anglo-Irish relations).
  • His economic record was weak: his coalition tried to reduce the deficit but unemployment and the debt kept rising, and Labour left the government in January 1987 over spending cuts.
  • Strengths: intellect, integrity and a vision of a modern, tolerant Ireland; weaknesses: a tendency to overreach and difficulty in holding a coalition together on economic policy.
  • Contemporaries admired his honesty and energy but saw him as unworldly; historians credit him with helping to open Irish politics to new ideas, but judge his economic record the weakest part.

Referendums of 1983 and 1986 and the pluralism debate

  • The Eighth Amendment, which recognised the right to life of the unborn, was approved on 7 September 1983 by about two to one in a referendum.
  • It was promoted by anti-abortion groups, and both Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael leaders gave way to the pressure, although FitzGerald had concerns about the wording.
  • The divorce referendum of 26 June 1986 sought to remove the constitutional ban on divorce and was defeated by about two to one.
  • The results showed that the church still had great influence over social questions in the 1980s, and that liberalisation had not progressed as FitzGerald had hoped.
  • Debates about pluralism and the Constitution in the 1980s are an important background to the changes of the 1990s (see the notes on culture and religion).

Unemployment and emigration in the 1980s

  • Unemployment rose from about 7 per cent in 1979 to over 15 per cent by the mid-1980s, with the numbers on the live register approaching a quarter of a million.
  • Emigration returned on a large scale, with about 200,000 more people leaving than arriving over the decade, many of them well-educated young people heading for Britain, the United States and continental Europe.
  • The public debt rose to more than 100 per cent of national income by 1987, and interest payments absorbed a large part of tax revenue.
  • Tax rates rose to pay for services, which discouraged investment and made the burden on workers heavy.
  • Cuts in spending in 1987 and the PNR brought the deficit down, laying the base for the recovery of the 1990s.

Assessment: the leadership of the 1970s and 1980s

  • Lynch: kept the state out of the Northern conflict and held the party together, but left the economy in difficulty after 1977.
  • Haughey: showed drive, and in 1987 presided over a turn towards financial discipline, but his earlier government was marked by deficits and scandal.
  • FitzGerald: put pluralism and the Northern question on the agenda, but was unable to deliver social change in referendums or economic recovery.
  • Contemporary view: voters repeatedly turned governments out, partly because each had to impose or defend painful measures.
  • Later view: historians describe the 1980s as a lost decade for growth, though they see the late 1980s as the beginning of the recovery, and debate how far credit belongs to Haughey, MacSharry, Dukes and the European funds.

Key terms

Balance of payments
The record of a country's trade and financial dealings with the rest of the world; a deficit means more money leaves than enters.
Liberalisation
The relaxation of legal or social controls, for example on contraception, divorce or censorship.
Pluralism
The principle that a society contains several different groups and beliefs, and that the state should accommodate them rather than favour one.
Arms Crisis
The 1970 crisis in which Lynch dismissed Haughey and Blaney over a plan to import arms for Northern nationalists.
GUBU
An acronym, from Conor Cruise O'Brien, for the 1982 scandals, taken from the description grotesque, unbelievable, bizarre and unprecedented.
New Ireland Forum
The 1983 to 1984 forum of nationalist parties from the Republic and the SDLP that reported on possible futures for Ireland.
Programme for National Recovery
The October 1987 agreement between government, unions, employers and farmers on pay restraint and a reduction in the deficit.
Tallaght Strategy
Fine Gael leader Alan Dukes's decision in 1987 not to oppose the government's budget cuts so long as they continued.
National debt
The total amount owed by the state, which rose rapidly in the late 1970s and 1980s as the government borrowed to fund services.
Eighth Amendment
The 1983 constitutional amendment recognising the equal right to life of the unborn.
