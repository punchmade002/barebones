Membership of the United Nations, 1955

Ireland had been outside international organisations since the League of Nations and wished to join the UN as soon as it was founded.

  • Ireland applied for UN membership in 1946, but the Soviet Union used its veto in the Security Council, citing Ireland's neutrality in the Second World War and the lack of diplomatic relations between the two states.
  • The deadlock over new members lasted until 1955, when a package deal in the General Assembly allowed sixteen states to be admitted together.
  • Ireland was admitted on 14 December 1955, alongside states as varied as Italy, Spain, Austria, Hungary and Jordan.
  • Ireland was neutral in the Cold War sense of belonging to no military bloc, but it was a Western, Christian democracy and generally sympathetic to the West on issues of communism.
  • Membership gave a small state a voice on world affairs and a means of showing independence of Britain.

Frank Aiken and an independent Irish line at the UN

Frank Aiken, Minister for External Affairs from 1951 to 1954 and from 1957 to 1969, used the UN to pursue a policy not tied to the Western powers.

  • Aiken was a veteran of the War of Independence and Civil War who had been in de Valera's governments since 1932, and he led the Irish delegation to the General Assembly nearly every year.
  • From 1957 Ireland would not support the American-led move to block debate on the seat of the People's Republic of China, a position that irritated Washington.
  • In 1959 Ireland and Malaya sponsored a resolution at the UN criticising the Chinese suppression of human rights in Tibet.
  • Ireland backed discussion of Algeria's self-determination and later opposed apartheid in South Africa, which reflected an anti-colonial approach.
  • Aiken's main contribution was in the effort to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons: Irish resolutions were introduced from 1958, and Resolution 1665 of 4 December 1961 called on all states to agree not to transfer or acquire nuclear weapons.
  • That resolution led to negotiations on the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, which opened for signature in 1968, and Ireland was the first state to sign it.
  • Critics said the Irish line was naive or inconsistent with Ireland's position in the West; supporters said it showed that a small state could influence world opinion.

Peacekeeping: the Congo, Cyprus and Lebanon

Irish soldiers served in UN peacekeeping from 1958, and the Congo was the first major test.

  • Irish officers served as UN observers in Lebanon from 1958, and Irish troops joined the UN force in the Congo (ONUC) in July 1960, soon after the Congo gained independence from Belgium and fell into disorder.
  • On 8 November 1960 an Irish patrol was ambushed at Niemba in Katanga, and nine soldiers were killed, the first big loss of Irish life in UN service.
  • In September 1961, 155 men of A Company, 35th Battalion, under Commandant Pat Quinlan, were besieged at Jadotville by Katangese forces and foreign mercenaries; after five days, with ammunition and water short, they surrendered, and were held for weeks before being released (see the notes on the decolonisation of Africa).
  • About 6,000 Irish soldiers served in the Congo until 1964, and twenty-six lost their lives.
  • Irish troops joined the UN force in Cyprus (UNFICYP) from 1964, to keep apart the Greek and Turkish Cypriot communities.
  • From 1978 an Irish battalion served with the UN Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL), set up after Israel's invasion of southern Lebanon.
  • Peacekeeping linked Ireland's identity as a neutral state to practical service, and it gave the Defence Forces an important role and good reputation abroad.

Frederick Boland and the General Assembly, 1960

  • Frederick Boland, formerly Secretary of the Department of External Affairs and ambassador in London, was Ireland's permanent representative at the UN from 1956.
  • He was elected President of the fifteenth session of the General Assembly, which began in September 1960, an honour for a small state that had joined only five years earlier.
  • The session was dominated by the crisis in the Congo and by Cold War tension, and in October 1960 Boland broke his gavel while trying to restore order during a heated exchange involving the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev.
  • Boland's election showed the respect Ireland had gained, and the session brought more African states into the UN as colonies became independent.
  • Ireland also served on the Security Council in 1962, the first of several terms.

The EEC and the first application, 1961–1963

The Treaty of Rome in 1957 created a common market of six states, and Irish policy-makers soon saw that staying outside it carried risks.

  • The European Economic Community was founded by France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg in 1957, with a customs union and a common external tariff.
  • When Britain, Ireland's main market, applied to join, Irish ministers feared that a British entry would leave Ireland outside a market where its farm exports would face barriers.
  • Ireland formally applied on 31 July 1961, and the Dáil and the main parties and farmers' groups supported the application.
  • Supporters hoped to gain access to a large market for beef and dairy goods and to attract investment, and Lemass argued that membership would help to modernise Irish industry.
  • French president Charles de Gaulle vetoed British entry on 14 January 1963, which stopped negotiations, although the Irish application remained on the table.
  • To prepare, the government set up bodies to improve industrial competitiveness and signed the free trade agreement with Britain in 1965 (see the notes on economic planning).

The second application, negotiations and accession, 1967–1973

  • Ireland, Britain, Denmark and Norway applied again in 1967, but de Gaulle vetoed British entry a second time in November 1967.
  • After de Gaulle resigned in 1969, the Hague summit of December 1969 agreed to enlarge the community, and negotiations opened in Luxembourg in 1970.
  • Patrick Hillery, as Minister for External Affairs, led the Irish negotiating team; Ireland won a five-year transitional period for industry and agreed terms on farm prices and a limited fisheries arrangement.
  • The Treaty of Accession was signed in Brussels on 22 January 1972.
  • The government held a referendum on 10 May 1972 to amend the Constitution to allow membership; about 83 per cent of those who voted were in favour, on a turnout of about 71 per cent.
  • Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael and farmers' organisations supported entry, while Labour, Sinn Féin and some trade unionists opposed it on the grounds of loss of sovereignty and neutrality.
  • Ireland, Britain and Denmark became members on 1 January 1973; Norway's voters had rejected membership in September 1972.
  • Hillery became Ireland's first member of the European Commission in 1973.

The Single European Act and the Crotty case

  • The Single European Act, signed in February 1986, aimed to complete the single market by the end of 1992 and extended qualified majority voting to more decisions.
  • It also gave a legal basis to European political cooperation, the effort of member states to coordinate foreign policy.
  • Raymond Crotty, an economist, challenged the government's plan to ratify the Act, and the High Court rejected his case on 12 February 1987.
  • On 9 April 1987 the Supreme Court ruled that the provisions on political cooperation limited the state's freedom to conduct its foreign policy, and that ratification therefore required an amendment to the Constitution.
  • The Tenth Amendment, approved by referendum on 26 May 1987 by about 70 per cent to 30 per cent, on a low turnout of about 44 per cent, allowed ratification.
  • The Crotty judgment established that major transfers of power to the EEC and the EU would require a referendum in Ireland.

Neutrality, defence and the Irish role abroad

  • Ireland refused to join NATO in 1949, with partition as the stated reason, and kept to a policy of military neutrality in the Cold War.
  • The Defence (Amendment) Act 1960 allowed the government to send Irish contingents abroad on service under the United Nations, which provided the legal basis for the Congo mission.
  • Irish troops served only under UN command and with the approval of the Dáil, a condition that later became formalised in policy.
  • Peacekeeping allowed a neutral state to take part in world affairs without joining an alliance, and it became a source of national pride.
  • The question of whether EEC membership would affect neutrality was raised by opponents in 1972, and the government replied that membership did not require Ireland to join a military alliance.

Why Ireland sought EEC membership, and the opposition

  • Economic motives: the failure of protection, the need for markets for beef and dairy goods beyond Britain, and the hope of attracting industry looking for access to a large market.
  • Political motives: membership would end the dependence on the British market and give a small state a seat among the major European powers.
  • The Common Agricultural Policy, which would pay guaranteed prices, was the strongest attraction for farmers and the Irish Farmers' Association.
  • Opponents, grouped in the Common Market Defence Campaign, argued that entry would cost sovereignty and neutrality, force small firms and farms out of business and give control of the economy to foreign companies.
  • Labour opposed entry in 1972, as did Sinn Féin and parts of the trade union movement, but the main parties and employers' and farmers' organisations campaigned for a yes vote.
  • Raymond Crotty, who later challenged the Single European Act, was a prominent economist critic of the EEC.
  • The size of the yes vote in 1972 showed how strongly the public linked membership to economic hopes.

Assessment: which mattered more, the UN or the EEC?

  • The UN gave Ireland international recognition, a platform for independent policies on China, nuclear weapons and decolonisation, and a respected peacekeeping role.
  • The EEC had greater practical effect: it gave farmers guaranteed prices and access to a large market, brought regional and social funds, attracted foreign investment and reduced Ireland's dependence on Britain.
  • Contemporary view: supporters of the UN saw it as the best guarantee of Irish independence and neutrality; supporters of the EEC saw it as the only way to modernise the economy and end dependence on Britain.
  • Later view: historians generally judge that EEC membership had the deeper impact on Irish society and economy, while the UN involvement shaped Ireland's self-image and foreign policy and has remained an important part of its identity.

Key terms

Common market
A group of countries that trade without tariffs among themselves, apply a common tariff to outsiders and allow free movement of goods, workers and capital.
European Economic Community
The community of six states founded by the Treaty of Rome in 1957, which Ireland, Britain and Denmark joined on 1 January 1973.
Peacekeeping
The use of lightly armed, UN-authorised troops and observers to monitor ceasefires and keep apart parties in a conflict.
Non-proliferation
The effort to stop nuclear weapons spreading to more states, to which Ireland contributed through Resolution 1665 of 1961 and the 1968 treaty.
Neutrality
Ireland's policy of not joining military alliances, which allowed it to take an independent line at the UN.
Veto
The power of a state to block a decision: used by the Soviet Union against Irish UN entry in 1946 and by de Gaulle against British EEC entry in 1963 and 1967.
Single European Act
The 1986 treaty that set out to complete the single market by 1992 and gave a legal basis to political cooperation.
Crotty case
The 1987 Supreme Court ruling that ratifying the Single European Act needed a constitutional amendment, because of its provisions on foreign policy cooperation.
ONUC
The UN Operation in the Congo, in which Irish troops served from 1960 to 1964.
