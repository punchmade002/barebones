The Republic of Ireland Act and the Ireland Act, 1949

The declaration of a republic completed the separation from Britain in the South but hardened partition in British law.

  • The Republic of Ireland Act 1948 came into force on 18 April 1949 and ended the External Relations Act link with the Crown; Ireland formally left the Commonwealth.
  • Britain responded with the Ireland Act 1949, which declared that the Republic was not a foreign country and that Irish citizens in Britain would keep the right to vote, work and receive benefits.
  • The Act also stated that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of the Parliament of Northern Ireland, which gave unionists a veto over any change.
  • The Irish government saw this as a guarantee of partition and a betrayal of nationalists, and Dublin protested that the Northern Ireland parliament was unrepresentative.
  • The Common Travel Area allowed free movement between the two islands and continued after 1949, which mattered to the large number of Irish emigrants in Britain.
  • Articles 2 and 3 of the 1937 Constitution claimed the whole island as the national territory, which unionists and the British government cited as proof that Dublin did not accept Northern Ireland.

The anti-partition campaign and the 1950s

  • The coalition government of 1948 to 1951 launched an anti-partition campaign with support from all the main parties, aiming to win international sympathy for Irish unity.
  • The campaign included speeches abroad, especially in the United States, and the argument that Northern Ireland's nationalists were treated unfairly.
  • Britain and the unionist government at Stormont refused to discuss the question, and the campaign had no practical result.
  • The IRA's Border Campaign, from December 1956 to 1962, had little support in the Republic; Fianna Fáil interned IRA suspects at the Curragh from 1957 to 1959.
  • By the late 1950s Irish governments moved to a more pragmatic approach, concentrating on economic recovery.

Trade dependence on Britain

  • In the late 1950s roughly three-quarters of Irish exports, mainly live cattle and other farm produce, went to Britain.
  • Britain was also the main source of imports, and the Irish pound was tied to sterling, so Irish prices and interest rates followed British conditions.
  • This dependence explains why Irish policy-makers sought to join the EEC only when Britain did, and why the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement of 1965 mattered.
  • Once both countries were in the EEC from 1973, and Ireland joined the European Monetary System in 1979 without Britain, the share of exports to Britain fell steadily, to less than half by the early 1980s.

Lemass, O'Neill and the Free Trade Agreement, 1965

In 1965 relations between Dublin, Belfast and London became more cooperative than at any time since partition.

  • Lemass travelled to Stormont on 14 January 1965 and met Terence O'Neill, the Northern Ireland Prime Minister, who then visited Dublin on 9 February 1965.
  • The meetings were the first between the heads of government of the two parts of Ireland for decades, and the discussions concentrated on trade, tourism and electricity rather than on partition.
  • Lemass's approach implied that the North would not change without the consent of its people, which marked a change from the anti-partition rhetoric of the 1950s.
  • Ian Paisley and other unionists denounced the meetings, and some in O'Neill's own party mistrusted them.
  • The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement was signed on 14 December 1965 and began to remove tariffs on trade between Ireland and Britain from 1966.

Lynch and the Troubles, 1969–1973

Violence in Northern Ireland tested relations between the Irish and British governments from 1969.

  • After rioting in Derry and Belfast in August 1969, Lynch said that the state could not stand by, but he refused to send troops across the border, and the British government sent the army onto the streets.
  • The Arms Crisis of 1970 showed the division inside Fianna Fáil over how to respond (see the notes on Lynch, Haughey and FitzGerald).
  • In 1971 Lynch met Edward Heath, the British Prime Minister, and Brian Faulkner, the Northern Ireland Prime Minister, at Chequers, where Lynch argued that only an Irish dimension could end the violence.
  • The introduction of internment on 9 August 1971 was condemned by the Irish government, which took Britain to the European Court of Human Rights; in 1978 the court found that interrogation methods used on internees had been inhuman and degrading.
  • On Bloody Sunday, 30 January 1972, soldiers of the Parachute Regiment shot dead thirteen civilians in Derry, and a fourteenth died later; the Irish government recalled its ambassador and declared a day of mourning.
  • On 2 February 1972 a crowd burned the British embassy in Merrion Square, Dublin, and the British government protested.
  • Heath suspended Stormont and introduced direct rule in March 1972, which the Irish government welcomed as a step towards a new settlement.
  • In August 1969 the Minister for External Affairs, Patrick Hillery, visited the Falls Road in Belfast and the Irish government asked the United Nations to consider sending a peacekeeping force, a request Britain blocked.

Joint EEC entry and the Sunningdale Agreement, 1973–1974

  • Ireland and Britain joined the EEC together on 1 January 1973, and their ministers began to meet regularly in Brussels, which improved personal contacts.
  • A Fine Gael-Labour coalition under Liam Cosgrave took office in March 1973, with Garret FitzGerald as Minister for Foreign Affairs.
  • The Sunningdale Agreement of December 1973 was agreed by Heath, Cosgrave and the leaders of the Ulster Unionists, the SDLP and the Alliance Party.
  • It provided for a power-sharing executive with Brian Faulkner as chief executive and Gerry Fitt as deputy, and a Council of Ireland to give the Republic a say in North-South cooperation.
  • The Irish government accepted that the status of Northern Ireland would not change without the consent of a majority there.
  • Loyalists opposed the Council of Ireland, and the Ulster Workers' Council strike from 15 to 28 May 1974 brought down the executive.
  • On 17 May 1974, during the strike, loyalist bombs in Dublin and Monaghan killed thirty-three people, and no one was convicted.

Haughey, Thatcher and the Falklands, 1979–1982

  • In August 1979 the IRA killed Lord Mountbatten at Mullaghmore, Co. Sligo, on the same day as eighteen British soldiers died at Warrenpoint, and Lynch and Thatcher agreed to improve security cooperation.
  • Margaret Thatcher became Prime Minister in May 1979 and met Haughey in London in May 1980 and in Dublin on 8 December 1980.
  • The Dublin summit produced a joint statement that studies would be made of the totality of relationships within these islands, which Haughey presented as a major step towards a new relationship and Thatcher treated as limited.
  • The 1981 hunger strikes, in which ten republican prisoners died, including Bobby Sands, strained relations between the two governments.
  • In November 1981 FitzGerald and Thatcher set up an Anglo-Irish Intergovernmental Council.
  • During the Falklands War in 1982 Ireland, which sat on the UN Security Council, at first supported EEC sanctions on Argentina, but in early May, after the sinking of the General Belgrano, Haughey's government withdrew its support and called for a ceasefire.
  • Thatcher was angered and saw it as a breach of solidarity, and trust between the two leaders did not recover while Haughey was in office.
  • Earlier, in July 1976, the IRA killed Christopher Ewart-Biggs, the British ambassador to Ireland, near Dublin, and the Irish government responded with emergency powers legislation.

FitzGerald, the New Ireland Forum and the Anglo-Irish Agreement, 1983–1985

The agreement signed at Hillsborough was the main achievement of Anglo-Irish diplomacy in this period.

  • The New Ireland Forum, which met in Dublin from May 1983, brought together Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael, Labour and the SDLP, and its report in May 1984 offered three options: a unitary state, a federal or confederal state, and joint authority.
  • Thatcher publicly rejected all three options in November 1984, but the Forum had strengthened the SDLP and shown that nationalists had a common position.
  • Negotiations between officials followed, and FitzGerald and Thatcher signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement at Hillsborough Castle, Co. Down, on 15 November 1985.
  • In it both governments affirmed that the status of Northern Ireland would not change without the consent of a majority of its people.
  • It set up an Intergovernmental Conference, co-chaired by the Northern Ireland Secretary and the Irish Minister for Foreign Affairs, with a permanent secretariat at Maryfield, near Belfast, through which the Irish government could put forward views on Northern Ireland affairs.
  • Unionists protested strongly: a rally in Belfast drew a huge crowd, and fifteen unionist MPs resigned their seats in January 1986 to force by-elections.
  • Fianna Fáil opposed the agreement in the Dáil, and Desmond O'Malley, expelled from the party for supporting it, founded the Progressive Democrats in December 1985.
  • Thatcher accepted the agreement partly for security reasons: she wanted closer cooperation with Dublin against the IRA and hoped that a role for Dublin would strengthen the SDLP against Sinn Féin.

State of relations by 1989

  • By 1989 the Intergovernmental Conference met regularly and cooperation on security and cross-border matters had grown, although unionists continued to boycott it.
  • Disputes over extradition, over alleged miscarriages of justice in cases of Irish people in British courts and over the British army's actions in Northern Ireland continued to cause friction.
  • The IRA's campaign continued, and the North remained divided and violent.
  • Haughey, who had opposed the agreement in 1985, left it in place when he formed a coalition with the Progressive Democrats after the 1989 election.
  • Joint membership of the EEC, the growth of North-South contacts and the habit of regular meetings meant that relations were closer than in 1949, even though the fundamental dispute over Northern Ireland remained.

Assessment: partition, cooperation and the limits of agreement

  • Achievements: from the Ireland Act of 1949, an Irish government moved to accept the consent principle, gained a formal consultative role in 1985 and built regular channels of contact with London.
  • Limits: Sunningdale collapsed within five months, the 1985 agreement did not end the violence, and each government kept different aims.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists saw the Anglo-Irish Agreement as recognition of their rights; unionists saw it as a threat to the union made without their consent.
  • Later view: historians see the agreement as a foundation for later developments such as the Downing Street Declaration of 1993 and the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, although some judge that its immediate effect was limited.

Key terms

Ireland Act 1949
The British law that kept Northern Ireland in the United Kingdom unless its parliament consented, and treated the Republic as not a foreign country.
Anti-partition campaign
The Irish government's diplomatic and publicity effort of the late 1940s to win support for ending the division of the island.
Common Travel Area
The arrangement allowing Irish and British citizens to travel, live and work in each other's country without immigration controls.
Sunningdale Agreement
The December 1973 agreement on a power-sharing executive in Northern Ireland and a Council of Ireland, which collapsed in May 1974.
Ulster Workers' Council strike
The loyalist general strike of May 1974 that brought down the power-sharing executive.
New Ireland Forum
The 1983 to 1984 meeting of nationalist parties that proposed three options for the future of Ireland, all rejected by Thatcher.
Anglo-Irish Agreement
The November 1985 agreement giving the Irish government a consultative role in Northern Ireland through an Intergovernmental Conference.
Totality of relationships
The phrase in the December 1980 Haughey-Thatcher communique about studying the relationship between the two islands as a whole.
Consent principle
The principle that the constitutional status of Northern Ireland would change only with the agreement of a majority of its people.
