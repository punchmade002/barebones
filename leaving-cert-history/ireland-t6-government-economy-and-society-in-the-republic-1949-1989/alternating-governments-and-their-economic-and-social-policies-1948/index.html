The fall of Fianna Fáil and the First Inter-Party Government, 1948

After sixteen years of Fianna Fáil rule, a coalition of the parties of opposition took office in February 1948 and governed until 1951.

  • Fianna Fáil had been in power since 1932 and remained the largest party in the February 1948 general election, but it lost its majority.
  • Clann na Poblachta, a new republican party founded in 1946 by Seán MacBride, took only a small number of seats in 1948 but held the balance of power in the Dáil.
  • The first Inter-Party Government united Fine Gael, Labour, the National Labour Party, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan and some independents.
  • Richard Mulcahy, the Fine Gael leader, stood aside because of his Civil War record, and John A. Costello became Taoiseach.
  • William Norton of Labour was Tánaiste, Seán MacBride was Minister for External Affairs and Noël Browne of Clann na Poblachta became Minister for Health in 1948.
  • The coalition had no common economic programme beyond ending Fianna Fáil's long hold on office, and it contained both conservative and radical members.

Achievements of the First Inter-Party Government, 1948–1951

The government's best-known act was constitutional, but it also made economic and health decisions that mattered.

  • Costello announced during a visit to Canada in September 1948 that the government would repeal the External Relations Act of 1936 and declare a republic.
  • The Republic of Ireland Act 1948 came into force on 18 April 1949 (Easter Monday), ending the state's last formal link with the Crown and its Commonwealth membership.
  • Britain responded with the Ireland Act 1949, which guaranteed that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of its parliament, and which said that the Republic was not a foreign country and that Irish citizens in Britain were not aliens (see the notes on Anglo-Irish relations).
  • Ireland declined an invitation to join NATO in 1949, with MacBride citing partition as the obstacle.
  • The Industrial Development Authority (IDA) was set up in 1949 to encourage new industry, including investment from abroad, though in the 1950s it had little money or power.
  • Ireland took part in the Marshall Plan (the European Recovery Programme) and received about 146 million dollars, much of it as loans; a large share went on land reclamation and agriculture rather than industry, which later critics saw as a missed chance.
  • The government expanded house building and nationalised the public transport companies into Córas Iompair Éireann in 1950.
  • Noël Browne led a campaign against tuberculosis, a major killer; the state built new sanatoria, funded partly from the Hospitals Sweepstake, and a BCG vaccination programme was begun. Deaths from TB fell steeply in the 1950s.

The Mother and Child Scheme crisis, 1950–1951

The scheme became the clearest example of the Catholic hierarchy's power over social policy in the 1950s.

  • Browne drew on the Health Act 1947 to propose free care for mothers before and after childbirth and for children up to the age of 16, available to all without a means test.
  • The Irish Medical Association opposed the scheme as a threat to doctors' private practice and independence.
  • The Catholic hierarchy objected that the scheme gave the state too much power over family life, that it might lead to state-provided education in sexual matters, and that it undermined the rights of parents.
  • Archbishop McQuaid advised Browne and Costello on the church's objections, and the bishops wrote to Costello in late 1950 and again in April 1951 that the scheme was contrary to Catholic social teaching.
  • Seán MacBride, who wanted to protect his government from a clash with the church, asked Browne to resign, and Browne resigned on 11 April 1951 and then published the correspondence.
  • Costello and the cabinet accepted the hierarchy's view; Costello told the Dáil that the government was bound to accept the bishops' decision on a matter of Catholic moral teaching.
  • The affair helped bring down the government: Clann na Poblachta lost ground, the coalition lost support in the Dáil and Costello called a general election for May 1951.
  • The Fianna Fáil government that took office replaced the scheme with the Health Act 1953, which was means-tested and so left many people without free care.

Key personality: Archbishop John Charles McQuaid (part 1: Church power and the Mother and Child Scheme)

McQuaid was Archbishop of Dublin from 1940 to 1972 and the most influential churchman in the state in the 1950s.

  • John Charles McQuaid was born in 1895 in Cootehill, Co. Cavan, joined the Holy Ghost Fathers and was president of Blackrock College before he became Archbishop of Dublin in 1940.
  • He was a participant in the Mother and Child crisis, as the leading churchman to advise the government on the scheme and as the author of the argument that it conflicted with Catholic teaching on the family.
  • His objections did not stop at medical detail: he objected to the principle that the state, not parents and the church, should decide how mothers and children were cared for.
  • His influence rested on personal contact with ministers, notably with de Valera, whom he had advised on the Constitution of 1937 and its article on religion.
  • Contemporaries who agreed with him saw him as a defender of Catholic social teaching; Browne and his supporters saw him as an interfering prelate who used his power to defeat a good scheme.
  • Historians who studied the church-state relationship after the 1951 events, such as John Whyte, concluded that the government deferred to the hierarchy; later work using archives suggests that Browne's own political weakness and the doctors' opposition were also important, but that the bishops' stance was decisive.

Fianna Fáil in office, 1951–1954

De Valera returned as Taoiseach, but without a majority and with an economy short of foreign currency.

  • Fianna Fáil formed a minority government in 1951 with de Valera as Taoiseach, Seán Lemass as Tánaiste and Minister for Industry and Commerce, and Seán MacEntee as Minister for Finance.
  • MacEntee's 1952 budget was deflationary: it cut subsidies on basic foods and raised duties, which pushed prices up, in order to reduce imports and the balance of payments deficit.
  • The budget was unpopular, and in the 1954 general election Fianna Fáil lost seats.
  • The government passed the Underdeveloped Areas Act 1952, which gave grants for new industry in the poorer western counties.
  • The Health Act 1953 provided a framework of means-tested maternity, child and hospital care in place of the Mother and Child Scheme.
  • Emigration continued at a high level throughout these years, and unemployment stayed high.
  • The minority government depended on the votes of independent deputies, which limited how far it could go with unpopular measures.

The Second Inter-Party Government, 1954–1957

Costello returned as Taoiseach in 1954 at the head of a coalition that again had no programme for growth.

  • The second coalition contained Fine Gael, Labour and Clann na Talmhan, with Clann na Poblachta supporting it from outside government.
  • Gerard Sweetman, the Minister for Finance, was a strong believer in private enterprise and low state spending.
  • A balance of payments crisis built up in 1955 and 1956 because imports ran well ahead of exports and foreign currency reserves fell.
  • The government responded with higher taxes, credit restrictions and cuts in current spending, which reduced demand and pushed up unemployment.
  • A Capital Investment Advisory Committee reviewed proposals for public capital spending in the mid-1950s; it was an early step towards planning public investment.
  • In December 1956 the IRA began its Border Campaign, and the government faced pressure from Clann na Poblachta over its response.
  • Clann na Poblachta withdrew its support in January 1957, and Costello called an election.

Stagnation, unemployment and emigration in the 1950s

While most of western Europe boomed, the Irish economy grew only slowly and people left in large numbers.

  • The census showed a population of 2,960,593 in 1951 and 2,818,341 in 1961, a fall of about 140,000 despite a high birth rate.
  • Net emigration in the 1950s was about 400,000, roughly 200,000 in each half of the decade, and most of those who left were young people heading for Britain.
  • Britain offered work in construction, factories, the health service and transport, and the British economy was growing faster than the Irish.
  • Agriculture still employed about 40 per cent of the workforce, mostly on small farms of low productivity, and cattle exports to Britain were the main earner.
  • Industry was behind protective tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts, which required that most shares in new firms be held by Irish citizens and so discouraged foreign investment (see the notes on the First Programme for Economic Expansion).
  • Irish growth in the 1950s was a fraction of the growth in western Europe, and the gap with other countries widened.
  • The Irish pound was tied to sterling, so Irish interest rates and trade followed British conditions, including the British government's 'stop-go' cycles.

Fianna Fáil's return and the wasted years debate

The 1957 election ended the second coalition, and the decade is remembered as one of missed opportunities.

  • In the March 1957 general election Fianna Fáil won 78 seats and an overall majority, and de Valera became Taoiseach for the last time.
  • Lemass returned to Industry and Commerce and also served as Tánaiste, and James Ryan became Minister for Finance.
  • T.K. Whitaker had become Secretary of the Department of Finance in 1956 and began the work that led to Economic Development (see the notes on economic planning).
  • Historians such as J.J. Lee and Tom Garvin criticise the leaders of the 1950s for lacking ambition and for preferring stability to change, which gave rise to the term 'wasted years'.
  • Others point to constraints: a small economy that depended on the British market and the sterling link, and the weak foreign exchange position.
  • A third view is that the Inter-Party governments did lay groundwork, through the IDA, the Underdeveloped Areas Act and public investment, that Fianna Fáil later built on, and that policy-makers then lacked planning tools that were developed only in the late 1950s.

Assessment: alternating governments and economic policy, 1948–1959

  • Achievements: the Republic of Ireland Act completed the move to a republic; the IDA, the Underdeveloped Areas Act and Marshall Aid laid foundations for later industrial policy; the anti-tuberculosis campaign saved lives.
  • Limits: no government drew up a long-term plan, and each balance of payments crisis led to deflation that made unemployment and emigration worse.
  • The Mother and Child affair showed the limits of state action in social policy when the church and the medical profession opposed it.
  • Contemporary view: voters punished each government in turn for high taxes and emigration, and the sharp swings between coalition and Fianna Fáil governments reflected that dissatisfaction.
  • Later view: historians generally accept that the decade was one of slow growth and missed opportunities, but they differ about how far this was caused by policy mistakes rather than by Ireland's dependence on Britain.

Key terms

Balance of payments
The record of a country's trade and financial dealings with the rest of the world; a deficit means more money leaves the country than comes in, which forces borrowing or cuts.
Deflation
A policy of raising taxes and cutting spending or credit to reduce demand and imports, at the cost of lower output and fewer jobs.
Inter-Party Government
A coalition of Fine Gael, Labour and smaller parties, in office from 1948 to 1951 and from 1954 to 1957, formed to remove Fianna Fáil from power.
Clann na Poblachta
The republican party founded in 1946 by Seán MacBride, which won seats in 1948 and joined the first Inter-Party Government.
Mother and Child Scheme
Noël Browne's 1950 plan for free health care for mothers and children, withdrawn in 1951 after opposition from the Catholic hierarchy and the Irish Medical Association.
Marshall Aid
American aid under the European Recovery Programme, paid to Ireland from 1948 as grants and loans to help rebuild the economy after the Second World War.
Industrial Development Authority
The state agency set up in 1949 to promote industry and attract investment, including foreign investment.
Wasted years
A description of the 1950s as a decade of slow growth, high unemployment and emigration, in which governments failed to plan for development.
Republic of Ireland Act
The 1948 act, in force from 18 April 1949, that declared the state a republic and ended its Commonwealth link.
