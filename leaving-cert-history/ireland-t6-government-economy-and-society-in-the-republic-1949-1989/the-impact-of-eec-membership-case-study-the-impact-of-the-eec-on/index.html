What membership changed

From 1 January 1973 Ireland was part of a common market with free movement of goods and a common external tariff, and a member of its institutions.

  • Tariffs on trade with other member states were phased out over a transitional period that ended in 1977, and Ireland adopted the common external tariff on goods from outside the community.
  • EEC law took precedence over Irish law in the areas covered by the treaties, and the Third Amendment to the Constitution, approved in 1972, made this possible.
  • Irish ministers sat on the Council of Ministers, Irish members sat in the European Parliament, and Patrick Hillery served as the first Irish member of the Commission from 1973.
  • Ireland held the presidency of the Council of Ministers for the first time in the first half of 1975.
  • Membership reduced dependence on Britain: the share of Irish exports going to Britain fell as trade with the continent grew.
  • Net payments from the EEC budget to Ireland amounted to several per cent of national income by the 1980s, which helped to pay for farm support, training and infrastructure.
  • Membership was the context for Ireland's decision in March 1979 to join the European Monetary System, which ended the link between the Irish pound and sterling.

Agriculture and the Common Agricultural Policy

  • The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) guaranteed minimum prices for products such as beef, milk and cereals, and bought surplus produce into intervention stores.
  • Irish farm prices were raised towards EEC levels during the transition, and farm incomes rose sharply in the early years of membership, especially for beef and dairy farmers.
  • Land values rose and farmers invested in machinery, buildings and drainage, helped by grants.
  • The benefits were uneven: larger and more productive farms in the east and south gained most, while small farms on poor land in the west gained less.
  • Guaranteed prices encouraged overproduction, and in 1984 the EEC introduced milk quotas, limiting output on Irish dairy farms.
  • The number of people working on the land continued to fall, and agriculture's share of employment and output declined.

Regional and social funds

  • The European Regional Development Fund was created in 1975, partly because Britain, Ireland and Italy pressed for help for poorer regions, and Ireland received a considerable share of it.
  • The European Social Fund paid for training and employment schemes for young and unemployed people.
  • Funds were spent on roads, water and sewerage schemes, telecommunications and industrial estates, mainly under the direction of the Irish government.
  • A reform of the structural funds in 1988, linked to the Single European Act, doubled the money available, with Ireland as a major beneficiary of the programmes for 1989 to 1993.
  • The Social Fund helped to pay for the training schemes of AnCO, the state training authority.

Foreign investment, industry and the decline of protected firms

  • American and other foreign firms set up factories in Ireland to sell into the EEC, drawn by IDA grants, a young English-speaking workforce and low taxes; in 1981 a 10 per cent rate of corporation tax on manufacturing profits was introduced.
  • Electronics, pharmaceuticals, chemicals and engineering became leading sectors, with firms such as Digital in Galway and, later, Intel at Leixlip.
  • Protected Irish industries such as clothing, textiles and footwear lost their tariff protection and many firms closed, with heavy job losses.
  • Employment in traditional manufacturing fell, and the rise in unemployment of the late 1970s and 1980s showed that membership did not by itself solve the economy's weaknesses (see the notes on the economic challenges of the 1970s and 1980s).
  • Irish exports became more diversified, with manufactured goods overtaking farm produce in the value of exports.

Social effects, equality and sovereignty

  • EEC directives on equal pay (1975) and equal treatment in employment (1976) required changes in Irish law, and they reinforced the Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 and the Employment Equality Act 1977 (see the notes on the status of women).
  • The marriage bar in the public service was lifted in 1973, and a directive on equal treatment in social welfare, adopted in 1978, forced changes to payments to married women in the 1980s.
  • Irish citizens gained the right to live and work in other member states, which eased emigration, and young people had more contact with continental Europe.
  • Irish judges were obliged to apply EEC law, and the European Court of Justice became a route for individuals to challenge Irish law.
  • Critics argued that membership cost Ireland some of its independence, and the debate over sovereignty returned in the Crotty case and the later referendums on European treaties.

Case study: the Irish fishing industry before 1973

Irish fishing was small and under-developed, but it was important to coastal communities and became a sensitive issue in the accession negotiations.

  • Irish waters were rich in fish, but the industry was small: most boats were old, small and inshore, and the fleet lacked the capacity to fish far from land.
  • Fishing employed only a few thousand full-time fishermen, concentrated in Donegal, Kerry, Cork, Galway and parts of the east coast, and many combined it with farming.
  • Irish consumers ate little fish outside Fridays, and the home market was small, so the industry exported little.
  • Foreign trawlers from France, Spain, Belgium, the Netherlands and Britain fished close to the Irish coast under historic rights, because the exclusive Irish limit was narrow.
  • Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM), the state fisheries board, was created in 1952 to develop the industry through grants and loans for boats, training and marketing.
  • In the 1960s and early 1970s BIM helped to build a more modern fleet, including larger boats able to catch herring, whitefish and shellfish.
  • Fish made up a tiny share of Irish exports and national output before 1973, which is why governments treated the fishing terms as less important than the farming terms.

Case study: the accession terms and the Common Fisheries Policy

  • The EEC had agreed a principle of equal access for all member states' fleets to community waters in 1970, just before the applicant states opened negotiations.
  • Irish negotiators argued that fishing was important to poor coastal communities, and Ireland obtained a derogation: an exclusive six-mile zone for Irish boats and a 12-mile zone in parts of the coast, for ten years to the end of 1982.
  • Critics said the government gave away too much in order to secure the farming terms, and that the fishing industry was treated as expendable.
  • In 1976, at The Hague, member states agreed to extend their fishing limits to 200 nautical miles from 1977 and recognised that Ireland needed preferential treatment.
  • Negotiating a new common policy took until January 1983, when member states agreed on a Common Fisheries Policy (CFP).
  • The CFP set annual total allowable catches (TACs) for the main species, divided into national quotas according to the principle of relative stability, based on past catches, with some preference for Ireland.
  • It kept a 12-mile coastal belt reserved for local fishermen, and the arrangements were to be reviewed after ten years.
  • Irish fishermen and ministers complained that Ireland's quotas were small compared with the value of the fish in its waters.

Case study: development, ports and foreign fleets

  • With EEC and state grants, BIM financed new and larger vessels, and the Irish fleet grew in the late 1970s and 1980s.
  • Killybegs, Co. Donegal, developed into Ireland's leading fishing port, with a fleet of larger trawlers and pelagic boats and with processing factories and a growing service industry.
  • Castletownbere, Co. Cork, became a major whitefish port, with boats landing fish for sale and export.
  • Other ports such as Dunmore East, Howth, Rossaveel and Kilmore Quay also received investment in piers and harbours.
  • Foreign fleets from Spain, France and elsewhere fished in Irish waters, and the Irish Naval Service was used to inspect and arrest vessels that broke the rules.
  • Disputes over illegal fishing, over the size of quotas and over the shares given to other states recurred through the 1980s.

Case study: benefits, costs and conflicting views

  • Benefits: investment in harbours, boats and processing plants created jobs in some coastal towns, and the 200-mile limit gave Ireland a large area of sea to manage.
  • Costs: quotas limited what Irish boats could land, small inshore fishermen often lost out to larger vessels and fewer ports, and young people left declining harbours.
  • Fishermen's organisations claimed that Ireland received a small share of the fish caught in its own waters, and argued that the government treated fisheries as secondary to farming.
  • The government replied that the 12-mile zone and the preferences obtained at The Hague were the best terms available and that Ireland, as a small state, could not manage the waters alone.
  • Supporters of EEC membership pointed to the new money for the industry; critics, including the Common Market Defence Campaign, pointed to the loss of control over a national resource.
  • The fishing industry remained a source of grievance, and its history became a standard example in debates on the costs of membership.
  • Fish processing and exports to continental markets expanded through the 1980s.

Assessment: the gains and losses of membership

  • Achievements: secure and better-priced markets for farmers, large transfers from Europe, foreign investment in new industries, equality legislation and a wider international outlook.
  • Limits: protected firms closed, unemployment rose in the 1980s, small farms and the west benefited least, and the fishing industry gained less than farming.
  • Contemporary view: supporters said membership was the only way to modernise an economy that relied on protection and emigration; opponents said it threatened sovereignty and traditional industries.
  • Later view: historians generally accept that membership underpinned the transformation of the economy and society, while stressing that the full benefits came later, from the 1990s, and that farmers and fishermen did not all gain equally.

Key terms

Common market
A group of countries that trade without tariffs among themselves and apply a common tariff to outsiders, with free movement of goods, workers and capital.
Common Agricultural Policy
The EEC system of guaranteed farm prices, intervention buying and support payments that shaped Irish farm incomes after 1973.
Common Fisheries Policy
The EEC policy on access to fishing waters, total allowable catches and national quotas, agreed in full in January 1983.
Bord Iascaigh Mhara
The state fisheries board, set up in 1952 to develop the Irish fishing industry through grants, loans and training.
Total allowable catch
The maximum amount of a fish species that may be landed in a year in an area, fixed by the EEC and divided among states as quotas.
Relative stability
The principle that each member state keeps a fixed share of the total allowable catch of each species, based on past catches.
Quota
A limit on the amount of something, such as milk or fish, that a country or producer may produce or land.
European Regional Development Fund
The EEC fund created in 1975 to help poorer regions with infrastructure and industry.
Equal pay directive
The EEC directive of 1975 requiring equal pay for men and women for the same work or work of equal value.
Derogation
A special exception from a general rule, such as Ireland's reserved fishing zone in the accession treaty.
