Changing attitudes to the Irish language, 1949–1973

The language was the first official language of the state, but it was losing ground as a community language, and by the 1960s public attitudes were turning against compulsion.

  • The 1937 Constitution declared Irish the national and first official language, and successive governments backed its revival through schools, the civil service and the Gaeltacht.
  • Irish was a compulsory subject in school, a pass in Irish was required for the Leaving Certificate and Irish was needed for entry to the civil service.
  • Gael Linn, founded in 1953, tried to promote the language through popular means, including records, the Amharc Éireann newsreel from 1956 and the feature film Mise Éire in 1959.
  • The Gaeltacht was in decline in the 1950s because of emigration, and the state set up Gaeltarra Éireann in 1957 to encourage industry in Irish-speaking areas.
  • The Commission on the Restoration of the Irish Language reported in 1963, and a government White Paper of 1965 accepted the aim of restoration but proposed a more flexible approach.
  • The Language Freedom Movement, founded in 1966, campaigned against compulsory Irish, arguing that it turned pupils against the language, and it provoked counter-campaigns.
  • In 1973 the Fine Gael-Labour government, with Richard Burke as Minister for Education, ended the rule that students must pass Irish to gain the Leaving Certificate, and the requirement for civil service entry was also relaxed.

Institutions and the Gaeltacht

  • The Gaeltacht Civil Rights Movement, formed in 1969, demanded better services and Irish-language radio, and in 1970 it ran a pirate station, Saor Raidió Chonamara.
  • Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting from Connemara on Easter Sunday, 2 April 1972, giving Irish speakers a regular service in their own language.
  • Bord na Gaeilge was set up in 1975 to coordinate efforts to promote Irish.
  • Údarás na Gaeltachta, created in 1980, took over responsibility for economic and social development in the Gaeltacht.
  • The Irish language retained its symbolic status, but the number of everyday speakers continued to decline outside the Gaeltacht, which itself was shrinking.

Key personality: Breandán Ó hEithir

Ó hEithir was a writer, journalist and broadcaster whose work in Irish and English brought the culture of the Gaeltacht and the Irish language to a modern audience.

  • Breandán Ó hEithir was born on 18 January 1930 on Inis Mór, one of the Aran Islands, and died on 26 October 1990.
  • He was a journalist and a broadcaster with RTÉ, presenting and reporting on current affairs and on cultural and sporting matters in Irish and English, and wrote a regular newspaper column.
  • His novel Lig Sinn i gCathú (1976), a satirical novel about the life of a Galway student, was widely read in Irish, and he translated it himself as Lead Us into Temptation in 1978.
  • His Over the Bar (1984) was a personal history of the GAA, and The Begrudger's Guide to Irish Politics (1986) was a witty account of political life in English.
  • He was a participant in the cultural change of the period rather than a leader of a movement: he wrote about the Irish language as a living medium for modern subjects, not as a duty.
  • Contemporaries valued him as a bridge between the Gaeltacht and the English-speaking world and as a rare Irish-language writer with a wide readership.
  • Later critics and his biographer Liam Mac Con Iomaire see him as one of the most accomplished bilingual writers and broadcasters of his generation.

Traditional music and cultural revival

  • Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann was founded in Mullingar in 1951, and the fleadh cheoil competitions held from 1952 helped to spread traditional music and dance.
  • Seán Ó Riada composed the score for Mise Éire (1959), and from 1961 his group Ceoltóirí Chualann played traditional music in a concert style, which brought it to a wider audience.
  • Ó Riada's Ceol an Aifrinn (1968), an Irish-language Mass set to sean-nós style music for the church at Cúil Aodha, linked the music revival to the changes brought by Vatican II.
  • The Chieftains were formed in 1962, and the Clancy Brothers, The Dubliners and later groups such as Planxty made Irish ballads and traditional music popular at home and abroad.
  • The revival gave Irish culture a modern commercial and international dimension and reduced the old divide between a rural traditional culture and urban popular culture.

Vatican II and the Irish church

The Second Vatican Council, held in Rome between October 1962 and December 1965, changed Catholic worship and the church's relationship with other Christians and the modern world.

  • Pope John XXIII opened the council on 11 October 1962 and it closed on 8 December 1965 under Paul VI; Irish bishops, including McQuaid, attended.
  • The council allowed the Mass to be said in the vernacular instead of Latin, and the first Masses in English in Ireland were celebrated in 1965.
  • Priests now faced the congregation, laypeople could be readers and distribute communion, and parish councils and lay organisations were encouraged.
  • The council promoted ecumenism, dialogue with other Christian churches: the Catholic ban on attending Trinity College Dublin was lifted in 1970 and the first inter-church talks were held at Ballymascanlon, Co. Louth, in 1973.
  • It declared that religious freedom was a right, which weakened the claim that a Catholic state should have a special position in law.
  • Many Irish Catholics welcomed the changes, but others, mostly older, felt unsettled by the loss of Latin and old devotions.
  • Vatican II also encouraged people to think for themselves on moral questions, which contributed to a questioning of church authority.

Key personality: Archbishop John Charles McQuaid (part 2: Vatican II and overall evaluation)

McQuaid, Archbishop of Dublin from 1940 to 1972, was the most powerful churchman in Ireland and was cautious about the changes brought by the council.

  • McQuaid attended Vatican II and, on returning in December 1965, assured Dubliners that the council would not disturb their faith, a message of reassurance in tune with his conservative outlook.
  • He implemented liturgical reforms in Dublin, but slowly, and he was wary of ecumenism, which he regarded as risky for Catholic doctrine, and of lay initiatives outside the control of the clergy.
  • He was a participant in church-state relations, with direct access to ministers, and had been involved in the Mother and Child dispute of 1951 (see the notes on alternating governments).
  • He was also involved in education, social work and the building of new churches and schools in the Dublin suburbs, and founded charitable bodies such as the Catholic Social Service Conference.
  • He retired in January 1972 and died in 1973; he was succeeded by Dermot Ryan.
  • Contemporaries admired his efficiency and devotion to the church, but critics complained of his authoritarian style and his readiness to intervene in politics and in people's lives.
  • Historians, notably John Cooney in his biography of 1999, portray him as the ruler of Catholic Ireland whose style came to seem out of date; other historians stress his role in building schools and welfare services and argue that the decline of church power was the result of social change as much as of his policies.

Secularisation and pluralism

  • A survey in 1973 and 1974 found about nine in ten Catholics attending Mass weekly, so practice remained high even as other signs of change appeared.
  • Pope Paul VI's encyclical Humanae Vitae in 1968 reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception, and many Irish married Catholics found it difficult to accept.
  • Vocations to the priesthood and religious life began to fall from the late 1960s, and fewer young people entered seminaries and convents.
  • Pope John Paul II visited Ireland from 29 September to 1 October 1979, and a mass in the Phoenix Park drew more than a million people, but the visit did not halt the long-term shift.
  • Secularisation, the decline in the influence of religion on public life and private behaviour, advanced through the 1970s and 1980s, helped by education, television, emigration and contact with Europe.
  • Pluralism, the idea that the state should respect different beliefs and not enforce one, was at the centre of the 1983 referendum on abortion and the 1986 referendum on divorce, and the results showed that traditional views remained strong (see the notes on the leadership of the 1970s and 1980s).

Censorship and liberalisation

  • The Censorship of Publications Act 1929 set up a board that could ban books for being indecent or obscene, and the Act of 1946 added an appeals board; thousands of books were banned, including works by leading Irish writers.
  • John McGahern's novel The Dark was banned in 1965, and McGahern lost his teaching job after the Archbishop of Dublin's intervention, which became a symbol of the censorship of the time.
  • The Censorship of Publications Act 1967, introduced by the Minister for Justice, Brian Lenihan, provided that a ban lasts twelve years, after which a book is freed unless banned again, and thousands of titles were released.
  • Film censorship became more liberal during the 1960s and 1970s, as television and cinema showed material from abroad.
  • Contraceptives and publications about them were covered by separate laws until the Health (Family Planning) Act 1979 (see the notes on the status of women).
  • Censorship in broadcasting took a different form, through the powers of the minister over RTÉ (see the notes on the impact of RTÉ).

The communications revolution

  • The number of cars on the road rose several times over between 1960 and 1980, and better roads, bus services and air travel reduced rural isolation.
  • Telephone services, once poor and slow to expand, were improved and in 1984 Bord Telecom Éireann was set up to run them as a separate state company.
  • Television reached most households in the 1960s and early 1970s, and RTÉ 2 started in 1978, bringing more programmes and choice; British channels could be received in much of the east and north (see the notes on the impact of RTÉ).
  • The Irish Times under the editor Douglas Gageby, from 1963, became a forum for new ideas, and magazines such as Magill, founded in 1977, expanded political journalism.
  • Pirate radio stations grew in the 1970s and 1980s, challenging RTÉ's monopoly, and the Radio and Television Act 1988 licensed independent local radio.
  • Computers were adopted in business and government from the 1970s, and American firms such as Digital opened plants in Ireland, laying the base for the electronics industry.
  • These changes exposed people to a wider range of ideas and lifestyles, and helped to break down the isolation on which traditional authority had rested.

Assessment: culture, religion and change

  • Achievements: the Irish language gained broadcasting and institutions, traditional music was revived and reached a world audience, the church embraced liturgical reform and ecumenism, and censorship was relaxed.
  • Limits: the language continued to decline as a community language, the compulsion debates left many pupils indifferent, and the church's leadership, especially under McQuaid, was slow to adapt.
  • Contemporary view: traditionalists feared that television and liberal reform threatened Irish identity and Catholic values, while reformers saw the changes as overdue.
  • Later view: historians see the period as the start of the shift to a more secular and pluralist society, with the church retaining strong influence on law and public life well into the 1980s.

Key terms

Ecumenism
The movement for closer relations and dialogue between Christian churches, promoted by Vatican II.
Secularisation
The decline in the influence of religion over public life, institutions and personal behaviour.
Censorship
The official banning or restriction of books, films and other material considered indecent, obscene or otherwise unacceptable.
Pluralism
The acceptance that a society contains different beliefs and traditions, none of which should be privileged by the state.
Vatican II
The Second Vatican Council of 1962 to 1965, which reformed Catholic worship and the church's relations with other faiths and the modern world.
Vernacular Mass
A Mass said in the language of the people instead of Latin, introduced in Ireland from 1965.
Language Freedom Movement
The group founded in 1966 that campaigned against compulsory Irish in schools and public service.
Gael Linn
The organisation founded in 1953 to promote the Irish language through popular means such as films, records and newsreels.
Gaeltacht
The districts, mainly in the west and south-west, where Irish remained the community language.
Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann
The organisation founded in 1951 to promote traditional Irish music, song and dance.
Humanae Vitae
The 1968 papal encyclical that reaffirmed the Catholic ban on artificial contraception.
