The post-war religious revival

Church membership and religious observance grew strongly in the 1950s, and the government began to express the nation's faith in public symbols.

  • Church membership rose sharply in the 1950s, with the proportion of Americans belonging to a church reaching a high point by the end of the decade.
  • Congress added the words under God to the Pledge of Allegiance in June 1954, and a bill of 1956 made In God We Trust the national motto.
  • Religion was seen by many commentators as a bulwark against communism, and presidents from Eisenhower onwards attended church and spoke of the nation's faith.
  • New churches were built in the suburbs in large numbers, and church groups and social clubs became central to suburban community life.
  • Black churches, such as the Baptist congregations of the South, provided the meeting places, leadership and fundraising networks on which the civil rights movement depended, as the Montgomery boycott showed.
  • Eisenhower said, in the early 1950s, that American government made no sense unless founded on a deeply felt religious faith, and he was baptised soon after taking office in 1953; his comment is often quoted as an example of the civil religion of the period.
  • Norman Vincent Peale's The Power of Positive Thinking (1952) was a best seller that mixed religion with self-help, and the rise of the megachurch and television ministry followed.
  • The Cold War gave religion a political role: communism was condemned as atheistic, and the national motto and Pledge changes were part of this.

Key personality: Billy Graham

Graham was a participant in the religious revival and a figure with close ties to the presidents. His relationship to the religious right is covered in the notes on the Moral Majority.

  • William Franklin Graham was born on 7 November 1918 near Charlotte, North Carolina, and trained as a Baptist minister at Wheaton College in Illinois.
  • His Los Angeles crusade of September and October 1949 was extended from three weeks to eight after Hearst newspapers reported it widely, and it made him a national figure.
  • He founded the Billy Graham Evangelistic Association in 1950 and began a radio programme, Hour of Decision, which later reached a worldwide audience.
  • He held a crusade at Madison Square Garden in New York in 1957, which ran for about sixteen weeks, and he later spoke at large stadium rallies across the world.
  • He co-founded the magazine Christianity Today in 1956 to provide a conservative evangelical voice alongside the mainstream press.
  • He became a regular adviser and visitor to presidents from Eisenhower to Nixon, and he preached at presidential events, which brought him close to the centre of political life.
  • Graham refused to hold segregated crusades from 1953, which was an early sign of his commitment to racial integration, though he was criticised for not supporting King's protests more openly.
  • Contemporaries were divided: admirers saw him as a unifying figure who spoke to millions, while liberal theologians and critics saw him as a tool of Cold War patriotism.
  • Historians have judged his influence on modern evangelicalism as very large, while noting that his closeness to presidents, and in particular to Nixon, compromised his independence; tapes released in 2002 caught him making anti-Semitic remarks to Nixon in 1972, for which he later apologised.
  • Graham's association with Truman began awkwardly in 1950, when he was criticised for revealing his talk with the president, but he became a friend and adviser of Eisenhower, and later of Nixon, whom he supported in 1960 and 1968.
  • Watergate damaged his standing, and Graham said afterwards that he had been used and that he would avoid partisan politics; he declined to join Falwell's Moral Majority in 1979 and warned against mixing religion with party politics.
  • He took his crusades abroad, to London in 1954, Moscow in 1982 and Eastern Europe in the 1980s, and he organised the Lausanne Congress on World Evangelization in 1974, which made him a leader of world evangelicalism.
  • He invited Martin Luther King to pray at his New York crusade of 1957, but he declined to endorse the mass protests of the early 1960s and urged patience.

Catholic America and the 1960 election

The Catholic faith of John F. Kennedy became a political issue in 1960, and his response showed the place of religion in public life.

  • Catholics were a large minority in the United States, and the election of a Catholic president was a question that had been open since the nineteenth century.
  • Kennedy addressed the Greater Houston Ministerial Association on 12 September 1960, declaring that he believed in an America where the separation of church and state was absolute, and that his religion was his private affair, to quieten Protestant fears that he would take orders from the Pope.
  • The only Catholic to run for president before him, Al Smith in 1928, had been defeated amid anti-Catholic prejudice, and in 1960 some Protestant ministers, including Norman Vincent Peale, voiced doubts about a Catholic president.
  • Kennedy won narrowly in November 1960 with strong support from Catholic voters, becoming the first Catholic president, and his election weakened the old Protestant dominance of national politics.
  • Catholic life in the 1950s was strong, with parish schools, church building and a public figure in Bishop Fulton Sheen, whose television series Life Is Worth Living reached millions between 1952 and 1957.
  • Catholics and Jews, as well as Protestants, were seen as part of a common American religious tradition, a view popularised by Will Herberg's Protestant, Catholic, Jew (1955).

Fundamentalism, evangelicalism and televangelism

Conservative Protestant churches grew in influence and used television to reach millions of viewers from the 1960s onwards.

  • Fundamentalism rejected modernist interpretations of the Bible and held to the literal truth of scripture, a tradition traced to the early twentieth century and the 1925 Scopes trial.
  • The National Association of Evangelicals, founded in 1942, brought together a broader group of conservative churches that stressed personal conversion and evangelism.
  • Televangelists such as Jerry Falwell, Pat Robertson and Jim and Tammy Bakker built large audiences through religious broadcasts, using television to raise money and to influence politics.
  • The Bakker scandal of 1987, in which the PTL ministry was found to have misused funds, damaged the reputation of televangelism and led to Jim Bakker's imprisonment.
  • Televangelists reached audiences far larger than most local churches could, and their programmes often combined religious teaching with political messages.
  • The Supreme Court had ruled in 1962 in Engel v Vitale that state-sponsored prayer in public schools was unconstitutional, a decision that provoked strong protest from religious conservatives.
  • In Abington School District v Schempp (1963) the Supreme Court also ruled against compulsory Bible reading in public schools, and these decisions helped to mobilise conservative Christians.
  • Roe v. Wade (1973) and the pressure for the Equal Rights Amendment gave conservative Christians further issues, as did the campaign of the singer Anita Bryant against gay rights in Florida in 1977.
  • Jimmy Carter, a born-again Southern Baptist, was elected in 1976, and the press called 1976 the year of the evangelical, but many evangelicals turned against him by 1980 over abortion and the Equal Rights Amendment.
  • Pat Robertson founded the Christian Broadcasting Network, and its programme The 700 Club began in 1966; Falwell's Old Time Gospel Hour reached a national audience from the 1970s.
  • The scandals of Jim Bakker in 1987 and Jimmy Swaggart in 1988 damaged the image of televangelism.

The Moral Majority and the religious right

From 1979 conservative Christians became an organised political force, closely associated with the Republican Party and Ronald Reagan.

  • The Moral Majority was founded in 1979 by Jerry Falwell and others, campaigning against abortion, pornography, the decline of the family and the secular influence in schools.
  • The Moral Majority mobilised conservative voters and churches before the 1980 election, and its support helped the Republican campaign of Ronald Reagan.
  • Its influence was greatest in the early 1980s, and it declined after its leaders were embarrassed by the scandals of the late 1980s and the failure of its political strategy.
  • Falwell, a Baptist pastor from Lynchburg, Virginia, founded the Moral Majority in 1979 with the encouragement of conservative organisers such as Paul Weyrich, and it used direct-mail lists to register voters.
  • In August 1980 Reagan told a conference of conservative Christians in Dallas that he knew they could not endorse him but that he endorsed them, and the Republican platform of 1980 opposed abortion and backed school prayer.
  • The movement's agenda included opposing abortion, the Equal Rights Amendment and gay rights, and supporting school prayer and a strong defence; it was seen by critics as a threat to the separation of church and state.
  • Falwell disbanded the Moral Majority in 1989, but its voters and organisers moved into groups such as Robertson's Christian Coalition (1989), and the religious right remained a lasting part of the Republican coalition.

Mass media: television, news and Watergate

Television became the main source of news and entertainment in most American homes, and it shaped politics, the Vietnam War and the Watergate crisis.

  • By 1960 most American homes had a television set, and network news programmes became the principal source of national news for millions of viewers.
  • The first televised presidential debate, between Kennedy and Nixon on 26 September 1960, was watched by a large audience and is widely credited with shaping the outcome of the election.
  • The quiz show scandals of 1958, in which contestants were given answers in advance, damaged public trust in television and led to new rules on rigging.
  • Walter Cronkite's broadcast of 27 February 1968, calling the war a stalemate, was among the reports that influenced public opinion on Vietnam.
  • The Washington Post's reporting of the Watergate burglary and its cover-up, led by Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, showed the power of the press to expose a presidency.
  • The Pentagon Papers and other press revelations about government deception strengthened the role of the press as a check on power.
  • The Public Broadcasting Act of 1967 created the Corporation for Public Broadcasting, which funded the public television network that began broadcasting in 1970.
  • Advertising expanded rapidly on television and in magazines, and the mass media turned consumer goods and celebrity into central features of public life.
  • CBS and NBC extended their evening news to thirty minutes in September 1963, and surveys showed that by the mid-1960s television overtook newspapers as the main source of news for most Americans.
  • Vietnam was called the first television war: the CBS reporter Morley Safer's film of Marines burning the village of Cam Ne in August 1965, the broadcast execution of a Viet Cong prisoner in Saigon during Tet in 1968 and the photograph of a child burned by napalm in 1972 were among the images that shaped opinion.
  • The Senate Watergate hearings of 1973 were televised live, and the revelation of the White House tapes made the media a central actor in the downfall of Nixon.
  • The FCC abolished the Fairness Doctrine in 1987, which helped talk radio and partisan broadcasting to grow, and cable television expanded with CNN (1980) and MTV (1981).

Mass higher education

The number of students in colleges and universities grew sharply after 1945, supported by the GI Bill and later federal aid, and campuses became centres of protest.

  • The GI Bill allowed large numbers of veterans to attend college after 1945, and many of them were the first in their families to do so.
  • The number of college students rose from about 2.6 million in 1950 to more than 8 million by 1970, more than tripling in twenty years.
  • The California Master Plan for Higher Education of 1960 set out a three-tier system of universities, state colleges and community colleges that became a model for other states.
  • The Higher Education Act of 1965 provided federal scholarships and loans, and it extended federal support to students and institutions.
  • The Free Speech Movement at the University of California, Berkeley, began in September 1964 when students protested against restrictions on political activity on campus.
  • Mario Savio's speech of 2 December 1964 urged students to put their bodies upon the gears of the machine, and the sit-in in Sproul Hall that followed became the movement's best-known protest.
  • Campus protest spread to many universities through the anti-war movement, and the killings at Kent State University in May 1970 brought mass student strikes across the country.
  • The GI Bill sent about 2 million veterans to college, and the Sputnik shock led to the National Defense Education Act of 1958 and more federal funding for universities.
  • The University of California's president Clark Kerr described the huge research university as a multiversity, and Berkeley's size and impersonality were among the Free Speech Movement's grievances.
  • The Free Speech Movement's occupation of Sproul Hall ended in the arrest of about 800 students on 3 December 1964, and the university conceded to most of the movement's demands soon after.
  • Students occupied buildings at Columbia University in April 1968, and the shootings at Kent State and at Jackson State College, Mississippi, in May 1970 were followed by strikes at hundreds of campuses.

Assessment: did religion and media shape public life?

Examiners reward judgements that weigh the influence of religion, media and education against their limits and against the views of contemporaries and later historians.

  • Supporters of the religious revival argue that faith gave Americans a common identity against communism and a moral basis for civil rights, as Graham and King both claimed.
  • Critics argue that the religious right of the 1980s narrowed debate on social issues, and that televangelism encouraged dependence on wealthy donors.
  • Television made politics more visible and personal, which helped Kennedy and Reagan but also exposed governments to scrutiny over Vietnam and Watergate.
  • Mass higher education gave many more Americans social mobility, but it also produced campus unrest and debates about the role of universities in politics and society.

Key terms

Fundamentalism
A conservative strand of Protestantism that holds to the literal truth of the Bible and rejects modernist interpretations of scripture.
Moral majority
The organised conservative Christian political movement founded in 1979 by Jerry Falwell, campaigning on abortion, the family and school issues.
Mass media
Television, radio, newspapers and magazines, which by 1960 reached most American homes and shaped politics, war and culture.
Public opinion
The views of ordinary Americans, measured by polls and shaped by the media, which in this period influenced responses to religion, war and politics.
Evangelicalism
A Protestant movement stressing personal conversion, the authority of scripture and evangelism, represented by the National Association of Evangelicals.
Televangelism
The use of television broadcasts by preachers to reach large audiences, raise money and promote religious and political causes.
Free Speech Movement
The student protest at the University of California, Berkeley, from September 1964, which defended political activity on campus.
Mass higher education
The expansion of college and university places after 1945, supported by the GI Bill and federal aid, which made higher education available to far more Americans.
Civil religion
The shared public faith in America's God-given mission, expressed in the Pledge, the motto and presidential appeals to religion, which was strong in the 1950s.
Fairness Doctrine
The FCC rule requiring broadcasters to present contrasting views on controversial issues, abolished in 1987.
