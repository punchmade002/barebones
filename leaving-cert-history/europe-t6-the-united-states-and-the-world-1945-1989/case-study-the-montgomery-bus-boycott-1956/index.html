Background: segregation on Montgomery's buses

Montgomery, Alabama, was the first capital of the Confederacy, and segregation on its buses was enforced by city ordinance and Alabama state law. Black passengers made up most of the riders.

  • Montgomery's city buses reserved the front rows for white passengers and the rear for black passengers, and the driver could move the dividing line backwards to make more room for white riders, forcing black passengers to stand or give up their seats.
  • Black passengers had to pay at the front and then get off and re-enter by the rear door, and drivers, who carried guns and had police powers, often insulted black riders or drove off before they re-boarded.
  • Black riders made up the large majority of bus passengers, so the segregation rules affected most of the city's black working population, many of them maids and labourers who rode to work in white homes and businesses.
  • The Supreme Court's Brown v Board of Education decision of May 1954 raised black expectations, and the Women's Political Council (WPC), led from 1950 by Jo Ann Robinson, wrote to Mayor W. A. Gayle in May 1954 warning that a boycott of the buses was possible.
  • Earlier challenges had failed: in March 1955 Claudette Colvin, a fifteen-year-old schoolgirl, was arrested for refusing to give up her seat, and in October 1955 Mary Louise Smith, aged eighteen, was arrested on the same charge.
  • Local leaders including E. D. Nixon decided not to make Colvin the test case, because of her youth and because she became pregnant, and they judged that a respectable adult would command more support.
  • Bus boycotts had been tried before, including in Baton Rouge, Louisiana, in 1953, which Montgomery's organisers knew of.
  • Alabama's white Citizens' Councils, which had grown across the South after Brown, were committed to resisting any change.

The arrest of Rosa Parks and the boycott begins

The arrest of Rosa Parks on 1 December 1955 gave the organisers the case they wanted, and a one-day protest turned into a boycott lasting 381 days.

  • Rosa Parks, a seamstress in a department store and the secretary of the Montgomery branch of the NAACP, was arrested on 1 December 1955 for refusing to give up her seat to a white passenger on a Cleveland Avenue bus when the driver, James Blake, demanded it.
  • Parks was no accidental protester: she had investigated cases of sexual violence against black women for the NAACP, and in the summer of 1955 she had attended a workshop on racial equality at the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee.
  • E. D. Nixon, president of the Montgomery NAACP and a Pullman porters' union leader, bailed Parks out with the help of the white couple Clifford and Virginia Durr, and he persuaded her to let her case be used to challenge the law.
  • Parks was tried on 5 December 1955, found guilty of violating the segregation law and fined 10 dollars plus costs, and her lawyer Fred Gray appealed.
  • That night Jo Ann Robinson and the WPC, with students at Alabama State College, ran off tens of thousands of leaflets calling for a one-day boycott of the buses on Monday, 5 December 1955.
  • Black ministers announced the boycott from their pulpits on Sunday 4 December, and the leaflets were handed out through churches and schools.
  • The boycott was almost completely observed on 5 December; and that evening a mass meeting of about 5,000 people at the Holt Street Baptist Church voted to continue the boycott.
  • The demands were modest: courteous treatment by drivers, seating on a first-come, first-served basis with black passengers filling seats from the back and white from the front, and the hiring of black drivers on mainly black routes; they did not at first demand an end to segregation.
  • The city refused to negotiate on these terms, and the bus company and city commissioners hardened their stance, so the demand for desegregation grew as the boycott continued.

Organising the boycott: the Montgomery Improvement Association

The Montgomery Improvement Association (MIA) co-ordinated the boycott, and its car pool and church networks kept about 40,000 black bus riders off the buses for more than a year.

  • The MIA was formed on the afternoon of 5 December 1955, and the Reverend Martin Luther King Jr., a 26-year-old pastor of the Dexter Avenue Baptist Church who had arrived in Montgomery in 1954 and had no record of conflict with the white authorities, was elected its president.
  • King's address at the Holt Street meeting combined a demand for justice with a commitment to non-violence and Christian love, and it launched him as the movement's voice.
  • Other leaders included Ralph Abernathy, E. D. Nixon, Jo Ann Robinson and the Reverend Uriah Fields, and the movement drew on the black churches, whose ministers were free from white employers' control.
  • A car pool, run by volunteers with several hundred cars and by black taxi drivers who charged the bus fare of ten cents, took passengers to work and school, with dispatch points at churches and a pick-up system organised by the MIA.
  • Churches raised money to buy station wagons for the car pool, and funds and support came from northern supporters, unions and churches across the country.
  • Thousands of people walked, and many black domestic workers continued to walk long distances to their jobs in white households, a mark of the protest's determination.
  • The boycott hit the bus company's income hard, and city centre shops lost custom from black shoppers, so the economic pressure on white Montgomery was real.
  • Bayard Rustin, a pacifist and experienced organiser, and Glenn Smiley of the Fellowship of Reconciliation came to Montgomery in February 1956 to advise King on non-violence, and the movement was told to stay disciplined in the face of attacks.
  • King later said that in January 1956, after threatening phone calls, he had prayed at his kitchen table and felt a sense of divine reassurance, which strengthened his resolve.

White resistance and the legal challenge

Montgomery's white authorities responded with police harassment, legal action and violence, while the black community also began a federal court challenge that settled the matter.

  • The city's leaders first tried to divide the boycotters: the mayor and city commissioners announced falsely that the boycott had been settled, and the police arrested drivers in the car pool for minor offences and told taxi drivers to charge a minimum fare.
  • King was arrested on 26 January 1956 for driving 30 miles an hour in a 25 mile zone, a minor charge that showed the police were watching the leaders.
  • On 30 January 1956 a bomb was thrown on the porch of King's house while his wife Coretta and baby daughter were inside, and nobody was hurt; an angry crowd gathered, but King urged them to go home and not to retaliate.
  • In February 1956 a grand jury indicted 89 leaders of the boycott, including about twenty-four ministers, under a 1921 Alabama law against boycotts; King was tried in March 1956 and convicted, fined 500 dollars, and the sentence was suspended pending appeal.
  • The indictments produced national press attention, and the images of ministers lining up to be booked turned the arrests into a symbol of dignity.
  • On 1 February 1956 the lawyer Fred Gray filed Browder v Gayle in the federal court on behalf of four black women, Aurelia Browder, Susie McDonald, Claudette Colvin and Mary Louise Smith, challenging the constitutionality of segregation on the buses.
  • Rosa Parks was not a party to Browder, because her case was still in the Alabama courts and it could have taken years.
  • On 5 June 1956 a three-judge federal court ruled by two votes to one that segregation on buses violated the Fourteenth Amendment, and the city and state appealed to the Supreme Court.
  • On 13 November 1956 the Supreme Court affirmed the decision, in a brief unsigned order that relied on Brown v Board of Education and later decisions, and denied the city's request for a rehearing on 17 December.
  • On the same day Montgomery's city lawyers won a state court injunction against the car pools as a public nuisance operating without a licence, and King was in court for that hearing when reporters brought news that the Supreme Court had upheld the ruling.
  • The formal Supreme Court order was served on Montgomery on 20 December 1956, and the MIA meeting voted to end the boycott on that day.

Desegregation and its immediate results

The boycott ended bus segregation in Montgomery, but violence and continuing segregation elsewhere limited what it changed.

  • The buses were desegregated on 21 December 1956, the 382nd day, when King and Ralph Abernathy rode a bus with white passengers, in front of a press corps.
  • The MIA issued rules telling its members to be courteous and not to retaliate, and to sit anywhere without boasting.
  • In the weeks that followed, snipers fired on buses, a black teenager was shot in the legs, and on 10 January 1957 bombs damaged four black churches and the homes of Robert Graetz and Ralph Abernathy; the city suspended bus service for a time.
  • Several white men were arrested for the bombings, but they received little punishment.
  • In January 1957 King and other ministers met in Atlanta to form a regional organisation, which became the Southern Christian Leadership Conference in August 1957, with King as president.
  • Segregation remained in Montgomery's schools, housing, restaurants and parks, and black voters were still largely excluded.
  • Parks lost her job and received threats, and she moved to Detroit with her family in 1957.

Violence and the wider Alabama response, 1956

The boycott provoked a campaign of intimidation across Alabama and showed how far white authorities would go to protect segregation.

  • The home of Robert Graetz, a white Lutheran minister serving a black congregation who supported the boycott, was bombed in August 1956.
  • Autherine Lucy became the first black student admitted to the University of Alabama, in February 1956; she was suspended after white rioting, and was later expelled, showing that the state's resistance extended beyond the buses.
  • The state authorities banned the NAACP in Alabama in June 1956, which was designed to cut off the boycott's legal support; the ban was overturned by the Supreme Court in 1958.
  • The White Citizens' Councils and the Ku Klux Klan threatened leaders, and employers dismissed black workers who took part, while the city police harassed car pool drivers.
  • The Southern Manifesto of March 1956 showed that most Southern members of Congress backed resistance to desegregation.
  • The violence did not break the boycott, and the national press coverage of the bombings and arrests brought sympathy from northern opinion and brought the matter to the federal courts.

Key personality: Martin Luther King Jr. (role in the boycott)

King was the leader of the boycott rather than its originator, and Montgomery made him a national figure. His overall career is covered in the notes on racial conflict and the civil rights movement.

  • King, born in 1929, the son of a prominent Atlanta pastor and holder of a Boston University doctorate, had been pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church for only a year when the boycott began, and he was chosen partly because he was new and had no enemies.
  • He was a participant who shaped the boycott's tone: he insisted on non-violence and on love for the opponent, based on Christian teaching and on Gandhi's methods, which he had studied as a student and which Rustin and Smiley helped him to apply during the boycott.
  • His arrest in January and February 1956, the bombing of his home on 30 January and his conviction in March made him a target and a symbol, and national magazines made him famous.
  • His memoir Stride Toward Freedom (1958) described the boycott and his thinking about non-violence.
  • Contemporaries in Montgomery's white leadership called him an outside agitator and tried to portray the boycott as run by communists; black contemporaries saw him as an inspired speaker, though some older leaders such as E. D. Nixon resented his rise.
  • Historians such as Taylor Branch (Parting the Waters, 1988) emphasise King's growth into a national leader through Montgomery, while Aldon Morris and others argue that the movement depended on local leaders and the church networks which existed before him, so that King was a product as well as a maker of the movement.

Significance: King, non-violence and the movement

Historians see the Montgomery bus boycott as a model for the mass civil rights movement of the 1960s, though they disagree on how far it started the movement.

  • Non-violence was adopted as the movement's guiding principle, and the boycott brought together the Christian ideas of love and forgiveness with Gandhi's methods of mass non-violent resistance.
  • The boycott showed that black communities could organise sustained economic pressure on segregation, a method later used in Tallahassee (1956) and in sit-in campaigns.
  • It made the church the base of protest, and the MIA became a model for the Southern Christian Leadership Conference.
  • The role of women, including Rosa Parks, Jo Ann Robinson and the Women's Political Council, was crucial, though men such as King and Nixon were more often given public recognition.
  • Historians of Parks, such as Jeanne Theoharis in The Rebellious Life of Mrs Rosa Parks (2013), argue that the popular image of a tired seamstress who acted on impulse hides her long career as an activist.
  • The legal outcome, Browder v Gayle, rested on Brown, so some historians argue that the courts rather than the boycott ended bus segregation, while others argue that the boycott kept the case alive and made the ruling effective.
  • The boycott also gave the movement international publicity, which mattered in the Cold War, when the Soviet Union used American racism as propaganda.

Assessment: how far did Montgomery bring change?

  • Achievement: Montgomery ended bus segregation in the city and defeated white resistance after 381 days, showing that organised, disciplined protest could defeat segregation.
  • Achievement: it produced a national leader, King, a lasting organisation, the SCLC, and a method of non-violent mass action that was used in Birmingham and Selma.
  • Limit: the legal victory applied only to buses, and it did not change schools, housing, jobs or the voting restrictions across Alabama.
  • Limit: violence followed the victory, and white resistance across the South hardened after Brown and Montgomery, so change came slowly until the laws of 1964 and 1965.
  • Contemporary white opinion in Montgomery condemned the boycott and used intimidation, while black residents who had endured segregation for decades saw the victory as a long overdue success.
  • Later historians stress the importance of the organisation the boycott created, the leaders it produced and the example it set, and they debate whether it was the start of the civil rights movement or one phase of a longer struggle that began in the 1930s and 1940s.

Key terms

Montgomery Improvement Association
The organisation formed on 5 December 1955 to co-ordinate the Montgomery bus boycott, with King as its president.
Discrimination
The unequal treatment of black Americans on Montgomery's buses, where segregation rules required them to give up seats to white passengers.
Browder v Gayle
The federal case filed on 1 February 1956 challenging Montgomery's bus segregation, which the Supreme Court upheld on 13 November 1956.
Car pool
The volunteer transport system that carried boycotting passengers to work and school in Montgomery during the boycott.
Women's Political Council
The Montgomery black women's organisation led by Jo Ann Robinson, which planned a bus boycott from 1954 and distributed its leaflets on 1 December 1955.
Non-violent direct action
The strategy used in the Montgomery boycott of peaceful protest and refusal to comply with segregation, without retaliation against violence.
White Citizens' Council
A network of white organisations founded in 1954 after Brown, which used economic and social pressure to defend segregation.
NAACP
The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, whose Montgomery branch was led by E. D. Nixon, with Rosa Parks as secretary.
