Hollywood and the American Dream on screen, 1945 to 1960

The studio system dominated American film in the postwar years, and its films celebrated family, prosperity and patriotic values.

  • The studio system, in which major companies such as MGM, Warner Brothers and Paramount controlled production, distribution and cinema chains, was weakened by the Supreme Court's Paramount decree of 1948, which required the studios to sell their cinemas.
  • Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life (1946) portrayed small-town community and decency against greed; it did poorly on release but became a Christmas television favourite from the 1970s and a lasting image of the American Dream.
  • Hollywood films of the 1950s increasingly addressed family life, race and juvenile delinquency, while the Production Code continued to restrict the treatment of sex and crime.
  • Television drew audiences away from cinemas from the early 1950s, and the studios began to sell films to television and to produce television series.
  • The Best Years of Our Lives (1946) won seven Academy Awards and dealt with the return of veterans, and Hollywood also produced social-problem films on anti-Semitism and race, such as Gentleman's Agreement (1947) and Pinky (1949).
  • Cinema attendance, which had peaked at about 90 million a week in 1946, roughly halved by 1960 as television spread, so the studios turned to wide-screen CinemaScope (1953), colour and epics such as The Ten Commandments (1956) and Ben-Hur (1959).
  • Musicals such as Singin' in the Rain (1952) and family comedies promoted cheerful abundance, and the American Dream of home, family and consumption was the staple of studio films and of the new television comedies.
  • Westerns and science fiction carried Cold War messages: High Noon (1952) was read as an allegory of the blacklist, while Invasion of the Body Snatchers (1956) was read as a fable of conformity or of communist infiltration.
  • The Production Code, enforced by the Hays Office, was weakened in the 1950s by films such as The Moon Is Blue (1953) and was replaced by a ratings system in November 1968.

The red scare in Hollywood

The House Un-American Activities Committee's investigations of the film industry from 1947 created a blacklist that ended many careers.

  • HUAC opened hearings on communist influence in Hollywood in October 1947, and the Hollywood Ten, screenwriters and directors who refused to answer questions, were sentenced to prison for contempt of Congress in 1948.
  • The studios' Waldorf Statement of November 1947 committed them to dismiss suspected communists, and a blacklist operated for years afterwards.
  • Writers who could not work under their own names, such as Dalton Trumbo, continued to write scripts under pseudonyms or the names of friends.
  • Kirk Douglas and Otto Preminger broke the blacklist in 1960 by openly crediting Trumbo on Spartacus and Exodus, a sign that the industry's rules were changing.
  • Friendly witnesses before HUAC included the actor Ronald Reagan, then president of the Screen Actors Guild, and Walt Disney, and in 1952 the director Elia Kazan named former communist colleagues, a decision he defended in On the Waterfront (1954).
  • Hundreds of writers, actors and directors were blacklisted in the 1950s, and Charlie Chaplin left the United States in 1952 after his re-entry permit was withdrawn.
  • Anti-communist films such as I Was a Communist for the FBI (1951) were made by the studios to show their loyalty.

Key personality: Marilyn Monroe (career, image and death)

Monroe was a major film star and a public figure. Her role as an image of women is covered in the notes on the family and women.

  • Her screen career began with small roles in the late 1940s, and she gained wider fame in Asphalt Jungle (1950) and All About Eve (1950) before becoming a star in 1953.
  • Gentlemen Prefer Blondes (1953), How to Marry a Millionaire (1953) and The Seven Year Itch (1955) made her one of the most popular box office stars of the decade.
  • She studied at the Actors Studio in New York in the mid-1950s, and her marriage to the playwright Arthur Miller in 1956 brought her an association with serious drama.
  • Some Like It Hot (1959), directed by Billy Wilder, won critical praise and remains her most admired comedy.
  • Her difficulties with studio executives, including disputes over her contract and her choice of roles, were widely reported in the press.
  • She sang Happy Birthday, Mr President to John F. Kennedy at a Democratic Party fundraiser in Madison Square Garden on 19 May 1962.
  • She died at her home in Los Angeles on the night of 4 August 1962, found early the next morning, from an overdose of barbiturates; the death was ruled a probable suicide, and it remains a subject of debate.
  • Contemporaries judged her as a glamorous star whose personal life was a matter of public fascination, while later critics argued that her career showed how the studio system exploited women performers.
  • Her last completed film was The Misfits (1961), written by Miller, and in June 1962 Twentieth Century-Fox dismissed her from Something's Got to Give after repeated absences.
  • Her death at the age of 36 produced years of conspiracy theories, including claims of a link to the Kennedys, which are not supported by the official investigation.

Youth culture and the beat generation, 1950s

Teenagers became a distinct consumer group in the 1950s, and new music and writing challenged the conformity of the era.

  • Rock and roll drew on black rhythm and blues and reached white teenagers through radio and records, with Elvis Presley's Heartbreak Hotel (1956) one of the first to become a national hit.
  • The actor James Dean, who starred in Rebel Without a Cause (1955) and died in a car crash in September 1955, became a symbol of teenage alienation, as did Marlon Brando in The Wild One (1953).
  • The Beat writers, including Jack Kerouac, whose On the Road appeared in 1957, and Allen Ginsberg, whose poem Howl was published in 1956, rejected the conventions of suburban life.
  • Bill Haley's Rock Around the Clock (1955) and Elvis's television appearances on the Ed Sullivan Show in 1956 and 1957 made rock and roll a national craze, and Dick Clark's American Bandstand, on the national network from 1957, made youth culture into a commercial market; older critics condemned the music as a threat to morals.

Key personality: Norman Mailer

Mailer was a novelist, essayist and public figure whose writing and persona made him a central voice of the 1960s debate.

  • Norman Mailer's first novel, The Naked and the Dead (1948), drew on his service in the Pacific during the Second World War and became a best seller.
  • He was a co-founder of the Village Voice newspaper in New York in 1955, which was part of the counter-cultural press of the period.
  • His essay The White Negro (1957) argued that the hipster, who borrowed from black culture and lived for the moment, offered a model of rebellion against the conformity of postwar America.
  • The Armies of the Night (1968) described the anti-war march on the Pentagon in October 1967, and it won the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award.
  • Mailer was arrested at the Pentagon march in October 1967, and he wrote about himself in the third person in the book; his public persona, including a campaign for mayor of New York in 1969, made him a celebrity as well as a literary authority.
  • Contemporaries were divided: admirers saw him as a serious critic of American culture, while critics attacked his self-promotion, his machismo, and his stabbing of his second wife Adele in 1960, for which he received a suspended sentence.
  • Historians today generally treat his non-fiction, especially The Armies of the Night, as an important account of the 1960s protest movement.
  • The White Negro was widely attacked for romanticising black life, and James Baldwin criticised Mailer for it.
  • In his later career Mailer wrote Miami and the Siege of Chicago (1968), The Fight (1975) about Ali and Foreman in Zaire, and The Executioner's Song (1979), which won the Pulitzer Prize for fiction.

Key personality: Muhammad Ali

Ali's career linked sport, religion and the anti-war movement, making him a symbol of black pride and political resistance.

  • Born Cassius Clay in Louisville, Kentucky, in 1942, he won the Olympic gold medal in the light-heavyweight division at Rome in 1960.
  • He won the world heavyweight title by beating Sonny Liston in Miami Beach on 25 February 1964, and announced his membership of the Nation of Islam and his new name, Muhammad Ali, in March 1964.
  • He had told reporters in 1966 that he had no quarrel with the Viet Cong; on 28 April 1967 he refused induction into the army in Houston as a Muslim minister and conscientious objector, and boxing authorities stripped him of his title and licence. In June 1967 he was convicted of draft evasion, sentenced to five years in prison and fined 10,000 dollars, but he stayed free on appeal.
  • The Supreme Court overturned the conviction on 28 June 1971 by 8 votes to 0 (Justice Marshall took no part), ruling that the government had not given a valid basis for rejecting his conscientious objector claim.
  • He was barred from boxing for over three years, returned to the ring in October 1970 and lost to Joe Frazier in the Fight of the Century at Madison Square Garden on 8 March 1971, before winning back the title by beating George Foreman in Kinshasa in October 1974.
  • Contemporaries were sharply divided, with many white Americans and sports writers condemning him, while many young black Americans and anti-war activists saw him as a principled leader.
  • His famous saying float like a butterfly, sting like a bee summed up his style, and he became one of the best known sportsmen in the world by the 1970s.
  • Ali beat Foreman in Kinshasa, Zaire, on 30 October 1974 in the Rumble in the Jungle, and he beat Joe Frazier in the Thrilla in Manila in 1975, before retiring in 1981 and living with Parkinson's disease.
  • He was Cassius Marcellus Clay Jr. and rejected his birth name as a slave name; Elijah Muhammad, leader of the Nation of Islam, gave him the name Muhammad Ali.
  • Historians such as David Remnick (King of the World, 1998) treat Ali as a sportsman who became an international symbol of black pride and of resistance to the Vietnam War, and his reputation became widely admired from the 1970s.

The counter-culture and its politics, 1967 to 1971

The late 1960s counter-culture rejected the consumer and corporate values of the 1950s and linked personal liberation to political protest.

  • The Summer of Love of 1967 drew thousands of young people to the Haight-Ashbury district of San Francisco, where a counter-culture of music, drugs and communal living developed.
  • The Woodstock music festival, held near Bethel, New York, from 15 to 18 August 1969 attracted an estimated crowd of about 400,000 people and became a symbol of the generation.
  • The Stonewall riots in New York in June 1969 began the modern gay rights movement, after police raided a gay bar in Greenwich Village.
  • The Students for a Democratic Society and the Black Panther Party were among the political groups that drew on counter-cultural style and protest in the late 1960s.
  • Timothy Leary, a former Harvard psychologist, told young people in 1966 to turn on, tune in and drop out, and LSD was a central part of the hippie scene until it was banned in California in 1966.
  • Rock musicians such as Bob Dylan, Jimi Hendrix and the Beatles, whose Sgt Pepper album appeared in 1967, gave the movement its soundtrack, and the film Easy Rider (1969) put the counter-culture on screen.
  • The festival at Altamont Speedway in California in December 1969, where a man was killed by the Hells Angels during a Rolling Stones concert, was seen as the end of the dream of peace and love.
  • The Yippies, led by Abbie Hoffman and Jerry Rubin, mixed theatre and politics and took part in the protests at the Democratic Convention in Chicago in 1968.
  • Most young Americans were not hippies, and the counter-culture provoked a backlash among the silent majority that Nixon appealed to in 1968 and 1972.

Multiculturalism and the collapse of consensus

The 1960s and 1970s saw minority groups assert their identities and demand equal rights, challenging the idea of a single American culture.

  • The Chicano movement, led in part by Cesar Chavez and the National Farm Workers Association, organised farm workers in California from the early 1960s.
  • The American Indian Movement was founded in Minneapolis in 1968 to campaign against discrimination and for treaty rights.
  • The women's liberation movement disrupted the Miss America pageant in Atlantic City in September 1968, protesting the way women were presented to the public.
  • The concept of consensus, which historians used to describe the 1950s, was challenged as Americans debated race, gender, sexuality and national identity in the 1960s and 1970s.
  • The National Farm Workers Association merged with another union to become the United Farm Workers in 1966, and the Delano grape strike and boycott from 1965 won national support for Chavez.
  • In November 1969 American Indian activists occupied Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay, an occupation that lasted until 1971, and AIM occupied Wounded Knee, South Dakota, in 1973.
  • The Immigration Act of 1965 and the Bilingual Education Act of 1968 encouraged a more diverse society and gave a legal basis to minority languages in schools.
  • The Stonewall riots of 28 June 1969 followed a police raid on the Stonewall Inn, and the Gay Liberation Front was founded shortly afterwards; marches commemorating Stonewall began in 1970.
  • Black Power, the Black Panther Party and the black arts movement asserted pride in African American culture, and the television series Roots (1977) drew about 100 million viewers.

Assessment: did a consensus exist and did it collapse?

Examiners reward judgements that test the idea of consensus against evidence of conflict within the postwar culture.

  • Supporters of the consensus view argue that most Americans shared a belief in prosperity, family and anti-communism in the 1950s, as shown by the popularity of the films and television of the period.
  • Critics of the consensus view argue that the 1950s were marked by conflict over race, sex and loyalty, and that the Red Scare shows the limits of the supposed consensus.
  • The 1960s counter-culture showed that a sizeable minority rejected the values of the 1950s, but the majority of Americans in 1968 still backed conventional candidates and the war.
  • Historians differ over whether the collapse of consensus was a sudden break in 1968 or a gradual shift that began with the civil rights movement in the 1950s.

Key terms

Liberalism
In the cultural context, the tradition of civil liberties and tolerance that the 1950s consensus tested, and which the 1960s protest movements extended to race, sex and sexuality.
Mass media
Film, television, radio and print, which shaped the consensus of the 1950s and carried the images of youth culture and protest to a national audience.
Counter-culture
The youth movement of the late 1960s that rejected mainstream values and linked personal freedom to political protest, centred on Haight-Ashbury and Woodstock.
Blacklist
The industry-wide list of writers, actors and directors refused work because of alleged communist sympathies, a practice that followed the Hollywood hearings of 1947.
Multiculturalism
The assertion in the 1960s and 1970s of the rights and identities of ethnic, racial and sexual minorities against a single model of American culture.
Consensus
The historians' term for the shared values of prosperity, family and anti-communism that many saw as characteristic of American culture in the 1950s.
Beat generation
The writers, such as Kerouac and Ginsberg, who in the 1950s rejected suburban conformity and influenced the 1960s counter-culture.
Stonewall riots
The June 1969 protests in Greenwich Village, New York, after a police raid on a gay bar, which began the modern gay rights movement.
Production Code
The film industry's self-censorship code, enforced by the Hays Office from 1934, which restricted the portrayal of sex and crime until it was replaced by a ratings system in 1968.
Hippie
A member of the late 1960s counter-culture who rejected conventional dress, work and sexual morals and favoured communal living, drugs and music.
Nation of Islam
The black religious and political organisation led by Elijah Muhammad that Ali joined in 1964.
