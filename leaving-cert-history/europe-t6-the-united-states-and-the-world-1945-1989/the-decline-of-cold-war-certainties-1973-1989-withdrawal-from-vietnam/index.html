Withdrawal from Vietnam, 1973 to 1975

The United States withdrew from the Vietnam War through a negotiated settlement, a result that weakened the consensus in favour of containment.

  • The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973 by the United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Viet Cong, and they provided for the withdrawal of American forces.
  • The last US combat troops left Vietnam by late March 1973, and the release of American prisoners of war was completed in the same period.
  • Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto on 7 November 1973, requiring the President to report to Congress and to withdraw forces within sixty days unless Congress approved their continued use.
  • From August 1973 the Case-Church amendment barred further US combat in Indochina, so when the North broke the Accords the United States could not intervene, and South Vietnam depended on its own forces and on aid from Congress.
  • The North Vietnamese offensive of 1975 overran South Vietnam, and the capture of Saigon on 30 April 1975 ended the war.
  • In April 1975 Congress refused Ford's request for emergency military aid for Saigon, a sign of how far the American consensus behind the war had collapsed.
  • Ford told a university audience on 23 April 1975 that the war was finished as far as America was concerned, and the last Americans and many South Vietnamese were airlifted from Saigon by helicopter (Operation Frequent Wind) on 29 and 30 April.
  • Cambodia fell to the Khmer Rouge on 17 April 1975, and Laos came under communist control later that year, so all three Indochinese states had communist governments by the end of 1975.
  • About 58,000 Americans had died in the war, and the defeat produced a lasting caution about overseas military commitments, sometimes called the Vietnam syndrome.

Détente: Nixon, Kissinger and the Soviet Union, 1969 to 1975

Détente was a policy of easing tensions with the Soviet Union and China through negotiation, based on a balance of power rather than on trust.

  • Henry Kissinger, as national security adviser and later Secretary of State, designed a policy of linkage that tied arms control to Soviet behaviour in other regions.
  • Nixon visited China from 21 to 28 February 1972, meeting Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai, and the Shanghai Communiqué set out a process to normalise relations.
  • Nixon visited Moscow in May 1972, when the first major arms control agreements were signed and trade agreements were discussed.
  • The Helsinki Final Act of August 1975, signed by thirty-five states including the United States and the Soviet Union, set out principles on borders and human rights, which the Soviet Union later failed to honour.
  • Critics of détente, including conservative Democrats such as Senator Henry Jackson, argued that the Soviet Union was gaining military strength while the United States made concessions.
  • The Jackson-Vanik Amendment of 1974 linked trade with the Soviet Union to freedom of emigration for Soviet Jews, and the Soviet Union rejected the link.
  • Détente faced strain in October 1973 when the Yom Kippur War in the Middle East led Nixon to put US forces on a nuclear alert (DEFCON 3) after Soviet threats of intervention.
  • Ford and Brezhnev reached a framework at Vladivostok in November 1974 for a second SALT agreement.
  • Soviet and Cuban support for the MPLA in Angola in 1975 and 1976 convinced critics that the Soviet Union was exploiting détente.

SALT I and SALT II

The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks aimed to limit the size of the nuclear arsenals of the superpowers, and produced two agreements of very different outcomes.

  • SALT I was signed in Moscow on 26 May 1972 and contained an Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty limiting missile defence systems, and an Interim Agreement limiting the number of strategic launchers.
  • The Interim Agreement froze the numbers of land-based ICBMs and submarine-launched missiles for five years, but it did not limit the number of warheads each missile carried.
  • SALT II was signed in Vienna on 18 June 1979 by Carter and Brezhnev, setting an equal ceiling of 2,400 strategic delivery vehicles for each side, to fall to 2,250, and limiting the number of multiple-warhead missiles.
  • The Senate did not ratify SALT II, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 led Carter to withdraw it from Senate consideration in January 1980.
  • Both sides continued to build up their arsenals under SALT I and after SALT II collapsed, so that the agreements limited growth without reducing the number of warheads.
  • SALT I was negotiated by Nixon and Brezhnev with Kissinger as the key American negotiator, and the Senate approved the ABM Treaty by 88 votes to 2 in August 1972.
  • Critics of SALT II, led by Senator Henry Jackson, said it let the Soviet Union keep a lead in heavy land-based missiles, and the Committee on the Present Danger campaigned against it.
  • The Soviet Union and the United States both said they would observe SALT II's limits informally after 1980, and Reagan's government dropped that restraint in 1986.

Carter and the end of détente, 1977 to 1981

Carter combined a human rights emphasis with a sharp hardening of policy after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.

  • Carter made human rights a central theme of US foreign policy from 1977, and he criticised Soviet treatment of dissidents such as Andrei Sakharov.
  • The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan on 24 to 25 December 1979 was condemned by Carter as an unacceptable threat to regional stability, and it ended the era of détente.
  • Carter announced a grain embargo against the Soviet Union in January 1980, proclaimed in his State of the Union speech that the United States would resist any attempt to gain control of the Persian Gulf (the Carter Doctrine), and led a boycott of the Moscow Olympics: the US Olympic Committee voted to stay away in April 1980, and dozens of other countries did too, though Britain, France and Ireland sent teams.
  • Carter's Iran policy, including the admission of the deposed Shah to the United States in October 1979, led to the seizure of the US embassy in Tehran on 4 November 1979.
  • The failed rescue attempt of April 1980 (Operation Eagle Claw) was abandoned after helicopters broke down, and a helicopter collided with a transport aircraft at a desert staging point, killing eight servicemen; it contributed to Carter's defeat in the 1980 election.
  • Carter also gave full diplomatic recognition to the People's Republic of China from 1 January 1979, building on Nixon's opening.

Reagan's build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative

Reagan rejected the logic of mutual deterrence and pursued a large increase in military spending, combined with a missile defence programme.

  • Reagan described the Soviet Union as an evil empire in a speech to the National Association of Evangelicals on 8 March 1983.
  • He announced the Strategic Defense Initiative on 23 March 1983 in a television address, proposing space-based systems that could destroy incoming missiles.
  • The programme, nicknamed Star Wars by critics, drew scientists' doubts about feasibility and cost and was never deployed, but it alarmed Soviet leaders who feared an American technological lead they could not match.
  • Reagan's military budget rose sharply between 1981 and 1985, and the B-1 bomber, MX missile and Pershing II missiles in Western Europe were among the projects funded.
  • The Soviet Union's leadership changed rapidly from Brezhnev's death in November 1982 to Andropov, Chernenko and then Mikhail Gorbachev, who came to power in March 1985.

Summits and the INF Treaty, 1985 to 1989

The meetings between Reagan and Gorbachev produced the first treaty to abolish a whole category of nuclear weapons, and the end of the Cold War followed.

  • Reagan and Gorbachev met at Geneva in November 1985, and at Reykjavik in October 1986, where they came close to agreeing deep cuts in nuclear weapons but the talks broke down when Gorbachev insisted that SDI be confined to the laboratory and Reagan refused.
  • The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was signed in Washington on 8 December 1987 and eliminated all US and Soviet land-based missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres.
  • Reagan spoke at the Berlin Wall on 12 June 1987, declaring Mr Gorbachev, tear down this wall.
  • Gorbachev's policies of glasnost and perestroika reduced Soviet control over Eastern Europe, and the Soviet Union did not intervene when communist governments fell in 1989.
  • The Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November 1989, and the collapse of communist regimes across Eastern Europe during 1989 ended the division that had defined the Cold War.
  • The Senate ratified the INF Treaty in May 1988, and Reagan visited Moscow for a fourth summit in May and June 1988; a Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan was completed in February 1989.
  • Reagan's successor George H. W. Bush met Gorbachev off Malta in December 1989, and the two leaders declared that the Cold War was ending.

Central America: Nicaragua and El Salvador, 1979 to 1989

Cold War competition extended into Central America, where revolutions and civil wars drew in US military and financial support.

  • The Sandinista National Liberation Front overthrew the Somoza dictatorship in Nicaragua in July 1979, and it established a government with close links to Cuba and the Soviet Union.
  • Carter's administration gave aid to the Sandinista government in its early years, but Reagan opposed it as a Soviet and Cuban foothold in the Americas and backed the Contra rebels against it.
  • The Contra war in Nicaragua was funded in part by secret arrangements that Congress had sought to prevent through the Boland Amendments of 1982 to 1984.
  • In El Salvador the government fought left-wing guerrillas from 1980, and the United States gave military aid to the government while criticising its record on human rights.
  • Carter's administration suspended aid to the Sandinistas in January 1981, and Reagan authorised covert support for the Contras in late 1981.
  • In El Salvador, the murder of Archbishop Oscar Romero in March 1980 and of four American churchwomen in December 1980 drew attention to death squads linked to the security forces, yet Reagan continued military aid.

Public opinion and the nuclear freeze, 1982 to 1984

Popular fear of nuclear war grew in the early 1980s, and the nuclear freeze movement pressed for limits on arms that Reagan's build-up had not satisfied.

  • The Nuclear Freeze campaign called for a bilateral freeze on the testing, production and deployment of nuclear weapons, and it gathered wide public support in 1982 and 1983.
  • A very large demonstration in New York's Central Park in June 1982 was among the largest peace rallies in the country's history.
  • The House of Representatives passed a non-binding freeze resolution in 1983, which showed how far the nuclear issue had moved into mainstream politics.
  • The television film The Day After, about a nuclear attack on Kansas, was seen by about 100 million Americans on 20 November 1983, and in January 1984 Reagan said that a nuclear war could not be won and must never be fought.
  • Massive protests in Western Europe, including at Greenham Common in Britain, opposed the deployment of American Pershing II and cruise missiles from late 1983.

Assessment: did the Cold War end because of US policy?

Examiners reward judgements that weigh US policy against Soviet internal failure and the choices of Gorbachev.

  • Supporters of Reagan argue that his build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative forced the Soviet Union into an arms race it could not afford, and that his firm stance created the conditions for Gorbachev's reforms.
  • Critics of Reagan argue that the Soviet economy was already failing before 1980 and that Gorbachev's reforms were the decisive cause of the change.
  • Détente and arms control under Nixon, Ford and Carter laid the foundations for the later treaties, and the INF Treaty built on the principle of verifiable limits established in the 1970s.
  • The failures of the period included the collapse of détente after 1979, the Iran hostage crisis and the costs of the Afghan war, which weakened US confidence before Reagan's arrival.
  • The Soviet Union faced economic stagnation, the costs of the Afghan war and falling oil revenues in the mid-1980s, and Reagan's own adviser Jack Matlock argued that Gorbachev's reforms drove the change, rather than American pressure.
  • Reagan's turn to negotiation after 1984, once he had rebuilt American strength, is credited by supporters as proof that strength led to a settlement.

Key terms

Détente
The easing of Cold War tensions between the United States and the Soviet Union in the early 1970s through negotiation and arms control.
SALT
The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks between the superpowers, which produced SALT I in 1972 and SALT II in 1979, the latter never ratified.
Strategic Defense Initiative
Reagan's 1983 proposal for a space-based missile defence system, nicknamed Star Wars, which was never fully built.
INF Treaty
The December 1987 agreement by which the United States and the Soviet Union eliminated their land-based intermediate-range nuclear missiles.
War Powers Resolution
The 1973 law, passed over Nixon's veto, requiring the President to consult Congress and withdraw forces within sixty days unless Congress approved their use.
Technological development
The advances in weapons and space systems, such as the proposed missile defence and the satellites used for verification, that shaped the arms race of the period.
Internationalism
The continuing US commitment to active engagement in world affairs, which was debated in the 1970s and 1980s as the Vietnam experience made Americans wary of overseas wars.
Linkage
Kissinger's policy of tying progress on arms control and trade to Soviet behaviour in other regions, such as the Middle East and Africa.
