Internationalism and the origins of containment

After 1945 the United States abandoned its pre-war isolation and committed itself to resisting Soviet expansion, a policy known as containment.

  • The United States emerged from the Second World War as the richest industrial power and, from 1945, the only country with atomic weapons.
  • The atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, on Truman's authority, and the Soviet Union tested its own bomb on 29 August 1949.
  • George Kennan, a diplomat in Moscow, sent his Long Telegram in February 1946 arguing that Soviet expansion could be checked by firm, long-term resistance rather than by negotiation.
  • Kennan's article The Sources of Soviet Conduct, published in Foreign Affairs in July 1947 under the signature X, gave containment its name.
  • The Truman Doctrine of 12 March 1947 asked Congress for about 400 million dollars in aid to Greece and Turkey, and it committed the United States to support free peoples resisting armed minorities or outside pressure.
  • The Communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 convinced many in Washington and Western Europe that the Soviet threat was immediate.
  • Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949, and Republicans later used the question "Who lost China?" to attack Truman's Asia policy.
  • In April 1950 the National Security Council paper NSC-68 called for a large rise in defence spending to meet the Soviet threat, and it was adopted after the Korean War began.

The Marshall Plan and NATO

Economic aid and a military alliance were the two pillars of containment in Western Europe.

  • George Marshall, the Secretary of State, proposed a European recovery programme in a speech at Harvard University on 5 June 1947.
  • The European Recovery Program, passed by Congress in April 1948, provided about 13 billion dollars of aid to Western European countries between 1948 and 1952.
  • The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and pressured its satellite states in Eastern Europe to refuse it too, so the division of Europe became more fixed.
  • Most aid was given as grants of food, fuel and machinery rather than loans, and the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC) was set up in April 1948 to allocate it; Britain, France and West Germany were the largest recipients.
  • The North Atlantic Treaty was signed on 4 April 1949 by twelve countries, including the United States, Britain, France, Canada and Italy.
  • Article 5 of the NATO treaty stated that an armed attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, the basis of collective defence.
  • Germany was divided: the Federal Republic of Germany was founded in May 1949 in the Western zones, and the German Democratic Republic followed in October 1949 in the Soviet zone.

Berlin: the airlift, the Wall and Kennedy's visit

Berlin, divided into four sectors, was the most dangerous point of Cold War contact in Europe.

  • The Soviet Union began blocking road, rail and canal access to West Berlin on 24 June 1948, aiming to force the Western powers out of the city.
  • The Western powers responded with an airlift that flew food, coal and other supplies into West Berlin; more than two million tons were carried before the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949.
  • In November 1958 Khrushchev demanded that the Western powers withdraw from Berlin within six months, and the issue was debated at the Vienna summit with Kennedy in June 1961.
  • The Berlin Wall was built from 13 August 1961, closing the border between East and West Berlin and stopping the flow of East German refugees.
  • In October 1961 American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, the crossing point for foreigners, in a tense standoff that ended without shots.
  • Well over two million East Germans had fled to the West between 1949 and 1961, most of them by crossing from East to West Berlin, which drained the East's skilled workforce.
  • Kennedy visited West Berlin on 26 June 1963 and declared in German "Ich bin ein Berliner" (I am a Berliner), a statement of solidarity that was widely praised in the West.

Korea, 1950 to 1953

The Korean War was the first major armed conflict of the Cold War, fought under a United Nations flag.

  • Korea had been divided at the 38th parallel in 1945; the Republic of Korea in the South under Syngman Rhee and the Democratic People's Republic in the North under Kim Il Sung were both founded in 1948.
  • US troops had left South Korea in 1949, and Secretary of State Dean Acheson's speech of January 1950 left Korea outside the American defence perimeter in the Pacific, which communist leaders may have read as a green light.
  • North Korean forces invaded the South on 25 June 1950, and the UN Security Council, with the Soviet delegate absent, condemned the attack and then recommended that members help South Korea repel it.
  • Truman sent American forces without asking Congress for a declaration of war, and he described the operation as a police action.
  • General Douglas MacArthur led the UN forces; his landing at Inchon on 15 September 1950 cut North Korean supply lines and led to the recapture of Seoul on 28 September.
  • UN forces advanced north of the 38th parallel in October 1950, and Chinese forces entered the war in large numbers from late October and November 1950, driving them back.
  • Truman dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 after the general publicly argued for attacking China, a decision that caused a major domestic row.
  • An armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953; the frontier stayed close to the 38th parallel, and no peace treaty was ever signed.
  • About 36,000 American servicemen died in the war, and US troops remained in South Korea after the armistice.

Cuba: revolution to the missile crisis, 1959 to 1962

Cuba lay ninety miles from Florida, and the United States had long treated the island as within its sphere of influence.

  • Under the Platt Amendment of 1901 the United States had the right to intervene in Cuba, a clause repealed in 1934 but leaving a legacy of US economic dominance.
  • Fidel Castro's revolution took power on 1 January 1959 after Batista fled, and the new government nationalised US-owned sugar, oil and other property in 1960.
  • Eisenhower cut the Cuban sugar quota in July 1960, imposed a partial trade embargo in October 1960 and broke off diplomatic relations in January 1961; Cuba turned to the Soviet Union for trade and oil.
  • CIA-trained Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs on 17 April 1961; they were defeated within three days, a humiliation for Kennedy.
  • Operation Mongoose, a covert campaign against Castro's government, was authorised in November 1961.
  • Soviet medium-range missiles were shipped to Cuba from September 1962, and photographs taken by a US U-2 spy plane on 14 October 1962 revealed the launch sites; Kennedy was shown them on 16 October.
  • Khrushchev's motives for the missiles included defending Cuba from another invasion, answering US Jupiter missiles in Turkey and Italy, and offsetting Soviet weakness in long-range missiles.
  • Kennedy and his advisers (the Executive Committee, ExComm) rejected an air strike or invasion, and on 22 October he announced on television a naval quarantine (blockade) of Cuba.
  • On 24 October Soviet ships turned back from the quarantine line; on 27 October ("Black Saturday") a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson.
  • On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed on Moscow radio to remove the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
  • Kennedy also secretly agreed to withdraw US Jupiter missiles from Turkey within months, a deal kept private for years; a hotline between Washington and Moscow was installed in 1963.

Vietnam to 1972: from the French war to Nixon's withdrawal

US involvement in Vietnam grew out of the containment of communism in Asia. Johnson's escalation of 1964 to 1968 is covered in the case study on Johnson and Vietnam.

  • Vietnam was part of French Indochina; the Viet Minh under Ho Chi Minh fought the French, who were defeated at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954.
  • From 1950 Truman gave aid to the French war, and by 1954 the United States was paying about three quarters of its cost.
  • In April 1954 Eisenhower described the domino theory: if Vietnam fell to communism, the rest of Southeast Asia would follow, like a row of falling dominoes.
  • The Geneva Accords of July 1954 divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with elections planned for 1956 that were never held.
  • The United States backed Ngo Dinh Diem in South Vietnam and joined the Southeast Asia Treaty Organisation (SEATO) in 1954.
  • Under Eisenhower the United States trained the South Vietnamese army with a military advisory group, and the first American advisers killed in the conflict died in 1959.
  • The National Liberation Front, known to the Americans as the Viet Cong, was formed in December 1960 to fight the government in the South.
  • Kennedy backed the Strategic Hamlet programme (1962), which moved peasants into fortified villages and was deeply resented.
  • In 1963 Diem's persecution of Buddhists provoked protests, including the self-immolation of the monk Thich Quang Duc in Saigon in June 1963.
  • Diem was overthrown and killed in a coup that US officials had tacitly encouraged, on 2 November 1963, after which the South suffered a series of unstable governments.
  • Kennedy increased US military advisers in South Vietnam to about 16,000 by late 1963.
  • The Nixon Doctrine, announced at Guam in July 1969, stated that Asian nations should take the main responsibility for their own defence, and US troop numbers began to fall.
  • Nixon's Vietnamisation handed the ground war to the South Vietnamese army while US troops were withdrawn, falling from about 540,000 in early 1969 to under 30,000 by late 1972.
  • In April 1970 Nixon sent US and South Vietnamese troops into Cambodia to attack communist bases, which sparked protests at home, including Kent State; secret bombing of Cambodia had begun in March 1969.
  • In 1971 South Vietnamese forces invaded Laos with US air support and were driven back; in 1972 Nixon answered North Vietnam's Easter Offensive by mining Haiphong harbour and the Linebacker bombing raids, and ordered the Christmas bombing of Hanoi in December 1972.
  • The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973, and the last US combat troops left Vietnam by late March 1973; the fall of Saigon in 1975 is outside this subsection.

From massive retaliation to flexible response

  • Eisenhower's Secretary of State John Foster Dulles relied on massive retaliation (1954): the threat of nuclear attack to deter communist aggression, which was cheaper than large conventional forces.
  • Eisenhower is said to have signalled through intermediaries in 1953 that he might use nuclear weapons if the Korean talks failed, though historians dispute how far this forced the armistice.
  • Kennedy replaced massive retaliation with flexible response, building up conventional and special forces (such as the Green Berets) so the United States could fight limited wars.
  • After the missile crisis Kennedy and Khrushchev signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty (August 1963), banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.

Key personality: Harry Truman (foreign policy and overall evaluation)

Truman's domestic record is covered in the notes on US politics. This block covers his foreign policy and how he is judged.

  • At the Potsdam conference of July and August 1945 Truman met Stalin for the first time, and he authorised the atomic bombings shortly afterwards.
  • The Truman Doctrine speech to Congress (12 March 1947) set out the principle that the United States would support free peoples against communist pressure.
  • He recognised the new state of Israel on 14 May 1948, within minutes of its declaration of independence, against the advice of parts of his State Department.
  • He decided in June 1950 to send American forces to defend South Korea without a congressional vote, a decision that set the pattern for later presidential war-making.
  • He was a direct participant in every decision: he chose the airlift rather than an armed convoy to Berlin (1948) and accepted a limited war in Korea rather than MacArthur's wider war with China.
  • A sign on his desk read "The buck stops here", summing up his view that the President must take final responsibility.
  • Contemporaries were divided: Republicans attacked him over China and the spy scandals of the period, and by 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply.
  • Revisionist historians from the 1960s, such as William Appleman Williams, argued that US economic expansion and the atomic bomb helped cause the Cold War; post-revisionists such as John Lewis Gaddis stressed Stalin's own responsibility and argued that containment worked in Europe.

Assessment: how successful was US foreign policy to 1972?

  • Success in Western Europe: the Marshall Plan, NATO and the Berlin airlift contained Soviet power in Europe and helped to create a prosperous Western alliance.
  • Failure in Asia: the Korean War ended in a stalemate after about three years, with heavy American losses and no victory.
  • Korea can also be judged a limited success: South Korea survived, and Truman kept the war from spreading to China or becoming nuclear.
  • The Cuban missile crisis avoided nuclear war, but the Bay of Pigs was a clear failure and the Soviet missiles were removed only in exchange for a secret US concession.
  • Vietnam showed the limits of containment: a commitment that began with advisers had escalated to over half a million troops by 1968.
  • Supporters argue that US commitments in Asia deterred further Soviet and Chinese expansion, while critics say they drew the United States into costly wars in places of little strategic importance.
  • Contemporaries and later historians differ over whether the Cold War could have been managed by diplomacy earlier, and the debate shapes judgements of Truman, Kennedy and Johnson.

Key terms

Internationalism
The policy of active US involvement in world affairs after 1945, in place of the pre-war isolation of the United States.
Imperialism
The domination of one country over others through political, military or economic control, a term applied to US influence in Cuba and to Soviet control of Eastern Europe.
Containment
The US policy, set out by George Kennan, of checking Soviet expansion by firm resistance wherever it threatened to spread.
Truman Doctrine
The 1947 commitment to give US aid to free peoples resisting communist pressure, first applied to Greece and Turkey.
Marshall Plan
The European Recovery Program of 1948 to 1952, which gave about 13 billion dollars in aid to Western Europe to rebuild its economies.
NATO
The North Atlantic Treaty Organisation, founded in April 1949 as a military alliance in which an attack on one member is an attack on all.
Berlin airlift
The Western airlift of supplies into West Berlin from June 1948 to May 1949, which defeated the Soviet blockade of the city.
Missile crisis
The October 1962 confrontation over Soviet missiles in Cuba, ended when the Soviet Union removed them in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
Geneva Accords
The July 1954 agreements that ended the French war in Indochina and divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel.
Domino theory
Eisenhower's 1954 argument that if one Southeast Asian country fell to communism its neighbours would follow, used to justify US involvement in Vietnam.
Vietnamisation
Nixon's policy from 1969 of withdrawing US troops while training and equipping the South Vietnamese army to fight the war itself.
Flexible response
Kennedy's defence strategy of building conventional and special forces so that the United States could answer communist moves without immediately resorting to nuclear weapons.
Massive retaliation
Eisenhower and Dulles's 1954 strategy of deterring communist aggression by the threat of overwhelming nuclear response rather than large conventional forces.
NSC-68
The April 1950 National Security Council paper that called for a large build-up of American military strength to contain the Soviet Union.
