Segregation and the legal challenge, 1896 to 1957

Segregation was enforced by state law across the South and by custom in much of the North, and the courts were the first route to change.

  • Jim Crow in the South meant separate schools, buses, waiting rooms and water fountains, and black voters were kept off the rolls by poll taxes, literacy tests and intimidation by the Ku Klux Klan and local officials; in the North discrimination in housing and jobs was enforced by custom and by restrictive covenants.
  • Plessy v Ferguson (1896) upheld segregation under the doctrine of separate but equal facilities, which in practice meant inferior schools, transport and public services for black Americans.
  • The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) led the legal campaign against segregation through cases on education and voting in the 1930s and 1940s.
  • Brown v Board of Education, decided unanimously on 17 May 1954, ruled that segregated public schools were unconstitutional, and Brown II in 1955 ordered desegregation with all deliberate speed.
  • Southern states responded with the Southern Manifesto of March 1956, signed by most southern members of Congress, which pledged to resist desegregation by all lawful means.
  • In September 1957 nine black students entered Little Rock Central High School under the protection of federal troops, sent by Eisenhower after Governor Orval Faubus used the National Guard to block them.
  • The brutal murder of the fourteen-year-old Emmett Till in Mississippi in August 1955, and the acquittal of the white men accused of it, shocked black Americans and made him a symbol of the cause.
  • The Civil Rights Act of 1957, the first federal civil rights law since Reconstruction, set up a Civil Rights Commission and a Civil Rights Division in the Justice Department, but it did little for voting in practice.

Direct action: sit-ins, Freedom Rides and Birmingham, 1960 to 1963

From 1960 students and activists turned to non-violent direct action, which exposed segregation to national and international audiences.

  • Four students from North Carolina A and T College sat in at a Woolworth lunch counter in Greensboro on 1 February 1960, and sit-ins spread to more than fifty cities within months.
  • The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) was formed in April 1960 to coordinate student protest across the South.
  • The Freedom Rides of May 1961, organised by the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE), tested the desegregation of interstate bus terminals, and a bus was burned by a mob near Anniston, Alabama.
  • The Birmingham campaign of April and May 1963 was led by King and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, and the police commissioner Eugene Bull Connor used dogs and fire hoses against demonstrators.
  • Photographs and television footage of the Birmingham violence shocked national opinion and helped to build support for federal civil rights legislation.
  • The March on Washington on 28 August 1963 drew about a quarter of a million people, and King's I Have a Dream speech became the defining statement of the movement.
  • The Albany Movement in Georgia in 1961 to 1962, led by King, failed because the police chief avoided violence and arrested protesters quietly, a lesson that shaped the choice of Birmingham.
  • In Birmingham in May 1963 hundreds of schoolchildren marched and were arrested, the so-called Children's Crusade, and the city agreed to desegregate its shops in May after bombings and riots.
  • In June 1963 Governor George Wallace stood in the door at the University of Alabama to block black students, and the NAACP leader Medgar Evers was murdered in Mississippi.
  • President Kennedy called civil rights a moral issue in a television address on 11 June 1963 and sent a civil rights bill to Congress, and he supported the March on Washington despite fears of violence.

The civil rights laws of 1964 and 1965

Johnson's administration secured the two main civil rights laws of the period, which used federal power to end legal segregation and to protect voting rights.

  • The Civil Rights Act of 2 July 1964 banned racial segregation in public accommodations such as hotels, restaurants and theatres, and it created federal power to end segregation in schools.
  • The Voting Rights Act of 6 August 1965 banned literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register black voters in areas with a record of discrimination.
  • The march from Selma to Montgomery in March 1965 followed the attack on marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge on 7 March 1965, known as Bloody Sunday.
  • Within a few years of the Voting Rights Act, the number of black registered voters in the South rose sharply, and black elected officials began to take office in the region.
  • The Civil Rights Act passed after a Senate filibuster by southern senators that lasted 54 days, ended in June 1964 by a vote for cloture.
  • The Act also created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, and it allowed federal funds to be cut from segregated programmes.
  • Freedom Summer in Mississippi in 1964, when volunteers registered voters, saw the murder of three civil rights workers, Chaney, Goodman and Schwerner, in June 1964.
  • King led the Selma campaign in early 1965 to dramatise the denial of the vote in Alabama, and Johnson called for the Voting Rights Act in a speech to Congress on 15 March 1965 in which he used the movement's words We shall overcome.

Key personality: Martin Luther King Jr. (overall career)

King was a participant and leader of the civil rights movement. His role in the Montgomery bus boycott is covered in the notes on that case study.

  • Martin Luther King Jr. was born in Atlanta, Georgia, on 15 January 1929, the son of a Baptist minister, and he was ordained as a Baptist minister himself.
  • He earned a doctorate in theology at Boston University in 1955, and his study of Gandhi shaped his commitment to non-violent direct action.
  • He was a founder and first president of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, formed in 1957 to coordinate church-based civil rights protest across the South.
  • His Letter from Birmingham Jail of April 1963 argued for civil disobedience against unjust laws, and it was written in response to white Alabama clergy who had called the protests untimely.
  • He was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1964, at the age of 35, the youngest recipient up to that time.
  • King campaigned in Chicago in 1966 against housing discrimination, a campaign that met fierce resistance from white residents and produced little formal change.
  • In 1967 he linked the civil rights struggle with poverty and the Vietnam War, and his Poor People's Campaign was planned to bring poor Americans of all races to Washington in 1968.
  • He was assassinated at the Lorraine Motel in Memphis, Tennessee, on 4 April 1968; James Earl Ray later pleaded guilty to the murder.
  • Contemporaries were divided: many whites in the South opposed him, and militant activists such as Malcolm X and later Black Power leaders criticised him as too moderate.
  • The FBI conducted surveillance of King and attempted to discredit him through its COINTELPRO programme, a fact revealed after the 1970s.
  • Historians now regard him as the most important leader of the civil rights movement, and Congress established a national holiday in his honour, first observed in 1986.
  • King's philosophy of non-violence combined Christian teaching on love with Gandhi's methods, and he argued that the movement should win over white opinion by accepting suffering rather than returning violence.
  • His main successes were Montgomery (1955 to 1956), Birmingham (1963) and Selma (1965); the Albany Movement (1961 to 1962) and Chicago (1966) were his main setbacks.
  • In his last years he turned to economic issues and to criticism of the Vietnam War, which lost him the support of Johnson and of some civil rights leaders, and in Memphis he was supporting a strike by black sanitation workers when he was killed.

Black Power, Malcolm X and the urban riots

From 1965 frustration with slow progress produced a more radical black politics and a wave of violence in the cities.

  • Malcolm X, who had left the Nation of Islam in 1964, was assassinated in the Audubon Ballroom in Harlem on 21 February 1965.
  • The slogan Black Power, popularised by Stokely Carmichael in 1966, expressed a demand for black self-determination and community control.
  • The Watts riot in Los Angeles in August 1965 left about 34 people dead, and the Detroit riot of July 1967 caused more than forty deaths.
  • The Kerner Commission, appointed after the 1967 riots, reported in February 1968 that the United States was moving towards two societies, one black and one white, separate and unequal.
  • The Fair Housing Act of April 1968, passed days after King's death, banned discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing.
  • Malcolm X, a minister of the Nation of Islam, criticised integration and non-violence in the early 1960s, but after leaving the Nation and a pilgrimage to Mecca in 1964 he adopted a more inclusive position, and his Autobiography (1965) made him a hero to many young black people.
  • The Black Panther Party for Self-Defense was founded in Oakland, California, in October 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale, and it combined armed self-defence with community programmes such as free breakfasts for children.
  • The Watts riot began on 11 August 1965 after a police traffic stop, with the National Guard called in and large areas burned; Detroit in July 1967 required federal paratroops, and Newark that month had 26 deaths.
  • The Kerner Report blamed white racism, poverty and police behaviour for the riots, and Johnson did not act on its recommendations for more spending.

Urban poverty and the War on Poverty

Poverty in the inner cities became a national concern in the 1960s, and Johnson's War on Poverty was the federal response.

  • Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) estimated that tens of millions of Americans lived in poverty and were largely invisible to the affluent majority.
  • Johnson declared an unconditional War on Poverty in January 1964, and the Economic Opportunity Act of that year created the Job Corps and community action programmes.
  • The War on Poverty's programmes were underfunded relative to the war in Vietnam, and many were cut back as the 1960s ended.
  • Poverty rates among black Americans remained far higher than among whites through the 1960s and 1970s, reflecting unemployment, poor schooling and housing segregation.
  • The official poverty rate fell from about 22 per cent in 1959 to about 12 per cent in 1969, partly because of economic growth and partly because of Social Security and programmes such as food stamps and Medicaid.
  • Daniel Patrick Moynihan's report The Negro Family (1965) blamed family breakdown for black poverty and aroused controversy.
  • The Office of Economic Opportunity ran Head Start and the Community Action Programs, which required the maximum feasible participation of the poor and brought conflict with city governments, so mayors lobbied against them.

Drugs, crime and the limits of racial equality, to 1989

Rising crime and the drug trade were given political attention from the 1970s, and their effects fell heavily on poor black communities.

  • Richard Nixon declared drug abuse public enemy number one in 1971 and launched a war on drugs, funding enforcement and treatment.
  • Crack cocaine spread through poor urban neighbourhoods in the mid-1980s, producing a wave of violence and drug-related crime.
  • The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 set mandatory minimum sentences that treated one gram of crack the same as one hundred grams of powder cocaine, a disparity that fell heavily on black defendants.
  • Ronald Reagan's Just Say No campaign, launched in 1982 with his wife Nancy, promoted drug prevention and was linked to tougher enforcement policies.
  • Harold Washington became the first black mayor of Chicago in 1983, and Jesse Jackson ran for the Democratic presidential nomination in 1984 and 1988, reflecting new black political power.
  • By 1989 black Americans had gained political office and legal protection, but segregation in housing and schools, and high rates of poverty and imprisonment, remained major problems.
  • Crime and urban disorder were used by Nixon in 1968 and by later Republican candidates as a political issue under the slogan of law and order.
  • Heroin addiction was concentrated in inner cities such as Harlem in the 1960s, and heroin use among US soldiers in Vietnam drew public attention in 1971.
  • The prison population rose steeply in the 1980s, and black men were imprisoned at several times the rate of white men.
  • Affirmative action in college admissions was limited, but not ended, by the Supreme Court decision in Regents of the University of California v. Bakke in 1978, and the busing of children to achieve school integration provoked riots in Boston in 1974.

Assessment: how far was racial equality achieved?

Examiners reward judgements that weigh the legal achievements of the movement against the persistence of inequality.

  • Achievements: the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act and the Fair Housing Act ended legal segregation and gave black Americans the vote on equal terms in practice.
  • Limits: economic inequality, residential segregation and the failure of schools in poor areas meant that legal equality did not bring equal opportunity.
  • Contemporary white opinion was divided, with many northern whites supporting civil rights laws but resisting busing and open housing in their own neighbourhoods.
  • Later historians differ: some see the period as a great success that was later undermined, while others argue that its gains were narrower and less lasting than they appeared.

Key terms

Discrimination
The unequal treatment of people because of their race, sex or other characteristic, which the civil rights laws of the period were designed to end.
Liberalism
The belief in an active federal government that uses its powers to secure civil rights and social welfare, the basis of Johnson's civil rights and poverty programmes.
Jim Crow
The system of state and local laws that enforced racial segregation in the South from the late nineteenth century until the 1960s.
Black Power
A movement from the mid-1960s demanding black self-determination and community control, popularised by Stokely Carmichael in 1966.
War on Poverty
Johnson's federal programmes from 1964, including the Job Corps and community action, aimed at reducing poverty.
Kerner Report
The February 1968 report of the Kerner Commission, which found that US society was moving towards two societies, one black and one white.
Civil rights movement
The campaign from the 1950s to the 1960s by black Americans and their allies to end legal segregation and secure voting and civil rights.
Non-violent direct action
The civil rights strategy of protest through sit-ins, marches and boycotts that deliberately accepted arrest and violence without retaliation.
Southern Christian Leadership Conference
The church-based civil rights organisation founded in 1957 with King as president, which led the Birmingham and Selma campaigns.
Freedom Rides
The 1961 bus journeys by black and white activists, organised by CORE, to test the desegregation of interstate bus terminals in the South.
