The wartime base of the boom

The Second World War left the United States with the strongest industrial economy in the world, and wartime production and savings provided the platform for the postwar boom.

  • US factories produced vast quantities of aircraft, ships, tanks and munitions between 1941 and 1945, and the economy expanded rapidly, with unemployment falling to very low levels.
  • The United States produced roughly half of the world's manufactured goods in 1945, while its European and Japanese rivals had been devastated by the war.
  • Wartime wage and price controls, and the savings that Americans built up during the war, left households with large sums to spend once rationing and controls ended.
  • Wartime shortages of consumer goods gave way to a pent-up demand for cars, houses and household appliances after 1945.
  • The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, known as the GI Bill, paid tuition and living costs for veterans and offered government-guaranteed loans for homes and businesses.
  • The GI Bill's home loans helped to create a mass market for housing, which fed demand for consumer goods and for the construction industry.
  • The wartime government became the largest customer in the economy, and it built factories that private firms bought cheaply after 1945, in industries such as aluminium, synthetic rubber and aircraft.

International financing: Bretton Woods and Marshall Aid

The United States designed the postwar international monetary order, which made the dollar the central currency and financed recovery abroad.

  • The Bretton Woods conference of July 1944 in New Hampshire created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank to support exchange rates and reconstruction.
  • Under the Bretton Woods system the US dollar was convertible to gold at 35 dollars an ounce, and other currencies were pegged to the dollar.
  • The Marshall Plan of 1948 to 1952 gave about 13 billion dollars of US aid to Western Europe, much of it spent on American goods, which boosted US exports.
  • The dollar's role as the reserve currency, and the shortage of dollars in war-damaged Europe (the dollar gap), meant that Marshall aid and American loans were spent on US goods, and American companies could invest abroad cheaply.
  • The Korean War and NSC-68 raised the federal defence budget from about 14 billion dollars in 1950 to about 50 billion dollars by 1953, which fed the economy's growth.
  • The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) of 1947 committed the United States and other states to reduce trade barriers, which suited the world's biggest exporter.

Public investment: highways, defence and research

Government spending on infrastructure, defence and science drove growth in several key industries during the 1950s and 1960s.

  • The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 funded an Interstate Highway System of about 41,000 miles, which opened up suburban development and boosted car production and steel.
  • Federal defence spending remained high through the 1950s and 1960s and supplied a large market for aircraft, electronics and missile manufacturers.
  • The National Science Foundation was created in 1950 and federal research funding grew steadily, with universities acting as partners to government research.
  • The space programme, created after the launch of Sputnik in 1957 and the founding of NASA in 1958, employed thousands of engineers and contractors in the 1960s.
  • Federal support for education was expanded through the National Defense Education Act of 1958, which funded science and language teaching after Sputnik.
  • The Interstate programme was paid for by a Highway Trust Fund financed by a petrol tax, and it was justified partly on defence grounds, since Eisenhower had seen the German autobahns.
  • Defence research funded the development of jet aircraft, radar, nuclear power for submarines and later computers, which spread into civilian industry.

The military-industrial complex

Defence spending created a network of contractors, military officers and politicians with shared interests, a development that Eisenhower warned of in 1961.

  • Eisenhower used the phrase military-industrial complex in his farewell address of 17 January 1961, warning of the acquisition of unwarranted influence by a combination of the armed forces and the defence industry.
  • Major defence contractors such as Lockheed, Boeing, General Dynamics and Douglas received large government contracts, and many companies depended on them for much of their revenue.
  • The warning came from a former general whose own administration had built up the nuclear arsenal and relied on it, a point critics raised about the speech.
  • Defence spending and the Cold War gave high-technology industries, including electronics and computing, a stable source of contracts that encouraged the growth of Silicon Valley.
  • C. Wright Mills's The Power Elite (1956) had argued that a small group of corporate, military and political leaders made the major decisions, an idea that Eisenhower's speech echoed in warning form.

Key personality: the Organization Man (William H. Whyte's type of corporate employee)

The Organization Man is not a single person. It is the type of corporate employee that William H. Whyte described in his 1956 book, The Organization Man.

  • William H. Whyte, an editor at Fortune magazine, published The Organization Man in 1956 as a study of white-collar workers in large corporations and suburban communities.
  • Whyte argued that the corporate employee had accepted a social ethic that valued belonging, teamwork and conformity over individualism and personal conscience.
  • Whyte described a social ethic with three beliefs: that the group is the source of creativity, that belonging is a person's greatest need, and that science can be applied to achieve belonging.
  • He studied the suburb of Park Forest, Illinois, where young corporate families moved often and valued being sociable, and he criticised the personality tests that companies used in hiring.
  • The same worry about conformity appeared in David Riesman's The Lonely Crowd (1950), which described the other-directed person, and in C. Wright Mills's White Collar (1951), which described anxious salaried workers.
  • The Organization Man was a salaried manager or professional, often moving between cities and suburbs as the company required, whose career depended on loyalty to the corporation and on fitting in with its expectations.
  • Sloan Wilson's novel The Man in the Gray Flannel Suit (1955) portrayed a similar figure, a corporate executive torn between career success and family life.
  • Contemporaries praised the corporation for stability and rising living standards, but many critics saw the Organization Man as a threat to individual creativity and independence.
  • Historians have used the figure to explain the conformity of the 1950s, although later scholars have argued that the book overstated the conformity of American corporate life.

The multinational corporation abroad

From the 1950s American companies expanded overseas, creating multinational corporations that linked the US economy to the rest of the world.

  • General Motors, IBM, Coca-Cola and the International Telephone and Telegraph Corporation (ITT) were among the American multinationals that established large overseas operations by the 1960s.
  • IBM's World Trade Corporation, created in 1949, managed the company's overseas operations, and by the 1960s a large share of IBM's revenue came from outside the United States.
  • Coca-Cola's bottling networks spread across Europe, Latin America and Asia from the 1950s, using local franchises to supply the local market.
  • US multinationals invested heavily in Western Europe after the Treaty of Rome of 1957 created the European Economic Community, which offered a large single market.
  • The expansion of the multinationals was supported by US foreign policy, which encouraged open trade and investment as part of containment.
  • Critics in Europe, such as the French writer Jean-Jacques Servan-Schreiber in The American Challenge (1967), argued that US companies were gaining control of key European industries.
  • General Motors owned Opel in Germany and Vauxhall in Britain, and Ford had large European subsidiaries, so American carmakers built and sold cars across Europe.
  • ITT under Harold Geneen became a conglomerate in the 1960s, buying firms in many industries and countries, and it later became notorious for its involvement in Chile.
  • The Fortune 500 list of the largest American industrial companies was first published in 1955, and it showed how far a few big firms dominated the economy.

Organised labour and the postwar bargain

Trade unions were strong in the core industries of the boom, and their agreements with large firms helped to share its gains.

  • Union membership reached a peak of roughly a third of non-farm workers in the mid-1950s, concentrated in steel, car manufacturing, mining and transport.
  • The United Auto Workers and General Motors signed a five-year contract in 1950, often called the Treaty of Detroit, which tied wage rises to productivity and provided pensions and health insurance.
  • Such agreements gave many manual workers rising real wages and benefits, and they became a model for other unions in the core industries.
  • The Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 restricted union tactics such as secondary boycotts, and it required union leaders to sign affidavits disavowing communism until 1959.
  • The merger of the American Federation of Labor and the Congress of Industrial Organizations in 1955 created a single labour federation led by George Meany.
  • Unions were weakest in the South and among service and farm workers, who were often excluded from the postwar bargain.

Keynesian economic policy: the 1964 tax cut

Government economists believed that spending and tax policy could sustain growth, and the 1964 tax cut was a major test of that belief.

  • John Maynard Keynes's ideas, that governments could manage demand through spending and taxation, were accepted by the economic advisers of Kennedy and Johnson.
  • Walter Heller, chairman of the Council of Economic Advisers under Kennedy, argued for a tax cut to stimulate demand and growth.
  • The Revenue Act of 1964 cut income tax rates across the board and reduced the top rate from 91 per cent to 70 per cent.
  • The tax cut was followed by strong economic growth through the mid-1960s, and it is often cited as a successful example of demand management before the costs of the Vietnam War appeared.
  • Critics such as Milton Friedman, a monetarist, argued that governments could not fine-tune the economy, a view that gained ground in the 1970s when demand management failed to cope with stagflation.

Assessment: achievements and limits of the boom

Examiners reward evaluation that weighs the scale of the boom against its dependence on US dominance and on defence spending.

  • Achievements: a sustained rise in living standards, widespread home ownership and a large middle class were features of the period from 1945 to 1968.
  • Limits: the boom depended partly on the temporary dominance of the US economy after 1945, which was bound to decline as Europe and Japan recovered.
  • Critics argue that the boom was financed by defence spending and by the large public investment that followed the war, rather than by the free market.
  • Some groups did not share fully in the boom, and black Americans and many southern workers faced discrimination in jobs, housing and access to the GI Bill benefits.
  • John Kenneth Galbraith's The Affluent Society (1958) argued that the boom created private wealth but left public services such as schools and parks neglected.
  • Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) showed that tens of millions still lived in poverty despite the boom.

Key terms

Corporate capitalism
An economic system dominated by large corporations, with managers and salaried employees, which expanded rapidly in the United States after 1945.
Globalisation
The growing integration of national economies through trade, investment and the activities of multinational companies, which accelerated after 1945.
Military-industrial complex
The network of defence contractors, military officers and politicians whose shared interests promoted high defence spending, a phrase used by Eisenhower in 1961.
Multinational corporation
A company that owns or controls production in several countries, such as General Motors, IBM and Coca-Cola in this period.
Bretton Woods system
The postwar monetary order agreed in 1944, which made the dollar the central currency and tied other currencies to it, with the dollar convertible to gold.
GI Bill
The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, which gave veterans education and training benefits, home and business loans and unemployment pay.
Organization Man
The type of corporate employee described by William H. Whyte in his 1956 book, who accepted corporate loyalty and conformity as a way of life.
Interstate Highway System
The network of federally funded motorways authorised by the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, which reshaped American cities, suburbs and the car industry.
Treaty of Detroit
The 1950 five-year agreement between General Motors and the United Auto Workers that exchanged union acceptance of management control for rising wages, pensions and health insurance.
