The baby boom and population shifts

Rising birth rates after the Second World War, and migration to the suburbs and the Sun Belt, reshaped American society and its demand for goods and services.

  • The baby boom ran from about 1946 to 1964, with the birth rate peaking in 1957 at about 4.3 million births in a single year.
  • Families were larger and married earlier than in the 1930s, and the average age of marriage for women fell during the 1950s.
  • Population moved from the northeastern cities to the suburbs, and many urban areas lost white middle-class residents to the outer rings of the metropolitan regions.
  • The Sun Belt, the states of the South and the Southwest from Florida to California, grew rapidly after 1945, helped by air conditioning and federal defence spending.
  • Between 1950 and 1970 the cities of the Northeast and Midwest lost population while the suburbs of the West and South expanded quickly.
  • Dr Benjamin Spock's The Common Sense Book of Baby and Child Care (1946) sold tens of millions of copies and shaped how the boom generation was raised.
  • The 1950 and 1960 censuses showed that the suburbs were the fastest-growing part of the country, and by 1960 more Americans lived in suburbs than in the central cities or on farms.
  • Black Americans continued the Great Migration from the South to northern and western cities such as Chicago, Detroit and Los Angeles, while white families moved out to the suburbs, a pattern called white flight.
  • California overtook New York as the most populous state in the early 1960s, a measure of the Sun Belt's growth.

Suburbia and the mass market for housing

The mass production of houses on new suburban estates, financed by federal mortgage guarantees, became the physical image of the American Dream in the 1950s.

  • William Levitt's firm built Levittown on Long Island, New York, beginning in 1947, with about 17,000 standardised houses on mass-produced lines.
  • Federal Housing Administration and Veterans Administration mortgage guarantees allowed buyers to borrow with little or no deposit on long repayment terms.
  • Most of the new suburban houses were sold to white families; restrictive covenants and lending practices kept black families out of many new estates.
  • The suburbs were built around the car, with new highways and shopping centres replacing the public transport and downtown retail of the older cities.
  • Television sets were in most American homes by 1960, and advertising on television promoted the goods that suburban households were expected to buy.
  • Levitt built houses on a production line, with teams specialising in each stage, and the first Levittown houses were small Cape Cod style homes sold for under 8,000 dollars with a washing machine and a television set included in some models.
  • The Levittown leases and covenants excluded black buyers, and an ordinary suburban estate was in effect closed to black families.
  • Nixon and Khrushchev's Kitchen Debate in Moscow in July 1959 was staged in a model American house, where Nixon argued that suburban abundance showed the superiority of capitalism.

Consumerism, credit and advertising

Mass production, consumer credit and advertising made the purchase of goods a central part of American life and identity.

  • Diners Club, launched in 1950, was among the first charge cards in the United States, and BankAmericard, launched in 1958, became the forerunner of Visa.
  • Credit allowed families to buy cars, appliances and furniture on instalments, so that spending was no longer restricted to current income.
  • Advertising agencies used television, magazines and market research to create demand for new products, and brand loyalty became a central marketing aim.
  • The economist John Kenneth Galbraith described the affluent society in his 1958 book of that title, arguing that consumers were persuaded to want goods they did not need.
  • Shopping malls, supermarkets and department stores spread through the suburbs, and consumer goods such as dishwashers, freezers and automatic washing machines became common in middle-class homes.
  • Television, which was in about 9 per cent of homes in 1950 and about 87 per cent in 1960, brought advertising into the living room, and sponsors paid for programmes such as I Love Lucy.
  • Vance Packard's The Hidden Persuaders (1957) accused advertisers of using psychological methods to manipulate buyers.
  • Disneyland opened in California in July 1955, and the leisure industry of theme parks, motels and package holidays grew with the car.

Leisure and the role of work

Shorter working hours, paid vacations and rising incomes gave Americans more leisure, but work in large organisations increasingly shaped their identity.

  • The standard working week remained about forty hours, while paid holidays became more widespread in the 1950s and 1960s.
  • Leisure centred on television, the car, the family holiday, and home ownership, with the suburban backyard a key place of recreation.
  • The number of white-collar jobs in management, sales and services grew faster than manufacturing jobs from the 1950s onwards.
  • Corporate culture, with its emphasis on teamwork and loyalty, shaped the working lives of many middle-class men, as William H. Whyte's The Organization Man described.
  • Many men's working lives centred on the family breadwinner role, which the suburban culture of the 1950s celebrated.

Women, work and the family

The 1950s promoted domesticity for women, but rising employment and feminist protest challenged that ideal from the early 1960s.

  • Many women left war-time industrial jobs after 1945 and were encouraged to return to the home, a message promoted in magazines, films and advice literature.
  • From the 1950s women's share of the paid workforce rose steadily, as married women with children increasingly took jobs outside the home.
  • The contraceptive pill, Enovid, was approved by the Food and Drug Administration in May 1960, giving women greater control over the timing of births.
  • The Equal Pay Act of 10 June 1963 required equal pay for equal work between men and women in the same establishment.
  • Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 banned discrimination in employment on grounds of sex as well as race, colour, religion and national origin.
  • The Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974 made it illegal to refuse credit to a woman on the basis of her sex or marital status.
  • Divorce rates rose in the 1960s and 1970s, and the number of single-parent families grew, changing the ideal of the traditional nuclear family.
  • Women were about 30 per cent of the labour force in 1950 and about 38 per cent by 1970, but they were concentrated in clerical, teaching, nursing and sales jobs at lower pay.
  • The Supreme Court decision in Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) struck down a ban on contraception for married couples, and Roe v. Wade (22 January 1973) ruled that the Constitution protected a woman's decision to have an abortion, in the early stages of pregnancy.
  • Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 banned sex discrimination in federally funded education, opening college admissions and sport to women.
  • Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA campaign, begun in 1972, mobilised conservative women who argued that the amendment threatened the family, and it helped to block ratification.

Key personality: Betty Friedan

Friedan was a participant in the feminist movement and a critic of the domestic ideal.

  • Betty Friedan was born in 1921 in Peoria, Illinois, and studied psychology at Smith College before becoming a magazine writer and suburban housewife.
  • Her survey of former Smith College classmates about their lives in the 1950s formed the basis of her book The Feminine Mystique, published in 1963.
  • The Feminine Mystique argued that American women were trapped by a domestic ideal (the feminine mystique) that denied them fulfilment, and she described their condition as the problem that has no name.
  • The book sold very widely and is widely credited with helping to start the second wave of the American feminist movement.
  • In June 1966 Friedan was a founder of the National Organization for Women (NOW) and became its first president, aiming to secure equal rights for women in employment and law.
  • NOW pressed for the Equal Rights Amendment, which passed Congress in March 1972 but fell short of ratification by the 1982 deadline, with 35 of the 38 states needed.
  • Contemporaries were divided: supporters admired her as a campaigner for women's rights, while critics attacked her as too middle-class and too concerned with professional women.
  • Historians have generally judged her a central figure in the feminist movement, although later writers have criticised The Feminine Mystique for focusing on white, middle-class women.
  • Friedan organised the Women's Strike for Equality on 26 August 1970, the fiftieth anniversary of the Nineteenth Amendment, and helped to found the National Women's Political Caucus in 1971.
  • Younger radical feminists criticised her as too moderate, and she was also criticised for her early hostility towards lesbians in the movement.

Key personality: Marilyn Monroe as an image of women

Monroe's screen image represented ideals of female beauty and desirability in the 1950s, a role that critics and later historians have examined. Her career, death and legacy are covered in the notes on culture and the collapse of consensus.

  • Born Norma Jeane Mortenson in Los Angeles in 1926, she became Marilyn Monroe after a modelling career and a first contract with Twentieth Century-Fox in 1946, which was dropped and renewed in 1951 as her roles grew.
  • Her films, including Gentlemen Prefer Blondes (1953) and The Seven Year Itch (1955), presented an ideal of glamorous, sexually attractive womanhood that was widely copied in advertising.
  • Monroe's image was closely tied to the consumer culture of the 1950s, appearing in advertisements and magazines that sold products by associating them with her appeal.
  • Contemporaries admired her beauty and screen presence, but she was also criticised by the press for the way Hollywood marketed her as a sex symbol rather than as a serious actress.
  • Later feminist critics read her image as a product of a male-dominated film industry, which limited women's roles to decorative or passive parts.
  • Historians now treat her as a symbol of both the glamour and the constraints placed on women in the 1950s and early 1960s.
  • Monroe tried to take control of her career by founding Marilyn Monroe Productions in 1955 and by studying at the Actors Studio in New York, and she won acclaim for her comic role in Some Like It Hot (1959).
  • Her public image of a childlike, available blonde fitted the 1950s ideal of femininity that Friedan attacked, but Monroe herself fought Hollywood's control and the typecasting it imposed.

Assessment: did affluence deliver the American Dream?

Examiners reward judgements that weigh the material gains of the period against the exclusion of many groups from the American Dream.

  • Achievements: home ownership, rising incomes and the spread of consumer goods gave many white middle-class families a standard of living that earlier generations had not known.
  • Limits: federal mortgage policy and private lending excluded most black families from the suburbs, so the Dream was unevenly shared by race.
  • Women's employment grew strongly, but women were often confined to lower-paid jobs and had limited access to credit until the 1974 Act.
  • Friedan's critique showed that many women felt the ideal of domestic contentment failed them, which later critics argued was a sign of the Dream's narrowness rather than its success.

Key terms

Consumerism
The culture that encouraged the continual purchase of goods, promoted through advertising and credit, and which became central to American life after 1945.
Feminism
The movement for equal rights for women, which revived in the 1960s and campaigned for equal pay, employment protection and access to contraception and abortion.
The Feminine Mystique
Betty Friedan's 1963 book arguing that American women were trapped by a domestic ideal that denied them fulfilment.
National Organization for Women
The organisation founded in June 1966 by Betty Friedan and others to campaign for equal rights for women.
Levittown
The mass-produced suburban development on Long Island, New York, begun in 1947, which became a symbol of suburban life.
Sun Belt
The southern and south-western states, from Florida to California, which grew rapidly in population and industry after 1945.
Baby boom
The rise in the birth rate after the Second World War, lasting from about 1946 to 1964.
American Dream
The belief that hard work and opportunity would bring home ownership, prosperity and upward mobility to every family.
White flight
The movement of white families from central cities to the suburbs, which left many cities poorer and more racially segregated.
Roe v. Wade
The Supreme Court decision of 22 January 1973 that held that the Constitution protected a woman's right to choose an abortion in the early stages of pregnancy.
