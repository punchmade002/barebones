Guns and butter: the war and the deficit, 1965 to 1968

Spending on the Vietnam War and on the Great Society together pushed up federal deficits and inflation, creating the economic problems of the 1970s.

  • Federal spending on the Vietnam War rose sharply after 1965, and Johnson chose not to raise taxes to pay for it while also expanding domestic programmes.
  • Inflation rose from the mid-1960s as demand increased faster than supply, and consumer prices began to climb steadily in the late 1960s.
  • Congress passed a 10 per cent income tax surtax in June 1968 to restrain spending and reduce the deficit, but it did not stop inflation.
  • The federal budget deficit grew through the late 1960s and 1970s, and deficits became a persistent feature of US government finance.
  • The Vietnam War cost the federal government well over 100 billion dollars in total, and Johnson's Great Society programmes were added to this spending at a time when the economy was already near full employment.
  • Because spending rose faster than output, inflation, which had been about 1 per cent in 1964, passed 4 per cent in 1968.
  • Johnson asked Congress for a tax increase only in 1967, and Congress, led by the Ways and Means chairman Wilbur Mills, made agreement conditional on spending cuts, which delayed the surtax until 1968.
  • The balance of payments also worsened, as US military spending abroad and foreign investment sent dollars overseas faster than exports earned them, and foreign governments began to exchange dollars for gold.

The end of Bretton Woods and the oil shocks, 1971 to 1979

The postwar monetary and energy order collapsed in the 1970s, leaving the US economy exposed to inflation and to rising oil prices.

  • On 15 August 1971 Nixon suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold and imposed a temporary wage and price freeze, ending the Bretton Woods system.
  • The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971 tried to fix exchange rates again, but it did not last, and currencies were allowed to float by 1973.
  • The Arab members of OPEC imposed an oil embargo on the United States in October 1973 after the Yom Kippur War, and oil prices roughly quadrupled.
  • Oil prices rose again after the Iranian Revolution of 1978 to 1979, causing a second shock that deepened inflation and recession.
  • Inflation reached above 13 per cent in 1980, and the combination of high inflation and stagnant growth became known as stagflation.
  • Nixon's measures of August 1971 also included a 10 per cent surcharge on imports, and 1971 was the first year since 1893 in which the United States imported more goods than it exported.
  • The oil embargo of 1973 to 1974 brought petrol queues at filling stations and rationing by odd and even number plates in some states, and it was lifted in March 1974.
  • The recession of 1973 to 1975 was the deepest since the 1930s, with unemployment reaching about 9 per cent in 1975.
  • Ford's Whip Inflation Now campaign of 1974 relied on voluntary measures and was widely mocked, and Carter's first attempts at stimulus did not stop inflation rising again in 1978 and 1979.

The rust belt and international competition

American manufacturing in steel and cars lost ground to Japanese and European rivals, and whole industrial regions suffered decline.

  • The steel industry lost ground to imports from Japan and Europe in the 1970s, and steel mills in the Midwest and northeast reduced production or closed.
  • The Youngstown Sheet and Tube mill in Ohio closed in September 1977, a closure that cost about 5,000 jobs and became a symbol of the wider loss of industrial jobs in the region.
  • Japanese car makers, including Toyota and Honda, exported smaller and more fuel-efficient cars to the United States, taking a growing share of the market from Detroit's manufacturers.
  • The Japanese car makers' gains led the Reagan administration to negotiate voluntary export restraints in 1981, limiting Japanese car imports.
  • The US trade balance moved into deficit in the 1970s, and the deficit with Japan grew steadily through the 1980s.
  • The Plaza Accord of September 1985, signed by the leading industrial countries, aimed to lower the dollar's value to restore the competitiveness of American exports.
  • Japan overtook the United States as the world's largest car producer in 1980, and the Chrysler Corporation was saved from bankruptcy in 1979 by federal loan guarantees of 1.5 billion dollars.
  • Foreign cars were attractive after the oil shocks because they were small and fuel-efficient, while Detroit built large cars, and imports captured about a quarter of the American car market by the early 1980s.
  • Rust belt cities such as Detroit, Cleveland and Youngstown lost population and tax revenue as factory jobs vanished, while the Sun Belt in the South and West gained.
  • The growth of service and high-technology jobs, in Silicon Valley and the defence industries of California, partly offset the losses in manufacturing.

Reaganomics: tax cuts, deregulation and the recession

Reagan's economic policy aimed to cut inflation and taxes, reduce regulation and rebuild defence, and it produced a severe recession before recovery.

  • Paul Volcker became chairman of the Federal Reserve in August 1979 and raised interest rates sharply to bring down inflation, which helped to cause the 1981 to 1982 recession.
  • The Economic Recovery Tax Act of August 1981 cut income tax rates by about 25 per cent over three years, reducing the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent.
  • The Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982 raised some taxes to reduce the deficit, which had grown as the tax cuts took effect.
  • Deregulation continued the trend begun under Carter, including the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 and the Staggers Rail Act of 1980, which reduced government control of transport prices.
  • Unemployment rose above 10 per cent in late 1982, the highest level since the Great Depression, before recovery began in 1983.
  • Economic growth returned strongly in the mid-1980s, and inflation fell sharply, though unemployment and the deficit remained issues for the rest of the decade.
  • Reaganomics rested on supply-side theory, associated with the economist Arthur Laffer, which said that lower tax rates would encourage work and investment and so raise growth.
  • The Tax Reform Act of 1986 cut the top income tax rate to 28 per cent while removing many deductions.
  • The Savings and Loan crisis followed deregulation of the thrifts in the early 1980s: hundreds of savings institutions failed, and the federal bailout of 1989 cost taxpayers tens of billions of dollars.
  • Federal spending on social programmes was cut in 1981 (food stamps and aid to families) while the Gramm-Rudman Act of 1985 tried to force deficit reduction by setting targets.

Deficits, debt and the 1987 crash

The combination of tax cuts and high defence spending produced large deficits, which were financed by borrowing from abroad.

  • Government spending on defence rose sharply under Reagan, and the federal budget deficit remained large in every year of his presidency.
  • The national debt nearly tripled during the 1980s, reaching almost 3 trillion dollars, and by the late 1980s the government's interest payments had become a major item in the budget.
  • In 1985 the United States became a net debtor nation, owing more to foreigners than it owned abroad, for the first time since the early twentieth century.
  • On 19 October 1987 the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell by about 22 per cent in a single day, a crash that prompted the Federal Reserve to add liquidity to the banking system.
  • The rise in foreign borrowing and the strong dollar of the early 1980s drew in large amounts of foreign capital, which financed the deficits but hurt exporters.
  • The trade deficit reached about 150 billion dollars in 1987, and the Plaza Accord of 1985 and the Louvre Accord of 1987 were attempts to bring the dollar down in an orderly way.

Energy policy after the oil shocks, 1973 to 1979

The oil crises made energy a central economic issue, and government responses ranged from conservation measures to deregulation of prices.

  • Congress passed a national maximum speed limit of 55 miles per hour in January 1974 as a measure to reduce petrol consumption.
  • The Department of Energy was created in August 1977 under Carter, bringing together federal energy programmes that had been spread across several agencies.
  • The National Energy Act of 1978 encouraged conservation and phased in higher natural gas prices, and it was a compromise reached after long debate in Congress.
  • Domestic oil production remained below its peak, and the United States depended on imports from the Middle East and other producers for a growing share of its oil by the late 1970s.
  • Nixon's Project Independence (1973) aimed to end dependence on foreign oil by 1980, but American oil imports kept rising.

Wages, incomes and inequality, 1973 to 1989

The rise in living standards that had marked the postwar boom slowed after the early 1970s, and the gains of the 1980s were unevenly shared.

  • Real average hourly earnings of production workers stagnated from the early 1970s, so that the growth in household incomes depended increasingly on more family members working.
  • The gap between high-income and low-income households widened during the 1980s, as the tax cuts of 1981 and 1986 favoured higher earners and wages for less-skilled workers fell in real terms.
  • The decline of manufacturing reduced the bargaining power of many manual workers, and the share of workers in unions, already falling since the 1950s, fell faster in the 1980s.
  • Women's entry into paid work (see the notes on affluence) raised household incomes, but the extra earnings often only offset the stagnation of male wages.
  • The poverty rate, which had fallen to about 11 per cent in 1973, rose to around 15 per cent in the early 1980s before easing.
  • Service jobs in retail and catering paid less than the factory jobs they replaced, and the minimum wage was not raised between 1981 and 1990.
  • Black unemployment was roughly double white unemployment throughout the period, and urban areas with lost industries suffered most.

Assessment: success or decline?

Examiners reward judgements that weigh the recovery of the 1980s against the longer-term problems of deficit, trade and manufacturing.

  • Supporters of Reagan point to the recovery of growth after 1983, the fall of inflation and the expansion of employment in service industries.
  • Critics point to the rising national debt, the trade deficit and the loss of manufacturing jobs in steel and cars, which made the United States more dependent on foreign capital.
  • The 1970s were marked by stagflation and falling real incomes for many families, while the wealthy gained most from the tax cuts of the 1980s.
  • Historians disagree about whether the decline of manufacturing was an inevitable result of global change or a result of policy choices, such as the high dollar and the lack of industrial policy.
  • The long expansion from late 1982 lasted until 1990 and created about 18 million jobs, a record to set against the weaknesses.
  • Declinists such as Paul Kennedy, in The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers (1987), argued that the United States was suffering imperial overstretch, while optimists pointed to US leadership in computers, services and finance.

Key terms

Globalisation
The growing integration of economies through trade and investment, which in the 1970s and 1980s increased competition from Japan and Europe for US industries.
Corporate capitalism
The system in which large corporations dominate the economy, and whose strength was challenged by foreign rivals and deregulation in this period.
Stagflation
The combination of high inflation and stagnant economic growth and rising unemployment that affected the United States in the 1970s.
Reaganomics
Reagan's policy of cutting taxes, reducing regulation and raising defence spending, aimed at restoring economic growth and reducing inflation.
Rust belt
The old industrial regions of the Midwest and northeast, centred on steel and car manufacturing, which lost jobs as those industries declined.
Federal deficit
The shortfall of federal government spending over revenue, which grew through the Vietnam War, the Reagan tax cuts and defence build-up.
Plaza Accord
The September 1985 agreement among the leading industrial countries to lower the value of the dollar and restore the competitiveness of US exports.
Bretton Woods system
The postwar monetary order with fixed exchange rates tied to the dollar, which ended in August 1971 when the dollar was no longer convertible to gold.
Supply-side economics
The theory behind Reaganomics that cutting taxes on income and investment would stimulate production and so raise growth.
Savings and Loan crisis
The failure of hundreds of thrift institutions in the 1980s after deregulation, which led to a federal bailout in 1989.
