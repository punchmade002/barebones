Background: Johnson and the inherited war

Johnson became president in November 1963 with an American commitment in South Vietnam that had already grown from advisers into a costly war against the Viet Cong.

  • Lyndon Baines Johnson was born in 1908 near Stonewall, Texas, worked in the New Deal's National Youth Administration in Texas, entered the House in 1937 and won his Senate seat in 1948 by 87 votes, a result that earned him the nickname Landslide Lyndon.
  • He became Democratic leader in the Senate in 1953 and Majority Leader in 1955, and in 1960 he accepted the vice presidency under Kennedy after losing the nomination.
  • He was a master of Senate politics and persuasion, using personal pressure on colleagues (the Johnson treatment) to pass legislation, but he could not apply those methods to a war against an enemy who would not bargain on his terms.
  • He was vice president under Kennedy and sworn in as president on 22 November 1963, after Kennedy's assassination in Dallas.
  • Kennedy had committed the United States to the survival of South Vietnam but had avoided combat troops, and the South Vietnamese government was in disarray after Diem's overthrow and killing on 2 November 1963.
  • Within days of taking office Johnson told his ambassador in Saigon that he would not lose Vietnam, and National Security Action Memorandum 273 (26 November 1963) confirmed the policy of supporting South Vietnam.
  • Johnson kept Kennedy's advisers, including Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara, Secretary of State Dean Rusk and National Security Adviser McGeorge Bundy, so there was continuity of thinking about the war.
  • Johnson feared that losing Vietnam would repeat the political damage of the loss of China, when Truman's party had been attacked as soft on communism, and he feared a right-wing backlash that would wreck his domestic programme.
  • When Johnson took office there were about 16,000 American military advisers in South Vietnam, and the government of Diem had been overthrown in November 1963.
  • Johnson inherited a South Vietnamese government that was unstable and a war in which the Viet Cong controlled large parts of the countryside.
  • He was a firm anti-communist who was also aware of the domestic cost of war, and he believed that losing South Vietnam would damage US credibility worldwide.

The Gulf of Tonkin and the 1964 election

The Gulf of Tonkin incidents gave Johnson congressional authority for a larger war, and the 1964 election confirmed his mandate.

  • On 2 August 1964 North Vietnamese torpedo boats attacked the US destroyer USS Maddox in the Gulf of Tonkin, which was on an intelligence-gathering patrol near the North Vietnamese coast, days after South Vietnamese commando raids on the coast (Operation 34A) that Hanoi linked to the patrol.
  • A second attack reported on 4 August 1964, on the Maddox and the destroyer USS Turner Joy, probably never happened: sonar and radar readings in bad weather were misread, and later documents showed that officials doubted the report even as it was used.
  • On the evening of 4 August 1964 Johnson announced retaliatory air strikes on North Vietnamese naval bases, which were carried out on 5 August.
  • The Tonkin Gulf Resolution, passed on 7 August 1964 by 416 votes to 0 in the House and 88 to 2 in the Senate, authorised the President to take all necessary measures to protect US forces and to prevent further aggression.
  • Critics later argued that the resolution was prepared before the incidents were fully understood, and Senator Wayne Morse and Senator Ernest Gruening voted against it.
  • In the 1964 election Johnson won about 61 per cent of the popular vote against Barry Goldwater, who had called for stronger action in Vietnam, and he presented himself as the candidate of restraint.
  • The campaign's restraint was a political strategy, and Johnson privately prepared for a larger war while publicly promising not to send American boys to fight an Asian war.

Escalation: Rolling Thunder and the first combat troops, 1965

The decision to bomb North Vietnam and to commit ground combat forces in 1965 turned an advisers' war into an American war.

  • In February 1965 a Viet Cong attack on the American base at Pleiku killed eight Americans, and Johnson ordered reprisal raids (Flaming Dart) that led on to the sustained bombing.
  • Operation Rolling Thunder, a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam, began on 2 March 1965, and it continued in stages until 1968.
  • The first US Marine combat units landed at Da Nang on 8 March 1965, the first American ground combat troops in Vietnam.
  • General William Westmoreland, commander of US forces from June 1964, argued that a war of attrition with large forces would wear down the North Vietnamese.
  • On 28 July 1965 Johnson announced at a press conference that US forces in Vietnam would rise from 75,000 to 125,000 at once and that more would be sent as commanders requested, without asking Congress for a declaration of war or calling up the reserves.
  • US troop numbers reached about 184,000 by the end of 1965, about 385,000 by the end of 1966 and over 500,000 in 1968, peaking at about 540,000 in 1969 before falling.
  • Johnson's strategy was to hold the South while signalling willingness to negotiate, and his April 1965 Johns Hopkins speech offered unconditional talks and a development plan for the Mekong Delta.
  • North Vietnam refused to negotiate while the bombing continued, and the Viet Cong kept fighting in the South, supplied through the Ho Chi Minh Trail in Laos and Cambodia.
  • Johnson's advisers were not united: Under Secretary of State George Ball argued in memos in 1965 that the United States would be dragged into an unwinnable land war, but Johnson, McNamara, Rusk and Bundy chose escalation.
  • Johnson avoided a formal declaration of war and wanted to keep the escalation low-key in order not to endanger his Great Society legislation, which was passing through Congress in 1965.

Westmoreland's strategy and the war on the ground

  • Westmoreland's strategy was search and destroy: large units hunted communist forces in the countryside, using helicopters, artillery and air power, and success was measured by the body count.
  • The communists could choose when to fight, and they replaced losses from a large population and from supplies carried down the Ho Chi Minh Trail, so attrition did not break their will.
  • Heavy firepower, including napalm and the defoliant Agent Orange, killed large numbers of civilians and drove peasants into government-controlled camps or towards the Viet Cong.
  • The pacification of the countryside and the building of a stable South Vietnamese government lagged behind: the Saigon regime was led by generals, and Nguyen Van Thieu became president in 1967 after elections criticised as flawed.
  • In November 1967 Westmoreland told Washington that the end was in view, which set up the shock of Tet.
  • In March 1968 American soldiers killed several hundred unarmed villagers at My Lai, an atrocity covered up until it was exposed in 1969.
  • American casualties passed 30,000 dead by the end of 1968, and the draft, which took young men who lacked college deferments, spread the war's costs through American families.

The credibility gap and the domestic critics

Johnson's public statements on Vietnam were increasingly doubted, and his critics in Congress and the media challenged the war from 1966.

  • The term credibility gap was used by journalists from the mid-1960s to describe the difference between official statements on the war and the reality reported from Vietnam.
  • Senator J. William Fulbright, chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, held televised hearings on Vietnam in early 1966 that questioned the war's basis.
  • Fulbright's book The Arrogance of Power (1966) argued that the United States was acting as a great power imposing its will on a small nation.
  • The teach-in movement began at the University of Michigan in March 1965, and student and church opposition grew steadily through 1966 and 1967.
  • Martin Luther King Jr. spoke against the war in his Beyond Vietnam speech at Riverside Church, New York, on 4 April 1967, linking the war to poverty and racism.
  • Muhammad Ali refused induction into the army on 28 April 1967, a decision that cost him his heavyweight title and was widely discussed in the press.
  • Johnson's critics said the war was consuming money and attention needed for the Great Society, while his supporters argued that withdrawal would damage America's position abroad.
  • Opposition spread beyond students: on 21 October 1967 about 50,000 people marched on the Pentagon, and in April 1965 the Students for a Democratic Society had led a march of about 20,000 in Washington.

The costs to the Great Society

Johnson tried to fund both the war and his domestic programme, and the cost of the war weakened his reform agenda.

  • Johnson tried to pay for the war and the Great Society together, a policy critics called guns and butter, and he delayed asking Congress for a tax increase until 1967.
  • Federal deficits grew and inflation rose after 1965, and Congress passed a 10 per cent income tax surtax in June 1968 to restrain spending.
  • Funding for the War on Poverty was cut or diverted as military spending grew, and many programmes were underfunded as they expanded.
  • The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 were passed before the war's costs became acute, and they stand as Johnson's most secure achievements.
  • By 1967 the rising cost of the war and the urban riots had turned public opinion against new domestic spending, weakening the Great Society's momentum.

Tet, the 1968 turning point and Johnson's withdrawal

The Tet Offensive of January 1968 did not defeat the Americans militarily, but it shattered their optimism and ended Johnson's chance of re-election.

  • On 30 January 1968, during the Tet holiday, Viet Cong and North Vietnamese forces attacked more than a hundred towns and cities across South Vietnam, including Saigon and the city of Hue.
  • A Viet Cong squad broke into the grounds of the US embassy in Saigon and held out for about six hours before being killed, and television pictures of it shocked American opinion, even though the offensive was militarily a failure for the communists.
  • General Westmoreland had said that the enemy could no longer mount major offensives, and Tet was taken by many Americans as proof that official optimism was false.
  • Tet was a heavy military defeat for the communists, who lost many of their best Viet Cong fighters, but American commentators judged it by Westmoreland's earlier promises.
  • On 27 February 1968 the television newsman Walter Cronkite broadcast a report calling the war a stalemate, and his views were widely reported as a sign of changing opinion.
  • In February 1968 Westmoreland and the Joint Chiefs asked for about 206,000 more troops; Johnson's new Secretary of Defense Clark Clifford studied the request and advised against it, and a group of senior outside advisers known as the Wise Men told Johnson in March that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost.
  • Eugene McCarthy's strong showing in the New Hampshire primary in March 1968 and Robert Kennedy's entry into the race weakened Johnson's position within his own party.
  • On 31 March 1968 Johnson announced a partial halt to the bombing of North Vietnam above the 20th parallel, offered peace talks and declared that he would not seek re-election.
  • Peace talks began in Paris in May 1968, but the war continued, and the Republican Richard Nixon won the presidency in November 1968 after promising an honourable end to the war.
  • 1968 turned into a year of crisis: Martin Luther King was assassinated on 4 April and Robert Kennedy on 5 June, and the Democratic convention in Chicago in August was marked by rioting between police and protesters, and Johnson's vice president Hubert Humphrey lost narrowly to Nixon.

Why Johnson could not win

Historians and contemporaries have offered several reasons why an enormous American military effort failed to defeat a determined enemy.

  • The war was a limited one: the US avoided invading North Vietnam, mining Haiphong harbour, or using nuclear weapons, and this limited the pressure on Hanoi.
  • North Vietnam and the Viet Cong could use bases in Laos and Cambodia that the US was reluctant to attack, so they could withdraw and rebuild.
  • The South Vietnamese government was weak and corrupt, and the Army of the Republic of Vietnam could not hold the territory cleared by American forces.
  • The strategy of attrition and the body count measure of success were criticised within the US army and in the press as misleading about the progress of the war.
  • Many historians argue that Johnson's gradual escalation, and his refusal to consider a clear withdrawal, kept options open while the costs and divisions at home grew.
  • Johnson played down the war in public to protect the Great Society, so he never explained clear aims to the public, and the later gap between his optimism and events hurt his standing.

Key personality: Lyndon Johnson

Johnson was the president who made Vietnam an American war and also the president of the Great Society. He was a decision-maker at the centre of events rather than a witness.

  • His career: congressman 1937, senator 1949, Senate Majority Leader 1955 to 1961, vice president 1961 to 1963, president from 22 November 1963 until January 1969.
  • His domestic record was one of the greatest legislative achievements of any president: the Civil Rights Act (1964), Voting Rights Act (1965), Medicare, Medicaid and federal aid to education.
  • In Vietnam he took decisions in 1964 and 1965 to bomb North Vietnam and send combat troops, always in steps, and he hoped to avoid a wider war with China or the Soviet Union, as in Korea in 1950.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a forceful, domineering leader who was crude, devious and driven, and the credibility gap made many doubt his word; his supporters pointed to his achievements for the poor and for black Americans.
  • Protesters chanted at him outside the White House, and the demonstrations helped to persuade him not to stand again in 1968.
  • Historians in the 1970s, such as the journalist David Halberstam in The Best and the Brightest (1972), blamed the arrogance of the advisers he inherited; later historians, such as Robert Dallek and Fredrik Logevall, stress that he had alternatives in 1964 and 1965 and chose to escalate partly to protect his domestic reputation.
  • Johnson's reputation has risen since the 1990s for civil rights and fallen over Vietnam, so most historians now regard him as a tragic figure.

Assessment: Johnson's successes and failures

Examiners reward evaluation that weighs Johnson's domestic achievements against his failure in Vietnam, and contemporary against later judgements.

  • Success at home: the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act, Medicare and Medicaid are judged as major achievements of the twentieth century's social reform.
  • Failure abroad: the war ended without victory in 1973 and cost about 58,000 American lives, and it damaged the Great Society and the trust of the public in government.
  • Contemporaries were divided: in 1964 and 1965 most Americans admired him as a strong, effective leader, but the war cut his approval below 40 per cent by late 1967, and he was jeered by protesters chanting about how many children he had killed that day.
  • Later historians disagree about whether Johnson was trapped by the assumptions of the Cold War or whether he made avoidable choices on escalation.
  • The phrase credibility gap summed up the lasting problem of trust between a president and the public, a theme that shaped politics after Vietnam and Watergate.

Key terms

Gulf of Tonkin Resolution
The resolution of 7 August 1964 by which Congress authorised the President to take all necessary measures in Vietnam, giving Johnson wide war powers.
Rolling Thunder
The sustained US bombing campaign against North Vietnam, which began on 2 March 1965.
Credibility gap
The difference, widely noticed from 1965, between the official government account of the Vietnam War and what was reported from the war zone.
Tet Offensive
The coordinated communist attacks across South Vietnam beginning on 30 January 1968, which shattered American confidence in official optimism.
Great Society
Johnson's programme of domestic reform, which included civil rights, health and education laws, and the War on Poverty.
Public opinion
The view of ordinary Americans, measured by polls and reported by the media, which turned against the war and against Johnson from 1967.
Imperialism
The critics' term for the US role in Vietnam, arguing that an outside power was imposing its will on a small nation.
Military-industrial complex
The network of defence contractors, the armed forces and government agencies whose shared interests promoted high defence spending, a phrase popularised by Eisenhower in 1961.
Search and destroy
Westmoreland's tactic of sending large American units to find and kill communist forces in the countryside, with progress measured by the body count.
Guns and butter
The policy of paying for the Vietnam War and the Great Society at the same time, without raising taxes at first, which fed inflation.
Attrition
The strategy of inflicting losses on the enemy faster than it could replace them, in the hope that Hanoi would give up.
Vietnam War draft
Conscription under which young men could be called into the army, with deferments for most college students, which fuelled the anti-war movement.
