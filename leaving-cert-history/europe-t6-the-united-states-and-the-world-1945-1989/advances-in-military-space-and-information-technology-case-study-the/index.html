Military technology: the nuclear age and its delivery systems

Nuclear weapons and the means of delivering them drove military technology after 1945, and the same research often fed the space and computing programmes.

  • The United States tested the first thermonuclear device, Ivy Mike, at Eniwetok Atoll in the Pacific on 1 November 1952, and the Soviet Union tested a thermonuclear device in August 1953.
  • Intercontinental ballistic missiles, first the Atlas in 1959 and then the Minuteman from 1962, gave the United States the ability to strike the Soviet Union within about thirty minutes.
  • Submarine-launched Polaris missiles, first deployed in 1960, gave the United States a second strike capability that could not easily be destroyed in a surprise attack.
  • The B-52 Stratofortress, a long-range jet bomber that entered service in 1955, carried nuclear weapons on strategic patrols during the Cold War.
  • Reconnaissance satellites, first successfully used in the Corona programme from 1960, allowed the United States to monitor Soviet missile sites without flying over Soviet territory.
  • Military research on computers, radar and jet propulsion created technologies that were later adopted in civil aviation and in commercial electronics.
  • The decision to build the hydrogen bomb in 1950 was opposed by scientists such as J. Robert Oppenheimer and supported by Edward Teller, and Truman approved it in January 1950 after the Soviet atomic test.
  • Warning of a missile gap in 1960, Kennedy's campaign argued that the Soviet Union was ahead in missiles; in fact the United States held a large lead, which later became clear from satellite pictures.
  • By the late 1960s the United States had about 1,000 Minuteman missiles, 41 Polaris submarines, and from 1970 multiple warheads (MIRVs) on single missiles, which made arms control more difficult.
  • Jet fighters and bombers, nuclear submarines (USS Nautilus, 1954) and guided missiles were funded by the Pentagon, and defence contracts supported the aerospace industry in California, Texas and Washington State.
  • Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative of 1983 sought a space-based missile shield, and it was not built, but its research funded lasers and sensors.
  • The satellite navigation system GPS began as a military programme with the first satellites launched in 1978.

The space race, 1957 to 1969

The Soviet launch of Sputnik in 1957 began a competition in space that the United States came to see as a test of its technological and political strength.

  • The Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, on 4 October 1957, and the American satellite Explorer 1 followed on 31 January 1958.
  • The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) was created in 1958 to direct the civil space programme, taking over work from the military and from the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics.
  • The Mercury programme of 1958 to 1963 sent the first American astronauts into space: Alan Shepard became the first American in space on a short suborbital flight on 5 May 1961, and John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth on 20 February 1962.
  • Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on 12 April 1961, a Soviet achievement that increased pressure on the United States to respond.
  • In a speech to Congress on 25 May 1961 President Kennedy committed the United States to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely before the end of the decade.
  • The Gemini programme of 1965 and 1966 tested the techniques of rendezvous and docking needed for a Moon landing, and it prepared the way for the Apollo missions.
  • Sputnik caused alarm in the United States about Soviet technology and education, and it led to the National Defense Education Act of 1958 and to the creation of NASA, which Eisenhower signed in July 1958.
  • The Soviet Union scored further firsts: the first woman in space (Valentina Tereshkova, 1963) and the first spacewalk by Alexei Leonov in March 1965.
  • Kennedy's Moon decision followed Gagarin and the Bay of Pigs, and he believed the Moon landing was a field in which the United States could win; at Rice University on 12 September 1962 he said the nation chose to go to the Moon, not because it was easy but because it was hard.
  • NASA chose lunar orbit rendezvous in 1962 as the method for the Moon landing, with a separate lunar module descending from the orbiting command module.
  • The Soviet Union's Moon rocket, the N1, failed in every test, and the Soviet crewed lunar programme was cancelled.

Case study: the Apollo programme and its organisation

The Apollo programme was the largest peacetime technological effort the United States had undertaken, and its cost and organisation shaped the Moon landing.

  • The Apollo programme was managed by NASA with major contractors such as North American Aviation, Grumman, Boeing and IBM, and it employed hundreds of thousands of people across the country.
  • The Saturn V rocket, the most powerful rocket built until then, was used for the Moon missions and was developed under the direction of Wernher von Braun at the Marshall Space Flight Center.
  • The Apollo programme cost about 25 billion dollars in the money of its time, and NASA's budget peaked in 1965 and 1966 at about 4 to 5 per cent of federal spending.
  • The Apollo spacecraft had three parts: the command module, which carried the crew back to Earth, the service module, and the lunar module, which took two astronauts to the surface.
  • The Apollo 1 fire on 27 January 1967, during a test on the launch pad, killed the astronauts Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee and led to a redesign of the spacecraft.
  • Apollo 8 in December 1968 was the first crewed mission to orbit the Moon, with Frank Borman, Jim Lovell and William Anders, and it returned images of the Earth rising over the lunar horizon.
  • The Apollo 13 mission of April 1970 was crippled by an oxygen tank explosion, and the crew returned safely, an achievement that was widely praised as a triumph of engineering and teamwork.
  • NASA administrator James Webb, appointed by Kennedy in 1961, managed the political side and the vast contracting system, and Mission Control in Houston, Texas, directed flights from the Manned Spacecraft Center, opened in 1963.
  • The Saturn V stood about 110 metres tall, and its first stage burned fuel at a rate of about 13 tonnes a second; it flew thirteen times without a failure that lost a crew.
  • The Apollo Guidance Computer, developed at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, was one of the first to use integrated circuits, which made the programme a major customer for the new technology.
  • Apollo 7 (October 1968) tested the command module in Earth orbit, and Apollo 9 and Apollo 10 in 1969 tested the lunar module, the last mission flying to within about 15 kilometres of the Moon's surface.
  • Employment in the programme was concentrated in Florida, Texas, Alabama and California, which tied the Sun Belt's growth to federal spending.
  • Amitai Etzioni's The Moon-Doggle (1964) criticised the programme as a waste, and some in Congress argued that the money would be better spent on poverty.

Case study: Apollo 11 and the Moon landing, 16 to 24 July 1969

Apollo 11 was launched from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida, landed in the Sea of Tranquillity, and returned its crew to the Pacific.

  • The crew were Neil Armstrong, commander, Edwin Buzz Aldrin, lunar module pilot, and Michael Collins, command module pilot, who remained in orbit around the Moon.
  • Apollo 11 was launched on 16 July 1969 from Kennedy Space Center, Florida, and entered lunar orbit on 19 July 1969.
  • The lunar module Eagle landed in the Sea of Tranquillity on 20 July 1969, and Armstrong stepped onto the surface several hours later.
  • Armstrong's first words on the Moon were one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind, and Aldrin joined him on the surface for about two and a half hours of activity.
  • The crew collected about 21 kilograms of lunar rock and soil, placed a plaque and a flag, and set up experiments that measured the Moon's seismic activity.
  • Apollo 11 splashed down in the Pacific Ocean on 24 July 1969, and the crew were recovered by the aircraft carrier USS Hornet.
  • The landing was broadcast live on television to an audience estimated at about 600 million people across the world, making it one of the most watched events in history.
  • The landing was risky: during the descent computer alarms sounded, and Armstrong took manual control to avoid a boulder field, landing Eagle with very little fuel left.
  • Armstrong's words as he stepped on to the surface at 10.56 pm Eastern time on 20 July were meant to be one small step for a man, and he said afterwards that he had intended the word a; Aldrin joined him, and the two spent about two and a half hours outside.
  • The crew left a plaque reading We came in peace for all mankind, and planted an American flag; they did not claim the Moon for the United States, as the 1967 Outer Space Treaty banned national claims.
  • Nixon spoke to the astronauts by telephone from the White House, and the crew spent 21 days in quarantine after their return in case of lunar germs.
  • The Soviet Union's Luna 15 probe crashed on the Moon on 21 July 1969 while trying to return samples, and Soviet media played down the Apollo achievement.
  • The mission came in the same year as the rise of the anti-war movement, and some commentators contrasted the technical triumph with the troubles at home.

Significance and later missions

The Moon landing ended the race to the Moon, but later missions and the space shuttle showed the limits of US ambitions in space.

  • The Apollo missions after 11 landed on the Moon in five further expeditions, the last being Apollo 17 in December 1972.
  • Public interest and political support for the Moon missions declined after 1969, and NASA's budget fell substantially in the early 1970s.
  • Skylab, the first American space station, was launched in May 1973, and the Apollo-Soyuz mission of July 1975 was the first joint US-Soviet space flight.
  • The Space Shuttle made its first flight on 12 April 1981, and it was intended to make space access routine and cheaper.
  • The Space Shuttle Challenger broke up 73 seconds after launch on 28 January 1986, killing all seven crew members, and the failure of the seals in the solid rocket boosters was identified as the cause.
  • Apollo 12 (November 1969) landed on the Moon after Apollo 11, and Apollo 13's near-disaster in April 1970 made the public aware of the risks.
  • Apollo 17 in December 1972 was the last crewed lunar mission, and no human has been beyond low Earth orbit since; budget cuts cancelled Apollo 18 to 20.
  • The Moon landing was a national and global event, a Cold War victory for the United States over the Soviet Union, and it was an image of American technology for the rest of the world.
  • The Challenger disaster killed the teacher Christa McAuliffe, and the Rogers Commission report of 1986 blamed the O-ring seals and failures in NASA's decision-making.

Information technology: from ENIAC to the personal computer

Computing developed from wartime machines into commercial and military systems, and the microprocessor made the personal computer possible.

  • ENIAC, completed in 1945 and publicly unveiled in February 1946 at the University of Pennsylvania, was one of the first general-purpose electronic computers, built to calculate artillery firing tables.
  • The transistor, invented at Bell Laboratories in 1947, replaced the vacuum tube and made computers smaller, faster and more reliable.
  • IBM's System/360 family of computers, launched in 1964, set a standard for compatible business computers and made IBM the dominant computer firm of the period.
  • The integrated circuit was developed independently by Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments in 1958 and Robert Noyce at Fairchild in 1959, placing many transistors on a single chip.
  • Gordon Moore's 1965 observation that the number of components on a chip doubled roughly every year, later revised to about two years, became a guide to the industry's growth.
  • Intel's 4004 microprocessor, released in 1971, put a complete central processing unit on a single chip.
  • The ARPANET, a Defense Department network, sent its first message between UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute on 29 October 1969.
  • Apple's Apple II (1977) and IBM's personal computer (1981) brought computing into homes and offices, and the Santa Clara Valley became known as Silicon Valley, the centre of the industry.
  • UNIVAC, the first commercial computer in the United States, was delivered in 1951, and it was used by CBS to predict the 1952 presidential election.
  • Defence money paid for much early computing: the SAGE air-defence system and the Pentagon's research agency ARPA, created in 1958 after Sputnik, funded the ARPANET.
  • Silicon Valley grew around Stanford University and firms such as Hewlett-Packard, Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel, which were helped by defence contracts and by university research.
  • Apple's Macintosh of 1984 popularised the graphical interface, and Microsoft supplied the operating system for the IBM PC, which made it the dominant software firm.

Satellites and civilian spin-offs

Space and military research produced satellites that transformed communications and weather forecasting, and their civilian uses became a justification for the programmes.

  • TIROS 1, launched on 1 April 1960, was the first successful weather satellite, and it sent back television images of cloud cover.
  • Telstar, launched on 10 July 1962, was the first active communications satellite to relay live television and telephone signals across the Atlantic.
  • The Communications Satellite Act of 1962 created the Communications Satellite Corporation, a private-public body that set up the Intelsat global satellite system.
  • Space technology also contributed to the miniaturisation of electronics, computer memory and materials, although the scale of these spin-offs has been debated by historians.

Assessment: was the Moon landing worth its cost?

Examiners reward judgements that weigh the scientific and prestige gains of the programme against its costs and its link to the Cold War.

  • Supporters argue that the Moon landing was a triumph of American engineering and organisation, and that it inspired advances in computing, materials and medicine.
  • Critics argue that the programme was driven by Cold War competition rather than scientific need, and that the cost of Apollo could have funded other priorities.
  • Contemporaries celebrated the landing as a national and global achievement, while some on the left saw the cost as a diversion from poverty and urban problems.
  • Later historians have stressed that the space race helped to shape the technologies of computing and satellites, but that the lack of follow-up missions suggests the programme was a short burst of Cold War spending.

Key terms

Technological development
The advances in military, space and computing technology after 1945, such as the thermonuclear bomb, the Apollo spacecraft and the microprocessor, which shaped the Cold War.
Military-industrial complex
The network of defence contractors, military officers and politicians whose shared interests promoted technological and defence spending, including the space and missile programmes.
ICBM
The intercontinental ballistic missile, a long-range nuclear-armed rocket, which the United States deployed from 1959, including the Atlas and the Minuteman.
Integrated circuit
A set of transistors and other components built on a single chip of semiconductor material, developed independently by Kilby and Noyce in 1958 and 1959.
Microprocessor
A chip that contains the central processing unit of a computer, first released by Intel in 1971.
Space race
The competition between the United States and the Soviet Union to achieve milestones in space, from Sputnik in 1957 to the Moon landing in 1969.
Apollo programme
NASA's programme of crewed missions to the Moon from 1967 to 1972, which culminated in the first landing in July 1969.
ARPANET
The Defense Department's early computer network, which sent its first message between UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute in October 1969.
Saturn V
The three-stage rocket designed under Wernher von Braun that launched the Apollo Moon missions.
Lunar orbit rendezvous
The method chosen in 1962 for the Moon landing, in which a small lunar module descends to the surface while the command module stays in lunar orbit.
