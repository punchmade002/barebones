Federalism and states' rights

The Constitution divides power between the federal government and the states, and that division shaped the civil rights and social policy fights of the period.

  • Federalism: powers not given to the federal government are reserved to the states under the Tenth Amendment, so states controlled schools, policing, voting rules and marriage law.
  • Before the 1960s most southern states enforced segregation by state law, and federal action against it was met with the argument that such matters belonged to the states.
  • In 1948 southern Democrats angry at Truman's civil rights programme left the party and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party (the Dixiecrats) with Strom Thurmond as candidate; it carried four Deep South states.
  • The Civil Rights Act 1957 was the first civil rights law since Reconstruction; it set up a Civil Rights Commission and gave federal authorities power to act over voting rights.
  • The Civil Rights Act 1964 rested on federal power to regulate interstate commerce, which allowed Congress to ban segregation in hotels, restaurants and other public places.
  • In September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus of Arkansas used the National Guard to stop nine black students entering Little Rock Central High School; Eisenhower sent federal troops, including the 101st Airborne Division, to enforce integration.

Separation of powers and checks and balances

The Constitution gives Congress, the President and the Supreme Court separate powers, and each can limit the others.

  • The Constitution (1787) gives Congress the power to make laws, raise taxes and declare war; the President executes the laws, commands the armed forces and conducts foreign policy; the Supreme Court interprets the Constitution.
  • The President can veto bills, but Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds majority in both houses; the Senate must confirm senior appointments and ratify treaties by a two-thirds vote.
  • Congress passed the Taft-Hartley Act in 1947, restricting trade union activity such as the closed shop, and overrode Truman's veto in June 1947.
  • Republicans won control of both houses of Congress in the 1946 midterm elections (slogan: "Had enough?") for the first time since 1928, leaving Truman to govern with a hostile Congress until 1948.
  • Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (June 1952): the Supreme Court ruled that Truman had exceeded his powers by seizing the steel mills during a strike in April 1952 without congressional authority.
  • The Warren Court (Chief Justice Earl Warren, appointed by Eisenhower in 1953) used judicial review to extend civil rights and liberties, e.g. Brown v. Board of Education (1954) and Miranda v. Arizona (1966) on the rights of arrested suspects.
  • The Twenty-Second Amendment, passed by Congress in March 1947 and ratified in 1951, limits a president to two elected terms, a direct response to Roosevelt winning four elections.
  • United States v Nixon (July 1974): the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Nixon must hand over the White House tapes, and his resignation followed in August 1974.
  • Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto in November 1973 to limit the President's power to commit troops without congressional approval.
  • The Boland Amendments (1982 to 1984) restricted US aid to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua; officials in the Reagan administration kept the Contras supplied in breach of them, the core of the Iran-Contra affair.

The growth of the presidential bureaucracy

The President's staff and agencies grew steadily from the 1930s, so that more policy was shaped inside the White House and its agencies.

  • The Executive Office of the President was created in 1939 under Roosevelt, giving the President a personal staff separate from the government departments.
  • The National Security Act of July 1947, signed by Truman, created the National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency and the National Military Establishment, later renamed the Department of Defense.
  • The National Security Council gave the President a forum of advisers on foreign and defence policy, often bypassing the State Department and Congress.
  • The Council of Economic Advisers was created by the Employment Act of 1946, which committed the federal government to promote maximum employment, production and purchasing power.
  • Kennedy held live televised press conferences from January 1961, using the press conference as a direct channel to public opinion.
  • Arthur Schlesinger Jr. described the expanded executive in The Imperial Presidency (1973), a phrase that became the standard label for concern over presidential power after Watergate.

Roosevelt's legacy and the Truman succession

Roosevelt's coalition and the wartime economy shaped the politics that Truman inherited in 1945.

  • Roosevelt won four presidential elections (1932, 1936, 1940 and 1944) and served from March 1933 until his death.
  • The New Deal coalition combined urban workers, trade unions, Catholics and Jews, farmers, southern whites and black voters who moved to the Democrats from the 1930s.
  • Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945, and Harry Truman, vice president since January 1945, became president.
  • Truman had chaired a Senate committee (the Truman Committee, 1941 to 1944) that investigated waste and fraud in wartime production, which gave him a reputation for careful oversight.
  • Truman inherited a wave of strikes in 1945 and 1946 as the wartime economy was converted to peacetime production.
  • Truman's party was divided between northern liberals and conservative southern Democrats, who often voted with Republicans in Congress (the conservative coalition) to block reform.

Key personality: Harry Truman (domestic record)

Truman was a participant in the domestic politics of his presidency and a figure whose popularity swung widely. His foreign policy record is covered in the notes on US foreign policy.

  • Born in Lamar, Missouri, in 1884, he worked as a farmer, a haberdasher and a county court official before being elected to the Senate in 1934 and taking his seat in 1935.
  • He served as an artillery captain in France in the First World War and owed his early political career to the Kansas City Democratic machine of Tom Pendergast.
  • As vice president from January 1945 he was not told of the atomic bomb project until he became president, a fact that shaped his early reliance on advisers.
  • In September 1945 he sent Congress a 21-point programme of reforms that continued the New Deal, but reconversion, inflation and strikes blocked most of it.
  • His domestic programme, the Fair Deal, was outlined in January 1949 and included national health insurance, a wider Social Security system, a higher minimum wage and public housing.
  • Congress blocked national health insurance and most other Fair Deal proposals; the Housing Act of 1949 and a rise in the minimum wage were among the main successes.
  • His civil rights programme began with the report To Secure These Rights (October 1947) from the President's Committee on Civil Rights, and Executive Order 9981 (26 July 1948) ordered the desegregation of the armed forces.
  • His loyalty programme (Executive Order 9835, March 1947) required checks on federal employees for disloyalty, and it fed the wider Red Scare.
  • Contemporaries were often dismissive: the 1946 joke "To err is Truman" mocked his handling of strikes and prices, and his Gallup approval rating fell to about 22 per cent in early 1952.
  • In 1948 he won an upset victory over Thomas Dewey after a campaign attacking the Republican Congress; the Chicago Daily Tribune had printed the headline "Dewey Defeats Truman" before the result was known.
  • In 1948 the Democrats split three ways (Thurmond's Dixiecrats on the right, Henry Wallace's Progressives on the left), yet Truman won 303 electoral votes to Dewey's 189 after a cross-country whistle-stop train campaign.
  • By 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply because of the Korean War, rising prices and corruption scandals in several federal agencies, and he announced in March 1952 that he would not seek re-election.
  • Historians since the 1970s, and David McCullough's biography Truman (1992), have generally ranked him among the stronger presidents for his decisiveness and his civil rights initiatives, while judging his domestic record more limited than he hoped and his loyalty programme damaging to civil liberties.

Eisenhower and Kennedy, 1953 to 1963

  • Eisenhower, a Republican and former Allied commander in the Second World War, kept most New Deal programmes and called his approach Modern Republicanism.
  • Eisenhower extended Social Security to about 10 million more workers in 1954 and created the Department of Health, Education and Welfare in 1953, accepting the New Deal's main structures.
  • Eisenhower's Farewell Address (January 1961) warned against the "military-industrial complex" (see the notes on the economic boom).
  • The Federal-Aid Highway Act of June 1956 funded an Interstate Highway System of about 41,000 miles, the largest federal public works programme of the period.
  • Kennedy won the 1960 election narrowly over Richard Nixon, and his New Frontier, presented in his nomination speech of July 1960, promised a more active federal role at home and in space.
  • Kennedy's civil rights bill was sent to Congress in June 1963, and it was only passed after his assassination.
  • Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas on 22 November 1963; Lyndon Johnson was sworn in as president on Air Force One that afternoon.
  • Kennedy's tax cut was proposed in January 1963 and passed as the Revenue Act of February 1964 under Johnson, cutting the top income tax rate from 91 to 70 per cent.

Johnson and the Great Society, 1963 to 1968

Johnson's domestic programme was the largest federal social effort since the New Deal. His Vietnam policy is covered in the notes on the case study.

  • Johnson, Senate Majority Leader from 1955 to 1961, used his legislative skills (the persuasive "Johnson treatment") and the national mood after Kennedy's death to pass Kennedy's stalled bills.
  • The Civil Rights Act of 2 July 1964 banned segregation in public places and discrimination in employment on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin.
  • In a speech at the University of Michigan on 22 May 1964, Johnson set out the Great Society, a programme to end poverty and racial injustice.
  • The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 launched the War on Poverty, which included Job Corps and community action programmes.
  • The Voting Rights Act of 6 August 1965 suspended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters in areas where discrimination was proven.
  • Medicare for the elderly and Medicaid for the poor were signed into law on 30 July 1965.
  • The Elementary and Secondary Education Act (April 1965) and the Higher Education Act (1965) provided federal aid to schools and colleges, and Head Start for poor pre-school children began in 1965.
  • In the 1964 election Johnson won about 61 per cent of the popular vote against Barry Goldwater, who carried only six states.
  • The Great Society also included the Immigration and Nationality Act (1965), which ended national-origin quotas, and the creation of the Department of Housing and Urban Development (1965).
  • The cost of the Vietnam War from 1965 drained money and attention from the Great Society, and Republican gains in the 1966 midterms slowed new legislation.

Nixon, Ford and Carter

  • Richard Nixon won the 1968 election narrowly and was re-elected in 1972 by carrying 49 states.
  • Nixon's domestic record included the Environmental Protection Agency (1970), wage and price controls (August 1971) and the expansion of affirmative action in federal contracts.
  • Five men were arrested for burgling the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate complex on 17 June 1972, and the Washington Post's reporting kept the scandal in the news.
  • Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974; Gerald Ford, who had become vice president in December 1973 after Spiro Agnew resigned, pardoned him on 8 September 1974.
  • Ford's pardon of Nixon damaged his standing; he faced inflation and recession in 1974 to 1975 and lost narrowly to Carter in 1976.
  • Jimmy Carter, a Democrat from Georgia, won the 1976 election and negotiated the Panama Canal treaties of 1977, under which the canal passed to Panama at the end of 1999.
  • The Camp David Accords of September 1978, brokered by Carter, led to a peace treaty between Egypt and Israel signed in March 1979.
  • Carter created the Departments of Energy (1977) and Education (1979), but high inflation and the 1979 oil shock undermined him; his televised speech of July 1979 on a "crisis of confidence", nicknamed the malaise speech, was seen by critics as blaming the public.
  • The Iran hostage crisis held 52 American diplomats and citizens from 4 November 1979 to 20 January 1981, 444 days; an attempted rescue in April 1980 failed, and it was a major cause of Carter's defeat.

Reagan and the conservative turn

  • Ronald Reagan defeated Carter in 1980 and was re-elected in 1984, carrying 49 states against Walter Mondale.
  • In his first inaugural address (20 January 1981) Reagan said that "government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem".
  • The Economic Recovery Tax Act of August 1981 cut income tax rates by about 25 per cent over three years and lowered the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent.
  • Reagan's first term combined cuts to many domestic social programmes with a large rise in defence spending, a pattern known as Reaganomics.
  • When air traffic controllers in the PATCO union struck in August 1981, Reagan dismissed the strikers, signalling a hard line on public sector unions.
  • Iran-Contra was revealed in November 1986: the administration had sold arms to Iran and diverted the money to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua despite congressional restrictions.
  • The Tower Commission (February 1987) criticised Reagan's detached management style; National Security Adviser John Poindexter and his aide Oliver North were among those prosecuted.
  • Reagan's popularity survived the scandal and he left office in January 1989 with high approval ratings, earning the nickname "the Teflon president" because scandals did not stick to him.

Assessment: did presidents act for the good of America?

  • Truman's Marshall Plan and NATO are usually seen as successes of US leadership; his failure to pass national health insurance and the unpopularity of the Korean War are the main criticisms.
  • Johnson's Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts are counted as major achievements, while his Vietnam policy is widely judged a failure.
  • Nixon's opening to China and his arms control agreements are credited as achievements, while Watergate and the secret bombing of Cambodia are judged serious failings.
  • Carter's Camp David Accords are judged a success, while the Iran hostage crisis and his 1980 defeat are the main charges against him.
  • Reagan's supporters credit his tax cuts and his role in ending the Cold War; critics point to the rising deficit, the 1981 to 1982 recession and Iran-Contra.
  • Public opinion polls made presidential popularity visible from the 1930s onwards, and the Watergate and Iran-Contra scandals led Congress to reassert its oversight of presidents.
  • Contemporary verdicts often differed from later ones: Truman left office in 1953 deeply unpopular, yet his reputation rose sharply in later historians' rankings, as did Eisenhower's, while Johnson's soared in 1964 and collapsed by 1968.

Key terms

Federalism
The division of power between the federal government and the individual states, with powers not given to the federal government reserved to the states.
Separation of powers
The division of government into Congress, the President and the courts, each with its own powers and responsibilities.
Checks and balances
The system by which each branch of government can limit the powers of the others, such as a presidential veto or a Supreme Court ruling.
Presidential bureaucracy
The White House staff, the Executive Office of the President and the agencies that advise and serve the President, which grew steadily from the 1930s.
Liberalism
In US politics, a belief in an active federal government that uses its power to promote social welfare and civil rights, associated with the New Deal and the Great Society.
Public opinion
The views of ordinary citizens as measured by polls and reported by the media, which increasingly shaped how presidents governed and campaigned.
Fair Deal
Truman's domestic programme, outlined in 1949, which extended the New Deal with health insurance, Social Security, housing and a higher minimum wage.
Great Society
Johnson's programme of 1964 to 1966 to end poverty and racial injustice through federal laws and spending on health, education and civil rights.
Reaganomics
The economic policy of Ronald Reagan, combining tax cuts, deregulation, a rise in defence spending and reduced domestic programmes.
Iran-Contra affair
The scandal revealed in November 1986 in which Reagan's officials sold arms to Iran and funnelled the proceeds to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua.
Judicial review
The Supreme Court's power to strike down laws or actions of Congress, the President or the states that break the Constitution, used by the Warren Court to advance civil rights.
Imperial presidency
Arthur Schlesinger Jr.'s 1973 phrase for a presidency that had grown so powerful, especially in war and secrecy, that it escaped the checks of Congress and the courts.
