The split and the road to war, January–June 1922

Ratification of the Treaty divided the Dáil, Sinn Féin and the IRA, and for five months two rival authorities existed side by side.

  • After the Dáil vote of 7 January 1922 the Provisional Government under Collins took over from the British, and a new National Army was recruited, while the Dáil continued to meet under Griffith.
  • The IRA divided: most of its senior officers, led by Rory O'Connor and Liam Lynch, rejected the authority of the Dáil in March 1922 and held their own convention.
  • British troops left Dublin from January 1922, and both pro- and anti-Treaty forces took over barracks, which led to local clashes.
  • The anti-Treaty IRA seized the Four Courts in Dublin on 14 April 1922 and held it as a headquarters.
  • The Collins–de Valera pact of 20 May 1922 proposed a joint panel of pro- and anti-Treaty Sinn Féin candidates in the election; Collins repudiated it on the eve of the poll.
  • In the general election of 16 June 1922 pro-Treaty candidates won 58 seats and anti-Treaty candidates 36, with Labour 17 and others making up the rest; the draft Constitution was published on the day of the poll.
  • On 22 June 1922 two London IRA men shot Field Marshal Sir Henry Wilson, a unionist MP and adviser to the Northern government, and the British government pressed Collins to act against the Four Courts garrison.
  • The kidnapping of a National Army general, J.J. ‘Ginger’ O'Connell, by the Four Courts garrison on 26 June was a further trigger.

The course of the Civil War, 1922–1923

The National Army, using British artillery and supplies, took Dublin and then the towns, and the war became a guerrilla campaign that the anti-Treaty side could not win.

  • On 28 June 1922 the National Army shelled the Four Courts with British field guns; the garrison surrendered on 30 June after an explosion in the Public Record Office destroyed centuries of records.
  • Fighting in Dublin lasted until early July, and Cathal Brugha was killed in action on 7 July.
  • The National Army held the larger forces and the better arms; it took Limerick and Waterford in July, and landed at Fenit, Co. Kerry, and at Cork in August.
  • By the end of August the anti-Treaty forces had lost the towns and turned to guerrilla warfare, ambushes and the destruction of roads, bridges and railways.
  • Arthur Griffith died of a cerebral haemorrhage on 12 August 1922, and Michael Collins, then commander-in-chief, was shot dead at Béal na Bláth, Co. Cork, on 22 August 1922.
  • Richard Mulcahy became commander-in-chief, and W.T. Cosgrave became head of the government.
  • The Army Emergency Powers Resolution of September 1922 let military courts impose the death penalty for possession of arms.
  • The first four executions took place on 17 November 1922, and Erskine Childers was shot on 24 November after being found with a pistol.
  • On 8 December 1922 the government shot Rory O'Connor, Liam Mellows, Joe McKelvey and Dick Barrett, in reprisal for the killing of the TD Seán Hales the day before.
  • In total the Free State government executed about 80 anti-Treaty prisoners; the exact number is disputed by historians.
  • Free State soldiers in Kerry killed prisoners with landmines, as at Ballyseedy on 7 March 1923, in a reprisal that shocked many in the pro-Treaty side.
  • Liam Lynch, the anti-Treaty chief of staff, was killed in the Knockmealdown Mountains on 10 April 1923; his successor Frank Aiken ordered the arms dump on 24 May 1923, with a message from de Valera supporting the order.
  • Estimates of the dead range from about 1,500 to several thousand, and the war left a legacy of bitterness that shaped party politics for generations.

State building under Cosgrave: the institutions

Cumann na nGaedheal, founded in 1923, set up the main institutions of the state. The emphasis was on continuity and law and order.

  • The Constitution of the Irish Free State came into force on 6 December 1922; it provided for a two-chamber Oireachtas, proportional representation, an Executive Council responsible to the Dáil, a Governor-General and the Treaty oath.
  • The Governor-General was Tim Healy from 1922 to 1928, followed by James McNeill.
  • The Civic Guard was founded in 1922 and renamed the Garda Síochána in 1923; it was unarmed, led by Eoin O'Duffy as Commissioner, and absorbed the Dublin Metropolitan Police in 1925.
  • The Courts of Justice Act 1924 set up a new court system of District, Circuit, High and Supreme Courts, replacing both the British and the Dáil courts.
  • The civil service and local government were kept largely as in British times, which gave the new state continuity and efficiency; the Ministers and Secretaries Act 1924 set up the government departments.
  • The Free State joined the League of Nations in 1923 and opened a legation in Washington in 1924.
  • The Land Act 1923 completed the transfer of land to tenants by compulsory purchase of untenanted land.
  • The Shannon Scheme was approved in 1925 and built by the German firm Siemens-Schuckert; the Electricity Supply Board was created in 1927 and the Ardnacrusha power station opened in 1929.

Threats to the state, 1924–1927

Within five years the government faced the army mutiny, the Boundary Commission crisis and the assassination of a leading minister.

  • The Army Mutiny of March 1924 came from a group of former IRA and IRB officers around Liam Tobin and Charles Dalton, who objected to demobilisation and to the influence of ex-British army officers.
  • The mutiny ended with the resignation of the Army Council and the Minister for Defence, Richard Mulcahy; the government reorganised the army under Kevin O'Higgins, which established civil control over the army.
  • The Boundary Commission crisis of November 1925 followed the leak of its expected findings to the Morning Post; Eoin MacNeill resigned and the Free State government agreed the Tripartite Agreement of 3 December 1925, which left the border unchanged.
  • Kevin O'Higgins, Minister for Justice and External Affairs, introduced the Public Safety Acts, which gave the government special powers against armed opposition.
  • O'Higgins was shot dead by three anti-Treaty IRA men on his way to Mass at Booterstown on 10 July 1927.
  • In response the government passed the Public Safety Act of 1927 and the Electoral Amendment Act, which required candidates to promise to take the oath, so Fianna Fáil had to decide whether to enter the Dáil or leave politics.

Consolidating democracy: Fianna Fáil and the 1932 transfer of power

Constitutional republicanism, led by de Valera, replaced armed resistance and the Free State's parliamentary system gained legitimacy.

  • De Valera and his supporters left Sinn Féin in March 1926 over abstention and founded Fianna Fáil on 16 May 1926, arguing that the oath could be treated as an empty formula.
  • In the June 1927 election Fianna Fáil won 44 seats and Cumann na nGaedheal 47, and in the second election in September 1927 Cumann na nGaedheal won 62 and Fianna Fáil 57.
  • After O'Higgins's murder Fianna Fáil entered the Dáil on 12 August 1927, signing the oath book as a formality.
  • The Referendum and Initiative clauses of the Constitution were removed in 1928 and the Governor-General's role was left unchanged.
  • The Depression and the government's spending cuts reduced the support of Cumann na nGaedheal, and Fianna Fáil promised protection, land annuity relief and the abolition of the oath.
  • In the February 1932 election Fianna Fáil won 72 seats and Cumann na nGaedheal 57; de Valera was elected President of the Executive Council on 9 March 1932 with Labour's votes.
  • Cosgrave's government handed over power peacefully, and the army and police accepted the change; this was the first democratic transfer of power in the new state after a civil war.

Legacy of the Civil War

The Civil War shaped party politics and the way the state was remembered for generations.

  • The two sides became the two main parties: Fine Gael descended from the pro-Treaty side and Fianna Fáil from the anti-Treaty side.
  • The 1923 general election, held in August, gave Cumann na nGaedheal 63 seats and the Republicans 44, though the republicans abstained.
  • De Valera was arrested in August 1923 and released in July 1924.
  • Anti-Treaty prisoners numbered in the thousands in 1923, and a hunger strike in October 1923 ended without success.
  • Both sides committed atrocities, and families and communities were divided, which made the war a subject of silence in Irish public life.

Key personality: W.T. Cosgrave

Cosgrave led the pro-Treaty side from 1922 to 1932 and built the state's institutions. He is judged as a cautious administrator who secured democracy.

  • William Thomas Cosgrave (1880–1965) was a Dublin publican's son, a Sinn Féin councillor from 1909 and a member of the South Dublin Union garrison in 1916; his death sentence was commuted.
  • He won Kilkenny City in August 1917 and served as Minister for Local Government in the Dáil ministry from 1919.
  • He became head of government after the deaths of Griffith and Collins in August 1922, and was President of the Executive Council from December 1922 to March 1932.
  • He led Cumann na nGaedheal and, after 1933, became first leader of Fine Gael, which merged his party with others, until 1944.
  • His government held the state together through the Civil War, the mutiny and the assassination of O'Higgins, and favoured rule of law and balanced budgets.
  • Contemporaries: supporters admired his determination and honesty, but critics called him a conservative who relied on a narrow group of ministers and who allowed the government to be seen as pro-British.
  • Historians: Tom Garvin (1922: The Birth of Irish Democracy, 1996) stresses his defence of democracy against militarism, while John Regan (The Irish Counter-Revolution, 1999) argues that the government was too conservative and authoritarian.

Assessment: the Cosgrave governments' achievements and limits

Judgements depend on whether the test is survival and legitimacy or social and economic performance.

  • Achievement: the state survived civil war, mutiny and assassination, and had working institutions by 1927.
  • Achievement: the peaceful transfer of power in 1932 showed that democratic institutions had become accepted.
  • Achievement: the Shannon Scheme and the ESB gave the state a modern electricity system.
  • Limit: the government used military courts, executions and public safety laws that opponents described as repression.
  • Limit: its economic policies, based on low spending and low taxation, did little for urban poverty and unemployment.
  • Limit: the Boundary Commission outcome of 1925 showed that the Treaty's northern hopes had been unrealistic.
  • Contemporary view: supporters saw the government as the defender of law, while opponents called it the Treaty government.
  • Later view: historians disagree about whether it was a successful counter to militarism, or a conservative counter-revolution.

Key terms

Provisional Government
The pro-Treaty government chaired by Collins from January 1922 until the Free State was established in December 1922.
Civil War
The conflict between the pro-Treaty National Army and the anti-Treaty IRA from June 1922 to May 1923.
Free State
The dominion created by the Treaty and the 1922 Constitution, in existence from 6 December 1922.
Republic
The form of state demanded by the anti-Treaty side, which refused to accept the Crown and the oath.
Physical force
The tradition of armed struggle, which the anti-Treaty IRA continued after 1922 against the Free State.
Dominion status
The Treaty status of the Free State as a self-governing member of the Commonwealth.
Cumann na nGaedheal
The pro-Treaty party founded in 1923 that governed until 1932.
Garda Síochána
The unarmed police force created in 1922 as the Civic Guard and renamed in 1923.
Public Safety Acts
Laws, notably in 1923 and 1927, giving the government emergency powers against armed opposition.
Electoral Amendment Act 1927
The law requiring election candidates to pledge to take their seats, which in effect forced Fianna Fáil to take the oath.
Fianna Fáil
The party founded by de Valera in May 1926 that entered the Dáil in 1927 and won power in 1932.
Shannon Scheme
The hydroelectric project at Ardnacrusha on the Shannon, begun in 1925 and opened in 1929, run by the ESB from 1927.
