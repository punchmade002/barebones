Northern Ireland at war: politics and neutrality seen from the North

As part of the United Kingdom, Northern Ireland was at war from 3 September 1939, in contrast to the neutral South, and unionists treated the war effort as proof of their loyalty.

  • Unionists contrasted Northern Ireland's loyalty with the South's neutrality, which strengthened the unionist case against a united Ireland.
  • Nationalists in the North were divided: some supported the war, many Catholics served in the forces, while others resented unionist rule.
  • Conscription was not applied to Northern Ireland, though Britain introduced it in 1939; in May 1941 Churchill's government considered extending it and dropped the idea after opposition from nationalists, the Catholic bishops and the Dublin government.
  • The IRA was active in Belfast: Tom Williams was hanged at Crumlin Road Gaol on 2 September 1942 for killing a policeman, and the case deepened nationalist resentment.
  • James Craig died on 24 November 1940, and John Miller Andrews succeeded him as Prime Minister.
  • Andrews was criticised for an elderly cabinet and slow response to the Blitz, and he resigned in April 1943; Basil Brooke took over and led until 1963.
  • Brooke, an energetic unionist, had been Minister of Commerce and Production and pushed war production and agriculture.

Industry, agriculture and employment in wartime

The war brought full employment to a region that had suffered mass unemployment since the 1920s.

  • Harland and Wolff built warships and merchant ships and repaired thousands of vessels for the Admiralty.
  • Short Brothers in Belfast built Stirling bombers and other aircraft.
  • Engineering firms made munitions, tanks parts and aircraft parts, and rope works and the linen mills made parachutes, tents and uniforms.
  • Farmers were ordered to plough more land and the region became an important food supplier to Britain.
  • Unemployment fell sharply as war contracts came in, and women entered factories in large numbers.
  • Wages rose, but industrial unrest occurred over pay and conditions in the shipyards and engineering works.
  • Belfast's linen, rope and engineering firms were all busy, and the city's population was swollen by workers moving in for war jobs.
  • The Northern Ireland government worked closely with the Ministry of Aircraft Production and the Admiralty in London.

Case study background: Belfast's unpreparedness for air attack

The Northern government assumed that Belfast was too far from German airfields to be attacked, and its civil defence was weak.

  • Belfast was an obvious target because of the shipyard, the aircraft factory, the docks and the engineering works.
  • Before April 1941 the city had few public air-raid shelters, and many working-class houses had no shelter at all.
  • Anti-aircraft defences were small: the city had only a handful of heavy guns and no night-fighter cover.
  • A German reconnaissance flight over Belfast in November 1940 showed that the city was within reach, but the government did not accelerate preparations.
  • Fire services were small and under-equipped, and the authorities depended on outside help.
  • Overcrowded terraced housing near the docks and mills increased the risk of casualties and fire.

The Belfast Blitz, April and May 1941

Four raids hit Belfast in spring 1941. The Easter Tuesday raid was the deadliest night of the Blitz in any British city outside London.

  • A smaller raid on the night of 7–8 April targeted the shipyard and killed about a dozen people.
  • The main raid took place on the night of 15–16 April 1941, Easter Tuesday, when around 180 German bombers attacked the city.
  • About 900 people were killed, and the working-class districts of north and east Belfast were worst hit.
  • The raid of 4–5 May 1941 was an incendiary attack on Harland and Wolff, Short Brothers and the docks; about 150 people were killed.
  • In all the Blitz killed about 1,000 people, and more than half of the city's houses were damaged.
  • Tens of thousands of people were left homeless, and in the days after the raids up to 100,000 people left the city each night to sleep in the countryside, known as ditchers.
  • After the raid of 15–16 April, fire brigades from Dublin, Dún Laoghaire, Dundalk and Drogheda crossed the border at de Valera's authorisation to help, a remarkable act of cross-border help.
  • Many bodies were laid out in public buildings such as the Falls Road baths, and some were buried in mass graves.
  • Many of the dead were in the streets around York Street and the Antrim Road, and the city mortuaries were overwhelmed.

Aftermath: evacuation and what it revealed

The raids showed up the city's poverty and the weakness of its public services.

  • Children and mothers were evacuated to country areas, and many people took refuge in barns and church halls.
  • Host families in rural areas were shocked by the poor health, clothing and hygiene of some evacuees from the slums.
  • The condition of the children became a theme in debates on housing and public health, as it had in Britain in 1939.
  • The Unionist government was criticised for its slow response, and the Blitz contributed to the fall of Andrews in 1943.
  • Many evacuees drifted back to Belfast within weeks, and housing remained scarce for years.
  • Churches, schools and halls in towns such as Lisburn and Antrim took in refugees, and the authorities opened emergency rest centres.

American troops and the wartime city

From 1942 Northern Ireland became a staging area for the Allied build-up, and the American presence changed daily life.

  • The first American troops landed at Dufferin Quay, Belfast, on 26 January 1942.
  • By 1944 there were well over 100,000 American servicemen in Northern Ireland, in camps, airfields and ports.
  • They brought money, goods unavailable in rationed Britain and a new popular culture, including dances and cinema.
  • Black American soldiers served in segregated units, and many local people treated them with more welcome than the US authorities.
  • Northern Ireland's ports and airfields supported the Atlantic convoy escort and the preparation for D-Day.

Social and political effects: housing, welfare and Labour

The war raised expectations that the state should provide better housing, health and employment.

  • The Beveridge Report of 1942 shaped discussion of a post-war welfare state, and Northern Ireland followed British legislation after 1945 under the policy of parity.
  • The destruction of housing made housing an urgent political issue after the war.
  • In February 1943 Jack Beattie won a Belfast West by-election at Westminster against the Unionist candidate, a sign of working-class discontent.
  • Labour candidates made gains in 1945, but the Unionist Party held power at Stormont.
  • The Education Act (Northern Ireland) 1947 and Health Services Act of 1948 came out of the wartime reform climate.
  • Sectarian divisions remained, and the Unionist government made no changes to the electoral system or policing.

Belfast in 1945 compared with 1939

The war changed Belfast's economy and politics but not its religious division.

  • Unemployment, high in 1939, was low in 1945 because of war contracts.
  • Large areas of housing had been destroyed or damaged, and many families lived in temporary housing.
  • Industries such as shipbuilding and aircraft were running at full capacity, but depended on wartime orders.
  • The Unionist Party was still in control, though challenged by Labour and criticised for the failures of civil defence.
  • The links with Britain were stronger, because of wartime co-operation and the promise of welfare reform.
  • Catholic and Protestant communities remained separate, and the border and the division from the South were more entrenched.

Assessment: the war's effects on Northern Ireland

Judgements depend on whether economic and social change or political continuity is stressed.

  • Achievement: war production ended mass unemployment and raised living standards for many workers.
  • Achievement: the war strengthened Northern Ireland's place in the United Kingdom and prepared it to take part in the welfare state.
  • Limit: the Blitz exposed poor preparation and housing, and the government had to answer for it.
  • Limit: the Unionist Party's control and the Catholic minority's exclusion were unchanged.
  • Contemporary view: unionists praised the contribution, and nationalists were critical of both the war role and the unionist government.
  • Later view: historians such as Brian Barton and Jonathan Bardon argue that the war was a turning point for the economy and social policy but not for politics.

Key terms

Neutrality
Seen from the North, the South's neutrality set Ireland apart from the British war effort and strengthened unionist arguments against unity.
Ulster Unionism
The loyalist political tradition that presented the war effort as proof of Northern Ireland's British identity.
Belfast Blitz
The German air raids on Belfast in April and May 1941, which killed about 1,000 people.
Ditchers
People who left Belfast at night after the raids to sleep in the countryside.
Evacuation
The removal of children and mothers from Belfast to rural areas after the raids.
Harland and Wolff
The Belfast shipyard that built and repaired warships and merchant ships.
Beveridge Report
The 1942 British report proposing a welfare state, which shaped post-war reform in Northern Ireland.
Andrews and Brooke governments
The wartime Unionist governments, led by John Miller Andrews (1940–43) and Basil Brooke (1943–63).
