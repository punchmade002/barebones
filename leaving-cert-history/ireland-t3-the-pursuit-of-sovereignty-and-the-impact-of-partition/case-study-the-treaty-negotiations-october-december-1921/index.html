Background and causes: from Truce to conference, July–October 1921

The Truce of 11 July 1921 opened talks about Ireland's status. Months of correspondence were needed before a formal conference could meet, and the central question was already clear: the Crown.

  • Lloyd George had invited de Valera to talks on 24 June 1921, after George V's call for reconciliation at Belfast, and the Truce came into force at noon on 11 July.
  • De Valera met Lloyd George in London four times between 14 and 21 July 1921, and neither side could shift the other on principle.
  • The British proposal of 20 July offered dominion status with restrictions: British control of defence and the ports, free trade, and no change to the position of the six counties.
  • The Dáil cabinet rejected the offer on 10 August; de Valera's counter-proposal was ‘external association’, in which Ireland would be a separate republic linked to the British Commonwealth, with the Crown recognised only as head of the association.
  • In an exchange of letters through the late summer Lloyd George insisted that Ireland accept the Crown and remain within the Empire, and de Valera insisted on the right of self-determination.
  • Lloyd George's invitation of 29 September proposed a conference to see how the association of Ireland with the Empire might be reconciled with Irish national aspirations; the Irish accepted it, and the conference opened on 11 October 1921.
  • Britain wanted a settlement because the war was costing money and reputation, the Empire's dominion premiers urged compromise, and the cabinet knew that a full-scale reconquest would need a very large army and martial law over the whole island.
  • The Irish side wanted a settlement because the IRA was short of arms and ammunition, and because of the cost to civilians in the war zones.

The delegations and the plenipotentiary issue

The decision to send plenipotentiaries and keep de Valera in Dublin shaped the whole outcome and the debate about it afterwards.

  • The Irish delegation was agreed by the Dáil cabinet in September 1921: Arthur Griffith (chairman), Michael Collins, Robert Barton, George Gavan Duffy and Éamonn Duggan.
  • Erskine Childers was the delegation's chief secretary, with Diarmuid O'Hegarty, John Chartres and others as staff.
  • The delegation lived at 22 Hans Place in Knightsbridge, while Collins stayed separately at Cadogan Gardens.
  • De Valera stayed in Dublin, saying that as head of state he should be held in reserve and that he had to keep the more extreme republicans on side; Cathal Brugha and Austin Stack also stayed at home.
  • Critics, including Collins, argued that it was a mistake: the man with most authority and most experience of Lloyd George was absent and later disowned the result.
  • The delegates' credentials described them as plenipotentiaries, which meant they had full power to negotiate and sign.
  • Their written instructions were different: they should report to Dublin on the text of any proposed treaty before signing; the exact meaning of this instruction was later disputed.
  • The British team was Lloyd George (Prime Minister), Austen Chamberlain (Lord Privy Seal), Lord Birkenhead (Lord Chancellor), Winston Churchill (Colonial Secretary), Sir Laming Worthington-Evans (War Secretary), Sir Hamar Greenwood (Chief Secretary for Ireland) and the Attorney General, Gordon Hewart.
  • Lionel Curtis and the cabinet secretary Tom Jones acted as advisers and go-betweens for the British.

The negotiations: issues and sub-conferences, October–November 1921

Plenary sessions at Downing Street gave way to small sub-conferences where Griffith and Collins argued about the Crown, Ulster, defence and finance.

  • The plenary sessions of 11–24 October produced no agreement, and after that small groups met, so most of the bargaining was done privately.
  • The Crown and the oath: the British demanded an oath of allegiance to the Crown and membership of the Commonwealth, and the Irish tried to substitute external association and an oath to the constitution.
  • Ulster: the Irish argued that Ireland was one nation and that the six counties should not be allowed to opt out; Lloyd George said he could not coerce them, and he suggested a Boundary Commission, which Griffith and Collins took as a way of reducing the North so much that it could not survive.
  • Defence: Britain demanded control of Irish ports and coastal defence for naval reasons, which the Irish saw as a limit on sovereignty.
  • Trade and finance: the British wanted free trade between the two islands and a share of the British public debt, while the Irish claimed offsetting counter-claims for overtaxation.
  • Craig and the Northern government refused to join the talks, and Lloyd George tried to extract concessions from Craig on the basis of a united Ireland within the Empire.
  • The delegates travelled to Dublin to consult the cabinet in November and December, so the process was not entirely independent of de Valera and the cabinet.
  • Throughout, Tom Jones and Lionel Curtis passed messages between Lloyd George and Griffith, and Collins formed a good relationship with Birkenhead.

The crisis and the signing, 1–6 December 1921

The final week was a negotiation under deadline pressure. The decision to sign without referring back to Dublin is the central controversy.

  • On 2 December Griffith privately gave Lloyd George a written assurance that he would not allow the talks to break on the Ulster question, provided the Boundary Commission was set up; Lloyd George later treated this as a commitment and republicans saw it as a concession made without the cabinet's knowledge.
  • At a cabinet meeting in Dublin on 3 December the cabinet instructed the delegates not to accept the oath as drafted, and the delegates returned to London with that line.
  • On 4 and 5 December the British pressed for signature; on 5 December Lloyd George told Griffith and Collins that there would be a break and war unless the Treaty was signed that night, and he said his courier was ready to take letters to Craig either way.
  • Barton later recalled that Lloyd George warned of ‘immediate and terrible war’, and the British minutes described a threat of war within days; whether the threat was a bluff is still disputed.
  • The delegates returned to Hans Place, where Collins and Griffith argued that the alternative to signing was war, and Barton, who had at first refused, gave way.
  • The Treaty was signed at Downing Street at about 2.10 am on 6 December 1921 by all five Irish delegates, with the British team.
  • Collins said afterwards that he had signed his own death warrant, a remark he made in private letters of that month.
  • De Valera was not told in advance; he learned of the signing on 6 December and was angry that the delegation had not sent the final text to Dublin.

The terms of the Treaty

The Treaty created the Irish Free State as a self-governing dominion in the Empire, with the Crown, an oath, British naval bases and partition qualified by a Boundary Commission.

  • Article 1: the Irish Free State was to have the same constitutional status as Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa.
  • The King was represented by a Governor-General, and the Free State's parliament and government were to be responsible to the Dáil.
  • The oath: members of the Free State parliament had to swear to be faithful to the Constitution and to the King, ‘in virtue of the common citizenship of Ireland with Great Britain’.
  • Defence: Britain would defend the coasts, and kept the use of the ports of Berehaven, Queenstown (Cobh) and Lough Swilly, with the right to use others in war; the Free State army was to be limited in proportion to population.
  • Finance: the Free State took on a share of the British public debt, subject to counter-claims; the Treaty said nothing of land annuities.
  • Ulster: Northern Ireland could opt out of the Free State within one month, and a Boundary Commission would then settle the border in line with the wishes of inhabitants, as far as compatible with economic and geographic conditions.
  • A Provisional Government would take over from the British administration until the Free State was established.
  • The Treaty had to be approved by the Dáil, and by the British Parliament; it was also to be ratified by a meeting of the House of Parliament of Southern Ireland.

The Dáil debates and outcome, December 1921–January 1922

The Dáil debated the Treaty at length. The split that followed divided cabinet, army and families, and led to the Civil War.

  • On 8 December the Dáil cabinet met and divided four to three in favour of the Treaty; de Valera, Brugha and Stack were against.
  • De Valera issued a statement to the press saying that he could not recommend the Treaty to the Dáil.
  • The Dáil debated in private from 14 to 17 December, where de Valera put forward his own alternative text, Document No. 2, based on external association; it was never presented to the British.
  • The public debate ran from 19 December to 10 January 1922, with an adjournment over Christmas; speakers included Griffith, Collins, Brugha, Mary MacSwiney and Childers.
  • Collins described the Treaty as giving ‘the freedom to achieve freedom’, a stepping stone to full independence, while Brugha attacked Collins's reputation.
  • Opponents argued that the oath and the Crown betrayed the republic proclaimed in 1916 and declared in 1919; supporters argued that the people did not want more war.
  • All six women deputies voted against the Treaty.
  • The Dáil approved the Treaty on 7 January 1922 by 64 votes to 57.
  • De Valera resigned as President on 9 January and lost a vote to be re-elected, and Griffith became President of the Dáil on 10 January.
  • A Provisional Government under Collins was formed on 14 January and took over from the British administration in Dublin Castle on 16 January.

Key personality: Arthur Griffith

Griffith was the chairman of the delegation, the senior negotiator and the first president of the Dáil to head a pro-Treaty government.

  • Griffith (1871–1922) was a Dublin printer and journalist who founded the United Irishman and, in 1905, Sinn Féin; he advocated a dual monarchy and economic self-reliance.
  • He was not a participant in the Rising but was interned afterwards, and he stepped aside for de Valera as Sinn Féin president in 1917.
  • He was acting head of the Dáil during de Valera's American tour in 1919–20 and was Minister for Foreign Affairs in 1921.
  • In London he led the negotiations on the Crown and Ulster, and he was prepared to accept dominion status within the Empire with the Boundary Commission.
  • He was a shy, scholarly man, in poor health after months in prison, and he was widely seen as the most honest of the delegates.
  • After his election as President of the Dáil in January 1922 he led the pro-Treaty side until his death from a cerebral haemorrhage on 12 August 1922.
  • Contemporaries: supporters of the Treaty praised his courage and integrity; republicans condemned him as having abandoned the republic.
  • Historians: Michael Laffan and Colum Kenny stress his role in creating the political movement that won independence and his realism in 1921.

Key personality: Michael Collins (role in the negotiations)

Collins was reluctant to go to London, but his reputation as the man who had defeated the British gave the delegation influence and made it hard for the Dáil to reject the result.

  • Collins was Minister for Finance and head of the IRB, and he warned the cabinet that he would be a poor negotiator; he believed that his name was needed for the military side but that the politicians should lead.
  • In London he handled the military and financial issues and built relationships with Birkenhead and Churchill.
  • He accepted the Boundary Commission clause in the expectation that Tyrone, Fermanagh and parts of Armagh and Down would be transferred, and he believed Northern Ireland could not survive after that.
  • He argued on the night of 5 December that the IRA could not win another round and that the Treaty gave the means to build up the army and the state.
  • He saw the Treaty as giving freedom to achieve freedom, and he signed it as a first step.
  • He was chosen as chairman of the Provisional Government in January 1922; his death in August 1922 is described in the notes on the Civil War.
  • Contemporaries: anti-Treaty republicans accused him of using his IRB position to override the cabinet, while his supporters said he was honest about the Treaty's limits.
  • Historians: Tim Pat Coogan sees him as a pragmatic realist, while Peter Hart draws attention to his ruthlessness.

Key personality: Éamon de Valera (role in the negotiations)

De Valera set the strategy and then rejected the outcome. Historians still argue about whether his decision to stay at home was shrewd or a mistake.

  • De Valera's talks with Lloyd George in July 1921 showed that the British would not allow an Irish republic, yet he remained committed to external association.
  • He chose the delegation and wrote its instructions, but did not join it; his reasons were to keep a power in reserve and to avoid committing the head of state.
  • He sent messages to London advising delegates to hold out on the Crown and oath, and kept in touch with Dublin cabinet colleagues.
  • After the signing he opposed the Treaty on the grounds that the delegates had signed without the cabinet's approval of the final text.
  • His Document No. 2 would have accepted the Crown as head of an association with the Commonwealth in the matter of common concern, with an oath to the constitution only; the Dáil did not adopt it.
  • Contemporaries: supporters of the Treaty said that he had set a trap for Collins and Griffith, and republicans said that he was upholding the Republic.
  • Historians: Lord Longford's Peace by Ordeal (1935) defended the delegates, while later biographers such as Coogan and Ferriter see him as a politician who used ambiguity to hold the movement together.

Assessment: significance and differing views of the Treaty

Judgements depend on whether the Treaty is measured against the republic of 1916 and 1919 or against the realistic alternatives of December 1921.

  • Achievement: Britain withdrew its army and administration from 26 counties, and the Free State got its own parliament, police, army and civil service.
  • Limit: the Treaty kept the Crown, the oath, British bases and partition, which republicans said betrayed the declared republic.
  • Pro-Treaty view: it gave the freedom to achieve freedom, and a refusal would have meant war in 1922 with the IRA short of arms.
  • Anti-Treaty view: the delegates were bullied into signing, and the Dáil had no right to give away the republic.
  • Public opinion: most newspapers, the Catholic bishops, farmers and business supported the Treaty, and the pro-Treaty side won about three-quarters of the votes in the June 1922 election.
  • Unionists saw the Treaty as removing the threat to the North, but the Boundary Commission disappointed nationalists in 1925.
  • Later view: historians debate whether the delegates were out-manoeuvred, whether the ultimatum was a bluff, and whether de Valera's absence made a bad outcome more likely.

Key terms

Dominion status
Self-government within the British Empire on the model of Canada, which the Treaty gave the Irish Free State.
Allegiance
Loyalty owed to the Crown; the Treaty oath tied members of the Free State parliament to the Constitution and to the King.
Sovereignty
Supreme power over a state's own affairs, which republicans said the Treaty limited with the Crown, the oath and British naval bases.
External association
De Valera's proposal that Ireland be an independent republic associated with the Commonwealth, with the Crown recognised only as head of the association.
Plenipotentiary
A negotiator with full power to act for his government, the status given to the five Irish delegates.
Treaty ports
Berehaven, Queenstown (Cobh) and Lough Swilly, naval bases which Britain kept under the Treaty.
Boundary Commission
The commission the Treaty provided for to settle the border between Northern Ireland and the Free State.
Oath of allegiance
The oath to the Constitution and faithfulness to the King required of members of the Free State parliament.
Document No. 2
De Valera's alternative to the Treaty, put to the Dáil in December 1921 but never adopted.
Provisional Government
The body chaired by Collins from 14 January 1922 that took over from the British administration before the Free State was formed.
