Irish language policy in the Free State

The Free State made the revival of Irish a central aim of cultural policy and used schools and the civil service to achieve it.

  • Irish was made a compulsory subject in national schools in 1922, and many infant classes were taught wholly through Irish.
  • A pass in Irish became necessary for State examinations at second level and for entry to the civil service and the Garda, so that language knowledge was tied to jobs.
  • Ernest Blythe, Minister for Finance, was a strong advocate, and the civil service was required to learn Irish.
  • The Gaeltacht Commission was set up in 1925 and reported in 1926, defining the Irish-speaking districts and recommending economic and educational support.
  • An Gúm was founded in 1926 to publish books in Irish, including translations of foreign works, to supply school texts and readers.
  • Taibhdhearc na Gaillimhe, an Irish-language theatre, opened in Galway in 1928.
  • Preparatory colleges were set up to train teachers who could teach through Irish.
  • Irish was recognised as the first official language by the 1937 constitution.

Results of the language policy

The policy raised the number of people who could say they knew Irish, but few used it in daily life.

  • Census returns showed a rise in the share of the population able to speak Irish, from about 18 per cent in 1926 to about a quarter in 1936, mostly as a result of schooling.
  • The Gaeltacht, the real Irish-speaking districts, continued to shrink because of emigration and poverty.
  • Many parents and teachers resented the compulsion, and some pupils learned little.
  • Irish remained a symbol of national identity, and the state kept it as a school subject even where it failed as a spoken language.

Education and the Catholic Church in the South

The Church ran most schools, and the state accepted its role.

  • National schools were managed by clergy of the local denomination, and the state paid salaries and set the curriculum.
  • The School Attendance Act of 1926 made school attendance compulsory for children from 6 to 14.
  • Secondary schools were mostly fee-paying and run by religious orders, and few working-class children reached them.
  • The Vocational Education Act of 1930 set up vocational committees to provide technical education.
  • The Church influenced the content of teaching, and the 1937 constitution recognised the family and the Church's role.
  • The Catholic Archbishop of Dublin, John Charles McQuaid, from 1940 was an influential voice on social and educational issues.

Catholic ethos, censorship and conformity in the South

Legislation reflected Catholic teaching on morality, and critics saw the result as an enforced conformity.

  • The Censorship of Films Act of 1923 set up a film censor who could cut or ban films.
  • In 1925 the Dáil changed its standing orders so that private bills for divorce could no longer be introduced; W.B. Yeats made a famous speech in the Seanad in defence of the Protestant minority.
  • The Censorship of Publications Act of 1929 set up a board with the power to ban books and periodicals considered indecent or immoral.
  • Among the writers banned were Samuel Beckett (More Pricks Than Kicks, 1934) and, later, Kate O'Brien and Seán Ó Faoláin.
  • The Public Dance Halls Act of 1935 required licences, in response to clerical concerns about dances.
  • The Criminal Law Amendment Act of 1935 banned the sale and import of contraceptives.
  • Article 41 of the 1937 constitution banned divorce, and Article 44 recognised the special position of the Catholic Church.
  • The Protestant population of the Free State fell in the 1920s and 1930s, partly through emigration, the Ne Temere rule on mixed marriages and Civil War violence.

Cultural institutions and identity in the Free State

The state promoted a national culture through games, theatre and broadcasting, and the GAA enforced a cultural boundary.

  • The Tailteann Games were held in Dublin in 1924, 1928 and 1932, a festival of sport and the arts modelled on the ancient games, and open to people of Irish descent abroad.
  • The Abbey Theatre received a state subsidy from 1925, the first for a national theatre in the English-speaking world.
  • The Gate Theatre was founded in 1928 by Hilton Edwards and Micheál Mac Liammóir to stage international plays.
  • The state radio station 2RN began broadcasting from Dublin on 1 January 1926, and a high-power transmitter at Athlone from 1932 was followed by the name Radio Éireann in 1938.
  • The Irish Folklore Commission was established in 1935 under Séamus Ó Duilearga, and the Schools' Collection of 1937–38 had children collect local folklore.
  • The GAA's Rule 27, in the rulebook from 1905, banned members from playing foreign games such as soccer and rugby, and was later extended to attending them; it was repealed in 1971.
  • In 1938 the GAA removed President Douglas Hyde as patron after he attended an international soccer match in Dublin.
  • Culture was linked to nationalism by the idea of a Gaelic and Catholic Ireland, which excluded many Protestants and urban people.

Education in Northern Ireland

Education was contested between the Unionist government, which wanted a non-denominational state system, and the churches.

  • The Lynn Committee was appointed in 1921 to review education under the first Minister of Education, Lord Londonderry, and reported in 1923.
  • The Education Act (Northern Ireland) 1923, known as the Londonderry Act, created county education committees and placed schools under local education authorities, with public elementary schools to be free of church control.
  • Protestant churches objected to the exclusion of religious instruction, and the Act was amended in 1925 to allow Bible instruction, and the Orange Order and the Protestant clergy gained a voice in management.
  • The Catholic Church refused to transfer its schools to state control, and Catholic schools stayed outside the system with only partial public funding.
  • The 1930 Amendment Act allowed transferred Protestant schools to have four representatives of the transferors and two of the local authority on their committees, which Catholics saw as favouring Protestant interests.
  • The result was a divided school system, which remained a source of segregation and a grievance for Catholics.

Cultural identity in the North

Unionist and nationalist communities expressed separate identities in a state that favoured the first.

  • Orange Order parades on 12 July and loyalist bands were central to unionist culture and enjoyed official protection.
  • The Protestant ethos of the state was symbolised by the flag, the Union Jack, and by the royal visits and ceremonies at Stormont.
  • Nationalists supported the GAA, Irish-language classes and Catholic schools, but the Irish language received little state support.
  • Under the Special Powers Act the authorities could ban processions and republican emblems, and nationalist parades were more likely to be banned.
  • Craig's statement of 1934 that Northern Ireland was a Protestant state was matched in the South by the Catholic emphasis of the 1937 constitution.

Key personality: Evie Hone

Hone was a leading modernist painter who turned to stained glass and became the best-known Irish stained-glass artist of her time.

  • Evie Hone (1894–1955) was born in Dublin, and studied art in London and later in Paris under André Lhote and Albert Gleizes.
  • With Mainie Jellett she was one of the first Irish painters to work in a cubist style.
  • In the 1930s she turned to stained glass, learned from Wilhelmina Geddes, and worked with An Túr Gloine, the studio founded by Sarah Purser in 1903 to revive Irish stained glass.
  • She converted to Catholicism in 1937, and much of her work was for churches.
  • My Four Green Fields, a window symbolising the four provinces, was made for the Irish pavilion at the 1939 New York World's Fair and won first prize for stained glass.
  • The Crucifixion east window of Eton College Chapel was commissioned in 1949 to replace glass destroyed by a bomb in 1940, and was installed in 1952.
  • Contemporaries praised her work as a modern Irish art with international standing.
  • Historians such as Nicola Gordon Bowe place her in the movement to give Ireland a modern cultural identity after independence.

Assessment: cultural policy, achievements and limits

Cultural policy gave the new state a distinct identity, but it also narrowed the range of what was acceptable.

  • Achievement: institutions such as An Gúm, the Folklore Commission, the Abbey and Radio Éireann preserved and promoted Irish culture.
  • Limit: compulsory Irish did not make it the daily language of most people, and the Gaeltacht continued to decline.
  • Achievement: arts and crafts, including the work of Hone, flourished with state and church patronage.
  • Limit: censorship and moral legislation restricted writers and individual choice, and minorities felt excluded.
  • In the North the divided education system and separate cultural traditions deepened the communal division.
  • Contemporary view: supporters saw cultural policy as defending Irish identity; critics, including writers such as Ó Faoláin, saw a narrow and conformist vision.
  • Later view: historians stress the tension between revival and restriction, and the influence of the Church.

Key terms

Conformity
The pressure to follow accepted Catholic and Gaelic values, enforced through law, schools and public opinion.
Censorship
State control of films from 1923 and of publications from 1929 through boards with power to ban.
Discrimination
In the North, the unequal treatment of Catholics, including in schools, where Catholic schools were funded less than state schools.
Gaeltacht Commission
The 1925–26 body that defined Irish-speaking districts and recommended support for them.
An Gúm
The state publishing agency for Irish-language books, founded in 1926.
Irish Folklore Commission
The body set up in 1935 to collect and archive oral tradition.
Tailteann Games
The sporting and cultural festival held in Dublin in 1924, 1928 and 1932.
An Túr Gloine
The Dublin stained-glass studio founded by Sarah Purser in 1903, where Hone worked.
Lynn Committee
The committee that reviewed Northern Ireland's education before the 1923 Act.
Rule 27
The GAA rule banning members from foreign games, in force from 1905 until 1971.
