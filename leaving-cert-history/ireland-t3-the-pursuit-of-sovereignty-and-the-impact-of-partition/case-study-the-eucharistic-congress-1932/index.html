Background and causes: what a Eucharistic Congress is and why Dublin

The Eucharistic Congress was an international Catholic gathering, held in Dublin in 1932 to mark an anniversary that tied Catholicism to Irish identity.

  • An International Eucharistic Congress is a gathering of Catholics from many countries to honour the Eucharist, the sacrament in which Catholics believe bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ; the first was held in 1881 and each is held in a different city.
  • The 31st Congress was awarded to Dublin, and 1932 was chosen as the 1,500th anniversary of the traditional date of St Patrick's arrival in Ireland in 432.
  • The invitation and the early planning were made while Cosgrave's government was in office, and the Church and the state treated the event as a national occasion.
  • Archbishop Edward Byrne of Dublin headed the organising effort, and the Pope sent a legate, Cardinal Lorenzo Lauri.
  • The Congress was an opportunity for the new state to show itself to the world, ten years after independence and a civil war.
  • The Catholic Church had a strong position in Irish life: the vast majority of the population was Catholic, and clergy managed schools and hospitals.

Preparations and the change of government

The Congress was planned under Cosgrave, but it took place under de Valera, who had come to power in March 1932.

  • Fianna Fáil won the February 1932 election and de Valera became head of government on 9 March 1932, three months before the Congress.
  • De Valera, who was known for his personal piety, took a leading part and his government gave the planning full support.
  • Dublin was prepared with altars, decorations, flags and illuminations, and the streets of poorer districts were decorated too.
  • A large altar was built in the Phoenix Park for the final Mass, and the arrangements for transport, accommodation and crowd control were extensive.
  • A radio station was set up at Athlone to coincide with the Congress, so that the ceremonies and the Pope's message could reach listeners around the country.
  • Visitors came from the United States, Britain and Europe, and thousands of pilgrims came from Northern Ireland.
  • A national committee raised money by public subscription, and parishes organised local groups for the processions.
  • Thousands of volunteers acted as stewards, and special first-aid and catering services were arranged for the Phoenix Park.

Church, state and the Governor-General

The Congress showed close co-operation between the Catholic hierarchy and the new Fianna Fáil government, and it exposed the unresolved question of the Crown's representative.

  • De Valera and Archbishop Byrne received Cardinal Lauri at Dún Laoghaire on his arrival on 21 June 1932, and he was escorted through crowds to Dublin.
  • Some 36,000 schoolchildren lined the route from Dún Laoghaire to the Pro-Cathedral.
  • The Governor-General, James McNeill, representing the Crown, was not invited to the reception for the legate at Dublin Castle, and press reports say that Fianna Fáil ministers would have walked out if he had attended.
  • McNeill responded by holding a dinner to which de Valera and the ministers were not invited, and wrote to de Valera on 7 July 1932 complaining of discourtesy.
  • The affair was part of the conflict that led to McNeill's dismissal in October 1932.
  • De Valera used the occasion to present his government as both Catholic and independent of the Crown.

The events of 22–26 June 1932

Five days of Masses, processions and services culminated in an open-air Mass in the Phoenix Park and a procession to O'Connell Bridge.

  • The Congress ran from 22 to 26 June 1932 and opened at the Pro-Cathedral in Dublin.
  • On 25 June a children's Mass was held in the Phoenix Park, attended by around 100,000 children.
  • On Sunday 26 June the closing Mass was celebrated at an altar in the Phoenix Park before a crowd often put at around a million, though the estimate may be exaggerated; it is described as about a quarter of the population of the state.
  • John McCormack, the famous Irish tenor, sang César Franck's Panis Angelicus at the Mass.
  • A message from Pope Pius XI was broadcast live by radio from the Vatican to the crowd.
  • After the Mass four processions went from the park to O'Connell Bridge, where Cardinal Lauri gave the closing benediction to a crowd of up to half a million people.
  • The legate's party left, and the Congress ended on 26 June with large crowds along the quays.
  • Ceremonies during the week included Masses in city churches and a procession of the Blessed Sacrament.
  • Cardinal Lauri addressed the crowds and the Pope's blessing was conveyed to the people of Ireland.
  • The closing Mass began at 1 pm on 26 June at an altar designed by the architect John J. Robinson, and the celebrant was Archbishop Michael Curley of Baltimore.
  • Cardinal Lauri, who had been made a cardinal in 1926, presided as the Pope's representative throughout.

Organisation, media and the image of the Free State

The size of the event tested the state's administration and brought international attention.

  • Special trains, buses and sailings brought crowds from all over Ireland and abroad, and the Garda and the Army managed the crowds.
  • The use of loudspeakers allowed huge crowds to hear the ceremonies, and radio carried the broadcast to the country.
  • Foreign newspapers reported the event, and it gave the Free State international publicity as a Catholic country.
  • The event was a success for de Valera, whose prestige grew at home and abroad.
  • The Congress passed off without major disorder in the Free State, which was a strong demonstration of public order only ten years after the Civil War.
  • Archbishop Byrne and the organising committee had to coordinate bishops from around the world, and papal flags and Irish flags were flown together.
  • The Athlone station that began broadcasting for the Congress later became Radio Éireann, in 1938.
  • Reports at the time and since claimed that the crowds exceeded those at Pope John Paul II's Phoenix Park Mass in 1979.

Northern Ireland and unionist reactions

In the North the Congress was seen very differently, and it highlighted the religious divide on the island.

  • Catholic pilgrims travelling by train and bus to and from Dublin were attacked in Northern Ireland; trains were stoned at places such as Portadown, Lisburn and Belfast, and at Ballymena a special train was attacked and some pilgrims were injured.
  • Police made baton charges at some stations to protect pilgrims.
  • Unionists saw the Congress as proof that the Free State was a Catholic state, which reinforced their view that partition was necessary.
  • Protestant newspapers and Orange leaders criticised the scale of Catholic display, and the attacks on pilgrims added to sectarian tension.
  • For Northern nationalists, the Congress was a source of pride and reinforced their sense of belonging to an Irish Catholic nation.

Significance and differing views

The Congress is usually assessed through three themes: relations between Church and state, Catholic identity, and Ireland's image abroad.

  • Church and state: it showed the alliance between the hierarchy and the political leadership which shaped the 1937 constitution and social legislation.
  • Catholic identity: it presented Ireland as a Catholic nation, in line with the Gaelic and Catholic ideal of the 1930s.
  • International image: it presented the Free State as stable, devout and independent, and made de Valera well known abroad.
  • Sovereignty: the Governor-General dispute showed the government's desire to downgrade the Crown's representative.
  • Unionist view: it confirmed fears of a state dominated by the Catholic Church.
  • Contemporary view: the Catholic press and most of the public regarded it as a great national triumph.
  • Later view: historians describe it as the high point of the Church's public influence, and a sign of the conformity that marked the 1930s; some stress the Congress as a political success for de Valera.
  • De Valera's critics in Cumann na nGaedheal noted that the Congress had been planned by Cosgrave's government but that Fianna Fáil gained the credit.
  • The Congress also gave de Valera's new government a chance to show it could work with the Church.
  • The Congress is often compared with the 1979 papal visit as the largest religious gathering in Irish history.

Assessment: significance of the Congress

The Congress was a great public success, but its effect was largely symbolic.

  • Achievement: it showed the state could organise a major international event soon after the Civil War.
  • Achievement: it united people across party lines at a time of political change.
  • Limit: it did not change any laws or institutions, and its influence was cultural and political.
  • Limit: it deepened the distrust between North and South, and Catholic and Protestant, on the island.
  • Contemporary view: a triumph for the Church and the nation.
  • Later view: a moment when church, state and nation appeared identical, which later generations judged more critically.

Key terms

Eucharistic Congress
An international Catholic gathering to honour the Eucharist; the 31st was held in Dublin on 22–26 June 1932.
Eucharist
The Catholic sacrament in which bread and wine are believed to become the body and blood of Christ.
Papal legate
The Pope's personal representative at the Congress, Cardinal Lorenzo Lauri.
Conformity
The pressure to share the dominant Catholic values of the state, expressed in events such as the Congress.
Sovereignty
The Free State's independent standing, shown by its international profile and by the dispute over the Governor-General.
Church–state relations
The close understanding between the Catholic hierarchy and the government in the 1930s.
Phoenix Park Mass
The closing open-air Mass on 26 June 1932, attended by hundreds of thousands.
Governor-General
The Crown's representative in the Free State, James McNeill in 1932, who was snubbed during the Congress.
