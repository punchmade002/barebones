Neutrality: the decision and its reasons

Ireland stayed out of the Second World War, which the government called the Emergency. Neutrality was seen as the proof of independent statehood.

  • The Dáil declared a state of national emergency on 2 September 1939, and Britain declared war on Germany on 3 September.
  • Neutrality demonstrated sovereignty: a state that followed Britain into war would look like a dominion in practice.
  • Partition was a reason: Ireland could not ally with Britain while Britain held Northern Ireland.
  • The return of the Treaty ports in 1938 made neutrality possible, since Britain no longer held the bases.
  • The Defence Forces were small and poorly equipped, and bombing of Irish cities was feared.
  • Civil War divisions meant that joining the war might have split the country again.
  • Most of the public supported neutrality, and the main parties, Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael and Labour, accepted it, though James Dillon of Fine Gael argued for entering the war on the Allied side.
  • Neutrality was a benevolent one in practice, leaning towards Britain, but it was never formally broken.

The Emergency Powers Act and censorship

The government took wide powers over the economy and public opinion, and used them to control information and public debate.

  • The Emergency Powers Act was passed on 3 September 1939 and let ministers make orders with the force of law, covering prices, supplies, movement and censorship.
  • Frank Aiken was Minister for the Co-ordination of Defensive Measures and was responsible for censorship.
  • Press censorship forbade reports on troop movements, ship sinkings, weather and criticism of neutrality.
  • Weather forecasts were banned from the radio and the press, since they might help the belligerents.
  • Letters and telegrams were opened and censored, and the Censorship of Publications Board, active since 1929, continued to ban books.
  • Radio Éireann avoided comment that might offend any belligerent, and war news was heavily controlled.
  • The war was called the Emergency, and the censorship policy also served to maintain public conformity with the official line.

Defence Forces, the Local Defence Force and the IRA

The government expanded the army and created reserves for defence against invasion, while dealing firmly with the IRA.

  • The regular army grew from under 20,000 men to about 40,000 by 1941, with Reserve forces added.
  • The Local Defence Force (LDF) was founded in June 1940 after the fall of France as a volunteer reserve, and tens of thousands joined.
  • The Local Security Force worked with the Garda in civil defence.
  • The government made secret contingency plans for co-operation with Britain if Germany invaded.
  • The IRA was banned in 1936, and the Offences Against the State Act of 1939 reinforced the law against it.
  • The IRA's S-Plan bombing campaign in Britain began in January 1939; the Coventry bomb of August 1939 killed five people.
  • On 23 December 1939 the IRA raided the Magazine Fort in the Phoenix Park and removed the army's reserve ammunition, and the government recovered most of it.
  • The Offences Against the State (Amendment) Act of 1940 introduced internment without trial, and hundreds of IRA men were held at the Curragh.
  • Several IRA men were executed during the war for the killing of Gardaí, and others died on hunger strike.

Supplies, rationing and the wartime economy

Shipping losses and British restrictions meant shortages of coal, petrol, tea and wheat. The government controlled production and distribution.

  • Seán Lemass was Minister for Supplies from 1939 to 1945, and the Department of Supplies controlled imports, prices and rationing.
  • Tea, sugar, butter, flour, bread and fuel were rationed, and petrol was restricted so that private motoring almost stopped.
  • Compulsory tillage orders required farmers to plough more land, and the wheat harvest increased, though bread became darker and coarser.
  • Coal imports collapsed, and turf production grew; huge turf stacks were built in the Phoenix Park and elsewhere, and trains ran on turf.
  • Gas supplies were limited to certain hours, and inspectors, known as glimmer men, checked on households.
  • Irish Shipping Ltd was set up in 1941 to buy and run ships, since British shipping was unavailable, and its ships sailed unescorted under the Irish flag.
  • Irish merchant seamen died when ships were sunk by submarines and aircraft despite their neutral markings.
  • The Wages Standstill Order of 1941 froze most wages while prices rose, so real incomes fell, and the Trade Union Act of 1941 was aimed at the unions.
  • Exports of cattle to Britain continued, and farmers gained from guaranteed prices for produce, though industry suffered from lack of raw materials.

Relations with Britain and the United States

Neutrality led to repeated pressure from Britain and, later, the United States.

  • In June 1940, after the fall of France, Malcolm MacDonald brought de Valera an offer: if Ireland joined the Allies, Britain would declare for a united Ireland in principle.
  • De Valera rejected the offer in early July 1940, because he doubted that Britain could deliver unity and because the Northern government was not consulted.
  • Churchill, who had opposed the return of the ports in 1938, publicly complained about the loss of the bases in the battle of the Atlantic.
  • The British considered occupying the ports in 1940–41 but decided against it.
  • The Irish government complained when Britain restricted supplies of coal, wheat and shipping to Ireland.
  • David Gray, the American Minister in Dublin, was strongly critical of neutrality, and the United States pressed the government after it entered the war in December 1941.
  • In February 1944 the United States and Britain presented the American Note, asking the government to expel the German and Japanese diplomats before the D-Day landings.
  • De Valera refused, and Britain restricted travel to Ireland in the following months.

Help to the Allies and the bombing of Dublin

In practice neutrality was benevolent towards the Allies. At the same time, the state suffered from German bombing.

  • Irish military intelligence (G2) shared information with British intelligence and the Garda watched German agents; the spy Hermann Görtz was caught and interned.
  • Allied aircraft were allowed to fly over Donegal to the Atlantic, and Allied airmen who crashed in Ireland were quietly released, while Germans were interned in the Curragh.
  • Weather reports from Blacksod Point lighthouse, Co. Mayo, in June 1944 contributed to the decision on the date of D-Day.
  • About 50,000 to 70,000 people from the Free State served in the British forces; soldiers who deserted from the Irish army to join them were barred from public employment in 1945.
  • German aircraft bombed Wexford (Campile creamery, August 1940, three killed) and other places.
  • On the night of 31 May 1941 German bombers dropped bombs on the North Strand in Dublin, killing about thirty people (estimates range from 28 to 34), and destroying or damaging many houses.
  • The government protested to Germany; Germany said it was an error, and West Germany paid compensation after the war.
  • Dublin and other fire brigades went north to help Belfast in April 1941, with the approval of de Valera.

The end of the war: Hitler's death and the Churchill exchange

De Valera's insistence on diplomatic correctness to the end brought a public attack from Churchill.

  • On 2 May 1945, after Hitler's death was announced, de Valera called on the German minister Eduard Hempel to express condolences.
  • He had paid a similar visit to the American Minister on the death of Roosevelt in April 1945.
  • De Valera defended the visit as diplomatic protocol that a neutral state had followed throughout the war, but many Irish and Allied commentators criticised it.
  • In his victory broadcast of 13 May 1945 Churchill attacked Irish neutrality and referred to the ports.
  • De Valera replied on Radio Éireann on 16 May, saying that Churchill's speech had shown how a great power might justify infringing a small state's rights, and that Ireland had stood alone.
  • The broadcast was widely praised in Ireland and strengthened de Valera's reputation, though it did not improve relations with Britain.

Emigration, war work and society

The war closed Irish ports and imposed austerity, but opened the British labour market.

  • Tens of thousands of workers went to Britain each year to work in factories and construction, and sent money home.
  • The Irish economy suffered from shortages, and unemployment remained high for those who stayed.
  • Prices rose faster than wages, and the poor in Dublin and other towns suffered most.
  • The government used its emergency powers to maintain control of the press and labour, with little organised protest.
  • The Children's Allowances Act of 1944 and the creation of a separate Department of Health (1947) showed the first steps in post-war social policy.
  • Many Irish women and men served in the British forces, and the Irish government gave them little recognition after the war.

Assessment: neutrality's achievements and costs

Judgements depend on whether the priority is sovereignty and survival or the moral and economic costs.

  • Achievement: the state avoided invasion and bombing on the scale of Belfast and kept its independence.
  • Achievement: neutrality was popular and strengthened national unity after the Civil War.
  • Limit: shortages, wage controls and censorship reduced living standards and freedom of expression.
  • Limit: the policy isolated Ireland from the Allies and left it without a voice at the end of the war.
  • Contemporary view: supporters called it a principled defence of independence; critics said it was selfish, as Churchill suggested.
  • Later view: historians such as Joe Lee and Brian Girvin stress the practical advantages of neutrality and its pro-Allied bias, but also question the moral cost.

Key terms

Neutrality
The policy of staying out of the war, adopted on 3 September 1939 as a demonstration of Irish sovereignty.
Emergency
The official Irish name for the Second World War period, during which special powers applied.
Emergency Powers Act
The 1939 Act giving ministers power to make orders with legal force over the economy, movement and censorship.
Censorship
Government control of the press, radio, letters and books during the Emergency, run through Frank Aiken's department.
Conformity
The pressure on citizens and media to follow the official line, strengthened by wartime censorship.
Local Defence Force
The part-time volunteer reserve founded in June 1940 to support the army.
Department of Supplies
The department under Lemass that controlled imports, prices and rationing during the war.
Irish Shipping Ltd
The state shipping company set up in 1941 to carry essential cargoes.
American Note
The request of February 1944 that Ireland expel Axis diplomats, which de Valera refused.
Internment
Detention without trial, used against the IRA at the Curragh under the 1940 Act.
