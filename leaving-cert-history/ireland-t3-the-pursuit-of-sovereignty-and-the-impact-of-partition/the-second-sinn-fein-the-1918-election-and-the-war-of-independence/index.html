The rise of the second Sinn Féin, 1917

Sinn Féin was a small party before 1916, but the British called the Rising the ‘Sinn Féin rebellion’ and survivors used the name. In 1917 it became the vehicle for separatists of every kind.

  • Arthur Griffith founded Sinn Féin in 1905; it stood for economic self-reliance, abstention from Westminster and a dual monarchy of Britain and Ireland on the Austro-Hungarian model.
  • Count Plunkett, father of the executed Joseph Plunkett, won the North Roscommon by-election in February 1917 as an independent separatist and then abstained from Westminster.
  • Joseph McGuinness, a prisoner in Lewes, won South Longford in May 1917 by 37 votes, helped by the slogan ‘Put him in to get him out’.
  • De Valera won East Clare in July 1917 by a large majority, and W.T. Cosgrave won Kilkenny City in August 1917.
  • Thomas Ashe, one of the 1916 leaders, died on 25 September 1917 after being forcibly fed during a hunger strike in Mountjoy, and his funeral drew huge crowds; Michael Collins gave the graveside oration.
  • At the Sinn Féin Ard Fheis of 25–26 October 1917 de Valera was elected president, with Griffith standing aside.
  • The new constitution was a compromise: the aim was an independent Irish republic, and once that was achieved the people could choose their own form of government.
  • Sinn Féin now drew in ex-Volunteers, the IRB, Gaelic Leaguers and many younger priests, and its clubs spread through the country; at the same time, de Valera was elected president of the Volunteers.

The conscription crisis and the German Plot, 1918

Conscription united nearly all Irish nationalist opinion against the government and gave Sinn Féin credit for leading the resistance.

  • The German spring offensive began in March 1918 and Britain was desperately short of men, so the Military Service Act of 18 April 1918 allowed conscription in Ireland.
  • An all-party anti-conscription conference met in Dublin on 18 April, with Dillon, de Valera, Griffith, William O'Brien, Tim Healy and Labour representatives, and the Catholic bishops, meeting at Maynooth, backed resistance.
  • A national pledge to resist conscription was signed at church doors on 21 April, and on 23 April a one-day general strike closed most of the country outside Belfast.
  • The government never applied conscription, but it replaced the Viceroy with Lord French and in May 1918 arrested about 70 Sinn Féin leaders on the claim of a ‘German Plot’, citing the landing of Joseph Dowling from a German submarine.
  • De Valera, Griffith, Count Plunkett and Markievicz were among those arrested and held in Britain, and Collins and Harry Boland avoided arrest.
  • The arrests removed the older leaders and strengthened the radicals; Sinn Féin won by-elections in the summer and Redmond's party, which had been weakened by Redmond's death on 6 March 1918, lost ground.

The 1918 general election

The first election after the end of the war was fought on a franchise that roughly tripled the electorate, and it transformed the Irish party system.

  • The Representation of the People Act 1918 gave the vote to all men over 21 and women over 30, and the Irish electorate rose from about 700,000 to about 1.9 million.
  • The election was held on 14 December 1918 and counted on 28 December.
  • Sinn Féin won 73 of Ireland's 105 seats; the Unionists won 26 and the Irish Parliamentary Party won only six.
  • Sinn Féin took about 47 per cent of the votes, and 25 of its seats were won without a contest.
  • The IPP kept some seats, such as Joe Devlin's in Belfast, thanks to an arrangement with Sinn Féin in a few constituencies.
  • Unionists won most of the seats in the north-east, and Belfast and Ulster supplied the evidence for partition.
  • Constance Markievicz won St Patrick's, Dublin, while in Holloway prison, and became the first woman elected to the House of Commons; she did not take her seat.
  • De Valera was elected for East Clare and East Mayo, and many successful Sinn Féin candidates were in prison.

The First Dáil and the counter-state, 1919–1921

Sinn Féin carried out its election promise to set up its own parliament and began to run a government in competition with Dublin Castle.

  • The First Dáil met at the Mansion House on 21 January 1919; only 27 of the elected deputies attended because many were in jail.
  • It adopted a Declaration of Independence, a message to the free nations of the world and the Democratic Programme, which put the nation's claim to its resources and the right of every citizen to a living above private property.
  • De Valera escaped from Lincoln Gaol on 3 February 1919 with the help of Collins, and was elected Príomh Aire on 1 April 1919.
  • The ministry included Griffith (Home Affairs, later Foreign Affairs), Collins (Finance), Cathal Brugha (Defence), Count Plunkett (Foreign Affairs) and Markievicz (Labour).
  • The Dáil Loan (the National Loan), organised by Collins, raised over £350,000 in Ireland and more than 5 million dollars in the United States.
  • Sinn Féin won most of the councils in the 1920 local elections, and local authorities then pledged loyalty to the Dáil.
  • The Dáil set up arbitration courts in 1919, which developed into Dáil courts from mid-1920, and republican police replaced the RIC in many areas.
  • In August 1920 the Volunteers swore an oath of allegiance to the Dáil, and from then on they were known as the Irish Republican Army (IRA).
  • The government banned the Dáil in September 1919, and in 1920 many of the republican ministers worked in hiding.

The IRA campaign, 1919–1921

The IRA waged a guerrilla war on the RIC and the army. A few thousand active men tied down many times their number.

  • The first shots came at Soloheadbeg, Co. Tipperary, on 21 January 1919, when Seán Treacy, Dan Breen and others killed two RIC constables guarding gelignite; the attack was not ordered by the Dáil.
  • The RIC withdrew from many isolated barracks in 1919–20, and the IRA burned the empty buildings and attacked others.
  • Active service units, known as flying columns, ambushed patrols and barracks; Tom Barry's West Cork column and Seán Hegarty's Cork forces were among the best known.
  • Collins was Director of Intelligence and used moles in Dublin Castle and the police, such as Ned Broy, to get information.
  • His Squad, formed in 1919, killed detectives of the Dublin Metropolitan Police's G Division and other agents.
  • Tomás Mac Curtain, Lord Mayor of Cork, was murdered by police in March 1920, and Terence MacSwiney, his successor, died on hunger strike in Brixton on 25 October 1920.
  • Kevin Barry, an 18-year-old medical student, was hanged on 1 November 1920 for his part in an ambush that killed three soldiers.
  • On Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920, the Squad killed 14 men, mostly British officers and intelligence agents, in Dublin.
  • That afternoon Auxiliaries and RIC opened fire on the crowd at a football match at Croke Park, killing 14 civilians, including a player, Michael Hogan.
  • At Kilmichael, Co. Cork, on 28 November 1920, Tom Barry's column killed 17 Auxiliaries and lost three men.
  • In May 1921 the IRA burned the Custom House in Dublin but lost five men killed and about 80 captured, a heavy blow to the Dublin Brigade.
  • About 2,000 people were killed in the war, including soldiers, police, IRA men and civilians.

British counter-measures and the road to the Truce, 1920–1921

The British added to the police and the army but could not defeat the IRA, and the reprisals cost them support at home and abroad.

  • The Black and Tans, recruits from ex-soldiers who were short of police uniforms and so wore mixed khaki and dark green, began arriving in March 1920; about 10,000 served.
  • The Auxiliary Division of the RIC (ADRIC), about 2,000 ex-officers, was formed in July 1920 and operated in companies with considerable independence.
  • The Restoration of Order in Ireland Act (August 1920) allowed courts-martial for civilians, and martial law was declared in several southern counties from December 1920.
  • Reprisals included the sacking of Balbriggan in September 1920 and the burning of Cork city centre on the night of 11–12 December 1920, after an IRA ambush at Dillon's Cross.
  • The Labour Party, the press and many Church leaders in Britain criticised reprisals, and the Liberal and Labour leaders called for talks.
  • The Government of Ireland Act of 1920 set up a Northern parliament, and the elections of May 1921 confirmed Sinn Féin's hold on the south and unionist control in the north-east.
  • In June 1921 George V's speech at the opening of the Northern parliament called for reconciliation, and Lloyd George invited de Valera to talks on 24 June.
  • The Truce began on 11 July 1921.
  • Britain went to the negotiating table because reprisals damaged its reputation, the IRA could not be crushed without a much larger force, dominion premiers and American opinion urged a settlement, and the cost of the war was rising.
  • De Valera spent June 1919 to December 1920 in the United States raising money and seeking recognition for the republic; this is covered in the notes on the diaspora.

Key personality: Michael Collins

Collins was the organiser of the IRA's intelligence war and of the republican finances. He was a participant in the Rising and the war and then a negotiator of the Treaty.

  • Collins (1890–1922) was born near Clonakilty, Co. Cork, worked in London in the post office and in a financial firm from 1906 to 1916, and joined the IRB there.
  • He fought in the GPO in 1916 as aide to Joseph Plunkett and was interned at Frongoch, where he built a network of future activists.
  • After his release he became a leader of the Volunteers and the IRB; he organised the National Aid funds and spoke at Ashe's funeral.
  • He was Minister for Finance from April 1919, organised the Dáil Loan, and as Director of Intelligence ran the Squad and the spy network.
  • He escaped capture throughout the war and was the most wanted man in the country.
  • His role in the Treaty talks and the Civil War is covered in the notes on those subjects.
  • Contemporaries: republicans admired him as the man who won the war; British officials regarded him as a ruthless organiser of murder, yet Churchill and others came to respect him in London.
  • Historians: Tim Pat Coogan (1990) portrayed a hero of the revolution, while Peter Hart (2005) gave a more critical account of the killings in which he was involved.

Key personality: Éamon de Valera (part 1, 1917–1921)

This part covers de Valera's leadership of Sinn Féin and the Dáil before the Treaty talks. His later career is in the notes on the Free State and the Republic.

  • De Valera (1882–1975) was born in New York, raised in Bruree, Co. Limerick, and was a maths teacher who joined the Volunteers in 1913.
  • He commanded the garrison at Boland's Mill in 1916; his death sentence was commuted, partly because he was born in America.
  • He was released in June 1917, won East Clare in July, and became president of Sinn Féin and of the Volunteers in October 1917.
  • He was arrested in the German Plot in May 1918, escaped from Lincoln on 3 February 1919, and was elected head of the Dáil in April 1919.
  • He was in America from June 1919 to December 1920, where his disputes with the Irish-American leaders John Devoy and Judge Cohalan limited his success.
  • After his return he took the lead in the talks with Lloyd George in July 1921.
  • Contemporaries: supporters saw him as the leader who held the movement together, while critics said he was rigid and out of touch with the IRA's campaign.
  • Historians: Tim Pat Coogan (1993) and Diarmaid Ferriter (Judging Dev, 2007) debate whether he was a pragmatist or an ideologue.

Key personality: Countess Markievicz (part 2, 1917–1921)

This part covers Markievicz after her release in 1917, as a Sinn Féin politician and Dáil minister.

  • After her release in June 1917 she joined Sinn Féin and the Volunteers' public campaign, and she was elected to the executive.
  • She was arrested in the German Plot of May 1918 and held in Holloway, where she was elected in December 1918; she was released in March 1919.
  • She was Minister for Labour in the Dáil ministry from April 1919 to January 1922, the only woman in the cabinet, and one of the first women in Europe to hold cabinet rank.
  • In September 1920 she was arrested again and sentenced to two years' hard labour; she was released in July 1921, after the Truce.
  • Her work as minister was limited by the fact that she was in jail or on the run for much of the time.
  • She opposed the Treaty in the Dáil vote in January 1922; her later career is covered in the notes on the Civil War.
  • Contemporaries admired her bravery but some Sinn Féin colleagues distrusted her socialism and her flamboyance.
  • Historians now emphasise her importance as a pioneer of women's participation in revolutionary politics.

Assessment: the Sinn Féin mandate and the War of Independence

Judgements depend on whether the 1918 election, the Dáil and the IRA are credited with winning the settlement or blamed for its costs.

  • Achievement: the 1918 result gave Sinn Féin a democratic mandate, and the counter-state showed Irish people could run their own affairs.
  • Achievement: the IRA campaign made Ireland costly and hard to govern and, with Dáil diplomacy, brought Britain to talks.
  • Limit: the IRA never controlled much territory, and it was short of arms and men by mid-1921.
  • Limit: the election was won on a minority share of the vote, and Sinn Féin's mandate said nothing about methods; many voters wanted an end to Home Rule rather than war.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists saw a legitimate government defending itself, while the British government called the Dáil and IRA a ‘murder gang’ before changing course.
  • Later view: historians debate how far the war was supported by the majority and how far it involved intimidation of informers and loyalists, as in Peter Hart's work on Cork.
  • The dual strategy of Dáil and IRA left the movement with two sources of authority that the Treaty split in 1922.

Key terms

Republic
A state without a monarch as its head, which Sinn Féin and the Dáil claimed for Ireland from 1917 and 1919.
Sovereignty
Supreme authority over a state's own affairs, which the Dáil claimed in its Declaration of Independence in 1919.
IRB/IRA
The Irish Republican Brotherhood was the secret republican society; the Irish Republican Army was the Volunteers' name from 1919, owing allegiance to the Dáil from August 1920.
Sinn Féin
The party founded by Griffith in 1905 on abstention and self-reliance, reorganised in 1917 around the demand for a republic.
Abstention
The policy of refusing to take seats at Westminster, adopted by Sinn Féin MPs after 1918.
Conscription crisis
The 1918 threat to extend military service to Ireland, which united nationalists in opposition and boosted Sinn Féin.
German Plot
The British government's claim in May 1918 that Sinn Féin was conspiring with Germany, used to arrest its leaders.
Dáil Éireann
The assembly of Sinn Féin MPs that met on 21 January 1919 and claimed to be the legitimate parliament of Ireland.
Democratic Programme
The social programme adopted by the First Dáil in 1919, giving priority to the nation's right to its resources over private property.
Flying column
A small mobile IRA unit that ambushed forces and then dispersed, the main fighting unit of the War of Independence.
Black and Tans and Auxiliaries
Two groups of ex-servicemen added to the RIC in 1920: the Tans as temporary constables and the Auxiliaries as an ex-officers' division.
Truce
The ceasefire that began on 11 July 1921 and made negotiations possible.
