Anglo-Irish relations under Cosgrave, 1922–1932

The Cosgrave governments accepted the Treaty but used the Commonwealth to widen the Free State's independence step by step.

  • The Free State registered the Treaty with the League of Nations in 1924, treating it as an international agreement rather than an internal British matter; Britain objected.
  • The Boundary Commission crisis ended with the Tripartite Agreement of December 1925, which released the Free State from its share of the imperial debt, and so put the border question aside.
  • The Imperial Conference of 1926 produced the Balfour Declaration, which described Britain and the dominions as autonomous communities, equal in status and freely associated; Kevin O'Higgins was one of its principal advocates.
  • The Statute of Westminster (December 1931) gave the dominions legislative independence, and removed the British Parliament's power to override dominion laws.
  • Cosgrave's government accepted the oath, the Governor-General and the Treaty ports, and held that independence should come by agreement with Britain.
  • The Free State continued to pay the land annuities, the repayments that tenant farmers owed for land bought under the Land Acts, to the British Exchequer under the financial arrangement of 1923–25.

De Valera takes office and dismantles the Treaty, 1932–1936

Fianna Fáil's election pledge was to remove the oath and the Treaty's symbols. De Valera did this by legislation, relying on the Statute of Westminster and the Free State's own Dáil majority.

  • De Valera became President of the Executive Council on 9 March 1932 and also took the post of Minister for External Affairs.
  • In 1932 he dismissed James McNeill as Governor-General (in October) and appointed Domhnall Ua Buachalla, a Fianna Fáil figure with no official role, in his place in November.
  • The Constitution (Removal of Oath) Act became law in May 1933 and ended the oath of fidelity to the King for members of the Oireachtas.
  • The Constitution (Amendment No. 22) Act 1933 ended the right of appeal to the Privy Council.
  • The Seanad was abolished in 1936.
  • The abdication of Edward VIII in December 1936 gave de Valera his opportunity: on 11 December the Constitution (Amendment No. 27) Act removed all references to the Crown and the Governor-General from the Constitution.
  • On 12 December 1936 the Executive Authority (External Relations) Act gave the King a role only in the appointment of diplomats and signing of international agreements.
  • Britain accepted these changes without military or economic action, partly because the Statute of Westminster supported de Valera's constitutional argument.

The Economic War, 1932–1938

De Valera's decision to stop the land annuities became a trade conflict, which was only ended by the agreements of 1938.

  • The annuities amounted to about £3 million a year; de Valera argued that they were a debt owed to the Irish government, not to Britain, and he stopped paying them in mid-1932.
  • Britain regarded the payments as obligatory under the 1925 agreement and imposed special duties on Irish cattle, beef, butter and other exports from July 1932.
  • The Free State responded with duties on British goods such as coal, cement and iron and steel products, and began a policy of self-sufficiency.
  • The Coal–Cattle Pact of 1935 allowed an exchange of Irish cattle exports for British coal, and slightly reduced the damage.
  • The farm economy and its effects are covered in the notes on economy and society.
  • Fianna Fáil argued that the conflict was a price for sovereignty, and in rural constituencies it kept its voters, while Cosgrave's party attacked the policy as damaging.

The Blueshirts and challenges to the government, 1932–1936

The first years of Fianna Fáil rule saw a challenge from the right, which strengthened the government's hand and led to the creation of a new opposition party.

  • The Army Comrades Association was founded in February 1932 by pro-Treaty ex-officers to protect Cumann na nGaedheal meetings from IRA disruption.
  • In February 1933 de Valera dismissed Eoin O'Duffy as Garda Commissioner, and O'Duffy took over the ACA in July 1933, when it adopted a blue shirt and a stiff-armed salute.
  • The movement was renamed the National Guard in July 1933, a planned march on Dublin was prohibited, and the organisation was banned in August 1933.
  • In September 1933 Cumann na nGaedheal, the Centre Party and the Blueshirts merged as Fine Gael under O'Duffy; he was forced out within a year, and Cosgrave became leader in 1935.
  • De Valera banned the IRA in June 1936, after the murder of Vice-Admiral Somerville, and removed the Blueshirt and IRA challenge to the state.
  • O'Duffy later led an Irish Brigade of about 700 men to fight for Franco in Spain in 1936–37.

Bunreacht na hÉireann, 1937

The new constitution replaced the 1922 Constitution and gave the state a more Irish and Catholic character, while avoiding the word republic.

  • It was drafted by de Valera with civil servants John Hearne and Maurice Moynihan, and with advice from clergy including the Archbishop of Dublin.
  • It was approved by a plebiscite on 1 July 1937 by 56 per cent to 44 per cent and came into force on 29 December 1937.
  • Article 4 named the state Éire, or in English Ireland, and Article 5 declared it a sovereign, independent, democratic state.
  • Articles 2 and 3 claimed the whole island of Ireland as the national territory, while limiting the exercise of the state's laws to 26 counties until reunification.
  • The head of state was a President elected for seven years, and the head of government was the Taoiseach.
  • Article 41 recognised the family as the foundation of society, spoke of the woman's life within the home, and banned divorce.
  • Article 44 recognised the special position of the Catholic Church as the guardian of the faith of the majority and also named other denominations, including the Jewish congregations; it was removed in 1972.
  • Unionists, women's groups and some nationalists criticised the document; Douglas Hyde was inaugurated as first President on 25 June 1938.

The Anglo-Irish Agreements of 1938

Three agreements signed in London on 25 April 1938 settled the main disputes between the two governments and removed the Treaty's last military restriction.

  • The defence agreement transferred the Treaty ports at Berehaven, Cobh (Spike Island) and Lough Swilly to Ireland, and the handover took place in July 1938.
  • The financial agreement ended the land annuities dispute for a single payment of £10 million, far less than Britain had claimed.
  • The trade agreement ended the special duties on both sides.
  • Neville Chamberlain's government agreed partly to improve relations as war approached, and Churchill opposed the surrender of the ports.
  • De Valera had linked the ports to partition in the talks, but Britain would not move on the border.
  • The ports allowed Ireland to remain neutral in 1939, a policy examined in the notes on the Second World War.

From Free State to Republic, 1948–1949

The inter-party government formally ended the External Relations Act and left the Commonwealth, while the British guaranteed Northern Ireland's position.

  • Fianna Fáil lost its majority in the election of February 1948, and John A. Costello of Fine Gael became Taoiseach on 18 February 1948 in a coalition including Labour, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan and others.
  • Seán MacBride, the Minister for External Affairs, wanted to repeal the External Relations Act, which he regarded as an anomaly.
  • Costello announced the decision while on a visit to Canada in September 1948.
  • The Republic of Ireland Act was passed in December 1948 and came into force on Easter Monday, 18 April 1949, repealing the External Relations Act and ending the state's link with the Commonwealth.
  • The British Ireland Act 1949 recognised that Ireland was no longer a member of the Commonwealth, but said Irish citizens would not be treated as foreigners.
  • It also declared that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of the Parliament of Northern Ireland, which strengthened the unionist position.
  • The southern parties united in an anti-partition campaign in 1949, but it achieved little.

Key personality: Éamon de Valera (part 2 and overall evaluation)

De Valera dominated the 1930s and 1940s and shaped the state's constitution, relations with Britain and neutrality. Judgements about him are divided.

  • He led Fianna Fáil in government from 1932 to 1948 and again in 1951–54 and 1957–59, and was President of Ireland from 1959 to 1973; he died in 1975.
  • He removed the oath, the Governor-General and the Privy Council appeal, drafted the 1937 constitution and secured the ports in 1938.
  • He kept Ireland neutral in the Second World War and banned the IRA in 1936.
  • He did not end partition, and the Economic War cost the farming economy dearly.
  • The social and economic record was weak: high emigration, unemployment, a conservative Catholic constitution and wartime censorship.
  • Contemporaries: supporters, especially small farmers and workers, saw him as the man who finished the work of independence, while opponents in Cumann na nGaedheal and the Blueshirts called him a dictator.
  • Historians: Joe Lee (Ireland 1912–1985) credits him with consolidating democracy and with strong political skills, while criticising the economy; Deirdre McMahon (Republicans and Imperialists, 1984) examined his diplomacy with Britain.
  • Biographers such as Tim Pat Coogan (1993) and Diarmaid Ferriter (Judging Dev, 2007) stress his stubbornness and also his pragmatism.

Assessment: Cosgrave or de Valera in Anglo-Irish relations

Both leaders increased Irish independence, but by different methods and at different costs.

  • Cosgrave's strengths: he secured the Free State's international recognition, used the Commonwealth to widen independence and avoided economic conflict with Britain.
  • Cosgrave's limits: he left the oath, the Governor-General and the ports in place, and the Boundary Commission failed.
  • De Valera's strengths: he removed the symbols of the Treaty, secured the ports and the annuities settlement and created a constitution that Britain accepted in practice.
  • De Valera's limits: the Economic War, the failure to end partition and the fact that most of his changes relied on the groundwork laid by Cosgrave at the Imperial Conferences.
  • Contemporary view: Fianna Fáil supporters said de Valera achieved real sovereignty; Fine Gael said he damaged the economy for the sake of symbols.
  • Later view: the Republic of 1949 is often judged mainly a formal act, as de Valera's earlier changes had already made the state independent in practice.

Key terms

Sovereignty
Full independent authority over the state's affairs, the aim of de Valera's constitutional changes of the 1930s.
Republic
A state without a monarch as head; Ireland was declared a republic by the Republic of Ireland Act, in force on 18 April 1949.
Dominion status
The Free State's Treaty status within the Commonwealth, which de Valera gradually dismantled.
Allegiance
The loyalty to the Crown expressed in the Treaty oath, which was removed in 1933.
Statute of Westminster
The 1931 British Act giving dominions legislative independence, which de Valera used to change the Treaty.
Removal of Oath Act
The 1933 Act ending the oath for members of the Oireachtas.
External Relations Act
The 1936 Act that gave the King a role only in diplomatic appointments and treaties; repealed in 1948–49.
Economic War
The 1932–38 trade dispute between Britain and Ireland over the land annuities.
Land annuities
The repayments tenant farmers made for land purchased under the Land Acts, collected by the Irish state and paid to Britain.
Blueshirts
The Army Comrades Association, later the National Guard, led by Eoin O'Duffy and banned in 1933.
Bunreacht na hÉireann
The constitution of 1937, which replaced the Free State Constitution.
Republic of Ireland Act
The 1948 Act, in force from 18 April 1949, that declared the state a republic and left the Commonwealth.
