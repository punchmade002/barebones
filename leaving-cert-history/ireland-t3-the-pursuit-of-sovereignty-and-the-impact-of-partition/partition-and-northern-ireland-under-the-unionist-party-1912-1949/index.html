Why partition happened, 1912–1920

Partition was the result of unionist resistance, British failure to find an all-Ireland settlement and the pattern of the 1918 vote.

  • Unionist resistance: the Ulster Covenant of 1912 and the UVF made clear that Home Rule for all Ireland could be enforced only by force.
  • Exclusion debates: county option for six years (March 1914), the failed Buckingham Palace Conference (July 1914) and the failed Lloyd George proposals of 1916 all assumed the exclusion of some Ulster counties.
  • The 1916 plan collapsed because unionist ministers in the cabinet insisted that exclusion be permanent, while nationalists were told it would be temporary.
  • The war and the Rising changed nationalist politics, and the Irish Convention of 1917–18 failed to produce agreement.
  • The 1918 election confirmed the divide: Sinn Féin won most of the island, while unionists won 26 seats, mostly in the north-east.
  • Only four of the nine Ulster counties had unionist majorities (Antrim, Down, Armagh and Londonderry), and Tyrone and Fermanagh had narrow nationalist majorities; the chosen six counties gave a safer unionist majority than nine.
  • Northern Ireland had about 1.25 million people, of whom about two-thirds were Protestant and about one-third Catholic.
  • British policy: Lloyd George's coalition, with a large Conservative element, was unwilling to coerce Ulster, and the Government of Ireland Act of 1920 gave the unionists the offer of their own parliament.

The Government of Ireland Act and the new state, 1920–1925

The Act of 1920 created two Home Rule parliaments. Only the northern one worked, and the Treaty then allowed it to stay out of the Free State.

  • The Government of Ireland Act (December 1920) set up a parliament for the six counties of Northern Ireland and one for the other 26 counties of Southern Ireland, with a Council of Ireland to link them.
  • In the Northern election of 24 May 1921 the Unionists won 40 of 52 seats, and Sinn Féin and the Nationalists won six each but would not recognise the parliament.
  • Craig became Prime Minister in June 1921, and George V opened the Northern parliament in Belfast on 22 June 1921.
  • Southern Ireland's parliament never worked, since Sinn Féin won 124 of 128 seats and treated the election as one for the Dáil.
  • Under the Treaty Northern Ireland could opt out of the Free State, and it did so on 7 December 1922, the day after the Free State came into being.
  • The Treaty provided for a Boundary Commission, chaired by Richard Feetham, with Eoin MacNeill for the Free State and J.R. Fisher for Northern Ireland, since Craig refused to nominate.
  • The commission's expected report was leaked to the Morning Post in November 1925; MacNeill resigned, and on 3 December 1925 the three governments agreed that the border would stay as it was.
  • The Tripartite Agreement also released the Free State from its share of the imperial debt and ended the Council of Ireland.

Violence and security, 1920–1922

The founding of the state was accompanied by sectarian rioting and an IRA campaign, to which the government responded with exceptional powers.

  • After the killing of an RIC officer in Cork in July 1920, thousands of Catholic and some Protestant labour supporters were expelled from the Belfast shipyards and engineering works.
  • Riots in Derry in June 1920 and in Belfast through 1920–22 killed about 500 people, most of them Catholics, and drove many families from their homes.
  • The Dáil organised a boycott of Belfast goods in 1920, which hurt Catholic workers and shopkeepers more than it hurt the unionist economy.
  • The Ulster Special Constabulary, formed in late 1920, had full-time A Specials, part-time B Specials and reserve C Specials; it was recruited almost entirely from Protestants.
  • The Royal Ulster Constabulary replaced the RIC in June 1922; on paper a third of its places were reserved for Catholics, but they never filled them.
  • The Craig–Collins pacts of January and March 1922 failed, and the IRA offensive on the border in May 1922 led the government to intern republicans.
  • The Civil Authorities (Special Powers) Act of April 1922 allowed internment, curfews, the banning of meetings and the arrest of suspects without warrant; it was renewed annually and made permanent in 1933.
  • Several hundred men were interned in 1922, including some held on the prison ship Argenta.

The Unionist government in power: control of the system

The Ulster Unionist Party governed without interruption and changed the electoral rules to keep it in power. Nationalists took little part in the system.

  • Nationalists abstained from Stormont at first; the Nationalist Party under Joe Devlin took its seats in 1925, but Sinn Féin remained abstentionist.
  • Tyrone and Fermanagh county councils and many other nationalist-controlled councils, which refused to recognise the Belfast government in 1920–22, were dissolved and replaced by commissioners.
  • The Local Government Act of 1922 abolished proportional representation in local elections and redrew electoral areas.
  • Proportional representation for Stormont elections was abolished in 1929 and single-member constituencies introduced, which helped the Unionists and weakened Labour and independents.
  • Derry Corporation stayed under unionist control from 1923 until 1969 even though Catholics were a majority of the city's population; this is the best-known case of gerrymandering.
  • The Unionist Party held every Stormont election from 1921 to 1969 by large margins, and a bloc vote on border issues kept Protestant workers within it.
  • Stormont Parliament Buildings, on the outskirts of Belfast, were opened by the Prince of Wales in 1932, a symbol of the permanence of the state.

Discrimination in jobs, housing and public life

Nationalists said that the state discriminated against Catholics. Unionists denied it, arguing that Catholics refused to take part in the state.

  • Catholics were about a third of the population but held a small share of senior posts in the civil service, the judiciary, and local government.
  • The shipyards and heavy industry employed few Catholics, and Protestant workers' networks and the Orange Order helped to secure employment.
  • Local councils controlled by unionists allocated public housing, and nationalists argued that houses were allocated to Protestant families first.
  • In the mid-1930s Basil Brooke urged unionists not to employ Catholics, a statement widely quoted as evidence of the attitudes of leading unionists.
  • On 24 April 1934 Craig told the Stormont parliament that he boasted of a Protestant parliament and a Protestant state; he said this in reply to southern Catholic claims, and it is the best-known statement of the attitudes of the unionist government.
  • Critics of the system, such as Michael Farrell in The Orange State (1976), see systematic discrimination, while others accept discrimination in some fields but stress that it was not centrally planned.
  • Nationalist abstention in the early years and the Catholic Church's separate schools left the minority with a limited voice in the state.

The economy and society in the North, 1921–1939

Northern Ireland entered the 1920s with high-value industries that depended on export markets, and the post-war slump and the Depression hit them hard.

  • Shipbuilding at Harland and Wolff and linen were the two big employers, and both fell back after the post-war boom ended in 1920–21.
  • Unemployment was around 20 per cent for much of the 1920s and reached around 25 per cent in the early 1930s, among the highest in the United Kingdom.
  • Agriculture remained important, with small farms exporting cattle and eggs to Britain.
  • The government followed a policy of ‘step by step’ with Britain on welfare benefits, which kept pensions and unemployment pay in line with British levels, financed with an imperial contribution.
  • Housing in the slums of Belfast was poor and overcrowded, and the government built little until the late 1930s.
  • In October 1932 unemployed Catholic and Protestant workers joined in the Outdoor Relief strike and riots in Belfast against the harsh relief rules; police shot dead two people, and the Board of Guardians increased the relief.

The impact of partition on the island's economy and society

Partition broke up an economy that had been built on all-Ireland trade and weakened both parts.

  • Customs controls on the border from 1923 interrupted the flow of goods and cattle, and the Free State's tariffs and Economic War of the 1930s made smuggling common.
  • Belfast's linen, shipbuilding and engineering, which had sold to a UK and world market, lost the southern market they had counted on.
  • The Free State lost the industrial north-east, which had provided the bulk of Irish manufactured exports, and was left with an economy dependent on agriculture.
  • Dublin and Belfast, once part of a single banking and railway system, were run from different centres, and railways such as the Great Northern crossed the border.
  • Society divided as well: Catholics in the North were cut off from the southern state's institutions, while Protestants in the South felt the pressure of the Catholic ethos.
  • Politically the border became the main theme of Northern nationalism and southern foreign policy, as seen in Articles 2 and 3 of the 1937 constitution.

Key personality: James Craig

Craig was a leader of Ulster resistance to Home Rule and the first Prime Minister of Northern Ireland. He is judged as the builder of a stable state and also as a leader of a sectarian one.

  • Craig (1871–1940) was born in Belfast, the son of a wealthy whiskey distiller, and worked as a stockbroker; he served in the Boer War and became MP for East Down in 1906.
  • He organised the UVF and the Larne gun-running of April 1914 alongside Carson.
  • He was a junior minister in the British government from 1916 to 1921, ending as Parliamentary and Financial Secretary to the Admiralty.
  • He succeeded Carson as Unionist leader in February 1921 and became Prime Minister of Northern Ireland in June 1921, serving until his death on 24 November 1940.
  • He was made Viscount Craigavon in 1927.
  • He negotiated with Collins in 1922 and with Lloyd George, but insisted that the border was settled; he refused to nominate to the Boundary Commission.
  • His government created the RUC and the Special Powers Act, and abolished proportional representation, so critics see him as a sectarian figure.
  • His defenders note that he held the state together against IRA violence and economic crisis and that his government copied British social policy.
  • Contemporaries: unionists revered him as their founder, while nationalists considered him responsible for their exclusion.
  • Historians: Patrick Buckland's biography (1980) stressed his cautious, conservative leadership and his failure to reach out to Catholics, while others criticise the lack of any attempt to win minority consent.

Key personality: Richard Dawson Bates

Bates was the minister who ran the machinery of control for more than 20 years, and his name is associated with the security system and electoral changes.

  • Richard Dawson Bates (1876–1949) was a Belfast solicitor who was secretary of the Ulster Unionist Council from 1906 to 1921 and helped to organise the UVF.
  • He was Minister of Home Affairs from 1921 to 1943, responsible for the RUC, the Special Constabulary, the Special Powers Act and local government and elections.
  • He introduced the Local Government Act of 1922 that abolished PR for local elections and the 1929 change for Stormont elections.
  • He used his powers to ban republican parades and organisations, to arrest and intern suspects and to censor some publications.
  • Contemporaries: nationalists described him as the architect of the system of control, and many unionists praised his efficiency.
  • Historians: Buckland, in The Factory of Grievances (1979), describes how his department's decisions, even if cautious, left a legacy of grievances; Bates left office in 1943 in ill health.

Assessment: the Unionist state's achievements and limits

Judgements depend on whether the state is measured by survival and stability or by its treatment of the minority.

  • Achievement: it survived the violence of 1920–22 and the Depression and secured the position of Northern Ireland within the United Kingdom.
  • Achievement: it maintained British standards in education, health and welfare through its step-by-step policy.
  • Limit: it relied on emergency powers, a largely Protestant police and manipulated electoral rules, so the minority never accepted it.
  • Limit: a one-party government with a permanent majority did not develop real opposition, and unemployment policy was timid.
  • Contemporary view: unionists regarded it as a bulwark against a Catholic all-Ireland state, while nationalists saw a Protestant state.
  • Later view: historians disagree on how far the system was planned discrimination, but accept that its legacy shaped the Troubles.

Key terms

Partition
The division of Ireland under the Government of Ireland Act 1920 into Northern Ireland (six counties) and Southern Ireland.
Ulster Unionism
The movement, led by Carson and Craig, that opposed Home Rule and then defended Northern Ireland's place in the United Kingdom.
Discrimination
The unequal treatment of Catholics in jobs, housing, policing and local government in Northern Ireland, as alleged by nationalists.
Government of Ireland Act 1920
The Act that created two parliaments in Ireland and is the legal basis of Northern Ireland.
Boundary Commission
The commission provided for in the Treaty to decide the border, which in 1925 left the border unchanged.
Special Powers Act
The 1922 law giving the Northern Ireland government powers of internment, curfew and banning; permanent from 1933.
Ulster Special Constabulary
The part-time police reserve formed in 1920, whose B Specials were almost wholly Protestant.
Gerrymandering
Drawing electoral boundaries to give one party an unfair advantage, as in Derry's local elections.
Proportional representation
An electoral system in which seats match shares of votes; abolished for Northern local elections in 1922 and for Stormont in 1929.
Stormont
The Northern Ireland parliament, which sat at the Presbyterian Assembly's College in Belfast until Parliament Buildings at Stormont opened in 1932.
