The Free State's economic inheritance and the impact of partition

The Free State took over a small, mainly agricultural economy tied to Britain, and partition removed its industrial region.

  • Agriculture was the main employer, and more than 90 per cent of exports, mainly cattle, butter, bacon and eggs, went to Britain.
  • Partition left the Belfast shipyards, the linen mills and most heavy engineering in Northern Ireland, so the Free State had little industry beyond Dublin brewing, biscuits, and food processing, and the Ford factory at Cork.
  • The Free State set up its own customs controls on the border in 1923, interrupting trade in cattle and manufactured goods, and creating a smuggling economy.
  • The Irish pound remained tied to sterling: the Currency Act of 1927 created the Saorstát pound at parity with sterling, so Irish monetary policy followed Britain's.
  • The Free State had a population of under three million, and a small home market limited the scope for industrial growth.
  • The civil service, taxation and financial institutions were largely inherited from British rule, and the Department of Finance took over the cautious Treasury approach.

Cumann na nGaedheal: free trade and agriculture first, 1922–1932

The Cumann na nGaedheal governments believed that the farm sector could carry the economy and that low taxes and free trade were the best policy.

  • Patrick Hogan, Minister for Agriculture from 1922 to 1932, concentrated on quality exports of beef, butter and bacon to Britain.
  • The Dairy Produce Act and the Creamery Act of 1924 and the Live Stock Breeding Act of 1925 improved standards, and the Agricultural Credit Corporation was founded in 1927 to lend to farmers.
  • Ernest Blythe, Minister for Finance from 1923, kept taxes and spending low, and the 1924 budget cut the old age pension from 10 shillings to 9 shillings a week.
  • Public-sector pay was also cut, and the government aimed for balanced budgets as a priority.
  • A Tariff Commission was set up under the Tariff Commission Act of 1926 to examine applications for protection, but it recommended tariffs on only a limited number of goods.
  • The Shannon Scheme was the chief exception to the policy of low state spending, and it was justified as providing cheap power for industry.
  • Critics said that the policy favoured large farmers and left cities, labourers and small farmers with little help.
  • The government set up the Agricultural Credit Corporation and promoted co-operative creameries as part of its farm policy.

Key personality: James J. McElligott

McElligott was a civil servant, not a politician, but as Secretary of the Department of Finance he was one of the most influential figures in economic policy for a quarter of a century.

  • James J. McElligott (1893–1974) joined the Department of Finance in 1923, as an Assistant Secretary, and was Secretary of the Department from 1927 to 1953.
  • He later served as Governor of the Central Bank, from 1953 to 1960.
  • He worked with Blythe and then with Fianna Fáil's Finance Minister Seán MacEntee in the 1930s, and he advised ministers on the budget, spending and taxation.
  • His approach was financial orthodoxy: balanced budgets, low taxes, limited state borrowing and strict control of spending by other departments.
  • He was sceptical of high-spending social programmes and of industrial protection as practised by Seán Lemass, and Finance regularly opposed the cost of new schemes.
  • Contemporaries in other departments, especially Industry and Commerce, saw Finance as a block on development.
  • Historians such as Ronan Fanning, in The Irish Department of Finance (1978), argue that Finance's caution shaped policy for decades and held back growth until the reappraisal of the late 1950s.

The world economic crisis, 1929–1933

The Wall Street Crash and the Depression cut the prices of farm exports and closed foreign markets just as Britain turned away from free trade.

  • The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 began a worldwide slump, and farm prices fell sharply.
  • The Free State's farmers lost income, and the number of those emigrating to the United States dropped as the American economy shut its doors.
  • In September 1931 Britain left the gold standard, but the Free State kept its currency tied to sterling.
  • In 1932 Britain introduced general import duties and imperial preference, which hurt Irish exports and showed the weakness of dependence on a single market.
  • Unemployment rose in the towns, and the Cumann na nGaedheal government cut spending and did not bring in tariffs on a large scale.
  • The crisis helped Fianna Fáil, which promised protection and self-sufficiency, win the election of 1932.
  • Irish farm prices fell steeply in the early 1930s, and the cost of the annuities became harder to bear.

From free trade to protectionism, 1932–1939

Fianna Fáil, with Lemass as Minister for Industry and Commerce, abandoned free trade and tried to build industry behind tariff walls.

  • Tariffs and quotas were imposed on a wide range of imports, from clothing and shoes to household goods.
  • The Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934 required that majority ownership of manufacturing firms in the Free State be Irish.
  • The Industrial Credit Company, set up in 1933, provided capital to new Irish firms.
  • The Irish Sugar Company was founded in 1933 to run the beet industry and the sugar factories.
  • The Turf Development Board (1934), Aer Lingus (1936) and the ESB were examples of the state's creation of semi-state bodies.
  • Wheat and beet growing were encouraged, and the wheat acreage rose roughly tenfold in the first years of the policy.
  • Industrial employment grew by tens of thousands over the 1930s, but many of the new firms were small, inefficient and depended on the home market.
  • Critics said protection raised prices and did not lead to exports; supporters said it created jobs which free trade would never have provided.
  • The policy of self-sufficiency was reinforced after 1939 by the Emergency, when imports were cut off.

The Economic War and cattle farmers, 1932–1938

The trade war with Britain over the land annuities hit the largest group of farmers hardest. The causes are covered in the notes on Anglo-Irish relations.

  • British duties on cattle, beef and butter from 1932 cut the price of cattle and made many exports uneconomic.
  • Cattle exports to Britain, the single largest item, fell sharply, and large and small cattle farmers saw their incomes drop.
  • The government paid bounties on exports and subsidised wheat and beet to make up for lost income, financed by higher taxes.
  • The Coal–Cattle Pact of 1935 allowed more Irish cattle into Britain in return for British coal.
  • Farmers in Munster and Leinster, with their large cattle farms, were hit more than small western farmers.
  • The 1938 agreement ended the dispute, but exports did not return to their former level before the war.
  • Dairy farmers and small farmers were affected by the fall in prices, and the cost of the trade war fell on the whole rural economy.

Housing, social policy and emigration

Fianna Fáil spent more than its predecessor on housing and welfare, but resources were limited and poverty remained widespread.

  • The Housing Acts of 1932 and 1936 raised state subsidies, and local authorities built tens of thousands of new houses, helped by slum clearance in Dublin.
  • The Unemployment Assistance Act of 1933 gave relief to those who had run out of insurance benefit.
  • The Widows' and Orphans' Pensions Act of 1935 introduced a contributory pension scheme.
  • Tenements in Dublin remained overcrowded, and the tuberculosis death rate was high.
  • Emigration fell in the early 1930s as the Depression closed American and British job markets, then rose again in the late 1930s and during the Second World War.
  • A marriage bar for national teachers, introduced in 1932, obliged women teachers to resign on marriage; a similar bar already applied in the civil service from the 1920s.
  • Emigrants' remittances were an important source of income for families in the west, and their reduction in the Depression hurt small farmers.

Assessment: economic success and failure, 1922–1945

Neither free trade nor protection produced rapid growth. Judgements depend on whether the aim is stability, self-sufficiency or living standards.

  • Cumann na nGaedheal's achievement: financial stability, a sound currency, an efficient farm export trade and the Shannon Scheme.
  • Cumann na nGaedheal's limit: dependence on the British market and low spending left little help for the poor when the Depression came.
  • Fianna Fáil's achievement: new industries and jobs, a larger state role through semi-state companies and more social housing.
  • Fianna Fáil's limit: the Economic War, a small and protected market, continued emigration and low living standards.
  • Why success was hard: a small home market, few natural resources, dependence on Britain, the Depression and then the Second World War.
  • Contemporary view: supporters of protection held that it was necessary for independence; critics held that it hurt consumers and farmers.
  • Later view: historians such as Mary Daly and Joe Lee say that protection created jobs but not an efficient industry, while the Finance Department's caution restricted investment.

Key terms

Free trade
The policy of keeping tariffs low or absent, followed by Cumann na nGaedheal, which suited an economy exporting farm produce to Britain.
Protectionism
The use of tariffs, quotas and licences to shield home industry from imports, adopted by Fianna Fáil from 1932.
Tariff Commission
The body set up under the Tariff Commission Act 1926 to examine applications for protection.
Department of Finance
The government department that controlled the budget and was a centre of financial caution, led by McElligott from 1927 to 1953.
Wall Street Crash
The collapse of the New York stock market in October 1929 that began the world Depression.
Control of Manufactures Acts
The 1932 and 1934 Acts requiring majority Irish ownership of manufacturing firms.
Industrial Credit Company
The state-backed lender set up in 1933 to provide capital to new industries.
Semi-state body
A company owned by the state but run commercially, such as the ESB, the Irish Sugar Company and Aer Lingus.
Economic War
The 1932–38 trade dispute with Britain over the land annuities, which hit cattle farmers.
Land annuities
The yearly repayments by farmers who had bought their land under the Land Acts, about £3 million a year.
Marriage bar
The rule requiring women in the civil service and national teaching to resign on marriage.
