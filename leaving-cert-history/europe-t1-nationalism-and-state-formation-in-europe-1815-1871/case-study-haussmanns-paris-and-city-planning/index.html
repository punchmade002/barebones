Why cities needed planning

Rapid urban growth in the nineteenth century created problems of overcrowding, disease, traffic and disorder that cities could no longer leave to chance.

  • Industrial and urban growth brought millions of people into cities, and existing streets, water supplies and housing could not cope with the numbers.
  • Narrow, winding medieval streets made traffic hard to move, and they were dangerous for pedestrians and for the carriages of the wealthy.
  • Overcrowded and unsanitary districts bred disease, and municipal authorities were slow to provide clean water and sewers.
  • Timber-framed and crowded housing in the older districts made fire a constant danger, and wider streets allowed fire engines to reach the buildings.
  • Governments feared revolution in crowded working-class districts, where barricades could be built quickly in narrow streets.
  • Planning was also a way of displaying the power and prestige of the state, with grand public buildings and open spaces.

Paris before 1853

  • Paris had about one million inhabitants by the early 1850s, and the city was divided between the wealthy west and the crowded working-class east and north.
  • The central districts around the Île de la Cité and Les Halles were a maze of narrow streets, with poor drainage and many old houses.
  • The city's water came from the Seine, the canal network and older aqueducts, and it was of poor quality in many districts.
  • The Hôtel-Dieu, the main hospital on the Île de la Cité, was overcrowded, and its wards held large numbers of sick patients in poor conditions.
  • The revolutions of 1830, 1848 and the June Days of 1848 had all been fought in narrow streets, which reinforced the case for broad avenues.

Napoleon III's aims and Haussmann's appointment

  • Napoleon III wanted to modernise Paris to improve health, reduce unemployment through public works and make the city easier to control.
  • Baron Georges-Eugène Haussmann, born in 1809, was appointed Prefect of the Seine, the official in charge of Paris and its department, in June 1853.
  • Haussmann served as Prefect for about seventeen years, working directly with the emperor and his ministers.
  • The expropriation law of 1841, extended by a decree of 1852, allowed the state to buy property compulsorily for public works, which gave Haussmann the legal power to clear whole blocks of houses.
  • Haussmann's methods were centralised, and he gave the Prefecture of the Seine large budgets and a large staff of engineers and architects.
  • The emperor wanted Paris to be the showcase of France, and it hosted the international exhibitions of 1855 and 1867.
  • The building programme employed large numbers of construction workers, one of the aims of the works, although much of the work was seasonal and uncertain.
  • Napoleon III had admired London's parks and squares during his exile there, and he drew up a plan marking the new streets he wanted.

The boulevards and the grands travaux

  • The grands travaux (great works) were a series of projects to cut new avenues through the old city, which Haussmann directed.
  • The Rue de Rivoli was extended eastward from the 1850s, and the Boulevard de Sébastopol was cut north–south through the centre in the 1850s, linking the Seine with the railway stations to the north.
  • The Boulevard Saint-Germain and the Boulevard Saint-Michel were cut through the Latin Quarter, and they were lined with trees and uniform buildings.
  • The avenues were designed in straight lines to create vistas, and they were lined with uniform stone facades of similar height.
  • The military value of the boulevards was clear, since troops could move quickly through the city and artillery could fire along the straight streets.
  • The Avenue de l'Opéra was built in the 1860s and 1870s to frame the view of the new Opéra, and the Avenue de l'Impératrice was laid out in 1854 as a route to the Bois de Boulogne; the Étoile became the centre of twelve radiating avenues.
  • Building regulations fixed the height of facades along the new streets, giving the uniform look of Haussmann's Paris.

Water supply, sewers and parks

  • The engineer Eugène Belgrand built a new system of aqueducts and reservoirs that brought clean water into the city from sources in the Dhuis and Vanne valleys.
  • Belgrand's underground sewer network carried waste away from the centre, which improved sanitation and reduced disease.
  • The Bois de Boulogne, on the western edge of the city, was redesigned in the 1850s as a park with lakes, paths and a racecourse, on the model of London's parks.
  • The Bois de Vincennes, on the eastern edge, was converted into a park in the 1860s, to give the working-class east a counterpart to the Bois de Boulogne.
  • The Buttes-Chaumont, a former quarry and rubbish tip in the north-east, was transformed into a park with a lake and a cliff-top waterfall, which opened in 1867.
  • The Parc Monceau was redesigned in the 1860s, the Parc Montsouris was laid out in the south, and small squares were laid out in the city's districts, giving residents access to green space.
  • Jean-Charles Alphand, the engineer in charge of the city's parks and promenades, designed much of the planting and layout of the new parks.
  • Belgrand's new aqueducts brought spring water from the Dhuis (completed 1865) and Vanne (1874) valleys, with covered reservoirs in the city.

Public buildings, markets and railway stations

  • The Paris Opéra, designed by Charles Garnier, was begun in 1861 and opened in 1875, after the fall of the Empire, and became a symbol of the imperial city.
  • The new Les Halles, the central food markets, were built as iron-and-glass pavilions by Victor Baltard from the 1850s.
  • The Gare du Nord was rebuilt in the 1860s and the Gare de l'Est was enlarged, and the new boulevards linked the main railway stations.
  • Public fountains, lamps, kiosks and tree-lined squares were added to the new streets, giving the city a uniform appearance.
  • The new buildings were designed to frame the vistas of the boulevards and to create impressive views of major monuments.

Annexation, financing and Haussmann's fall

  • In 1860 the suburbs of Paris were annexed to the city, and the number of arrondissements grew from twelve to twenty.
  • Among the annexed areas were Belleville, Montmartre and Passy, which brought large working-class populations into the enlarged city.
  • The annexation brought the population of the enlarged city to about 1.8 million by 1872, and it allowed Haussmann to plan the whole urban area.
  • The works were financed by municipal borrowing, large loans and taxes, and the city's debt grew considerably during the 1860s.
  • Critics such as the republican journalist Jules Ferry attacked the cost and the hidden borrowing in a pamphlet of 1868, The Fantastic Accounts of Haussmann.
  • Haussmann was dismissed in January 1870, when the new liberal ministry of Émile Ollivier refused to keep a prefect so unpopular with the opposition.
  • Haussmann published his Mémoires in 1890–93, defending his work, and he described himself as a demolition artist.

Social effects and criticism

  • The demolition of old housing displaced many working-class families, who were forced into the outer districts of the east and north.
  • Rents rose in the new central districts, which were occupied by wealthier residents and businesses.
  • The result was a sharper division between the wealthy west and the working-class east of the city, an effect that Zola satirised in his novel La Curée.
  • Displaced workers settled in eastern districts such as Belleville and Ménilmontant, which later became centres of radical politics.
  • The new boulevards were criticised for their cost and for destroying the historic character of many districts; the poet Baudelaire mourned the vanishing old Paris in Le Cygne (1860).
  • Some contemporaries argued that the boulevards were designed to make the city easier to control, and the Commune of 1871 showed the limits of that control when barricades were built in the eastern districts.

City planning elsewhere

  • Vienna's Ringstrasse, decreed in 1857, replaced the old city walls with a grand boulevard lined with public buildings, and it was built in the 1860s and 1870s.
  • Plots along the Ringstrasse were sold to private developers, who financed many of the new apartment blocks and houses.
  • In Barcelona the engineer Ildefons Cerdà produced a plan in 1859 for the extension of the city, the Eixample, with a regular grid of blocks.
  • In Britain the sanitary reform movement of the 1840s, led by Edwin Chadwick and others, pressed for drainage, water supply and public health legislation.
  • Haussmann's works were a response to the specific problems of a growing capital, and they were carried out by a centralised state authority with legal power over property.
  • Berlin's Hobrecht Plan of 1862 laid out the expansion of the city, and in the 1870s Joseph Chamberlain, mayor of Birmingham, cleared slums and built a new street, Corporation Street.

Assessment: success and failure of Haussmann's Paris

  • Success: the water and sewer systems greatly improved health and sanitation in the city, which had been among the worst-served capitals in Europe.
  • Success: the boulevards, parks and public buildings made Paris a model for city planners across Europe and the world.
  • Success: the works made Paris a safer, brighter city, with gas lighting and a more regular street plan, and raised property values in the central districts.
  • Failure: the displacement of the poor and rising rents created new social problems, and the working class was excluded from the new city.
  • Failure: the cost and debt of the works, and the way they were financed, provoked criticism that contributed to Haussmann's dismissal.
  • Overall: Haussmann's Paris solved many of the physical problems of a crowded city and became a model for modern planning, but it did so by displacing the poor and serving the interests of the regime and the wealthy.

Key terms

Grands travaux
The great public works, such as new boulevards, parks and sewers, that Haussmann carried out in Paris from 1853.
Prefect of the Seine
The senior official in charge of Paris and the Seine department, a post Haussmann held from 1853 to 1870.
Boulevard
A wide, straight, tree-lined avenue, which Haussmann used to open up the city and to improve traffic and military movement.
Expropriation
The compulsory purchase of property by the state for public works, governed in France by the 1841 law.
Annexation of 1860
The incorporation of the Paris suburbs into the city in 1860, which increased the number of arrondissements from twelve to twenty.
Sewer
An underground channel that carries waste water away from houses and streets, which Belgrand built on a large scale in Paris.
Ringstrasse
The grand boulevard built in Vienna from 1857, replacing the old city walls with public buildings.
Public health
The organised measures to protect the health of a population, through clean water, sanitation, housing and disease control.
