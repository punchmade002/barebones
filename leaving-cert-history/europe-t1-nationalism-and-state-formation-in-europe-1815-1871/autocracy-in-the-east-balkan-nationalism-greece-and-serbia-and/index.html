The Ottoman Empire and the Eastern Question

The Ottoman Empire ruled the Balkans and Greece in 1815. The decline of its authority over Christian subjects raised the Eastern Question: which Great Power would benefit when the Ottomans weakened.

  • The Ottoman Sultan ruled the Balkan Christians through local officials and the millet system, which gave religious communities some legal autonomy but no political rights.
  • Russia claimed to protect Orthodox Christians in the Balkans and gained influence over the Danubian principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia after its wars with the Ottomans.
  • Austria feared Russian expansion into the Balkans and wanted the Ottoman Empire kept intact as a barrier, while Britain also wanted to protect its route to India through the Mediterranean and the Straits.
  • The Treaty of Adrianople (1829), which ended a Russo-Ottoman war, gave Russia control of the mouths of the Danube and obliged the Sultan to honour Serbian autonomy.
  • The Ottoman Hatt-i Humayun of 1856 promised equal treatment for non-Muslim subjects, a reform the Great Powers had pressed for at the end of the Crimean War.

Serbia: risings and autonomy, 1804–1839

  • The First Serbian Uprising began in 1804, led by Karadjordje (Karađorđe) Petrović, against the Janissary garrison that had terrorised the Serbs.
  • The Ottomans crushed the first rising in 1813, and Karadjordje fled, but a second rising in 1815 was led by Miloš Obrenović.
  • Miloš negotiated autonomy with the Ottoman government, which a decree of the Sultan recognised in 1830, and he became hereditary prince.
  • Miloš ruled in an autocratic manner and rivals were removed, among them Karadjordje, murdered in 1817 on his orders according to most accounts; the liberal constitution of 1835 limited his power and was suspended within weeks under pressure from Russia, Austria and the Ottomans.
  • Serbia remained under Ottoman suzerainty and paid an annual tribute to the Sultan, which was the price of its autonomy.
  • After the Ottomans imposed a new statute in 1838, which gave power to a council of notables, Miloš was forced to abdicate in 1839 and was followed by his sons Milan, who died within weeks, and then Mihailo.
  • Serbia remained a tributary principality, with Ottoman garrisons in its fortresses until 1867, and gained full independence only in 1878; its example of autonomy under a local prince influenced other Balkan nationalists.
  • In 1844 Ilija Garašanin's Načertanije drew up a plan for a larger South Slav state under Serbian leadership.

Greece: the War of Independence, 1821–1827

  • The Philiki Etaireia (Friendly Society), founded in Odessa in 1814, planned an armed rising against the Ottomans and recruited among Greek merchants and intellectuals.
  • Alexander Ypsilantis launched an uprising in the Danubian principalities in March 1821, and it was defeated by the Ottomans.
  • A rising in the Peloponnese began in March 1821 and spread to central Greece, with a national assembly at Epidaurus declaring independence in January 1822.
  • The Ottoman response included massacres, such as the massacre on the island of Chios in 1822, which created a wave of sympathy for the Greek cause in Europe.
  • Philhellenism, the European enthusiasm for Greek independence, brought volunteers and money, and the English poet Lord Byron joined the cause and died at Missolonghi in April 1824.
  • The Egyptian ruler Muhammad Ali sent his son Ibrahim Pasha to the Peloponnese in 1825, who brought a new wave of destruction and reduced much of the region.
  • The battle of Navarino (20 October 1827), fought by British, French and Russian fleets against the Ottoman and Egyptian fleet, destroyed most of that fleet and made Greek independence possible.
  • The fall of Missolonghi to the Ottomans in April 1826 after a long siege strengthened European sympathy, and Delacroix's paintings of Chios and Missolonghi gave the cause a lasting image.

The Great Powers and the Greek kingdom

  • Britain, France and Russia had signed the Treaty of London (1827) to force an armistice on the Ottomans and Egypt, and Navarino was fought as a result.
  • The London Protocol of 1830 recognised Greece as an independent state, under a Great Power guarantee, with its borders set in the south.
  • The Treaty of London (1832) made Greece a monarchy under Otto, a young prince of Bavaria, who arrived as king in 1833.
  • Ioannis Kapodistrias, the first head of the Greek state, was assassinated in 1831 by members of the Mavromichalis family, whose leader he had imprisoned.
  • Athens became the capital of Greece in 1834, after the king's government moved there from Nafplio.
  • Otto's government was dominated by Bavarian officials and ruled autocratically until a revolt in 1843 forced him to grant a constitution; many Greeks also wanted a larger state, the Megali Idea.
  • Otto was deposed by a revolt in 1862, and the Powers chose a Danish prince as King George I in 1863.
  • Greece's first frontier, set in 1832, ran from Arta to Volos and left most Greeks outside the kingdom.

Balkan nationalism and the threat to European stability

  • Balkan nationalism threatened the Ottoman Empire and invited Russian and Austrian intervention, which turned the Balkans into a focus of Great Power rivalry.
  • The Crimean War (1853–56) began over the protection of Christians in the Holy Places and Russian claims on the Ottoman Empire, and Britain and France went to war against Russia.
  • Moldavia and Wallachia elected the same prince, Alexander Ioan Cuza, in 1859, and the union was formally recognised by 1862; Cuza was deposed in 1866 and replaced by Carol of Hohenzollern.
  • The Treaty of Paris (1856) guaranteed the territorial integrity of the Ottoman Empire and neutralised the Black Sea, which removed Russian naval power from the region for a time.
  • Balkan crises in the 1850s and 1860s kept the Great Powers in a state of tension, and the Eastern Question was a permanent source of conflict.
  • Russian attempts to claim a protectorate over the Sultan's Orthodox subjects, pressed by Menshikov's mission of 1853, set off the Crimean War.
  • The Greek nationalist programme of the Megali Idea, voiced by Ioannis Kolettis in 1844, aimed to bring all Greeks into one state.

Tsarist autocracy: Alexander I and Nicholas I

  • Russia was an autocracy in which the Tsar had unlimited authority, with no constitution, no elected national assembly and few restrictions on the Crown.
  • Alexander I (reigned 1801–25) promised reform but took little action on serfdom, and he was involved in the Holy Alliance and the Congress System after 1815.
  • His Law on Free Cultivators (1803) allowed some serfs to buy their freedom with their landlord's consent, but very few took advantage of it.
  • Nicholas I (reigned 1825–55) founded the Third Section, a political police force, in 1826 to monitor opponents of the regime.
  • In 1833 the minister Count Sergei Uvarov proposed the official doctrine of Orthodoxy, Autocracy and Nationality to define Russian identity.
  • The autocracy kept rigid control of the press, universities and education, and censorship was tight under Nicholas I.
  • Nicholas I presided over a codification of Russian law under Speransky (1833) and the reform of the state peasants under Kiselev (1837–41), but did not tackle private serfdom.

Serfdom in Russia: extent and obligations

  • Serfs were peasants bound to the land of their noble owners, who controlled their labour, marriage and movement and could buy, sell or mortgage them.
  • By the late 1850s privately owned serfs made up more than a third of Russia's population, and serfdom had largely disappeared from western Europe by this time.
  • The main obligations were labour dues (barshchina), when serfs worked on the lord's land for several days a week, and quitrent (obrok), a payment in money or goods.
  • Serfs were subject to corporal punishment and the lord's courts, and their lives were controlled by their owners through the estate system.
  • Serfdom held back agricultural productivity, since landlords had little incentive to invest in improved methods and serfs had little interest in improving land they did not own.
  • Many of the workers in Russian factories, especially in the Urals, were serfs or peasants tied to their villages by passports, which limited the growth of a free labour force.

The Decembrists and the reform debate

  • The Decembrists, a group of young officers and nobles, rose in St Petersburg on 14 December 1825 demanding a constitution and the end of serfdom.
  • The revolt was suppressed by Nicholas I within a day, and five leaders, including Pavel Pestel and Kondraty Ryleev, were hanged in July 1826, while others were exiled to Siberia.
  • The Decembrist revolt shaped Nicholas's rule, which was built on repression and strong censorship.
  • Serfdom was debated in official committees under Nicholas, but the government took no decisive action before the Crimean War exposed the weaknesses of the system.
  • The rising followed the confusion over the succession, when Constantine refused the throne after Alexander I's death in 1825; the Northern Society in St Petersburg and Pestel's Southern Society were its main groups.

Alexander II's emancipation edict, 1861

  • Russia's defeat in the Crimean War showed the economic and military backwardness of the empire, and Alexander II (reigned 1855–81) made emancipation a priority.
  • The fall of Sevastopol in September 1855 forced Russia to accept peace in 1856, and Alexander told the Moscow nobility that it was better to abolish serfdom from above than wait for it to be abolished from below.
  • The emancipation edict was issued on 19 February 1861, freeing the serfs from personal bondage, after editorial commissions had drafted its terms from 1859.
  • Serfs received legal freedom, the right to marry and move, and a share of land, but landlords kept much of the best land.
  • Peasants had to pay redemption payments to the government over 49 years, to repay what the state advanced to landlords as compensation, with the peasants paying a part to the landlord directly.
  • The peasants were organised into village communes called the mir, which held the land collectively and were responsible for the redemption payments.
  • Until they completed redemption, peasants remained temporarily obligated to their former lords, paying dues for the land they used.
  • The mir made it difficult for peasants to leave the village, and the redemption payments made the peasants poor and indebted for decades.
  • Redemption payments continued until they were cancelled in 1907, after the 1905 revolution.
  • Alexander II's other reforms followed, including elected local councils (zemstva) and a new judicial system, both in 1864.

Assessment: achievements and limits

  • Achievement: Greece and Serbia gained autonomy or independence, and the Greek kingdom became the first independent Balkan state within the Ottoman lands.
  • Limit: Greek independence was achieved only with Great Power intervention, and the new state's borders were set by the Powers, leaving many Greeks outside it.
  • Achievement: the 1861 emancipation ended personal serfdom and gave the peasants a legal status and some land.
  • Limit: in many areas the peasants received less land than they had worked and were burdened by redemption payments and the mir system, which kept many in poverty.
  • Overall: Balkan nationalism created new states but fed Great Power rivalry, while the 1861 emancipation was a major reform that left the peasants with real grievances.

Key terms

Eastern Question
The problem of what would happen to the Ottoman Empire's European possessions as its power declined, and which Great Power would benefit.
Philhellenism
European enthusiasm for the Greek cause in the 1820s, which brought volunteers, money and political pressure for intervention.
Serfdom
The condition of peasants bound to the land of their noble owners, who controlled their labour, movement and marriage.
Barshchina
The labour dues owed by Russian serfs, who worked on the lord's land for several days each week.
Quitrent (obrok)
The payment in money or goods that Russian serfs made to their lords instead of labour dues.
Mir
The Russian village commune, which held land collectively and was responsible for redemption payments after emancipation.
Redemption payments
The payments made by former serfs over 49 years after 1861 to repay the government's loans to landlords for the land they received.
Decembrists
The officers and nobles who rose in St Petersburg on 14 December 1825 demanding a constitution and the end of serfdom.
Third Section
The political police set up by Nicholas I in 1826 to monitor and repress opposition to the Tsar's government.
Autocracy
A system of government in which a single ruler holds unlimited power, which in Russia meant the Tsar's rule without a constitution.
Nationalism
The belief that a people sharing language, religion and history should form their own state, which drove the Greek and Serbian risings.
