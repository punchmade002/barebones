1848 across Europe: the year of revolutions

  • Revolution began in Palermo in January 1848 and in Paris on 22–24 February, when Louis-Philippe fell and the Second Republic was declared.
  • Within weeks risings followed in Vienna (13 March), Milan (the Five Days, 18–22 March), Venice, Berlin (18 March), Budapest and Prague.
  • Common causes: the crop failures and economic depression of 1846–47; middle-class liberal demands for constitutions; nationalist demands from Germans, Italians, Hungarians and Czechs; and the example of Paris, spread quickly by newspapers, railways and the telegraph.
  • Britain and Russia escaped revolution, and by 1849 nearly every rising had been defeated by the old monarchies and their armies.

Background and causes in Germany

The German revolution grew out of economic distress, liberal frustration with the system of 1815 and the wish for a united German nation.

  • Potato blight from 1845 and poor grain harvests in 1846 and 1847 sharply raised food prices and caused hunger riots in many areas.
  • A commercial and industrial depression in 1847 brought bankruptcies and unemployment, hitting artisans already threatened by factory competition; the Silesian weavers' rising of 1844 had shown the desperation of hand workers.
  • Liberals, mostly educated middle-class men, wanted constitutions, freedom of the press and assembly, trial by jury and a German national parliament.
  • Nationalists wanted a German nation state, but disagreed whether it should include the German lands of the Habsburg Empire.
  • Peasants in the south and west wanted an end to the remaining feudal dues and services owed to landlords.
  • Liberal opposition had grown in the southern state parliaments and in Prussia's United Diet of 1847, which refused to vote a railway loan without constitutional concessions.

The March days, 1848

Within weeks of the Paris revolution, rulers across the German states gave way to crowds rather than risk being overthrown.

  • A mass meeting at Mannheim in Baden on 27 February 1848 demanded a free press, trial by jury, citizens' militias and a German parliament, and these March demands spread across the states.
  • Rulers appointed liberal March ministries, promised constitutions and lifted censorship in Baden, Württemberg, Hesse, Saxony and Hanover.
  • In Bavaria, Ludwig I, already discredited by his affair with the dancer Lola Montez, abdicated in March 1848.
  • In Berlin, troops fired on a crowd on 18 March, and a night of barricade fighting left about 300 civilians dead.
  • On 19 March Frederick William IV withdrew the troops from Berlin and was forced to bare his head before the bodies of the dead in the palace courtyard.
  • On 21 March he rode through Berlin wearing the black, red and gold colours and declared that Prussia would merge into Germany.
  • He appointed a liberal ministry under the Rhineland businessmen Camphausen and Hansemann and called a Prussian National Assembly, elected in May 1848.
  • Peasant risings in the south-west forced governments to begin abolishing feudal dues.

The Vorparlament and the Frankfurt National Assembly

  • The Vorparlament (pre-parliament), a self-appointed gathering of liberal politicians, met at Frankfurt from 31 March to 3 April 1848.
  • It rejected the radicals' call for a republic and arranged elections to a national assembly by broad manhood suffrage, although each state set its own rules and some excluded servants and men on poor relief.
  • Disappointed radicals in Baden, led by Friedrich Hecker and Gustav Struve, launched a republican rising in April 1848, which federal troops defeated at Kandern on 20 April.
  • The Frankfurt National Assembly opened on 18 May 1848 in the Paulskirche (St Paul's Church).
  • About 585 deputies were elected, and some 800 men sat at some point including substitutes; most were lawyers, judges, civil servants, teachers and professors, so it was nicknamed the professors' parliament.
  • Craftsmen were few, and there were almost no peasants or workers among the deputies.
  • The Assembly elected Heinrich von Gagern, a moderate liberal from Hesse-Darmstadt, as its president.
  • On 29 June 1848 it chose Archduke John of Austria as Reichsverweser (imperial regent) at the head of a Provisional Central Power, but this government had no army, civil service or tax revenue of its own.

The Assembly's work: Basic Rights and the national question

  • The Basic Rights of the German People, passed in December 1848, guaranteed equality before the law, freedom of the press, speech, religion, assembly and movement, and trial by jury.
  • They abolished the nobility as a privileged estate, landlords' private courts and feudal dues, and abolished the death penalty except under martial law.
  • Grossdeutsch (Greater German): a Germany including the German-speaking Habsburg lands, favoured by Catholics, Austrians and many southerners.
  • Kleindeutsch (Lesser German): a Germany united under Prussia without Austria, favoured by Gagern and most Protestant northern liberals.
  • The Grossdeutsch plan failed when Austria's new chief minister, Prince Felix Schwarzenberg, insisted in March 1849 that the whole multinational Habsburg Empire must enter any German state or none of it would.
  • The Assembly also claimed mixed areas ruled by others, such as parts of Prussian Poland (Posen), showing that liberal nationalism could deny other nations' rights.

Schleswig-Holstein and the September crisis

  • The duchies of Schleswig and Holstein were ruled by the King of Denmark; Holstein, wholly German, belonged to the German Confederation, while Schleswig had a mixed Danish and German population and did not.
  • When the new Danish king, Frederick VII, moved to incorporate Schleswig into Denmark in March 1848, the German population rose and set up a provisional government at Kiel.
  • Prussian troops under General Wrangel invaded on the Confederation's behalf, which the Assembly hailed as a national war.
  • Under pressure from Britain and Russia, Prussia signed the Malmö armistice in August 1848 without consulting Frankfurt.
  • The Assembly first rejected the armistice and then, having no army of its own, accepted it on 16 September 1848.
  • Radicals rioted in Frankfurt on 18 September and murdered two conservative deputies, Prince Felix Lichnowsky and General Hans von Auerswald, and the Assembly had to be saved by Prussian and Austrian troops.
  • Fighting resumed in 1849, but Prussia made peace with Denmark in 1850, the duchies' army was defeated at Idstedt that July, and the London Protocol of 1852 confirmed the Danish king's rule.

The recovery of Austria and Prussia, late 1848

By late 1848 the old governments had regained control of their capitals through force, while the liberals at Frankfurt were still debating.

  • In June 1848 the Austrian general Windischgrätz bombarded Prague and crushed the Czech rising, and in July Radetzky defeated Piedmont at Custoza.
  • A radical rising in Vienna in October 1848 was crushed when Windischgrätz and the Croatian commander Jelačić retook the city on 31 October.
  • Robert Blum, a radical Frankfurt deputy from Saxony, was shot in Vienna on 9 November 1848, despite his immunity as a member of the Assembly.
  • Schwarzenberg became Austrian chief minister in November, and on 2 December 1848 the 18-year-old Franz Joseph replaced Emperor Ferdinand.
  • In Berlin, Frederick William IV appointed a conservative ministry under Count Brandenburg, and General Wrangel's troops entered the city on 10 November 1848 without resistance.
  • On 5 December 1848 the king dissolved the Prussian National Assembly and imposed a constitution of his own, which kept royal control of the army and ministers.
  • In May 1849 Prussia brought in the three-class franchise, which divided voters into three classes by the taxes they paid; each class chose a third of the electors, so a small wealthy minority had a vastly greater say than the poor majority.

The imperial constitution and the refused crown, 1849

  • The Frankfurt constitution, completed on 27–28 March 1849, created a federal German Empire with a hereditary emperor, a lower house elected by manhood suffrage and an upper house representing the states.
  • On 28 March 1849 the Assembly elected Frederick William IV Emperor of the Germans by 290 votes, with 248 deputies abstaining.
  • Frederick William gave an evasive answer to the Assembly's delegation on 3 April and formally refused the crown on 28 April 1849, because he would not accept a crown from an elected assembly rather than from his fellow princes.
  • In private he scorned it as a crown picked up from the gutter of revolution.
  • Austria rejected the constitution and recalled its deputies in April 1849.
  • Twenty-eight smaller states accepted the constitution by mid-April, but Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony and Hanover rejected it, so it never came into force.

The final phase: the rump parliament, the 1849 risings and Olmütz

  • In May 1849 radicals rose in Saxony, the Palatinate and Baden to force the princes to accept the constitution; Richard Wagner and Mikhail Bakunin took part in the Dresden rising.
  • Prussian troops crushed the Dresden rising in May and the Baden and Palatinate risings by July 1849, when the fortress of Rastatt surrendered and many rebels were executed.
  • The moderates left Frankfurt, and about 100 radical deputies moved to Stuttgart as a rump parliament until Württemberg troops dispersed them on 18 June 1849.
  • Thousands of revolutionaries emigrated, many to the United States, where the Forty-Eighters included Hecker and Carl Schurz.
  • Prussia's Erfurt Union plan, drawn up by Joseph von Radowitz, tried in 1849–50 to unite Germany under Prussia by agreement of the princes, and an Erfurt Parliament met in March–April 1850.
  • Austria, backed by Russia, threatened war, and by the Punctation of Olmütz (29 November 1850) Prussia abandoned the union; Prussians remembered it as a humiliation.
  • The old Confederation and its Diet were restored in 1851 under Austrian presidency, with Bismarck as Prussia's delegate at Frankfurt from that year.

Why the revolution failed

  • The Frankfurt Assembly had no army, money or administration, so it depended on the very governments it was trying to limit.
  • The rulers kept control of their armies and bureaucracies, which stayed loyal and were used to retake Prague, Vienna and Berlin once the shock of March had passed.
  • Liberals were divided from radicals and workers: middle-class deputies feared a social revolution and a republic more than they feared the princes.
  • Peasants lost interest once governments abolished most feudal dues in 1848, as Austria did in September 1848.
  • Months of debate on the Basic Rights and the Grossdeutsch–Kleindeutsch question gave Austria and Prussia time to recover.
  • National rivalries with Danes, Poles and Czechs split the revolutionaries and gave the Habsburg army willing allies, such as Jelačić's Croats.
  • The refusal of the crown by Frederick William IV left the constitution without the one state strong enough to enforce it.

Assessment: results, significance and differing views

  • Failure: no united Germany was created, the Basic Rights were repealed by the restored Diet in 1851, and the old Confederation returned.
  • Achievement: feudal dues and serfdom were abolished, and Prussia kept a constitution and parliament after 1850, however limited.
  • Failure: liberals were discredited as impractical, and many concluded that unity would come only through Prussian power, which Bismarck's later methods confirmed.
  • Achievement: the Assembly was Germany's first national parliament, created political parties and provided a model for the constitutions of 1919 and the Basic Law of 1949.
  • Contemporary views: conservatives celebrated the restoration of order, while radicals and exiles, including Marx and Engels, blamed the cowardice of the liberal middle class.
  • Later views: A. J. P. Taylor wrote that in 1848 German history reached its turning point and failed to turn, a view linked to the idea of a German special path (Sonderweg) away from democracy; more recent historians stress the revolution's lasting gains in political culture and participation.

Key terms

Liberalism
In 1848, the demand of the educated middle class for a constitution, an elected parliament, civil liberties and the rule of law.
Nationalism
In 1848, the demand that all Germans be united in a single nation state with a national parliament instead of the 39 states of the Confederation.
Democracy
Government by the people through elected representatives chosen by all adult men, demanded by the radicals of 1848, who also wanted a republic.
Vorparlament
The self-appointed pre-parliament that met at Frankfurt from 31 March to 3 April 1848 and arranged elections to a national assembly.
Frankfurt National Assembly
The elected German parliament that met in the Paulskirche from 18 May 1848 to draw up a constitution for a united Germany.
Basic Rights
The civil liberties declared by the Frankfurt Assembly in December 1848, including equality before the law, a free press and the end of feudal privileges.
Grossdeutsch
The Greater German solution: a united Germany that would include the German-speaking lands of the Habsburg Empire.
Kleindeutsch
The Lesser German solution: a united Germany under Prussian leadership that excluded Austria.
Reichsverweser
Imperial regent: the title given by the Frankfurt Assembly in June 1848 to Archduke John of Austria as head of a provisional German government.
Malmö armistice
The August 1848 truce between Prussia and Denmark over Schleswig-Holstein, which the Assembly was forced to accept, exposing its weakness.
Three-class franchise
The Prussian voting system of 1849 that divided voters into three classes by taxes paid, giving the wealthy most of the voting power.
Punctation of Olmütz
The November 1850 agreement by which Prussia, under Austrian and Russian pressure, abandoned the Erfurt Union and accepted the restored Confederation.
