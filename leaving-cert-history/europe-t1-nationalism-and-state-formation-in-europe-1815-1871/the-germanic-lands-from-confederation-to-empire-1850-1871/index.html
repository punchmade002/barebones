Prussia's economic strength and Austria's weakness, 1850–1861

After the failure of 1848, the balance between Prussia and Austria within the German Confederation turned on economic power and on the ability of each state to manage its own affairs.

  • The Zollverein, which by the 1850s included most German states except Austria, gave Prussia control of a large common market and a share of the tariff revenue.
  • The Ruhr and Silesia provided coal and iron, and the growth of railways linked the Prussian industrial regions to Berlin and to the Rhineland.
  • Krupp's steel works at Essen, expanded from the 1850s, produced guns and rails and symbolised Prussia's industrial power.
  • Austria's finances were weak and its government remained centralised and conservative, with the Habsburg monarchy heavily in debt.
  • Austria's defeat in the Second Italian War of Independence (1859), when it lost Lombardy at the battles of Magenta and Solferino, exposed its military weakness.
  • Austria's proposed reform of the Confederation, the Frankfurt Princes' Congress of August 1863, collapsed after Prussia, on Bismarck's advice, refused to attend.
  • Austria had alienated Russia by its stance in the Crimean War (1853–56), so it lacked a friend when Prussia challenged it.
  • Prussia's 1862 commercial treaty with France set a low-tariff course that the Zollverein states followed, which Austria's protectionist system could not match.

Army reform and the constitutional conflict, 1860–1862

  • William became regent in 1858 and king in 1861; the army reform planned by Roon kept three-year service, enlarged the regular army and reduced the role of the citizen Landwehr, which the liberal majority in the Prussian Landtag opposed.
  • The liberals refused to vote the army budget, the king considered abdicating, and the constitutional crisis deepened.
  • Albrecht von Roon, War Minister from 1859, and Helmuth von Moltke, chief of the general staff from 1857, were the leading figures behind the army reforms.
  • In September 1862 William I appointed Otto von Bismarck as Minister-President and Foreign Minister of Prussia to break the deadlock.
  • Bismarck's government declared that it was entitled to continue collecting taxes during the conflict, which lasted until the Indemnity Bill of 1866.

Bismarck and the Danish War, 1864

  • The duchies of Schleswig and Holstein were ruled by the Danish king, but the German population of Holstein and parts of Schleswig wanted links with the German Confederation.
  • In November 1863 Denmark adopted a constitution that brought Schleswig closer to Denmark, which broke an earlier agreement and provoked the German states.
  • Prussia and Austria jointly invaded Denmark in February 1864, defeated it in the battles of Düppel and Als, and forced the Treaty of Vienna (October 1864).
  • Denmark gave up Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg to the two victors, and the question of their future was left to be settled between Prussia and Austria.
  • The Convention of Gastein (August 1865) gave Austria the administration of Holstein and Prussia the administration of Schleswig, which set up the conflict of 1866.
  • Christian IX succeeded to the Danish throne in November 1863 and signed the new constitution, which contravened the London Protocol of 1852 that had settled the succession to the duchies.

The Austro-Prussian War, the Treaty of Prague and the North German Confederation, 1866–1868

  • Bismarck secured Italian support for the war by an alliance with Italy in April 1866, which promised Venetia to Italy if Prussia won.
  • Prussia declared the Gastein arrangements at an end and occupied Holstein in June 1866, and most German states sided with Austria in the Confederation.
  • Prussia's breech-loading needle-gun, Moltke's planning and rapid rail mobilisation allowed its armies to advance quickly into Bohemia.
  • The battle of Königgrätz (also called Sadowa), fought on 3 July 1866, was the decisive Prussian victory of the war.
  • The Treaty of Prague (August 1866) dissolved the German Confederation and excluded Austria from German affairs.
  • Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, Frankfurt and Schleswig-Holstein, and the southern states signed secret military alliances with Prussia.
  • Austria was required to accept the dissolution of the Confederation and gave up Venetia, which passed to Italy, and the Habsburg lands lay outside any German state.
  • The North German Confederation was formed in 1867 by the states north of the River Main, under Prussian leadership.
  • Its constitution, which took effect in 1867, made the king of Prussia president of the Confederation, with Bismarck as its Chancellor, and gave the president control of foreign policy and the armed forces.
  • The Reichstag of the Confederation was elected by universal male suffrage, while the Bundesrat, the upper house of the states' governments, was dominated by Prussia.
  • The National Liberal Party was founded in 1867, and many liberals accepted Bismarck's leadership in return for national unification.
  • A Customs Parliament (Zollparlament) met in 1868 with deputies from the southern states as well as the north, although the south elected many opponents of Prussia.
  • After Königgrätz, Bismarck persuaded William I to halt at the Nikolsburg preliminaries (July 1866) and not to march on Vienna or take Austrian territory, to keep Austria as a future friend.
  • The Indemnity Bill of 1866 asked the Landtag to approve retrospectively the years of government without a budget, and it split the liberals into those who gave way and those who stayed in opposition.

The Franco-Prussian War and the Ems telegram, 1870

  • In 1870 Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, a relative of the Prussian king, was offered the vacant Spanish throne, which alarmed France, which feared encirclement.
  • Leopold's candidacy was withdrawn on 12 July 1870 after French pressure, but France then demanded a guarantee that it would never recur.
  • On 13 July 1870 King William I at Bad Ems refused to give that guarantee, and his telegram to Bismarck was edited by Bismarck and published in a shortened form.
  • The edited Ems telegram made the exchange seem an insult to France, and France declared war on Prussia on 19 July 1870.
  • The southern German states, Baden, Württemberg and Bavaria, joined the war on the Prussian side under the terms of their treaties with Prussia.
  • The French army was defeated at Wörth and Spicheren in August 1870 and at Sedan on 2 September 1870, where Napoleon III was captured.
  • The siege of Paris lasted from September 1870 to January 1871, and the French government of National Defence continued the war until the city surrendered.
  • Napoleon III led the army to the front, and Moltke's armies, using railways and superior artillery, won quickly; after Sedan the Second Empire fell on 4 September 1870 and Gambetta's Government of National Defence carried on the war.
  • The French army at Metz surrendered in October 1870 and Paris capitulated in January 1871, with an armistice signed on 28 January.

The proclamation of the German Empire, 1871

  • In November 1870 treaties brought the southern states into the new union, and in late 1870 King Ludwig II of Bavaria sent a letter, drafted with Bismarck's help, inviting William I to accept the imperial title.
  • The German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, with William I as German Emperor.
  • The Treaty of Frankfurt (May 1871) ended the war, transferring Alsace (except Belfort) and part of Lorraine, including Metz, to Germany and requiring France to pay an indemnity of five billion francs.
  • The constitution of the German Empire, adopted in spring 1871 and based on that of the North German Confederation, created a federal state with a Reichstag, a Bundesrat and an emperor who was also King of Prussia.
  • The Chancellor answered to the emperor, not to the Reichstag, and Prussia, with its own conservative franchise, dominated the new state.

Key personality: Otto von Bismarck (unification, 1862–1871)

Bismarck was a Prussian conservative and Junker landowner who used war and diplomacy to create the German Empire. This part covers his role in the unification process; his later career is covered in the notes on the Second Reich and the Bismarckian system.

  • Born in 1815 into a Junker landowning family in the Prussian province of Saxony, Bismarck sat in the United Diet of 1847 as an ultra-conservative and in the Prussian Landtag from 1849.
  • He was Prussian representative at the restored German Diet in Frankfurt from 1851 to 1859, and then ambassador in St Petersburg and Paris before becoming Minister-President in 1862.
  • In his speech to the Budget Committee on 30 September 1862 he said that the great questions of the day would be decided not by speeches and majority votes but by blood and iron.
  • As Minister-President he argued that the constitution had a gap on what happened when king and Landtag disagreed (the gap theory), and so collected taxes and carried through the army reform without an approved budget.
  • He acted as a participant at each stage of unification, managing the Danish War, the Gastein settlement, the alliance with Italy and the Ems telegram.
  • Contemporaries were divided: conservatives admired his success, while many liberals saw him as an authoritarian who had broken the constitution.
  • Historians disagree: admirers in the Prussian school saw a master plan, A. J. P. Taylor portrayed an opportunist improvising, and Lothar Gall called him a white revolutionary who used revolutionary means to preserve conservative power.
  • He became Chancellor of the North German Confederation in 1867 and Imperial Chancellor in 1871, holding office until 1890.

Bismarck's methods: Realpolitik and opportunism

  • Realpolitik, a word coined by Ludwig von Rochau in 1853, meant pursuing state interests through power and diplomacy rather than through principles or ideology.
  • Bismarck used liberal demands for national unity to win support for his policies, while keeping the army and the monarchy under royal control.
  • He isolated Austria diplomatically: Russian goodwill after the Alvensleben Convention of 1863 against the Polish rising, Napoleon III's neutrality after their meeting at Biarritz in 1865, and the alliance with Italy in 1866.
  • He set out limited war aims and avoided humiliating Austria in 1866, which made it easier for Austria to become a partner later.
  • He used the Ems telegram to create a public crisis that drew the southern states into the war, which showed his readiness to exploit an opportunity.

Germany and Italy compared

  • Italy was unified between 1859 and 1871, with the Kingdom of Italy proclaimed in 1861 and Venetia and Rome added in 1866 and 1870.
  • Both unifications were led by a strong kingdom (Piedmont or Prussia) that won wars with the help of diplomacy: Piedmont used French help against Austria in 1859, while Prussia relied on the neutrality of Russia and France.
  • Italy's unification was driven by a mix of Piedmontese leadership and Garibaldi's volunteers, while Germany's was directed by the Prussian state and its army.
  • Italy won Venetia thanks to Prussia's victory in 1866, but Rome was blocked until the French garrison left in 1870.
  • Italy's unity came partly from below, through Garibaldi's volunteers and plebiscites, while Germany's came from above, through the Prussian army and the princes.
  • Italy left Italian-speaking Trentino and Trieste in Austria, while Germany placed Poles, Danes and Alsatians inside the Empire.

Assessment: plan or opportunism, and the limits of the settlement

  • Unity, the aim of the liberals of 1848, was achieved by 1871, but from above by Prussian arms rather than by a parliament.
  • The settlement left Austria, with its millions of Germans, outside the Empire (the Kleindeutsch solution), and the Empire contained Polish, Danish and, after 1871, French-speaking minorities.
  • The new Empire was dominated by Prussia, and the Reichstag, though elected by manhood suffrage, had no control over the Chancellor and less power than the liberals had hoped.
  • Liberal supporters of unification saw the Empire as a national triumph, while later critics argued that it was a Prussian-led autocracy built on militarism.

Key terms

Realpolitik
Politics based on the pursuit of state interests through power and diplomacy rather than on principles or ideology, associated with Bismarck.
Blood and iron
Bismarck's phrase from his 1862 speech to the Budget Committee, meaning that national questions would be decided by war and military strength rather than by majority votes.
North German Confederation
The federation of north German states formed in 1867 under Prussian leadership, which was the basis for the German Empire.
Treaty of Prague
The August 1866 treaty that ended the Austro-Prussian War, dissolved the German Confederation and excluded Austria from German affairs.
Ems telegram
The telegram from King William I at Bad Ems, edited by Bismarck in July 1870, whose shortened version provoked France into war.
Reichstag
The elected lower house of the North German Confederation and the German Empire, chosen by universal male suffrage.
Bundesrat
The upper house of the North German Confederation and the German Empire, made up of the governments of the member states and dominated by Prussia.
Autocracy
Rule by a single ruler with unlimited power; the Prussian-German system was a semi-autocracy in which the monarch, the army and a Chancellor answerable only to the emperor kept decisive power.
Nationalism
The belief that a people sharing language and culture should form a single state, which was the basis of the German unification movement.
Hohenzollern candidature
The 1870 proposal of a Prussian prince for the Spanish throne, whose French opposition led to the Ems crisis and the Franco-Prussian War.
