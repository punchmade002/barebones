The restored Bourbons and the Charter, 1814–1830

After Napoleon's first abdication the Bourbon monarchy was restored, but it accepted a constitution. Its later refusal to respect that constitution brought the July Revolution of 1830.

  • Louis XVIII returned to the throne in 1814 and issued the Charter (Charte constitutionnelle) in June, which kept many Napoleonic institutions and set up a two-chamber legislature, with the king alone proposing laws.
  • The franchise under the Charter was very narrow: only men who paid high direct taxes could vote, so the electorate was a small minority of wealthy property owners.
  • Louis XVIII's government carried out a White Terror in 1815 against Bonapartists and Revolutionaries, with summary violence and reprisals, especially in the south.
  • Charles X succeeded in 1824 and tried to restore the authority of the Church and the nobility, passing a law on sacrilege (1825) and compensating the émigrés for lands lost in the Revolution.
  • The elections of 1827 returned a liberal opposition majority against the government, and in August 1829 Charles X appointed Prince Polignac, whose ministry lacked support in the chamber.
  • On 25 July 1830 Charles X issued the July Ordinances, which dissolved the Chamber, restricted the press and changed the electoral rules.
  • Paris rose on 27–29 July 1830 (the Three Glorious Days), and Charles X abdicated on 2 August and went into exile in Britain; his one lasting success was the conquest of Algiers in 1830.

The July Monarchy of Louis-Philippe, 1830–1848

  • The liberal deputies chose Louis-Philippe, Duke of Orléans, as King of the French, which made the monarchy a constitutional one with a middle-class character.
  • The 1831 electoral law lowered the tax qualification, which raised the electorate from roughly 100,000 to about 170,000, and it had reached about 240,000 by 1848, still under 1 per cent of the population.
  • François Guizot, the leading minister from 1840 to 1848, opposed any further extension of the vote and urged the middle class to enrich themselves through work and thrift.
  • The opposition used the banquet campaign of 1847–48, public dinners at which reform was demanded, to put pressure on the government.
  • Guizot banned a banquet planned for Paris in February 1848, and the ban led directly to the crowds that brought down the July Monarchy.
  • Opposition came from legitimists, Bonapartists and republicans, and the regime met workers' risings in Lyon (1831 and 1834) and an assassination attempt in 1835 that led to repressive press laws.
  • An economic crisis in 1846–47, with poor harvests and unemployment, weakened the regime before the banquet campaign.

The February Revolution and the Second Republic, 1848

  • Fighting in Paris on 22–24 February 1848 brought barricades, the defection of much of the National Guard and the dismissal of Guizot, and Louis-Philippe abdicated on 24 February.
  • A provisional government was proclaimed the Second Republic on 24 February 1848, with the poet Alphonse de Lamartine as one of its leading members.
  • The provisional government granted universal male suffrage on 2 March 1848, which raised the electorate from about 250,000 to about 9 million men.
  • National Workshops (Ateliers nationaux) were set up in late February 1848 to give work to the unemployed, which became a heavy cost to the government and a target for conservatives.
  • The National Workshops were closed in June 1848, and workers resisted the closure with the June Days (23–26 June 1848), an insurrection in Paris.
  • General Louis-Eugène Cavaignac, given emergency powers, crushed the June Days with troops, leading to many deaths and thousands of arrests.

The presidency and Louis-Napoleon's election, 1848–1851

  • A constitution of November 1848 established a president elected by universal male suffrage for a single four-year term, with a single-chamber Legislative Assembly.
  • Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte, nephew of Napoleon I, was elected president on 10 December 1848 with about three-quarters of the vote, beating Cavaignac.
  • Louis-Napoléon had been imprisoned at Ham after an attempted rising at Boulogne in 1840, and he had lived in exile in Britain and elsewhere.
  • The Party of Order, made up of monarchists and conservatives, won a large majority in the Legislative Assembly elections of May 1849.
  • The Falloux Law (March 1850) gave the Catholic Church a major role in education, and in May 1850 the conservative majority restricted the franchise, removing about three million voters.
  • Louis-Napoléon's campaign to change the constitution to allow his re-election failed in the Assembly in July 1851.

The coup and the Second Empire, 1851–1860

  • On 2 December 1851 Louis-Napoléon carried out a coup d'état, arresting deputies and dissolving the Assembly, and armed resistance in Paris and the provinces was suppressed.
  • A plebiscite in December 1851 approved the coup by a very large majority and a new constitution of January 1852 gave the president a ten-year term and wide powers; the plebiscite of November 1852 approved the restoration of the empire.
  • Louis-Napoléon proclaimed the Second Empire on 2 December 1852 and took the name Napoleon III, with the title of Emperor of the French.
  • The Second Empire in its early years was authoritarian: the press was censored, political opponents were exiled, and elections were controlled by official candidates.
  • The Crédit Mobilier, founded in 1852 by the Pereire brothers, financed railways, industry and urban development, and rapid economic growth followed in the 1850s.
  • The Paris Exposition of 1855 and the rebuilding of Paris under Haussmann showed the regime's commitment to modern economy and prestige.
  • Napoleon III married Eugénie de Montijo in 1853, and the regime promoted railway building and large public works.

The liberal Empire and foreign policy, 1860–1870

  • The Cobden–Chevalier Treaty of 1860 with Britain reduced French tariffs, opening French markets to British goods and encouraging French industry to modernise.
  • From 1860 the regime gave the legislature more freedom to debate, and in 1864 it legalised the right to strike, though trade unions remained illegal; the press law of 1868 relaxed censorship.
  • France fought in the Crimean War (1854–56) as Britain's ally and it helped Piedmont in 1859, which led to the French gains of Nice and Savoy in 1860.
  • Napoleon III's plan for a French-backed empire in Mexico under the Habsburg Archduke Maximilian collapsed, French troops withdrew by 1867, and Maximilian was executed by Mexican republicans.
  • Napoleon III's government lost prestige after the Austrian war of 1866 and the Prussian victory, which had changed the balance of power in Europe.
  • Émile Ollivier formed a liberal ministry in 1870, and a plebiscite in May 1870 approved the liberal reforms by a large majority.

Key personality: Napoleon III

Napoleon III was a participant in the political events of 1848 to 1870 and a symbol of the French regime. Contemporaries and historians disagree about whether he was a modernising leader or an autocrat who led France into disaster.

  • Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte was born in Paris in 1808, the son of Louis Bonaparte, King of Holland, and the nephew of Napoleon I.
  • He attempted coups in Strasbourg (1836) and Boulogne (1840), both of which failed, and he spent about six years in prison at Ham, where he wrote on social questions, before escaping in 1846.
  • As president of the Second Republic he won the election of December 1848 by appealing to peasants and to the memory of his uncle, and he was supported by conservatives who expected to use him.
  • His coup of December 1851 and the Second Empire that followed made him the central political figure in France until 1870.
  • In foreign policy he backed Piedmont in 1859, joined the Crimean War, supported the Mexican adventure and then led France into war with Prussia in 1870.
  • He was captured at Sedan on 2 September 1870, was held in Germany and then went into exile in England, where he died in 1873.
  • Contemporaries were divided: supporters praised economic growth and Paris, while republicans and liberals such as Victor Hugo denounced him as a usurper.
  • Hugo and Marx, in Napoléon le Petit and The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, depicted him as a usurper and a parody of his uncle; revisionist historians such as Theodore Zeldin and Roger Price stress his interest in social reform, his economic programme and his liberalising steps, though they judge his foreign policy a failure.

Key personality: Victor Hugo, opponent and exile

Hugo was a politician as well as a writer. His political role in this period was as a deputy and an opponent of the Empire; his literary work is covered in the notes on cultural nationalism.

  • Victor Hugo, born in 1802, was a leading Romantic writer and a monarchist in the 1820s, and he had become a republican by 1848.
  • He was elected to the Constituent Assembly of 1848 and the Legislative Assembly, where he defended freedom of the press and education and opposed the restriction of the franchise.
  • He supported Louis-Napoléon's candidacy in 1848 but broke with him, and by 1851 was a leader of the democratic opposition in the Legislative Assembly.
  • After the coup of December 1851 he was forced to leave France, first to Brussels, then to Jersey (1852–55) and Guernsey (1855–70).
  • His pamphlet Napoléon le Petit (1852) and his satirical poems Les Châtiments (1853) attacked Napoleon III and made him a symbol of republican resistance.
  • He refused the amnesty offered in 1859 and stayed in exile on Guernsey as a protest against the regime.
  • Hugo returned to Paris on 5 September 1870, the day after the Third Republic was proclaimed; during the Commune he criticised its excesses but opposed the repression and offered asylum to fugitives in Brussels.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a republican hero, while critics saw his shifts from royalism to republicanism as inconsistent; his funeral in 1885 drew huge crowds, and historians now see him as a principled defender of liberty and democracy.

Sedan, the Third Republic and the Paris Commune

  • After Sedan the Empire collapsed, and a republic was proclaimed in Paris on 4 September 1870 by deputies from Paris, including Léon Gambetta.
  • A Government of National Defence continued the war against Prussia, and Paris was besieged from September 1870 until the armistice of January 1871; Gambetta left the city by balloon to organise resistance in the provinces.
  • The National Assembly elected in February 1871 was dominated by monarchists, with Adolphe Thiers as head of the executive, and it sat at Bordeaux and then at Versailles, not in Paris.
  • The Paris Commune of 18 March to 28 May 1871 began when Thiers's troops tried to seize the National Guard's cannon on Montmartre; it was a revolutionary government of Parisian radicals and socialists, suppressed by government troops in the Bloody Week with many thousands killed.
  • The Third Republic was confirmed in 1875 by constitutional laws, which established a parliamentary system with a president.

Assessment: why France was unstable

  • Five regimes between 1814 and 1871 (the Bourbon Restoration, the July Monarchy, the Second Republic, the Second Empire and the Third Republic) reflected the lack of agreement about the basis of government.
  • The Bourbon and July regimes were limited by narrow franchises, and the exclusion of most men from political life made opposition likely.
  • The Second Republic showed that universal male suffrage could bring a president who would destroy the republic, and it deepened the divide between the urban poor and the conservative countryside.
  • The Second Empire gave France economic growth and prestige but depended on the personal authority of Napoleon III, and its foreign policy failures weakened it.
  • Overall: France's regimes were unstable because each one lacked a broad consensus, and the authority of the state rested on the personal position of its rulers rather than on a settled constitutional system.

Key terms

Charter (Charte constitutionnelle)
The constitution granted by Louis XVIII in 1814, which kept many Napoleonic institutions and set up a two-chamber legislature with a narrow franchise.
July Ordinances
The decrees of 25 July 1830 by Charles X that dissolved the Chamber and restricted the press, which triggered the July Revolution.
July Monarchy
The constitutional monarchy of Louis-Philippe from 1830 to 1848, with a narrow franchise and power concentrated among wealthy property owners.
National Workshops
The state schemes set up in February 1848 to give work to the unemployed, which were closed in June 1848 and provoked the June Days.
June Days
The insurrection of 23–26 June 1848 in Paris, provoked by the closure of the National Workshops and crushed by General Cavaignac.
Coup d'état of 2 December 1851
Louis-Napoléon's seizure of power, which dissolved the Assembly and led to a plebiscite approving the extended powers of the president.
Second Empire
The regime of Napoleon III from 1852 to 1870, first authoritarian and later more liberal, which ended with defeat at Sedan.
Liberalism
The doctrine favouring constitutional government, civil liberties and limits on the power of rulers, which the July Monarchy and the Charter claimed to uphold.
Democracy
Government by the people through elected representatives, which in France meant universal male suffrage from 1848.
Autocracy
A system in which a single ruler holds unlimited power, which Napoleon III's authoritarian Empire represented in its early years.
Paris Commune
The revolutionary government of Paris from 18 March to 28 May 1871, suppressed by government troops in the Bloody Week.
