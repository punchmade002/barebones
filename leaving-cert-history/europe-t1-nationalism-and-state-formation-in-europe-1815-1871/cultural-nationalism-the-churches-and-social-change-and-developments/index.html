Romanticism and cultural nationalism

Romanticism celebrated emotion, the past and the spirit of a people. Its artists and writers gave nationalist movements their myths, symbols and languages.

  • Romantic writers and artists rejected the rational order of the Enlightenment in favour of feeling, imagination and the national past.
  • The idea of a national spirit, the Volksgeist, was developed by the German thinker Johann Gottfried Herder in the late eighteenth century and was taken up by nationalists.
  • Folklore collection and the revival of medieval legends were central to cultural nationalism, since they claimed to reveal the true character of a people.
  • Cultural nationalism was most powerful where a nation lacked its own state, as in Italy, Germany, Poland and the Czech lands.
  • Artists and composers often worked with patrons and publishers in national capitals, and their audiences helped to shape national identity.
  • Victor Hugo's preface to his play Cromwell (1827) served as a manifesto for Romantic drama.

Music, painting and national identity

  • Frédéric Chopin wrote mazurkas and polonaises that drew on Polish dance rhythms, and his music became a symbol of Polish national feeling in exile after 1831.
  • Franz Liszt, born in Hungary, wrote the Hungarian Rhapsodies in the 1840s and 1850s, which drew on Hungarian and Romani music and presented Hungary's national identity to European audiences.
  • Giuseppe Verdi's opera Nabucco (1842) included the chorus Va, pensiero, sung by Hebrew exiles, which Italian audiences came to hear as a call for national liberation.
  • Around 1859 the cry 'Viva VERDI' was popularly said to be a coded slogan, since the letters could stand for Vittorio Emanuele Re d'Italia, the king the nationalists wanted.
  • Richard Wagner used German myths and legends in his operas, and his Ring cycle, first performed in full at Bayreuth in 1876, drew on the Nibelungen legend and Norse sagas.
  • Eugène Delacroix's painting Liberty Leading the People (1830) showed a personification of liberty leading the Parisian crowd over the barricades during the July Revolution.
  • Caspar David Friedrich's landscapes, such as Wanderer above the Sea of Fog (about 1818), expressed Romantic feeling for nature and the German spirit.
  • Romantic historical painting showed scenes from national history, which gave citizens a shared image of their past.
  • Bedřich Smetana's opera The Bartered Bride (1866) and his cycle of symphonic poems Má vlast (1874–79) drew on Czech folk music and landscape to express Czech national feeling.

Literature and the standardisation of languages

  • The Brothers Grimm published their collection of folk tales, Children's and Household Tales, in 1812 and 1815, which presented German folklore as the expression of the German people; they also began a great German dictionary in 1838.
  • Alessandro Manzoni's novel I promessi sposi (The Betrothed), published in 1827, was set in Lombardy under Spanish rule, and Manzoni's revised edition of 1840–42, written in the educated Tuscan of Florence, became a model for standard Italian.
  • Adam Mickiewicz, the Polish poet, wrote Pan Tadeusz (1834), a national epic set in Lithuania, which celebrated the Polish national past.
  • Alexander Pushkin, author of Eugene Onegin, wrote in a Russian literary language that became the model for later writers, and his works were a central part of Russian national culture.
  • Standardising national languages, such as Italian and Czech, through grammars, dictionaries (Josef Jungmann's Czech dictionary of 1835–39) and schools helped to create a sense of a single national community; Elias Lönnrot's Kalevala (1835) did the same for Finland.

Key personality: Victor Hugo (literary role)

Hugo's literary work made him the most celebrated writer of French Romanticism. His political career and exile are covered in the notes on the French state.

  • Victor Hugo's play Hernani, staged in 1830, provoked a riot between Romantic supporters and classical critics, and it marked the victory of Romanticism in French theatre.
  • His novel Notre-Dame de Paris (1831) revived interest in the Gothic past of Paris and helped to inspire the restoration of medieval buildings.
  • His collection of poems Les Contemplations (1856) was written in part in memory of his daughter Léopoldine, who drowned in 1843, and it expressed grief and faith.
  • His novel Les Misérables (1862), written largely in exile on Guernsey, follows the convict Jean Valjean from 1815 to the Paris rising of 1832 and portrays the poor of France; it became one of the most widely read novels of the century.
  • Hugo was elected to the Académie française in 1841, a sign of his standing as a literary figure before his political career developed.
  • Contemporaries admired him as a national poet and a leader of Romanticism, while classical critics attacked his works for breaking the rules of form and taste.
  • Historians now regard Hugo as one of the greatest French writers, and his combination of literary and political engagement is seen as a model for later writers.

The Catholic revival in France

  • The Catholic revival of the early nineteenth century followed the anticlerical policies of the Revolution and Napoleon, and it restored the Church's role in education and public life.
  • Félicité de Lamennais founded the newspaper L'Avenir in 1830, which argued for a separation of Church and state and for freedom of the press and association.
  • Pope Gregory XVI condemned Lamennais's ideas in the encyclical Mirari vos in 1832, and Lamennais broke with the Church in 1834.
  • Frédéric Ozanam founded the Society of St Vincent de Paul in 1833, a lay Catholic charity that visited the poor and the sick in their homes.
  • The apparitions of the Virgin Mary to Bernadette Soubirous at Lourdes in 1858 gave rise to a major pilgrimage cult that spread through France.
  • The Falloux Law of 1850 allowed the Church to open secondary schools freely and gave bishops places on education councils.
  • Pius IX proclaimed the dogma of the Immaculate Conception in 1854 and issued the Syllabus of Errors, condemning liberalism, in 1864; his rule in Rome was protected by a French garrison under Napoleon III.

The churches and social change in Britain

  • Methodism, founded by John Wesley in the eighteenth century, grew rapidly among workers in the industrial towns and the mining districts.
  • John Keble's assize sermon of 14 July 1833 launched the Oxford Movement, which sought to restore Catholic elements in the Church of England.
  • Christian Socialism, led by F. D. Maurice and Charles Kingsley from 1848, sought to apply Christian principles to the problems of industrial society.
  • Sunday schools and the ragged schools provided education to poor children, and they were run mainly by churches and charities.
  • Temperance societies campaigned against drunkenness, which was seen as a cause of poverty and family breakdown among the working class.
  • Churches in the industrial towns ran city missions and charitable work, and they responded to urban poverty with preaching, relief and education.
  • The religious census of 1851 revealed that large numbers of working people did not attend church, and the Salvation Army began in East London as the Christian Mission of William Booth in 1865.
  • Evangelical Christians such as Lord Shaftesbury led campaigns for factory reform, and the Preston teetotal movement of 1832 helped to launch the temperance cause.

Developments in electricity and communication

  • Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831, which made the electric generator and transformer possible.
  • Faraday's laws of electrolysis (1833–34) showed that the amount of substance deposited at an electrode is proportional to the electric charge passed through the solution.
  • The electric telegraph was developed in Britain by William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone, who patented their system in 1837, and it was first used on railways from the late 1830s.
  • Samuel Morse's telegraph system was demonstrated in 1844 on a line between Washington and Baltimore, and Morse code was widely adopted.
  • James Clerk Maxwell's 1865 theory of the electromagnetic field showed that light is an electromagnetic wave, unifying the theories of electricity and light.
  • A submarine telegraph cable was laid across the Atlantic in 1858, but it failed after a few weeks, and a lasting cable was completed in 1866.
  • Werner von Siemens's dynamo of 1866 made the large-scale generation of electricity possible.

Key personality: Charles Darwin

Darwin was a naturalist whose theory of evolution by natural selection transformed biology. He was a witness to nature during his voyage and a theorist when he wrote his main work.

  • Charles Darwin was born in Shrewsbury in 1809, studied medicine at Edinburgh and then divinity at Cambridge, and joined the voyage of HMS Beagle as naturalist.
  • On the Beagle (1831–1836) he collected fossils, plants and animals in South America, the Galápagos Islands and Australia, and he observed the variation of species.
  • He developed the theory of evolution by natural selection in the years after his return, delayed publication for about twenty years, and published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, after Alfred Russel Wallace had reached the same idea.
  • The Origin argued that species change over time through variation and natural selection, and it proposed that all living things share common ancestors.
  • The Descent of Man, published in 1871, applied the theory to human origins.
  • Contemporaries were divided: scientists such as Thomas Henry Huxley defended him, while Bishop Samuel Wilberforce and many churchmen opposed his ideas at the Oxford debate of 1860, at which Darwin was not present.
  • Historians now regard Darwin as the founder of modern evolutionary biology, though his theory was later developed with genetics, which explained inheritance; he was buried in Westminster Abbey in 1882.

Developments in biology, medicine and technology

  • Gregor Mendel published his experiments on pea plants in 1866, showing that inheritance follows particular patterns, but his work was ignored until its rediscovery in 1900.
  • Louis Pasteur's experiments of the late 1850s showed that fermentation and decay are caused by micro-organisms, and his work developed the germ theory of disease.
  • Pasteur's swan-neck flask experiments, published in 1861, showed that micro-organisms do not appear spontaneously in sterile broth, which undermined the theory of spontaneous generation.
  • The daguerreotype photographic process was announced in 1839, and Henry Fox Talbot's calotype process followed in 1841, establishing photography as a new medium.
  • The steam railway, the telegraph and the steamship transformed communication and trade, linking nations and empires in a faster network.
  • Joseph Lister introduced antiseptic surgery in 1867 using carbolic acid, and chloroform anaesthesia was introduced by James Young Simpson in 1847.
  • Isambard Kingdom Brunel's iron screw steamship SS Great Britain (1843) and the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 transformed sea transport.

Assessment: cultural and scientific change

  • Achievement: cultural nationalism gave national movements powerful symbols, languages and histories, which helped to create a sense of national belonging.
  • Limit: cultural nationalism often idealised the past and excluded minorities, and national myths could be used to justify conflict.
  • Achievement: the Catholic revival and the churches' social work gave the poor relief and education, and it renewed the role of religion in public life.
  • Limit: the churches were divided between conservative and reforming wings, and their social work did not address the causes of poverty in industrial society.
  • Achievement: Darwin, Mendel and Pasteur transformed biology and medicine, and electricity and communication changed how people and states interacted.
  • Overall: the cultural and scientific developments of the period were major achievements that enriched national identities and expanded knowledge, but they also raised new questions about tradition, religion and national belonging that remained contested.

Key terms

Romanticism
The cultural movement of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries that celebrated emotion, imagination, the national past and the spirit of a people.
Volksgeist
The idea, developed by Herder, of a national spirit expressed in a people's language, folklore and history.
Cultural nationalism
The use of art, music, literature and language to express and create a sense of national identity.
Oxford Movement
The movement within the Church of England, begun by John Keble's 1833 sermon, that sought to restore Catholic elements to the Church.
Natural selection
Darwin's mechanism of evolution, in which individuals with variations suited to their environment survive and reproduce more successfully.
Germ theory
The theory, developed by Pasteur and others, that many diseases are caused by micro-organisms.
Electromagnetic induction
The principle, discovered by Faraday in 1831, that a changing magnetic field produces an electric current.
Electric telegraph
A system that sent messages along wires by electric current, developed in the 1830s and 1840s.
Nationalism
The belief that people sharing a language, culture and history form a nation that should have its own state, which artists, writers and musicians promoted in the nineteenth century.
Christian Socialism
The movement begun in 1848 by F. D. Maurice and Charles Kingsley to apply Christian principles to the problems of industrial society.
Falloux Law
The French law of 1850 that gave the Catholic Church a large role in schooling by allowing church secondary schools and giving bishops seats on education councils.
