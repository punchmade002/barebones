The Congress of Vienna, 1814–15

The powers that defeated Napoleon met at Vienna to redraw the map of Europe. Their aims were to contain France, restore the old order and prevent another bid for European domination.

  • The Congress met from autumn 1814, and its Final Act was signed on 9 June 1815, nine days before the battle of Waterloo (18 June 1815).
  • Its leading figures were Metternich for Austria, Castlereagh for Britain, Tsar Alexander I for Russia, Hardenberg for Prussia and Talleyrand for restored France.
  • Legitimacy: rulers deposed by Napoleon were restored, with the Bourbons returned to the thrones of France, Spain and Naples.
  • Balance of power: a ring of buffer states was built around France: the Kingdom of the Netherlands, enlarged to include Belgium; Piedmont-Sardinia, strengthened by gaining Genoa; and a neutral Switzerland.
  • Austria gave up the Austrian Netherlands but gained Lombardy-Venetia in northern Italy, and Habsburg relatives ruled Tuscany, Modena and Parma, making Austria the dominant power in the peninsula.
  • Prussia gained the Rhineland and Westphalia, which made it guardian of the Rhine frontier against France, together with about two-fifths of Saxony and Swedish Pomerania.
  • Russia gained most of the Duchy of Warsaw as the Congress Kingdom of Poland, with the Tsar as its king.
  • Britain kept colonies and bases taken during the wars, including Malta, Heligoland, Ceylon and the Cape Colony, and Castlereagh pressed for a moderate peace that did not humiliate France.
  • The Papal States were restored to the Pope.
  • Nationalist and liberal hopes awakened by the French Revolution and Napoleon were ignored: Germans, Italians, Poles and Belgians were placed under rulers chosen by the great powers.

The German Confederation and its Diet

  • The Congress replaced the Holy Roman Empire, dissolved in 1806, with a German Confederation (Bund) of 39 sovereign states, set out in the Federal Act of June 1815.
  • Its only institution was a Diet (Bundestag) at Frankfurt made up of delegates sent by the member governments, with Austria presiding; there was no elected national parliament.
  • Important decisions needed large majorities or unanimity, and the member states kept their own rulers and armies, so the Bund could act only when Austria and Prussia agreed.
  • Its main purpose was the security of its members against France and against revolution, not German unity.
  • Article 13 of the Federal Act promised each state a constitution based on assemblies of estates; Bavaria and Baden (1818) and Württemberg (1819) granted constitutions, but Austria and Prussia did not, which angered liberals.
  • Austria and Prussia were the two dominant members, and their rivalry shaped German politics until 1866.

The Concert of Europe and the Congress System

After 1815 the great powers met at congresses to manage crises. Metternich turned these meetings into a means of suppressing revolution.

  • The Quadruple Alliance of Britain, Russia, Austria and Prussia (November 1815) bound the four to uphold the settlement, keep France in check and meet at intervals.
  • The Holy Alliance (September 1815), proposed by Alexander I and signed by Russia, Austria and Prussia, pledged rulers to govern by Christian principles; Britain refused to join, and Metternich called it empty but used it to bind the eastern monarchies together.
  • Aix-la-Chapelle (1818) ended the allied occupation of France and admitted France to the Concert of Europe.
  • Troppau (1820): Austria, Prussia and Russia signed the Troppau Protocol, claiming the right to intervene against revolutions that threatened other states; Britain and France refused to sign.
  • Laibach (1821) authorised Austrian troops to crush the revolution in Naples, and Austria also put down the Piedmontese rising that year.
  • Verona (1822): the eastern powers backed a French invasion of Spain to restore Ferdinand VII, which went ahead in 1823; Britain, whose foreign secretary was now Canning after Castlereagh's death in August 1822, refused and left the Congress System.
  • Britain's withdrawal, and its later support for Latin American and Greek independence, left the system as an alliance of the three eastern autocracies.

Metternich's system of repression

Metternich's aim was to preserve the 1815 settlement and the multinational Habsburg Empire, which liberalism and nationalism threatened to break apart.

  • Austria was an autocracy under Emperor Francis I (to 1835) and then the feeble Ferdinand I: there was no constitution or parliament, and power rested with the emperor, his ministers, the army and the bureaucracy.
  • Inside Austria, police, informers, postal interception and strict censorship of books, newspapers and the theatre watched and silenced critics.
  • The Carlsbad Decrees followed the murder in March 1819 of the conservative writer August von Kotzebue by Karl Sand, a radical student; they were drawn up at a ministers' conference at Carlsbad in August and adopted by the Diet in September 1819.
  • The Decrees banned the Burschenschaften, put the universities under government supervisors, allowed liberal professors to be dismissed and imposed censorship on newspapers and shorter publications.
  • A Central Investigating Commission at Mainz was set up under the Decrees to hunt down revolutionary conspiracies across the German states.
  • The Six Articles of June 1832, passed by the Diet after the Hambach Festival, reasserted the princes' authority: state parliaments could not refuse taxes to force concessions, and the Diet could intervene in states where order broke down.
  • Further measures in July 1832 banned political associations and public meetings and tightened press controls.
  • Abroad, Metternich used the congresses and the alliance with Russia and Prussia to justify intervention, and Austrian troops crushed risings in Italy in 1821 and 1831.
  • The system relied on Prussia's cooperation inside the Bund and on Russia's support in Europe, so it was weakened when either went its own way.

Challenges to the settlement, 1817–1832

  • The Burschenschaften, student fraternities founded at Jena in 1815, spread liberal and nationalist ideas and adopted the black, red and gold colours later used by German nationalists.
  • The Wartburg Festival (October 1817) gathered students to mark the 300th anniversary of Luther's Reformation and the 1813 victory at Leipzig, called for German unity and burned conservative books.
  • The revolutions of 1820–21 in Spain, Naples and Piedmont forced kings to grant constitutions, but Austrian troops restored absolutism in Italy and French troops did so in Spain in 1823.
  • The Greek War of Independence (from 1821) broke the principle of non-intervention for the powers: philhellenism, boosted by Byron's death at Missolonghi in April 1824, led Britain, France and Russia to destroy the Turkish-Egyptian fleet at Navarino (1827).
  • The London Protocol (February 1830) recognised an independent Greece, and the Convention of London (1832) made it a monarchy under Otto of Bavaria; Metternich had opposed this support for rebels against a legitimate ruler.
  • The July Revolution in France (1830) overthrew Charles X, the last Bourbon king, after his July Ordinances, and brought Louis-Philippe of Orléans to the throne.
  • The Belgian revolt of August 1830 separated Belgium from the Netherlands; the London Conference recognised its independence, Leopold of Saxe-Coburg became king in 1831, and the Treaty of London (1839) guaranteed its neutrality.
  • The November Uprising (1830–31) in Russian Poland was crushed, and Nicholas I abolished the Polish constitution in 1832.
  • Risings in Modena, Parma and the Papal Legations in 1831 were put down by Austrian troops.
  • The Hambach Festival (May 1832) brought about 20,000–30,000 people to the Palatinate to demand German unity, a free press and popular sovereignty under black, red and gold flags.

Economic nationalism: the Zollverein

  • Prussia's Customs Law of 1818 abolished internal tariffs inside its scattered territories and set a low external tariff.
  • Neighbouring states joined Prussia's system, and the Zollverein (German Customs Union) took effect on 1 January 1834 with 18 states, under treaties agreed in 1833.
  • Members traded freely with each other behind a common external tariff and shared the customs revenue.
  • Austria stayed outside because its protected industries and separate tariff system could not fit a low-tariff union, and Metternich warned that the union was building Prussian influence.
  • Leadership of the Zollverein gave Prussia an economic and political lead in Germany and accustomed the smaller states to looking to Berlin rather than Vienna.

The fall of Metternich, March 1848

  • News of the February 1848 revolution in Paris sparked demonstrations in German and Austrian cities.
  • On 13 March 1848 students and workers demonstrated in Vienna, troops fired on the crowd, and the court made Metternich resign that evening; he fled and went into exile in England.
  • His fall removed the main defender of the 1815 settlement and coincided with revolutions across the Habsburg lands and the German states.
  • The Habsburg Empire then faced a crisis of nationalities, with Hungarian, Czech and Italian demands for self-rule (see the notes on the 1848 Revolution in Germany).

Key personality: Clemens Metternich (1773–1859)

Prince Clemens von Metternich, a Rhineland aristocrat, guided Habsburg foreign policy from 1809 to 1848 and gave his name to the conservative order of 1815–1848.

  • Born in Koblenz in 1773, he saw his family's Rhineland estates overrun by the French revolutionary armies, which shaped his lifelong hatred of revolution.
  • He became Austrian foreign minister in 1809 and state chancellor in 1821, and hosted and dominated the Congress of Vienna.
  • He believed that liberalism and nationalism would destroy the multinational Habsburg Empire, so he defended monarchy, legitimacy and the balance of power.
  • He was a direct participant: he drafted the Carlsbad Decrees and the 1832 measures, led the congresses of Troppau and Laibach and sent Austrian troops into Italy.
  • His policy was shaped by events he could not control: Greek independence, the 1830 revolutions and the Zollverein all showed the limits of his power.
  • He famously dismissed Italy as a geographical expression (1847), meaning it was not a nation.
  • He returned to Vienna in 1851 and died in 1859; his memoirs, edited by his son and published from 1880, presented him as the defender of order against revolution.
  • Contemporaries: conservative rulers praised him as the guardian of peace, while liberals and nationalists saw him as the symbol of reaction, censorship and police rule.
  • Historians: Heinrich von Srbik's biography (1925) and Henry Kissinger's A World Restored (1957) praised him as a statesman who gave Europe a stable peace; critics argue that he only repressed the causes of unrest, which returned with greater force in 1848.

Assessment: how successful was Metternich's system?

  • Success: the great powers fought no general war between 1815 and the Crimean War (1853–56).
  • Limit: the peace owed much to exhaustion after 1815 and to Britain's naval power, not only to Metternich.
  • Success: revolutions in Italy (1821, 1831) and liberal movements in Germany (1819, 1832) were crushed, and the Habsburg Empire survived intact until 1848.
  • Limit: the Congress System broke up in the 1820s, and Greek independence and Belgian independence overturned parts of the 1815 settlement.
  • Success: in the German Confederation, Austria kept its presidency and the smaller states usually followed its lead before 1848.
  • Limit: Prussia's Zollverein built a German economic unit without Austria, laying a foundation for Prussian leadership.
  • Overall: the system delayed liberal and national change for over thirty years but did not remove its causes, and it collapsed within days of the Vienna rising of March 1848.

Key terms

Legitimacy
The principle at the Congress of Vienna that rightful dynasties deposed by Napoleon should be restored to their thrones.
Balance of power
The arrangement of territories and alliances so that no single state could dominate Europe, used in 1815 to contain France.
Congress System
The post-1815 practice of the great powers meeting at congresses to manage crises, which Metternich used to justify intervention against revolutions.
Holy Alliance
The 1815 declaration by Russia, Austria and Prussia that rulers would govern by Christian principles, which came to stand for the alliance of the eastern autocracies against revolution.
Autocracy
Government by a single ruler with unlimited power and no constitution or elected body to limit it, as in Metternich's Austria and tsarist Russia.
Liberalism
The early nineteenth-century belief in constitutional government, an elected parliament, civil liberties such as a free press, and limits on the power of rulers.
Nationalism
The belief that people sharing a language, culture and history form a nation that should have its own state, which threatened the multinational empires of 1815.
German Confederation
The loose union of 39 German states created in 1815, with a Diet of government delegates at Frankfurt under Austrian presidency.
Carlsbad Decrees
The 1819 measures adopted by the German Diet that banned the Burschenschaften, supervised the universities and imposed press censorship.
Burschenschaft
A liberal and nationalist German student fraternity, first founded at Jena in 1815, banned under the Carlsbad Decrees.
Zollverein
The Prussian-led German customs union, in force from 1 January 1834, which removed tariffs between its members and excluded Austria.
