Owen's background and the Chorlton mills

Robert Owen was a Welsh-born manager who learned the cotton trade in Manchester before taking charge of a much larger mill complex in Scotland.

  • Robert Owen was born in Newtown, Montgomeryshire, in 1771, and he left school at about nine or ten to work in a draper's shop.
  • He moved to Manchester as a young man and was managing a cotton-spinning mill by the age of about nineteen, before becoming a partner in the Chorlton Twist Company in the 1790s.
  • Owen's Manchester experience taught him how mill workers and their families lived, and it shaped his view that environment forms character.
  • In 1799 he married Caroline Dale, daughter of David Dale, the Glasgow merchant who owned the New Lanark mills.
  • In the same year the Chorlton partners bought New Lanark from Dale, and Owen took over as its manager in January 1800, when he was in his late twenties.
  • His outlook was shaped by the environmentalist ideas of Enlightenment thinkers, which held that character is formed by surroundings.

New Lanark: the mills and the village

  • New Lanark stands on the River Clyde in Lanarkshire, Scotland, and was founded in 1785 by David Dale with the cotton pioneer Richard Arkwright.
  • The mills used water power from the Falls of Clyde, which was the reason Dale chose the site, and the cotton-spinning works were among the largest in Britain at the time.
  • The village around the mills housed about 2,000 people when Owen took over, including some 500 pauper children brought from workhouses in Edinburgh and Glasgow.
  • The pauper children lived in dormitories near the mills and were supervised by the mill managers; Dale was thought a comparatively humane employer, but the children still worked long days.
  • Owen's partnership was reorganised in 1813, when Owen and new backers, including the philosopher Jeremy Bentham and the Quaker William Allen, bought out the earlier partners.
  • The 1813 partners agreed to take a fixed 5 per cent return on their capital, leaving the remaining profit to be spent on the community.

Conditions Owen found

  • The mill day was about thirteen hours, and children from the age of five or six worked in the mills before attending evening classes, when they were tired.
  • By Owen's account, the village houses were dirty and overcrowded, the streets were filthy and sanitation was poor.
  • Owen described the workforce as marked by drunkenness, theft and disorder, and the people distrusted a manager from outside.
  • Owen said that discipline relied on fines and physical punishment, and he set out to replace it.
  • He saw these conditions as the result of environment rather than of individual failings, and he set out to change the environment to change character.
  • Owen later argued that the conditions at New Lanark reflected the wider failings of the factory system rather than the particular fault of its managers.

Owen's reforms in the mill

  • Owen stopped taking pauper children as apprentices and raised the minimum age of employment, so that children under ten no longer worked in the mills.
  • By 1816 he had reduced the working day to ten and three-quarter hours, and he argued that this did not reduce output.
  • In the American embargo of 1806 he kept paying wages to idle workers for about four months, which won their loyalty.
  • Owen opposed physical punishment, and he replaced it with a system of reward and example.
  • The Silent Monitor was a block of wood with a different colour on each side (black, blue, yellow and white, from bad conduct to excellent), hung beside each worker and turned each day to show their conduct, so that behaviour was recorded without shouting or punishment.
  • He used profits to pay for schools and welfare, which he treated as a sound investment in the workforce.

Community services at New Lanark

  • The Institute for the Formation of Character, opened in 1816, was a meeting hall and school for adults, which offered dancing, music, lectures and classes.
  • The infant school, opened in 1816, took children from the age of about one or two to six, and it used songs, games and practical activities rather than rote learning.
  • The village store sold goods at close to cost price and of good quality, and the sale of strong drink was controlled.
  • Owen rebuilt and improved the village housing, cleaned the streets and encouraged cleanliness and orderly living.
  • A school for older children was set up, which gave them some education in reading and writing before they entered work.
  • The Institute and school had no formal religious instruction, a feature that drew criticism from clergy and from his Quaker partners, who also objected to the dancing and music.
  • The infant school was one of the earliest of its kind in Britain and attracted visitors from abroad.

The campaign for factory legislation

  • Owen wrote A New View of Society (essays published 1813–1816), in which he argued that human character is formed by circumstances and that society should improve those circumstances.
  • The 1802 Health and Morals of Apprentices Act, promoted by the cotton manufacturer Sir Robert Peel (the elder), had already limited hours for pauper apprentices, so the state had begun to intervene before Owen's campaign.
  • Owen gave evidence to a parliamentary committee on factory conditions in 1816 and campaigned for a law to limit the employment of children.
  • The Cotton Mills and Factories Act of 1819 barred children under nine from working in cotton mills and limited the working hours of children aged nine to sixteen to twelve a day.
  • The Act was weaker than Owen wanted, because it had no factory inspectors and so depended on local magistrates, and it was poorly enforced.
  • In 1817 Owen presented his plan for self-supporting co-operative villages to the Committee for the Relief of the Manufacturing Poor in London, and in 1820 he set out a similar plan in a Report to the County of Lanark.
  • In August 1817 he damaged his reputation by denouncing all organised religions at a public meeting in London.

The outcome at New Lanark and Owen's departure

  • Owen claimed that New Lanark stayed profitable under his management, which he used to show that his reforms did not bankrupt the business.
  • His partners, especially the Quaker William Allen, objected to the cost of his community schemes and to his religious views, and these disputes caused conflict within the partnership.
  • Owen left for the United States in 1824 to found New Harmony, and he finally ended his connection with New Lanark in about 1829.
  • After Owen left, the mills carried on under his former partners, continuing to produce cotton until 1968, and New Lanark remained a reference point for later social reformers.

New Harmony, the co-operative movement and the model's fate

  • In 1825 Owen bought the town of Harmony in Indiana from a religious community and founded New Harmony, a co-operative experiment that failed within a few years.
  • Disputes among the members of New Harmony over its organisation led to its collapse, and Owen left the settlement in 1827.
  • Owen set up the Equitable Labour Exchange in London in 1832, which exchanged goods using labour notes, and it collapsed within a few years.
  • Owen's ideas influenced the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union of 1834, which he led, and his periodical of 1834 was titled The New Moral World.
  • Owen's writings inspired the co-operative movement, and the Rochdale Pioneers in Lancashire set up a co-operative store in 1844.
  • New Lanark was not widely copied because the reforms required a level of control that few owners were willing to accept, and the profits of most mills depended on low wages and long hours.
  • Later employers built model villages, such as Saltaire in Yorkshire in 1853, but these were usually paternalist rather than co-operative.

Reputation then and since

  • Contemporaries were divided: some manufacturers and reformers praised New Lanark as a model, while many employers and conservative critics attacked Owen's religious views and his socialism.
  • Visitors from Britain and abroad, thousands of them, including statesmen and royalty, toured New Lanark in the 1810s and 1820s to see the reforms for themselves.
  • Owen's critics said that his community schemes were impractical and that the reforms relied on his personal control of the mill.
  • Historians debate whether New Lanark was a genuine model of welfare or a paternalistic system that depended on Owen's authority.
  • Historians now treat New Lanark as a significant experiment in industrial welfare, and its reforms influenced the development of education and public health policies.
  • Owen died at Newtown in 1858, and New Lanark was made a World Heritage Site by UNESCO in 2001.

Assessment: New Lanark as a model

  • Success: New Lanark showed that shorter hours, schooling and decent housing could be combined with profit.
  • Limit: its success depended on Owen's personal control, which made it difficult to copy in other mills or communities.
  • Success: it helped to shape the Factory Act of 1819 and the infant school movement and inspired the co-operative movement.
  • Limit: the 1819 Act was weak, Owen quarrelled with his partners and New Harmony failed.
  • Contemporary view: the reformer's friends saw a benevolent experiment; critics saw a paternalist whose religious views were dangerous.
  • Later view: historians see a pioneer of industrial welfare, though one whose methods of control were paternalistic.

Key terms

Co-operation
The principle of people working together and sharing benefits, which Owen promoted at New Lanark and in later co-operative schemes.
Silent Monitor
Owen's wooden block with a different colour on each side, turned to show a worker's conduct, used instead of punishment at New Lanark.
Institute for the Formation of Character
The meeting hall and school opened at New Lanark in 1816 to provide adult education, music, dancing and lectures.
Infant school
The school opened at New Lanark in 1816 for young children, which used games, songs and practical activities rather than rote learning.
Model village
A planned community built by an employer to improve the housing, health, education and conduct of its workforce.
Cotton Mills and Factories Act, 1819
The Act that barred children under nine from cotton mills and limited the working hours of older children, a result of Owen's campaigning.
A New View of Society
Owen's essays of 1813–1816 arguing that human character is formed by circumstances and that society should improve them.
New Harmony
The co-operative community founded by Owen in Indiana in 1825, which failed within a few years.
