Early trade unions

Workers in the early industrial period first organised in craft societies and then in wider unions, which governments tried to restrict and later tolerated.

  • The Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800 made it illegal for workers to combine to raise wages or change conditions in Britain.
  • The Combination Acts were repealed in 1824, and a further act in 1825 restored the right to organise but limited strikes and violence.
  • Six Dorset farm labourers, the Tolpuddle Martyrs, were sentenced to transportation to Australia in 1834 for swearing an oath to a union; a public campaign secured their pardon in 1836.
  • The Grand National Consolidated Trades Union, founded in 1834 under Robert Owen's leadership, aimed to unite all trades but collapsed within months.
  • The Amalgamated Society of Engineers, founded in 1851, was a New Model union with a high subscription, a central fund and benefit payments for sick and unemployed members.
  • Trade unions in the 1850s were mostly confined to skilled workers, and unskilled labourers remained largely unorganised until later decades; the Trades Union Congress first met in 1868 and the Trade Union Act of 1871 gave unions legal recognition.

Socialism and utopian reformers

  • Socialism in the early nineteenth century meant common ownership of the means of production, and the term was used by early reformers before Marx gave it a scientific basis.
  • Utopian socialists such as Henri de Saint-Simon and Charles Fourier planned model communities based on co-operation, and they rejected competitive capitalism.
  • Robert Owen's ideas and his experiments at New Lanark and New Harmony in Indiana showed that cooperative communities could be run successfully in practice, a case treated in its own notes.
  • In France, Louis Blanc's Organisation of Labour (1839) urged state-backed workshops, and in London the Communist League was formed in 1847 from a society of German exiles, with Marx and Engels drafting its programme.

Key personality: Karl Marx

Marx was the most influential theorist of socialism. His ideas were written and developed in the 1840s and 1860s, and his followers only became a mass movement after his death.

  • Karl Marx was born in Trier in 1818, studied philosophy and became editor of the Rheinische Zeitung in Cologne in 1842.
  • He was expelled from Paris in 1845 and from Prussia in 1849, and he settled in London in 1849, where he lived until his death in 1883.
  • With Friedrich Engels he wrote the Communist Manifesto, published in London in February 1848, which declared that all history was a history of class struggles and ended with a call for working men of all countries to unite.
  • He was a participant in the revolutions of 1848 as editor of the Neue Rheinische Zeitung in Cologne, which was closed in 1849 when he was expelled from Prussia.
  • His theory of historical materialism held that class struggle drives history, and that capitalism would be overthrown by the working class.
  • The first volume of Das Kapital appeared in 1867, analysing how capitalists profit from surplus value produced by labour.
  • He helped to found the International Workingmen's Association (the First International) in London in 1864, wrote its inaugural address and was the dominant figure on its General Council.
  • Contemporaries in the 1850s and 1860s saw him as a dangerous exile, and most of the European governments kept him under surveillance.
  • Historians now judge Marx as a major social theorist whose predictions of revolution in advanced capitalist countries were not fulfilled, although his analysis of capitalism influenced later economics and politics.
  • Marx's collaborator Engels financed him and edited the later volumes of Das Kapital after his death.

Key personality: Mikhail Bakunin

Bakunin was a Russian revolutionary and the most influential anarchist of the period. His rivalry with Marx split the First International.

  • Mikhail Bakunin was born in 1814 into a noble family in Russia, and he left the army and became a revolutionary in the 1840s.
  • He was a participant at the Slav Congress in Prague in 1848, and he joined the Dresden rising of May 1849, after which he was sentenced to death, a sentence later commuted to life imprisonment.
  • He was handed over to Austria and then Russia in 1851, held in the Peter and Paul Fortress, exiled to Siberia in 1857 and escaped in 1861 by way of Japan and the United States to London.
  • Bakunin argued for the abolition of the state and of all authority, a position that became known as anarchism, and he rejected Marx's belief in the need for a workers' state.
  • His rivalry with Marx within the First International led to the expulsion of Bakunin at the Hague Congress in 1872, after which the International's General Council moved to New York; Bakunin died in 1876.
  • Contemporary governments saw him as a dangerous conspirator, while some radicals admired him as a champion of the oppressed.
  • Historians now treat Bakunin as a major figure in the anarchist tradition, although his influence on the mass labour movement was less than Marx's.

The Chartist movement

Chartism was the first mass working-class political movement in Britain, demanding parliamentary reform through peaceful methods, with the People's Charter of 1838 as its programme.

  • The People's Charter of 1838 demanded universal male suffrage, secret ballots, annual parliaments, equal electoral districts, the abolition of the property qualification for MPs and payment of MPs.
  • The London Working Men's Association, formed in 1836 by the cabinet-maker William Lovett, drafted the Charter with the radical Francis Place and others.
  • Chartist petitions to Parliament in 1839, 1842 and 1848 were rejected each time; the 1842 petition had over three million signatures, while the 1848 petition claimed nearly six million but was found to have far fewer, some of them forged.
  • The Newport Rising of November 1839, led by John Frost, was an armed uprising in Wales that was suppressed by troops, and Frost was sentenced to death but transported to Australia instead.
  • The Plug Plot strikes of August 1842 spread through the industrial north and Midlands, and they were suppressed after mass arrests.
  • The National Charter Association, formed in 1840, organised local meetings and campaigns, and its newspaper, the Northern Star, reached a wide readership.
  • The National Land Company, founded in 1845, bought estates and let subscribers draw lots for smallholdings, which would give them a vote in the counties, but it ended in failure and was wound up in 1851.
  • Chartism grew from the depression of 1837, hatred of the New Poor Law of 1834 and disappointment that the 1832 Act had given workers no vote.
  • Chartists divided between moral-force leaders such as Lovett and physical-force leaders; the movement failed because of its divisions, government repression, the refusal of Parliament to listen and the better economic conditions after 1848.

Key personality: Feargus O'Connor

O'Connor was the most prominent Chartist leader in the 1840s and the editor of the movement's newspaper. His leadership was enthusiastic but divided the movement.

  • Feargus O'Connor was born in Co. Cork in 1796, trained as a barrister and entered politics as a supporter of Daniel O'Connell.
  • He was elected MP for Co. Cork in 1832, lost the seat in 1835, and became the leading figure in Chartism in England and in the National Charter Association; he was imprisoned for seditious libel in 1840–41 and was elected MP for Nottingham in 1847.
  • He founded the Northern Star in Leeds in 1837, which became the most widely read radical newspaper in Britain.
  • He was a participant in the Chartist campaigns of 1839, 1842 and 1848, and he presented the 1848 petition to Parliament.
  • He led the National Land Company, which bought land for members and collapsed after financial mismanagement.
  • The Chartist demonstration on Kennington Common on 10 April 1848 was intended to present a petition, but it dispersed peacefully after the authorities banned a procession to Westminster.
  • Contemporaries split over him: many working-class radicals admired him, while government officials and middle-class reformers regarded him as reckless.
  • Older accounts blame his vanity and his quarrels with Lovett and the moral-force Chartists; Dorothy Thompson and other historians stress his gifts as an organiser and speaker for the rank and file; his mental breakdown in 1852 ended his influence and he died in 1855.

Mass politics and the franchise

  • The Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to male householders in the boroughs who paid rent of £10 a year and removed seats from rotten boroughs for the new industrial towns, but it still left only about one adult man in five with a vote.
  • The Second Reform Act of 1867 extended the vote to many urban working men, the secret ballot came in 1872, and universal male suffrage followed only in 1918.
  • The Second Republic in France gave universal male suffrage in 1848, and the North German Confederation adopted it for its Reichstag in 1867.

Key personality: Robert Peel

  • Sir Robert Peel, Conservative leader and Prime Minister in 1834–35 and again from 1841 to 1846, was a central figure in the reform of British government.
  • As Home Secretary he founded the Metropolitan Police in London in 1829, a professional force that became a model for others.
  • He conceded Catholic emancipation in 1829, with the Duke of Wellington, to avoid civil conflict in Ireland, and it angered many Tories.
  • The Tamworth Manifesto of December 1834 set out a moderate programme of reform within the Conservative tradition, which became the basis of the modern Conservative Party.
  • He reintroduced income tax in 1842 to pay for tariff reductions, and his budgets of 1842 and 1845 cut tariffs on many imports.
  • The Bank Charter Act of 1844 regulated the issue of banknotes by the Bank of England, and the Factory Act of 1844 limited hours for women and children.
  • Peel repealed the Corn Laws in 1846, partly in response to the Irish potato famine, which split the Conservative Party; he resigned in June 1846 and died after a riding accident in 1850.
  • The Corn Laws repeal was supported by free-trade reformers such as Richard Cobden and John Bright, who had campaigned through the Anti-Corn Law League.
  • The Conservative Party's critics, notably Disraeli, attacked him as a traitor in 1846; historians such as Norman Gash judge him one of the greatest British statesmen of the century.

Owen and Peel as reformers compared

  • Owen worked outside government to create model communities, while Peel reformed the state from within through legislation and institutions.
  • Owen's aims were co-operation and communal ownership, whereas Peel wanted to preserve private property and the existing social order, while making it more efficient.
  • Owen's reforms were confined to one community, while Peel's measures such as the Metropolitan Police and the Bank Charter Act affected the entire country.
  • Owen's ideas were rejected by most of the political establishment, while Peel's policies were accepted as sound by much of the middle class.

Assessment: achievements and limits of responses to industrial society

  • Achievement: trade unions lost their illegal status after 1824 and the New Model unions gave skilled workers lasting organisation.
  • Limit: the Combination Acts, the Tolpuddle sentences and the failure of the GNCTU showed how hard it was to organise workers in the early period.
  • Achievement: Chartism created a mass movement that kept parliamentary reform on the agenda, and several Chartist demands were later achieved.
  • Limit: Chartism's petitions were rejected, its leaders were divided, and the movement lost momentum after 1848.
  • Achievement: Peel's reforms modernised the police, the banking system and tariff policy, and they helped the British economy grow.
  • Overall: the responses to industrialisation included unions, socialist theory and Chartist agitation, yet the main political gains came from state reform led by Peel rather than from working-class movements, which won their goals only much later.

Key terms

Socialism
The belief that the means of production should be owned in common, rather than by private individuals, so that wealth is shared in society.
Chartism
The British working-class movement of 1838–1858 that demanded parliamentary reform through the People's Charter.
People's Charter
The 1838 document setting out the six demands of the Chartists: universal male suffrage, secret ballots, annual parliaments, equal districts, no property qualification for MPs and payment of MPs.
Democracy
Government by the people through elected representatives, which the Chartists and the Frankfurt and French reformers of the 1840s sought to extend to all men.
Laissez-faire
The doctrine that markets should be free from government control, which Peel applied in his free-trade budgets and the repeal of the Corn Laws, although the Factory Acts showed its limits.
Trade union
An organisation of workers that negotiates wages and conditions with employers, which was illegal under the Combination Acts until 1824.
Anarchism
The belief in abolishing the state and all forms of coercive authority, which Bakunin championed against Marx's workers' state.
Historical materialism
Marx's theory that economic relations and class struggle drive historical change, leading to the overthrow of capitalism by the working class.
