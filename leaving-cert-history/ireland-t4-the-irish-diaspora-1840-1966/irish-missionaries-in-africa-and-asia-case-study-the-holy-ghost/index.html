Why Ireland sent missionaries

Ireland sent Catholic missionaries to Africa and Asia out of proportion to its population, and this effort was given a national meaning.

  • The Catholic Church in Ireland was strengthened after the Famine by the reforms of Archbishop Paul Cullen, a change that Emmet Larkin called the devotional revolution, and this produced a large supply of priests, brothers and nuns.
  • Irish seminaries and novitiates, such as St Patrick's College, Maynooth, trained far more clergy than the Irish church needed, and the surplus went to the Irish diaspora and then to the missions.
  • The phrase Ireland's spiritual empire described the reach of Irish missionaries across Africa and Asia, and it gave missionary work a national identity.
  • Irish writers linked the missions to the monks of the early church, such as Columbanus, and to Ireland's reputation as an island of saints and scholars.
  • Irish nationalism and Catholicism were closely linked, so a country that had no empire of its own could present itself as a leader in spreading the faith without the taint of colonialism.
  • Irish parishes raised money through collections, missionary magazines and mission exhibitions, which linked congregations at home with the work abroad.
  • Edel Quinn, a Legion of Mary envoy to East Africa from 1936, who died in Nairobi in 1944, became a lay symbol of Irish missionary dedication.
  • Mission work gave Irish women a leadership role in nursing, teaching and medicine that was hard to find at home.

Irish missionary societies in Africa and Asia

  • The Maynooth Mission to China was founded in 1916, and it became the Missionary Society of St Columban, with its headquarters at Dalgan Park, Navan, from 1918.
  • The Columbans worked in China, then in Korea, Japan, the Philippines and Latin America.
  • The Missionary Society of St Patrick, founded at Kiltegan, Co. Wicklow, in 1932 by Father Patrick Whitney, sent priests to Nigeria and later to Kenya and other African countries.
  • The Society of African Missions (SMA), founded in France in 1856, had an Irish province in Cork, and its Irish members worked in West Africa, including Nigeria and Ghana.
  • The Holy Ghost Fathers, called Spiritans, were a French-founded congregation with a large Irish province that took on the missions of Nigeria, Kenya and Sierra Leone.
  • The Missionary Sisters of the Holy Rosary, founded by Bishop Joseph Shanahan in 1924 at Killeshandra, Co. Cavan, taught and nursed in Nigeria and other parts of Africa.
  • Other Irish groups included the Loreto sisters in India and the Presentation Sisters, and Irish Jesuits in Zambia and India.

Key personality: Bishop Edward Galvin

Edward Galvin was a Cork priest who founded the Maynooth Mission to China and became a bishop in Hanyang, central China.

  • Edward Galvin was born in Co. Cork in 1882 and was ordained for the diocese of Cork in 1909.
  • He went to China in 1912 as a missionary priest, and in 1916 he returned to Ireland, where with Father John Blowick he founded the Maynooth Mission to China, approved by the Irish bishops.
  • The first Irish missionaries went to China in 1920, and Galvin led the mission in the Hanyang area of Hubei province in central China.
  • He became prefect apostolic in 1924 and vicar apostolic of Hanyang in 1927, when he became a bishop; Hanyang became a diocese in 1946.
  • The mission ran churches, schools, orphanages and clinics, and it trained Chinese catechists and priests, though few Chinese priests were ordained before the 1940s.
  • He led the mission through the civil conflicts of the 1920s and 1930s and the Japanese invasion, in which Hanyang was bombed.
  • After the Communist takeover of 1949 the missionaries faced restrictions, and Galvin was placed under house arrest, tried and expelled in 1952; he died at Dalgan Park in 1956.
  • Contemporaries in Ireland saw Galvin and the China mission as heroes of the Catholic church, and their letters and talks raised vocations and money.
  • Chinese Communist authorities criticised the missionaries as agents of foreign influence, and this view shaped their expulsion.
  • Historians place the Columbans in the wider history of Western missions in China, and they debate how far missionary work helped or hindered local communities.

Key personality: Mother Mary Martin

Mary Martin founded the Medical Missionaries of Mary, whose sisters combined nursing and medicine with mission work in Africa.

  • Mary Martin was born in Dublin in 1892 and trained as a nurse and midwife; she went to Nigeria in the 1930s to work in mission medicine.
  • Until 1936 the Catholic Church forbade religious sisters to practise obstetrics and surgery, and a Vatican instruction of that year lifted the restriction, making her order possible.
  • She went to Nigeria and founded the Medical Missionaries of Mary, which is dated to 1937 in Nigeria; the order's mother house was at Drogheda, Co. Louth.
  • The order trained doctors, nurses and midwives, and it set up hospitals and clinics in areas where few medical services existed.
  • Its sisters worked in Nigeria, and the order's mission hospitals and nursing schools became central to its work there; it later worked in other African countries.
  • Martin directed the order's expansion and the training of its members, and she became known as Mother Mary of the Incarnation.
  • She was the first woman to receive the Freedom of the Borough of Drogheda, in 1966, and she died in 1975.
  • Contemporary Irish Catholic opinion praised the order and its work, and the sisters were presented as models of the mission spirit.
  • Historians present the Medical Missionaries of Mary as a pioneer of women-led medical missions, and they ask how far medical work served colonial aims.

Case study: the Holy Ghost mission to Nigeria, background

The Holy Ghost Fathers, called Spiritans, were a French-founded congregation whose Irish members led much of the mission in south-eastern Nigeria.

  • The Holy Ghost Fathers (Spiritans) began work at Onitsha on the lower Niger in 1885, before Britain's colonial control had been established in the interior.
  • The mission area was south-eastern Nigeria, where the Igbo people were the largest group, in the British colony and protectorate of Southern Nigeria and later the Eastern Region.
  • Joseph Shanahan, an Irish Spiritan born in Co. Tipperary in 1871, arrived in Nigeria in 1902 and made the school the main instrument of conversion, building village schools staffed by catechists.
  • He became prefect apostolic in 1920 and a bishop, appealed to Irish seminaries for recruits, and founded the Holy Rosary Sisters in 1924.
  • He left Nigeria in 1931 because of ill health, and he died in 1943; his remains were reburied in Onitsha Cathedral in 1956.
  • Methodist and Anglican missionaries competed with the Catholics for converts, and rivalry over schools was a feature of the region.
  • By 1945 the mission had a network of parishes, schools and a few hospitals in the region, which the postwar expansion built on.

Case study: expansion, 1945 to 1966

Between 1945 and 1966 the mission grew into a large network of schools, hospitals and parishes, staffed by Irish missionaries and increasingly by Nigerians.

  • After 1945 the mission opened new parishes, schools, hospitals and training colleges across south-eastern Nigeria.
  • The Archdiocese of Onitsha was created in 1950 when the Catholic hierarchy was established in Nigeria, with the Irish Spiritan Charles Heerey as its archbishop, and Joseph Whelan became the first bishop of Owerri.
  • The number of Irish priests, brothers and sisters working in the mission rose in the 1950s, with recruits from Irish seminaries and novitiates.
  • Mission schools became the main route to education in the region, and the Catholic mission ran a large share of primary and secondary schools.
  • Teacher training colleges prepared local teachers, which created a growing educated class in the region.
  • Hospitals and dispensaries run by the Holy Rosary Sisters and Medical Missionaries of Mary provided medical care in rural areas where government services were few.
  • The Catholic population of the region grew strongly, and local vocations to the priesthood and religious life increased; Michael Tansi, an Igbo, was ordained in 1937 and beatified in 1998.
  • Nigerians were trained as priests, teachers and nurses, and in 1965 Francis Arinze, aged 32, became auxiliary bishop of Onitsha.

Case study: the mission and the colonial state

The mission depended on the colonial government for funding and cooperated with it, though the two sometimes disagreed over schooling and policy.

  • The colonial government paid grants towards mission schools under a grant-in-aid system, in return for inspection and standards.
  • The grants made education cheaper for the mission and for families, but they also tied the schools to government requirements.
  • Missions and colonial officials sometimes disagreed over curriculum, language and the role of religious teaching in schools.
  • In the 1950s the regional government of Eastern Nigeria introduced free primary education, which increased demand for schools and strained mission resources.
  • Nigeria became independent on 1 October 1960, and the mission adapted to a new state in which Nigerians held most public offices.
  • After independence the mission's leadership passed increasingly to Nigerian priests and bishops, a process known as localisation.
  • Many of the educated Nigerians who served in the new state had passed through mission schools, which gave the mission a lasting role in the country's élite.

Case study: achievements, criticisms and the Biafran war

The mission's achievements in education and health can be set against criticisms of its cultural influence and against the damage of the Biafran war.

  • Achievements: the mission built schools, hospitals and churches, trained many local teachers and nurses, and helped create a Catholic church with Nigerian leadership.
  • Criticisms: mission education promoted Christian and Western values, and critics argued that it weakened local culture and languages.
  • Critics also said the mission was paternalistic, because Irish missionaries held the leadership long after local people were qualified for it.
  • Rivalry between Catholic and Protestant missions over schools and converts also divided communities.
  • On 30 May 1967 the eastern region declared itself the Republic of Biafra, and a civil war followed until January 1970.
  • During the war many mission stations, schools and hospitals were damaged, closed or abandoned, and missionaries were displaced; Irish Holy Ghost Father Anthony Byrne helped organise the relief airlift to Biafra.
  • The famine in Biafra moved many Irish people, and the Irish relief agency Concern was founded in 1968 in response to it.
  • After the war many foreign missionaries left the east, including Bishop Whelan of Owerri, and the state took over mission schools.

Significance: Ireland's spiritual empire

  • Irish Catholics saw the missions as a sign that Ireland was a nation with a mission of its own, not only a country that exported people.
  • The idea of an Irish spiritual empire combined Irish nationalism with the universal claims of the Catholic Church.
  • The missions raised money and vocations at home, and they linked Irish parishes to the lives of people overseas.
  • Later historians have questioned the empire idea, and they see it as a self-image that was closely tied to Irish Catholic identity and to the colonial world.
  • The decline in Irish vocations after the 1960s and the localisation of the churches in Africa and Asia ended the era of large-scale Irish mission work.

Assessment: missionaries' impact and success

  • Achievement: the Holy Ghost mission built schools, hospitals and parishes that outlasted colonial rule and produced educated Nigerian leaders.
  • Limit: the mission was tied to the colonial state through grants, and it was slow to hand leadership to local clergy.
  • Contemporary view: Irish Catholics praised the missionaries as heroes of the faith, while some African critics questioned their paternalism and cultural impact.
  • Later view: historians stress both the practical contribution of missions to health and education and the cultural and political costs of their work.
  • Galvin and Martin compared: both founded mission institutions in the 1910s and 1930s, but Galvin's China mission ended in expulsion in 1952, while Martin's medical order grew in Africa.

Key terms

Ireland's spiritual empire
The idea that Irish Catholic missionaries extended Irish influence across Africa and Asia, giving missionary work a national meaning.
Holy Ghost Fathers (Spiritans)
A French-founded Catholic congregation whose Irish members led much of the mission in south-eastern Nigeria.
Maynooth Mission to China
The Irish missionary society founded in 1916 to send priests to China, which became the Columbans and whose founders included Edward Galvin.
Medical Missionaries of Mary
A religious order founded by Mary Martin in 1937, with its mother house at Drogheda, whose sisters combined medicine, nursing and mission work.
Grant-in-aid
The colonial government's payment towards the cost of mission schools, in return for inspection and standards.
Localisation
The process by which mission leadership passed from foreign missionaries to local clergy and staff.
Vicar apostolic
A bishop who governs a mission territory that has not yet been made a full diocese.
Devotional revolution
The strengthening of Irish Catholic practice and church organisation after the Famine, which produced a large supply of clergy and religious.
Biafra
The breakaway republic declared in south-eastern Nigeria in 1967, whose war with the federal government lasted until 1970.
