Stereotypes of the Irish in Hollywood

Hollywood shaped the world's picture of Ireland, often through sentimental and comic stereotypes built on the older stage Irishman.

  • The stage Irishman was a 19th-century theatrical stereotype of the Irish as drunken, quick-tempered, sentimental and comic, and Hollywood inherited it.
  • Harrigan and Hart's Mulligan Guard comedies in New York in the 1870s and 1880s and the Irish-American stage of Chauncey Olcott made the stage Irishman familiar to American audiences.
  • John Ford, an American director of Irish descent born in Maine in 1894, whose parents came from Spiddal, Co. Galway, and the Aran Islands, made many films about Irish themes and Irish-American life.
  • Ford's The Informer, released in 1935, won him the Academy Award for Best Director, and it dealt with the Irish revolutionary period in Dublin.
  • Hollywood films often showed the Irish as rural, Catholic and emotional, and urban or industrial Ireland was rarely shown.
  • Going My Way, released in 1944, presented an Irish-American priest in New York, and it became one of the popular images of Irish Catholicism in America.
  • Irish-American priests, policemen and fighting Irishmen became stock characters, played by actors such as Pat O'Brien and Barry Fitzgerald.
  • Disney's Darby O'Gill and the Little People (1959) presented Ireland as a land of leprechauns and banshees.

The Quiet Man and tourism

The Quiet Man was filmed in the west of Ireland in 1951 and released in 1952, and it became the most influential film image of rural Ireland.

  • The Quiet Man was directed by John Ford and starred John Wayne and Maureen O'Hara, with Barry Fitzgerald and Victor McLaglen, and was based on a story by the Irish writer Maurice Walsh.
  • It was filmed around Cong in Co. Mayo and in Connemara in 1951, with Ashford Castle as one of its main locations.
  • The film won Academy Awards at the 1953 ceremony for Ford's direction and for the colour cinematography of Winton C. Hoch and Archie Stout.
  • The film's locations, including the Quiet Man Bridge in Cong, drew American visitors who wanted to see the places they had seen on screen.
  • Irish critics objected to the film's portrayal of rural people as comic and drunken, and they argued that it stereotyped Irish life.
  • The film helped to make Cong a tourist destination, and it shaped the expectations that many American visitors brought to Ireland.
  • The film's rural, comic, romantic Ireland suited the tourist board, even though real Ireland in 1952 was suffering mass emigration.

Key personality: Maureen O’Hara

Maureen O'Hara was a Dublin-born actress who became a Hollywood star and was closely identified with Irish heroines on screen.

  • Maureen O'Hara was born Maureen FitzSimons in Ranelagh, Dublin, in 1920, and she trained as an actress in Dublin at the Abbey Theatre school before moving to Britain and then America.
  • Her first Hollywood film was Jamaica Inn, released in 1939 and directed by Alfred Hitchcock, and she went on to a long Hollywood career.
  • She appeared in How Green Was My Valley in 1941 and in Miracle on 34th Street in 1947, which established her as a major star.
  • She worked repeatedly with John Ford, and their films included Rio Grande in 1950 and The Quiet Man in 1952, in which she played the fiery Mary Kate Danaher opposite John Wayne.
  • Contemporary Irish-American audiences embraced her as an Irish star, while Irish critics debated whether her roles presented a true picture of Irish women.
  • Historians note that her screen image of the Irish woman as spirited and independent was partly a Hollywood creation, and that her own sense of Irishness was shaped by her Dublin upbringing.
  • She became a US citizen, received an honorary Academy Award in 2014, and died in 2015; her career is often used as an example of how Irish stars helped to build a screen image of Ireland.

Irish music and song abroad

Music carried Irish images to audiences abroad, from the concert tenor of the early 20th century to the folk revival of the 1960s.

  • Sentimental songs such as When Irish Eyes Are Smiling (1912) and Danny Boy, written in 1910 by an English lyricist to an Irish air, were products of the Irish-American song industry and shaped an image of Ireland as a land of exile and longing.
  • John McCormack, born in Athlone in 1884, became an internationally famous tenor and recorded Irish ballads that were popular with Irish audiences abroad; he died in 1945.
  • McCormack's concerts in America and Britain made Irish songs part of mainstream concert repertoire, and he was a leading figure in Irish-American cultural life.
  • Céilí bands played traditional Irish dance music, and they performed for dancers in Irish halls and clubs in America and Britain.
  • Irish-American song and dance were kept alive in parish halls, clubs and dance halls, which gave emigrant communities a shared cultural life.
  • The Clancy Brothers, from Carrick-on-Suir, Co. Tipperary, and Tommy Makem, from Co. Armagh, became popular in America after appearing on the Ed Sullivan Show in 1961.
  • The folk revival of the 1960s brought Irish songs to a young audience in America and Britain, led by groups such as the Clancy Brothers and, from 1962, the Dubliners, and it changed how Irish music was seen abroad.

Tourism and heritage

The Irish state and private groups promoted tourism to attract visitors, including descendants of emigrants who wanted to see the places their families had left.

  • Bord Fáilte, the Irish Tourist Board, was set up under the Tourist Traffic Act of 1952 to promote tourism and to develop tourist facilities.
  • An Tóstal, a national festival of Irish culture and heritage, was launched in 1953 to draw visitors and to promote Irish events and traditions.
  • Shannon Airport opened in 1945 as a transatlantic stop for aircraft, which brought American travellers through Ireland.
  • Shannon Airport opened the world's first duty-free shop in 1947, which made it a point of contact between Irish life and travellers from America.
  • Aer Lingus began transatlantic flights between Dublin, Shannon and New York in 1958.
  • Heritage tourism grew in the 1950s and 1960s, with visits to ancestral places, church records and genealogy services for descendants of emigrants.
  • Roots tourism, the visiting of ancestral homes by people of Irish descent, linked the Irish state's promotion of tourism with the emigrant communities abroad.
  • Tourism was valued as an export industry that brought in dollars and sterling, and it fitted the economic planning of the late 1950s.

Other channels of the Irish image abroad

  • Carol Reed's film Odd Man Out (1947), set in Belfast and starring James Mason, showed the Irish revolutionary underworld to international audiences.
  • The Irish Hospitals' Sweepstake, run from 1930, sold tickets in large numbers in the USA and Britain, where it was illegal, and made the name of Ireland widely known.
  • Ireland's pavilion at the New York World's Fair of 1939 presented a national image to American visitors.
  • An Coimisiún le Rincí Gaelacha, set up in 1929 to standardise Irish dancing competitions, helped spread feiseanna and Irish dance classes among Irish communities abroad.
  • Gael-Linn, founded in 1953, promoted Irish-language recordings and cultural projects that reached emigrant audiences.

John F. Kennedy and Irish identity

John F. Kennedy's election and his visit in 1963 changed how Irish Americans and the Irish public saw one another.

  • Kennedy's great-grandfather Patrick Kennedy left Dunganstown, Co. Wexford, in 1849, and the family's Irish origins were well known in Ireland; his mother's family, the Fitzgeralds, also had Irish roots.
  • Kennedy was elected president in 1960, becoming the first Catholic president of the United States, and many Irish Americans saw his election as acceptance of Irish Catholics in public life, after the defeat of Al Smith in 1928.
  • Kennedy visited Ireland from 26 to 29 June 1963, going to Dublin, Wexford and Dunganstown, Cork, Galway and Limerick.
  • On 28 June 1963 he addressed the Oireachtas, the Irish parliament, in Dublin.
  • The visit drew large crowds and intense press coverage in Ireland, and it was widely celebrated as a moment of Irish pride.
  • Kennedy's image in Ireland combined ancestry with success in American politics, and it encouraged Irish people to see their emigrant relatives as part of a shared story.
  • His assassination on 22 November 1963 was mourned widely in Ireland, and his portrait hung in many Irish homes.
  • Historians argue that the visit fitted the self-confidence of Seán Lemass's government as the economy opened to the world, rather than causing it.
  • Kennedy's father's career is covered in the notes on Joe Kennedy, whose business and diplomatic career shaped the family's public profile.

Film production and censorship in Ireland

Ireland had little film production of its own before the late 1950s, and foreign productions and state censorship shaped what images reached Irish cinemas.

  • The Censorship of Films Act of 1923 gave the Irish state power to ban films, and the film censor removed or refused films on moral grounds.
  • Ardmore Studios opened at Bray, Co. Wicklow, in 1958 as the main film studio in Ireland.
  • Foreign productions, including Hollywood films, were shot in Ireland in the 1950s, and they brought Irish scenery and Irish actors to the screen.
  • Irish cinema-goers saw mostly Hollywood and British films, so the American image of Ireland reached Irish audiences as well as Americans.
  • Robert Flaherty's documentary Man of Aran (1934) presented a romantic picture of life on the Aran Islands that many islanders did not recognise.

Assessment: images of Ireland abroad

  • Achievement: Hollywood films, music and tourism made Ireland widely known abroad, and they attracted visitors and strengthened ties with emigrant descendants.
  • Limit: the images were often sentimental, rural and stereotyped, and they left out industrial and urban Ireland.
  • Contemporary view: the state's tourism bodies welcomed the images, while Irish critics objected that they stereotyped Irish people as comic or drunken.
  • Later view: historians see these images as a version of Ireland made for export, and they debate how far the Kennedy visit changed Irish self-image or only celebrated it.
  • Film versus music: films fixed a rural, comic image, while the folk revival gave Irish culture a younger and more confident voice abroad.

Key terms

Stage Irishman
A 19th-century theatrical stereotype of the Irish as drunken, quick-tempered, sentimental and comic, which later passed into film.
Céilí band
A group of musicians playing traditional Irish dance music, popular at dances in Irish halls and clubs in America and Britain.
Bord Fáilte
The Irish Tourist Board, set up in 1952 to promote tourism and develop tourist facilities.
An Tóstal
A national festival of Irish culture and heritage, launched in 1953 to attract visitors and promote Irish events.
Heritage tourism
Travel to places of cultural and historical interest, including sites associated with Irish history and the emigrant past.
Roots tourism
The visiting of ancestral homes and places by people of Irish descent, which linked Ireland with its emigrant communities abroad.
Folk revival
The popular interest in traditional and ballad music in the late 1950s and 1960s, in which the Clancy Brothers and the Dubliners brought Irish songs to a young audience.
Film censor
The official appointed under the Censorship of Films Act of 1923 who could cut or ban films in Ireland.
