The Catholic Church in the United States

Irish priests, bishops and religious sisters shaped Catholic communities overseas, and their institutions kept a sense of Irishness alive.

  • Irish-born and Irish-American priests and bishops dominated the American Catholic hierarchy for much of the 19th and early 20th centuries, because Irish emigrants formed the largest Catholic group and spoke English.
  • John Hughes, born in Co. Tyrone, became archbishop of New York in 1850, and John McCloskey of New York, of Irish parentage, became the first American cardinal in 1875.
  • Irish communities built parish churches, and many were dedicated to Irish saints such as Patrick and Brigid.
  • St Patrick's Cathedral in New York, built from the 1850s, was dedicated in 1879, and it became a symbol of the Irish-led Catholic presence in the city.
  • The Third Plenary Council of Baltimore in 1884 required every parish to establish a school for Catholic children, which made parochial schools central to Catholic life.
  • Religious orders with Irish members, including the Sisters of Mercy and the Christian Brothers, ran schools, hospitals and charitable houses.
  • Catholic fraternal societies such as the Ancient Order of Hibernians and the Knights of Columbus, founded in 1882 by an Irish-American priest, Michael McGivney, offered insurance, funeral benefits and social life.
  • The parish gave emigrants a social centre with dances, sodalities and sport, and the priest acted as an adviser and community leader.

The Catholic Church in Britain and Australia

  • Irish-born priests served many of the Catholic parishes in Liverpool, Glasgow and other British cities, where they built churches and schools for poor congregations.
  • The Catholic hierarchy was restored in England and Wales in 1850, at a time when Famine emigration was swelling Catholic numbers.
  • The Education (Scotland) Act of 1918 brought Catholic schools in Scotland into the state system while keeping their religious character, which secured their place in Scottish education.
  • Brother Walfrid, a Marist from Co. Sligo, founded Celtic Football Club in Glasgow in 1887 to raise money for poor Irish Catholic families, and the club became a symbol of the Irish community in Scotland.
  • Irish nuns ran hospitals, orphanages and schools in British cities, and their work reached poor Irish districts that the state had not served well.
  • In Australia the Catholic Church was led largely by Irish-born clergy and bishops, and Irish orders such as the Christian Brothers and the Sisters of Mercy staffed its schools.
  • When the Australian colonies ended state aid to church schools in the 1870s and 1880s, the Catholic Church built its own school system, funded by the Irish Catholic community.
  • The Catholic community in Britain and Australia was a visible minority, and its institutions were a focus for both loyalty to the Irish cause and sectarian suspicion.

The Catholic Church and the Irish in Canada

  • Irish Catholics were the largest Catholic group in English-speaking Canada, and the church built parishes, schools and hospitals in Montreal, Toronto and Ontario towns.
  • St Patrick's Basilica in Montreal, opened in 1847, was built to serve the city's growing Irish Catholic congregation.
  • Michael Power, the first Catholic bishop of Toronto and the son of Irish emigrants, died of typhus in October 1847 after caring for Famine emigrants.
  • In Newfoundland people of Irish Catholic descent formed a large share of the population, and the church was central to their community life.
  • In Ontario Catholic and Orange rivalry was constant, and disputes over separate Catholic schools were a recurring political issue.

Key personality: Archbishop Mannix

Daniel Mannix was an Irish-born Catholic archbishop in Melbourne, whose opposition to conscription and support for Irish independence made him a controversial figure.

  • Daniel Mannix was born in Charleville, Co. Cork, in 1864, trained at Maynooth, and was president of Maynooth College from 1903 until 1912.
  • He was appointed coadjutor archbishop of Melbourne in 1912, and he became archbishop in 1917.
  • As archbishop he opposed conscription in Australia during the First World War, and he took a leading part in the campaign in the referendums of 1916 and 1917, both of which were defeated.
  • His stand brought him into conflict with the prime minister, Billy Hughes, and with imperial loyalists, who called him disloyal.
  • He supported Irish independence and criticised British policy in Ireland after 1916, which made him a target for British loyalist opinion in Australia.
  • In August 1920, on a voyage from the USA to Ireland, he was taken off his ship at sea by a British naval vessel and landed in England, and he was banned from entering Ireland; he went on to speak to Irish Catholic communities in Britain.
  • He clashed with the Orange Order and other Protestant organisations in Victoria, and sectarian disputes shaped Melbourne politics in his time.
  • In the 1950s he supported the anti-communist Catholic Movement led by B. A. Santamaria, which contributed to the Labor split of 1955.
  • Contemporary attitudes were sharply divided: Irish Australians and many Catholics admired him, while imperial loyalists and Protestant organisations in Australia called him disloyal.
  • Historians present him as a major figure in Australian Catholic and political life, and they debate how far his nationalism was an Irish-Australian response to anti-Catholic prejudice; he died in November 1963, aged 99.

The GAA abroad

The Gaelic Athletic Association was founded in Ireland in 1884, and it reached Irish communities overseas through clubs and tours.

  • The GAA was founded at Hayes's Hotel in Thurles, Co. Tipperary, on 1 November 1884, and its founders included Michael Cusack and Maurice Davin.
  • The GAA promoted Gaelic football and hurling, and it also organised athletics, as a way of building a distinctly Irish sporting culture.
  • In 1888 a party of Irish athletes, hurlers and footballers toured the United States, playing exhibition matches and athletics before Irish-American crowds; the tour lost money, and some of the party stayed in America.
  • Irish-American clubs playing Gaelic football and hurling appeared in New York, Chicago, Boston and other cities with large Irish populations, and New York became the GAA's main centre in North America, with Gaelic Park in the Bronx opening in 1926.
  • A London GAA board was established in the 1890s, and clubs in Britain and Australia gave Irish emigrants a regular social life built around their identity.
  • The 1947 All-Ireland football final between Cavan and Kerry was played at the Polo Grounds in New York, to mark the Irish diaspora's place in the association.
  • The GAA linked sport with nationalism: it banned its members from playing or attending foreign games, a rule formalised in 1905, and clubs abroad were part of the same nationalist culture.

Protestant churches and Ulster-Scots identity

Protestant emigrants from Ulster, often called Scotch-Irish, kept a Presbyterian and Methodist identity that was distinct from Irish Catholic nationalism.

  • Ulster Presbyterians settled in Pennsylvania and along the Appalachians in the 18th century, and Francis Makemie, from Co. Donegal, helped found the Presbyterian church in the American colonies.
  • Their descendants in the USA became known as Scotch-Irish, a term that was widely used from the 19th century to distinguish them from Catholic Irish, especially after the Famine.
  • Presbyterian and Methodist congregations in the USA and Canada kept links with the churches in Ireland and recruited ministers from them.
  • Methodism in America and Canada was spread by Irish Palatine emigrants from Co. Limerick, such as Philip Embury and Barbara Heck, who helped found the first Methodist society in New York in the 1760s.
  • The Ulster-Scots identity stressed Protestant descent, loyalty to the British Crown and a sense of a separate heritage from the Irish Catholic majority.
  • Many Protestant emigrants assimilated quickly into American and Canadian denominations, and their sense of Irishness faded more quickly than that of Catholics.
  • In Canada Ulster-Scots and Presbyterian identity was strong in Ontario, and it was linked to loyalty to the British connection.

The Orange Order in Canada and Australia

The Orange Order was founded in Ireland in 1795, and it carried a Protestant and loyalist identity to Canada and Australia, where it was politically significant.

  • The Orange Order was founded in Co. Armagh in 1795, after the Battle of the Diamond between Protestant and Catholic groups.
  • Lodges reached Canada early in the 19th century with soldiers and settlers, and the Grand Orange Lodge of British America was formed in 1830 to coordinate them.
  • Orange lodges in Toronto and Ontario were politically influential, and Toronto, sometimes called the Belfast of Canada, was long governed by Orangemen as mayors.
  • Sir John A. Macdonald, Canada's first prime minister, was an Orangeman, and Mackenzie Bowell, prime minister from 1894 to 1896, was a Grand Master.
  • Orange parades on 12 July, known as the Twelfth, marked the Battle of the Boyne and were occasions of public display and occasional violence in Canada.
  • Orange lodges were established in colonial New South Wales from the 1840s and spread to Victoria, and they opposed Catholic influence and Home Rule.
  • In Australia the Orange Order was part of a loyalist and anti-Catholic culture, and it clashed with Catholic institutions in Melbourne and Sydney.
  • Orange parades in New York in 1870 and 1871 led to riots with Irish Catholics in which dozens died, which showed that Irish sectarian rivalry was carried to America.

How church, sport and community sustained Irishness

Church, sport and community life worked together to sustain a sense of Irishness abroad, though many emigrants and their children took part in only some of them.

  • Priests and nuns acted as community leaders, and parish events such as dances, picnics and fundraising kept Irish life visible in the city.
  • St Patrick's Day parades in New York date back to 1762, and they became a major public display of Irish identity in America.
  • Church schools taught Catholic children their faith and gave the second generation a grounding in parish and community life.
  • The second generation often moved towards American or Australian culture, and the church and GAA supported Irishness for those who wanted it.
  • Catholic and Orange rivalry in Canada and Australia shaped the public life of Irish communities, and sectarian tension was a constant feature.
  • The Catholic Church encouraged loyalty to the new country as well as faith, so it was a vehicle of assimilation as well as of Irishness.

Assessment: church, sport and identity abroad

  • Achievement: the Catholic Church and GAA gave Irish emigrants a community, a social life and an Irish identity that lasted for generations.
  • Limit: many emigrants' children assimilated, and the institutions reached only part of the emigrant population.
  • Contemporary view: Irish nationalists praised the church and the GAA as guardians of Irishness, while nativists and imperial loyalists regarded them as divisive.
  • Later view: historians argue that the Catholic Church and the GAA sustained identity but also helped Irish communities to organise and gain power in their new societies.
  • Protestant and Orange identity achieved political influence in Canada and Australia, but it was often a sign of loyalty to the British connection, not of Irish nationalism.

Key terms

Gaelic Athletic Association
The sporting body founded at Thurles in 1884 to promote Gaelic games and athletics, with clubs in Ireland and abroad.
Orange Order
The Protestant fraternal society founded in Co. Armagh in 1795, which had lodges in Canada and Australia that supported the British connection.
Ulster-Scots identity
The sense of Ulster Presbyterian descent among emigrants in North America, often called Scotch-Irish, which stressed Protestant heritage and loyalty to the Crown.
Parochial school system
The network of parish schools that the Catholic Church required in the USA from the 1884 Council of Baltimore.
Ancient Order of Hibernians
A Catholic fraternal society for Irish men, offering insurance and social support to members abroad.
Scotch-Irish
The term used in North America for descendants of Ulster Presbyterian settlers, who kept an identity separate from Irish Catholics.
Hierarchy
The bishops and archbishops who govern a church; Irish-born and Irish-American clergy dominated the American Catholic hierarchy.
Sectarianism
Hostility between religious groups, such as Catholics and Orangemen, which Irish emigrants carried into Canada, Australia, the USA and Britain.
The Twelfth
The Orange parades held on 12 July to mark the Battle of the Boyne.
