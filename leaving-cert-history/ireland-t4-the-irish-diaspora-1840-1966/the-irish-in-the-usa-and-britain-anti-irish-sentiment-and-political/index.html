Causes of anti-Irish sentiment

Hostility to the Irish rested on religion, poverty and competition for work, and it was reinforced by stereotypes that were widely printed.

  • Religion: most Irish emigrants were Catholic, and Protestant Americans and Britons often saw Catholicism as foreign and a threat to their institutions, with the Pope as a rival authority.
  • Poverty and disease: the Irish who arrived in the Famine years were poor, crowded into slums, and outbreaks of cholera and typhus were blamed on them.
  • Crime and drink: Irish migrants were blamed for crime, drunkenness and disorder in the cities where they lived, and they were over-represented in arrest figures.
  • Competition for work: Irish labourers and servants competed with native-born and other workers, and willingness to accept low wages created resentment, including strike-breaking tensions.
  • Racial stereotypes: the Irish were portrayed as a separate and inferior race, and in cartoons they were often drawn with apelike features.
  • Political fear: Protestants feared that Catholic voters would take orders from their priests and that Irish nationalists would involve the host country in Irish quarrels.

Anti-Irish sentiment in the United States

American nativism, the movement against immigrants and Catholic influence, reached its height in the 1840s and 1850s and continued in cartoons and job notices afterwards.

  • The Philadelphia riots of 1844 were anti-Catholic disturbances in which Irish Catholic churches and homes were attacked by nativist mobs after a dispute about the use of the Bible in schools.
  • Nativism grew into a political movement, and the Know-Nothings, formally the American Party, won major state victories in 1854, including Massachusetts.
  • The Know-Nothings took their name from the secrecy of their members, who were told to say they knew nothing when questioned about the movement.
  • The Know-Nothings wanted longer waiting periods before immigrants could become citizens and voters, and to bar Catholics from public office; the movement broke up before the Civil War over slavery.
  • Job advertisements and signs in the 19th century are said to have stated No Irish Need Apply; historians such as Richard Jensen have argued that written signs were rarer than later memory suggests, while Rebecca Fried's research found real examples, so the extent is debated.
  • Thomas Nast's cartoons in Harper's Weekly in the 1870s portrayed Irish Catholics, and especially Irish politicians, as ape-like and corrupt.
  • Irish Americans responded by building their own parishes, schools, newspapers and societies, and by using the vote.

Anti-Irish sentiment in Britain

In Britain anti-Irish feeling was mixed with sectarian rivalry, and it flared at moments of Fenian threat.

  • Punch magazine caricatured the Irish in the 1840s and 1860s, presenting them as simian, violent and backward.
  • The Stockport riots of 1852 saw Irish Catholic homes and churches attacked in Stockport after a Catholic procession in the town.
  • The Murphy riots of 1867 followed the anti-Catholic lectures of William Murphy and brought attacks on Irish districts in towns such as Birkenhead and Wolverhampton.
  • In Liverpool and Glasgow, Protestant Orange lodges and Catholic Irish communities clashed, and sectarian rivalry shaped local politics and street life.
  • After the Manchester Martyrs were executed in November 1867 and the Fenian bomb at Clerkenwell prison in London that December killed about a dozen people, fears grew that all Irish people in Britain were a security threat.
  • Fenian scares led to heavy policing of Irish districts, and Irish nationalists claimed that the state treated the whole community as suspect.
  • Writers blamed the Irish for typhus, low wages and crime in industrial towns, and the Victorian press often presented them as a separate race.

Irish political participation in the United States

Irish Americans turned their numbers into political power through the Democratic Party and the city machines.

  • Most Irish Americans voted Democratic, because the party defended immigrants against nativist attacks and offered jobs and help.
  • Ward politics: local party organisers in each ward gave immigrants jobs, housing and legal help in return for votes.
  • Tammany Hall, the Democratic organisation in New York City, controlled city politics through patronage, jobs and contracts; it was led by Irish bosses from 1872, beginning with John Kelly.
  • William R. Grace, born in Ireland, became the first Irish Catholic mayor of New York in 1880, and Hugh O'Brien became the first Irish Catholic mayor of Boston in 1885.
  • John F. Fitzgerald (Honey Fitz) and later James Michael Curley dominated Boston politics in the early 20th century.
  • Al Smith, a New York Catholic of Irish descent, became governor of New York and the Democratic presidential candidate in 1928.
  • Irish men were prominent in urban police forces and fire companies, and these jobs were a route into local politics.
  • Irish-American political success was built on group loyalty, and it gave immigrant communities a voice in city government.

Key personality: Boss Croker

Richard Croker turned Tammany Hall into the most powerful political machine in New York City.

  • Richard Croker was born in Co. Cork in 1843 and was taken to New York as a child, where he grew up in a poor Irish district.
  • He rose through the Fourth Avenue gang, the fire companies and the local Democratic clubs, and he held minor city offices such as alderman and coroner.
  • He became the leader of Tammany Hall in the mid-1880s and ran it until 1902, using patronage, jobs in city departments and contracts to build support among immigrants and the poor.
  • He controlled Democratic nominations and placed his allies in office, including Mayor Robert Van Wyck (1898 to 1901).
  • Investigations by the Lexow Committee (1894) into police corruption and the Mazet Committee (1899) exposed the system, and he left the leadership after Tammany lost the mayoral election of 1901.
  • He retired to Ireland, where he lived at Glencairn near Dublin and bred racehorses; his horse Orby won the Epsom Derby in 1907.
  • Contemporary reformers and much of the respectable press condemned Croker's corruption, while many Tammany voters regarded him as a friend of the poor.
  • Historians now present him as the clearest example of machine politics, in which immigrant political success came with corruption, though they also see his career as a route of upward mobility for an immigrant.

Key personality: Joe Kennedy

Joseph P. Kennedy showed how an Irish-American could rise to the top of business and then government, and his career was marked by controversy.

  • Joseph P. Kennedy was born in Boston in 1888, the son of Patrick J. Kennedy, a Boston politician and businessman, and he married Rose Fitzgerald, daughter of Mayor John F. Fitzgerald.
  • He built a fortune in banking, the stock market and the film industry before entering government.
  • He was the first chairman of the US Securities and Exchange Commission, from 1934, set up to regulate the stock market after the crash of 1929.
  • In 1937 he led the US Maritime Commission, and in 1938 President Roosevelt appointed him ambassador to Britain, the first Irish Catholic to hold the post.
  • As ambassador from 1938 to 1940 he supported Neville Chamberlain's policy of appeasement and doubted that Britain could win a war with Germany, and he resigned in late 1940 after an interview that was widely seen as defeatist about Britain.
  • Contemporary Irish-American opinion was divided: some were proud of his success, while others distrusted his conservative views and his closeness to the British government.
  • Historians judge him a self-made businessman and an appeaser whose pessimism damaged his career, and whose ambitions shaped his sons, including John F. Kennedy (see the notes on John F. Kennedy).

Key personality: Paul O’Dwyer

Paul O'Dwyer combined a career as a New York lawyer with campaigns for civil rights and for Irish causes.

  • Paul O'Dwyer was born in Bohola, Co. Mayo, in 1907 and emigrated to New York in 1925, becoming a lawyer there in the 1930s; he was the brother of William O'Dwyer, mayor of New York from 1946 to 1950.
  • He defended trade unionists and took on civil rights cases and causes for working people and immigrants, and supported the civil rights movement.
  • He was a Democratic politician who ran unsuccessfully for the US Senate in 1968 as an opponent of the Vietnam War and later served as president of the New York City Council in the mid-1970s.
  • He was active in Irish causes, and in later decades he supported civil rights for Catholics in Northern Ireland and criticised British policy there.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a liberal and a critic of the establishment, and some criticised his views on Northern Ireland as too sympathetic to the republican cause.
  • Historians place him in the tradition of liberal Irish-American politics, in which civil rights and Irish nationalism were linked.

Irish political participation in Britain

The Irish vote in British cities was strongest in Liverpool and Scotland, and it was used by nationalist and Labour politicians.

  • The Irish National League of Great Britain, founded in 1883, organised Irish voters behind the Irish Parliamentary Party.
  • In the general election of 1885 Parnell told Irish voters in Britain to vote against the Liberals, and they voted Conservative.
  • T. P. O'Connor was elected MP for Liverpool Scotland in 1885, and he held the seat until 1929 as a nationalist and a journalist.
  • O'Connor founded newspapers in London, including The Star, and he used the press to support Home Rule and Irish causes.
  • After Gladstone's first Home Rule Bill in 1886 Irish voters in Liverpool and Glasgow largely supported the Liberal Party as the party of Home Rule.
  • From the early 20th century Irish voters in Britain increasingly backed the Labour Party, which also drew support from the working class.
  • Irish-born men could vote and stand for Parliament as British subjects, so their political participation was easier than in the USA, where naturalisation was needed.

The Molly Maguires

The Molly Maguires were a secret society among Irish miners in the Pennsylvania anthracite coalfields, whose conflict with mine owners ended in executions.

  • The society operated in the anthracite coalfields of eastern Pennsylvania, especially in Schuylkill County, in the 1860s and 1870s, and drew on the tradition of the Ribbonmen and the name of an Irish agrarian secret society.
  • Irish miners worked in dangerous conditions for low pay, and many members belonged to the Ancient Order of Hibernians.
  • Violence against mine bosses and police was blamed on the Mollies, who were accused of murders and arson.
  • The owner of the Reading Railroad, Franklin B. Gowen, hired the Pinkerton detective James McParland, who infiltrated the society under the name James McKenna.
  • After the failed Long Strike of 1875 the miners' union was broken, and the trials took place in 1876 to 1878, with convictions depending heavily on the evidence of informers and of McParland.
  • Twenty men were hanged between 1877 and 1879; ten were hanged on a single day, 21 June 1877, known as the Day of the Rope, and the leader Jack Kehoe was hanged in 1878.
  • Labour historians see the Mollies as victims of an employer-backed frame-up, while others see a real secret society using violence; Kevin Kenny (Making Sense of the Molly Maguires, 1998) argued that they were a genuine society, rooted in Irish agrarian violence, but that the trials were unfair.
  • Jack Kehoe was pardoned after his death, by the Governor of Pennsylvania in 1979.

Assessment: discrimination and political participation

Judgements on Irish political life in America and Britain turn on whether the Irish gained real influence despite discrimination, or only built machines and patronage.

  • Achievement: Irish politicians built lasting power in American cities and in some British seats, and they gave immigrants a route into public life.
  • Limit: machine politics relied on patronage and corruption, and reform movements challenged it from the 1880s onwards.
  • Success against discrimination: by the early 20th century Irish Americans were prominent in the police and city government, a sign that discrimination was being overcome.
  • Contemporary view: nativists and reformers condemned Irish politicians as corrupt, while Irish communities defended them as champions of immigrants.
  • Later view: historians disagree on whether Irish political success advanced assimilation or delayed it, because machine politics kept communities separate.
  • Britain versus the USA: the Irish in Britain had votes sooner, but their political voice was channelled into the Irish Parliamentary Party and Labour rather than into city machines.

Key terms

Discrimination
Unfair treatment of a group in jobs, housing, politics or public life, which Irish emigrants faced in both the USA and Britain.
Assimilation
The process by which emigrants adopted the language, customs and social habits of their new country; political participation was one route to it.
Nativism
A movement in the United States that opposed immigration and Catholic influence in favour of native-born Protestants.
Know-Nothings
The nickname of the American Party, a nativist movement of the 1850s that won major state victories in 1854.
Tammany Hall
The Democratic organisation in New York City that controlled city politics through patronage, jobs and contracts.
Ward politics
Local party organisation in each city ward, which gave immigrants jobs and help in return for votes.
Molly Maguires
A secret society of Irish miners in the Pennsylvania anthracite coalfields, whose members were executed after trials in the 1870s.
Machine politics
Political organisation built on patronage and loyalty, in which a boss controls votes and jobs in return for support.
Pinkerton detective
A private detective employed by mine owners, such as James McParland, who infiltrated the Molly Maguires.
Patronage
The giving of jobs and favours by politicians in return for votes and loyalty.
