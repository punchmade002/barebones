Emigration before the Famine: origins, destinations and types of emigrant

Emigration was already an established feature of Irish life before 1845, but it changed in who left and where they went.

  • Ulster Presbyterians were the first large group of Irish emigrants to North America, sailing to the American colonies through the 18th century.
  • After 1815 the flow widened: about one million people left Ireland between 1815 and 1845, with estimates ranging up to 1.5 million.
  • Pre-Famine emigrants were often Protestant small farmers, weavers and tradesmen from Ulster and the midlands who could pay their own fares and had some capital.
  • From the 1830s Catholic emigrants from the south and west made up a growing share, and they were poorer and less skilled than earlier emigrants.
  • British North America (Canada) was the main transatlantic destination before 1845 because fares there were the cheapest, and many emigrants then walked or sailed on into the United States.
  • Britain received the largest number of the poorest emigrants, because the crossing to Liverpool or Glasgow cost only a few shillings.
  • The 1841 census counted about 420,000 Irish-born people in Britain, concentrated in Liverpool, Glasgow, Manchester and London.

Seasonal migration, occupations and settlement before 1845

  • Seasonal migration: tens of thousands of labourers from Mayo, Donegal, Galway and other western counties crossed each summer to work on English and Scottish harvests and returned home with cash to pay the rent.
  • Irish labourers in Britain worked as navvies on canals and railways, as dockers, as building labourers and in textile mills.
  • Irish districts grew in the poorest parts of British cities, such as Little Ireland in Manchester and St Giles in London.
  • In North America Irish men dug canals such as the Erie Canal (opened 1825) and built roads and early railways, while Irish women went into domestic service.
  • Push factors: subdivision of holdings into tiny plots, a population that had risen to about 8.2 million by 1841, dependence on the potato, the decline of domestic linen and wool spinning, and evictions for rent arrears.
  • Pull factors: higher wages and demand for labour in industrial Britain and North America, cheap land in Canada, and letters from earlier emigrants describing work abroad.
  • Timber ships carried Canadian timber to Britain and Ireland and returned westwards nearly empty, so they took emigrants cheaply as human ballast.

The Famine decade, 1845–1855: scale and character of emigration

Repeated failures of the potato turned a steady flow into a mass exodus.

  • Potato blight, caused by the water mould Phytophthora infestans, destroyed about a third of the crop in 1845 and almost all of it in 1846.
  • The 1847 crop was largely free of blight but very small because little seed had been planted, and blight returned in 1848; 1847 is remembered as Black 47 for its deaths and fever.
  • About one million people died of starvation and famine-related disease between 1845 and 1851; estimates vary.
  • Between 1.5 and 2 million people emigrated between 1845 and 1855 (estimates vary), the great majority to the United States, with several hundred thousand to British North America and to Britain.
  • The census showed Ireland's population falling from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.5 million in 1851.
  • Famine emigrants were mostly Catholic, poor, unskilled and Irish-speaking in many cases, and came heavily from the west and south-west; many travelled as whole families rather than as single young adults.
  • Emigration was panic flight rather than planned movement: in 1847 about 300,000 destitute Irish people landed at Liverpool alone, and many stayed there.
  • By 1851 the number of Irish-born in Britain had risen to about 730,000, and about a fifth of Liverpool's population was Irish-born.

Relief policy, evictions and assisted emigration

  • Public works were the main relief in 1846, but the pay was low and late and heavy labour weakened starving workers.
  • The Temporary Relief Act of 1847 replaced the public works with soup kitchens, which fed about 3 million people a day at their peak in the summer of 1847 before closing that autumn.
  • The Poor Law Extension Act of June 1847 made the Poor Law unions pay for relief, and its Gregory clause (quarter-acre clause) denied relief to anyone holding more than a quarter acre unless they gave up the land.
  • Rising poor rates gave landlords a reason to clear smallholders, and evictions rose sharply between 1847 and 1852.
  • Assisted emigration: some landlords paid passages because shipping tenants abroad was cheaper than paying rates to support them in the workhouse.
  • Major Denis Mahon of Strokestown, Co. Roscommon, sent 1,490 tenants via Liverpool to Quebec in 1847; hundreds died on the crossing or soon after at Grosse Isle, and Mahon was shot dead on 2 November 1847.
  • Lord Palmerston sent about 2,000 tenants from his Sligo estate to Canada in 1847, and many arrived destitute; Lord Lansdowne paid passages for several thousand tenants from Kenmare, Co. Kerry, in the early 1850s.
  • The Earl Grey scheme (1848–1850) shipped about 4,000 orphan girls from Irish workhouses to Australia as domestic servants.
  • The Encumbered Estates Act of 1849 allowed bankrupt estates to be sold, and many new owners cleared tenants to consolidate farms.

Pre-paid passages and chain-emigration in the Famine years

  • Pre-paid passages: relatives already abroad bought tickets from shipping agents in America and posted them home, so people with no money could still leave.
  • Much Famine emigration was funded this way rather than by landlords or the state; assisted emigration paid for only a small minority of those who left.
  • Chain-emigration: the first member of a family to settle abroad sent money and tickets for the next, who in turn paid for others, so whole families and parishes moved in stages.
  • Emigrants from the same district tended to settle in the same streets and parishes abroad, where kin found them lodgings and work.
  • Letters home described wages, prices and fares and were read aloud in villages, spreading information about where to go.

Routes, ports, fares and the coffin ships

  • Most transatlantic emigrants first crossed to Liverpool, which had the most frequent sailings; others sailed direct from Cork harbour, Dublin, Limerick, Sligo, Derry, Galway and smaller ports.
  • Fares to Quebec were cheaper than to New York, so the poorest emigrants went to British North America.
  • The US Passenger Acts of 1847 limited the number of passengers per ship and raised fares on the US route, diverting more of the poorest and sickest emigrants to Canada.
  • Steerage passengers were crammed into the lower decks of sailing ships for a voyage of six weeks or more, with poor ventilation, little water and food often below what the law required.
  • Typhus (ship fever), spread by lice, and dysentery killed thousands at sea; ships with high death rates became known as coffin ships.
  • Of about 100,000 people who sailed for British North America in 1847, an estimated 17,000 or more died at sea, in quarantine or soon after arrival.
  • Ports of arrival included New York, Boston, Philadelphia, Quebec and Saint John, where fever sheds and quarantine stations were set up.

Case study: Grosse Isle, background

Grosse Isle (Grosse Île) was the quarantine station for the port of Quebec and the first North American landfall for tens of thousands of Famine emigrants.

  • Grosse Isle is an island in the St Lawrence River about 48 km downstream from Quebec City.
  • The quarantine station was opened in 1832 to stop cholera reaching Quebec from emigrant ships.
  • Every ship bound for Quebec had to stop there; a medical officer inspected passengers, and the sick were landed at the hospital while the healthy were held in sheds or on board.
  • The medical superintendent was Dr George Mellis Douglas, who had been its medical superintendent since 1836.
  • The station was built for the normal flow of a season, and its hospital sheds held only a few hundred patients when the 1847 emigration began.

Case study: Grosse Isle, the 1847 season

  • The first ship of the season, the Syria, arrived in mid-May 1847 with fever on board, and the hospital was full within days.
  • By 31 May 1847 about 40 ships carrying some 12,500 passengers lay at anchor off the island, in a queue stretching down the river.
  • Ships waited days or weeks for inspection, and the sick and dead lay in the holds alongside the healthy, so fever spread among people who had been well.
  • Tents and new sheds were put up, but sick and dying people lay on the ground and on the beaches; healthy passengers were crowded into sheds where many caught typhus.
  • Dr Douglas warned the authorities in advance and asked for more help, and his reports are a major source on the season; several doctors on the island caught typhus and some died.
  • Catholic priests from the diocese of Quebec, many of them French-Canadian, and Anglican clergy took turns on the island; several caught typhus and some died.
  • More than 5,000 people were buried on the island in 1847, most of them Irish; some estimates of the deaths on the island are higher.
  • Passengers passed as healthy carried typhus up the river: about 6,000 Irish emigrants died in the fever sheds at Montreal in 1847, and Montreal's mayor John Mills died of typhus while helping the sick.
  • Hundreds of children orphaned at Grosse Isle and in the fever sheds were adopted by French-Canadian families, many of whom let them keep their Irish surnames.

Case study: Grosse Isle, comparisons, memory and significance

  • Partridge Island, the quarantine station at Saint John, New Brunswick, faced a similar fever crisis in 1847 with ships from Ireland and Liverpool.
  • New York received far more Famine emigrants than Quebec, but the poorest and sickest were more often diverted to Canada by the dearer US fares; New York's emigration commissioners opened the Castle Garden landing depot in 1855 to protect arriving emigrants.
  • The medical staff erected a monument on the island in 1853 to the dead of 1847.
  • The Ancient Order of Hibernians erected a large Celtic cross on the island's highest point, unveiled on 15 August 1909, with inscriptions in Irish, English and French; the Irish text blames the Famine on British rule more bluntly than the English text.
  • The quarantine station closed in 1937, and the island became a Canadian national historic site; after Irish-Canadian campaigning it became, in the 1990s, Grosse Île and the Irish Memorial National Historic Site.
  • Nationalists from John Mitchel onwards used Grosse Isle as evidence that British policy, not only the blight, killed the Irish poor; others stressed landlord clearances and the limits of colonial resources.
  • Grosse Isle shows the human cost of unregulated mass emigration, the failure of governments on both sides of the Atlantic to prepare, and the generosity of French-Canadian Quebec.

Assessment: Famine emigration

  • Relief versus loss: emigration saved hundreds of thousands from starvation, but it emptied the poorest districts and began a century of population decline.
  • Choice versus force: pre-paid tickets show emigrants making their own family decisions, while clearances and assisted schemes such as Mahon's show tenants being pushed out.
  • Contemporary view: officials, landlords and many economists welcomed emigration as a cure for overpopulation; John Mitchel and the Young Irelanders saw it as extermination.
  • Later view: Kerby Miller (Emigrants and Exiles, 1985) argued that emigrants saw themselves as exiles banished by England, a view that shaped Irish-American nationalism.
  • Revisionist historians of the 1950s to 1970s played down British blame, while Cormac Ó Gráda, Christine Kinealy and others since the 1990s have stressed failures of relief policy alongside the natural disaster.
  • Grosse Isle has become the leading memorial to the Famine dead outside Ireland, though the great majority of Famine emigrants went to the United States and Britain.

Key terms

Pre-paid passages
Tickets bought abroad by relatives or friends who had already emigrated and posted home, so that people with no money could leave; the main way Famine and post-Famine emigration was financed.
Chain-emigration
Emigration in stages along family and community networks, in which each emigrant paid for and housed the next, so that whole families and parishes moved over time.
Assisted emigration
Emigration paid for by landlords, Poor Law unions or governments, used during the Famine to clear estates and cut relief costs.
Seasonal migration
The yearly movement of western labourers to England and Scotland for harvest work, returning home with cash for the rent.
Coffin ship
An overcrowded, poorly provisioned emigrant sailing ship with a high death rate from fever and hunger.
Ship fever
Typhus, spread by lice in crowded holds, which killed many Famine emigrants at sea and in quarantine.
Quarantine station
A place where arriving ships and passengers were inspected and the sick held apart to stop disease spreading into a port city.
Gregory clause
The quarter-acre clause of the 1847 Poor Law Extension Act, which denied relief to anyone holding more than a quarter acre unless they gave up the land.
Push and pull factors
The conditions at home that drove people out (push) and the attractions abroad that drew them (pull).
Encumbered Estates Act
The 1849 Act that allowed bankrupt estates to be sold, often to buyers who cleared the tenants.
