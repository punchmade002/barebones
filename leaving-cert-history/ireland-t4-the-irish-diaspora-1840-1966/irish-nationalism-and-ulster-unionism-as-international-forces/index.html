Fenianism and Irish America, 1858 to the 1880s

Irish nationalism became an international force through emigrant organisations in the United States, Canada and Britain, which raised money and planned rebellion.

  • The Irish Republican Brotherhood was founded in Dublin on 17 March 1858 by James Stephens, aiming at an independent Irish republic by armed force.
  • The Fenian Brotherhood was founded in New York in 1858 by John O'Mahony as the American wing of the movement, and it drew on Irish emigrants and veterans of the American Civil War.
  • Fenian money came from Irish emigrant workers in American cities and paid for preparations for the Irish rising of 1867, which failed.
  • In June 1866 Fenians crossed the Niagara River into Canada and won a skirmish at Ridgeway, Ontario, before withdrawing under pressure from the US government.
  • A second Fenian raid into Canada in 1870 was defeated, and the raids damaged the movement's reputation in America and strengthened the case for Canadian confederation.
  • Clan na Gael was founded in New York in 1867 as a secret republican society for Irish Americans, and after the Fenian Brotherhood declined it became the strongest republican body in the United States.
  • The last convict ship to Australia, the Hougoumont, carried about 60 Fenian prisoners in 1867 to 1868, and an American campaign for the release of imprisoned Fenians drew support from Irish-American opinion.
  • Jeremiah O'Donovan Rossa's Skirmishing Fund, launched in New York in 1876, paid for the dynamite campaign in English cities in the 1880s, extending the use of American money for violence.

Key personality: John Devoy

John Devoy was the most important Irish-American republican of his generation, and his career links the Fenians of the 1860s with the rising of 1916.

  • John Devoy was born in Co. Kildare in 1842 and joined the Irish Republican Brotherhood in the 1860s, recruiting Fenians in the British Army.
  • He was arrested in 1866 and sentenced to 15 years' penal servitude for treason-felony, and he was released in 1871 on condition that he went into exile; he sailed to New York with other prisoners on the Cuba.
  • In America he became a leading figure in Clan na Gael and used Irish-American newspapers to argue for an independent Irish republic.
  • In 1876 Devoy and Clan na Gael organised the rescue of six Fenian prisoners from Western Australia, carried out by the American whaler Catalpa.
  • In 1878 he proposed the New Departure with Michael Davitt, a plan for Fenians to cooperate with Charles Stewart Parnell's constitutional nationalists for land reform and self-government.
  • He founded the Gaelic American newspaper in New York in 1903, and he edited it for many years to keep Irish-American opinion behind the republican cause.
  • In 1914 he and Judge Daniel Cohalan met the German ambassador in Washington, Count Bernstorff, to ask for German help for an Irish rising, and Clan na Gael sent money to the IRB before the Rising of 1916.
  • After 1919 he quarrelled bitterly with de Valera, and he supported the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921; he visited the Irish Free State in 1924 and died in 1928.
  • Contemporaries in Britain regarded Devoy as a dangerous revolutionary, while many Irish Americans saw him as an elder statesman of the republican cause; de Valera's supporters saw him as an obstinate old man.
  • Historians, such as Terry Golway in his biography Irish Rebel (1998), present him as a central link between Fenian conspiracy in Ireland and organised Irish-American support in the twentieth century; his memoir Recollections of an Irish Rebel was published in 1929.

Land League, Parnell and Davitt in America

Irish nationalists raised money in America for the land agitation of 1879 to 1882, and American support gave the movement a wide base.

  • Michael Davitt, a Fenian released from prison in 1877, visited America in 1878, and his work with Devoy produced the New Departure that linked physical-force republicans with constitutional nationalists.
  • The Irish National Land League was founded in Dublin in 1879, with Parnell as president and Davitt as a founder, to campaign against rack-renting and eviction.
  • Parnell toured America in early 1880, raising funds among Irish Americans in cities across the country and addressing the House of Representatives in Washington.
  • Patrick Ford's newspaper the Irish World raised large sums for the Land League and for evicted tenants.
  • American money paid for relief to evicted tenants and for the Land League's campaign when the League was suppressed in 1881, and the American Ladies' Land League, led by Fanny Parnell, was founded in New York in 1880.
  • The Land League funds helped to sustain tenants who had been evicted, and this made American money a direct force in Irish agrarian politics.

Irish nationalism in Britain and Australia

Irish nationalist organisations in Britain and Australia gave the movement support beyond the United States, though their influence was smaller.

  • The Irish National League of Great Britain, founded in 1883, organised Irish voters in Liverpool, Glasgow and other British cities behind the Irish Parliamentary Party.
  • The Irish Self-Determination League of Great Britain, founded in 1919, campaigned for the Irish Republic among Irish emigrants in Britain during the War of Independence.
  • Irish Catholic communities in Australia, especially in Melbourne and Sydney, supported Home Rule through their churches and press, and Irish Parliamentary Party delegates, including the Redmond brothers in 1883, toured Australia to raise funds.
  • After 1916 Irish Australians, led by Archbishop Mannix, opposed conscription and supported the cause of Irish independence (see the notes on the Catholic Church abroad).
  • The Fenian prisoners transported to Western Australia and the Catalpa rescue gave Australia a place in Fenian memory.

Irish America and the Rising of 1916

Irish-American organisations gave money and support to the 1916 Rising, and their divisions shaped Irish politics after 1918.

  • Clan na Gael supported the 1916 Rising with money and contacts, and Devoy and Cohalan sought German help through the German embassy in Washington.
  • The Irish Race Convention held in New York in March 1916 launched the Friends of Irish Freedom, led by Judge Daniel Cohalan and Devoy.
  • The arms ship Aud, sent from Germany for the Rising, was intercepted by the Royal Navy and scuttled off Cork harbour in April 1916.
  • After the Rising and the executions of its leaders Irish-American opinion moved strongly behind the Irish Republic, and the Friends of Irish Freedom raised money and lobbied Congress.
  • An Irish Race Convention in Philadelphia in February 1919 called on the Paris Peace Conference and President Wilson to support Irish self-determination.
  • Divisions among Irish-American leaders, especially between Devoy and Cohalan on one side and Éamon de Valera on the other, weakened the unity of the movement after 1919 (see the notes on de Valera in America).

The anti-partition campaign after 1945

After the partition of Ireland, nationalists sought international support against the border, and Irish America was a key source of lobbying.

  • The Government of Ireland Act of 1920 created Northern Ireland, and the border with the Irish Free State was confirmed after the Boundary Commission collapsed in 1925.
  • The Anti-Partition League was formed in Northern Ireland in 1945 to unite nationalists, and it sought support in Ireland, Britain and America.
  • Éamon de Valera, then leader of the opposition, toured the USA and other countries in 1948 to press the anti-partition case, though the tour did not win any US government move to end partition.
  • In September 1948 Taoiseach John A. Costello announced in Canada the intention to repeal the External Relations Act, and the Republic of Ireland Act took effect on 18 April 1949.
  • Britain answered with the Ireland Act of 1949, which guaranteed that Northern Ireland would remain in the United Kingdom unless its parliament decided otherwise.
  • Ireland declined to join NATO in 1949 because of partition, and Seán MacBride, as Minister for External Affairs, appealed to American politicians and press.
  • Congressman John Fogarty of Rhode Island proposed in 1950 that American aid to Britain be linked to ending partition; the proposal failed, and the Irish government's campaign faded after 1951.

Ulster Unionist lobbying among emigrants

Ulster Unionists sought support among emigrants of Ulster Protestant descent in North America and the Dominions, and their identity rested on a shared Scots-Irish heritage.

  • Ulster-Scots identity: the descendants of Ulster Presbyterians in the United States and Canada, often called Scotch-Irish, kept a sense of Ulster origins; a Scotch-Irish Society of America held its first congress in 1889.
  • Several US presidents, including Andrew Jackson and Woodrow Wilson, were claimed as Ulster-Scots.
  • The Ulster Covenant of 28 September 1912 was signed by about half a million Ulster Unionists to oppose Home Rule, and it became the symbol of loyalist resistance.
  • The Orange Order in Canada, with lodges in Ontario and Toronto, was politically significant and gave moral support to Ulster unionism during the Home Rule crisis.
  • In Australia Orange lodges in Victoria and New South Wales supported the British connection and opposed Home Rule for Ireland.
  • Ulster Unionist lobbying abroad relied on the Scots-Irish and Orange networks and on figures like Bonar Law, a Canadian-born Ulster-Scot; it had moral and some financial value, but little direct influence on British policy.

Key personality: Andrew Bonar Law

Bonar Law was born in Canada to an Ulster Presbyterian father, and he led the Conservatives in the Home Rule crisis before becoming prime minister in 1922.

  • Andrew Bonar Law was born in Kingston, New Brunswick, in 1858, the son of a Presbyterian minister, the Rev. James Law, who came from Ulster.
  • The family moved to Glasgow in 1870, and Bonar Law went into the iron trade there before entering Parliament in 1900.
  • He became leader of the Conservative Party in November 1911, succeeding Arthur Balfour.
  • At Blenheim Palace in July 1912 he told a rally he could imagine no length of resistance to which Ulster could go that he would not support.
  • He was a leading Unionist in the Home Rule crisis of 1912 to 1914, and his support for the Ulster Unionists made the exclusion of Ulster a Conservative goal.
  • He served in the wartime coalition governments and was Chancellor of the Exchequer from 1916 to 1919 in Lloyd George's government.
  • He became Prime Minister in October 1922, resigned in May 1923 because of cancer, and died in October 1923.
  • Contemporary opinion was divided, with Liberal and nationalist newspapers portraying him as a reckless partisan, while unionists praised his loyalty.
  • Historians such as Robert Blake (The Unknown Prime Minister, 1955) stress his role in shaping Conservative policy on Ulster, and they debate how far his stand made partition more likely.

Assessment: nationalist and unionist networks abroad

  • Achievement: Fenian and Clan na Gael networks funded and armed the nationalist movement, and Land League funds sustained the agitation of 1879 to 1882.
  • Limit: the Fenian rising of 1867 and the raids into Canada failed, and factional splits in America weakened support.
  • Anti-partition campaigners won sympathy in America after 1945 but failed to end partition.
  • Unionist networks gave moral and financial support through Orange and Scots-Irish links, but they had little direct effect on British policy.
  • Contemporary view: British officials regarded Irish-American networks as threats to security, while Irish-American leaders saw themselves as allies of the Irish cause.
  • Later view: historians debate whether diaspora money decided events, and many stress that the decisive politics took place in Ireland and Britain.

Key terms

Fenian Brotherhood
The republican organisation founded in New York in 1858 as the American wing of the Irish Republican Brotherhood.
Clan na Gael
A secret republican society for Irish Americans, founded in New York in 1867, which supported Irish independence through money and planning.
New Departure
The 1878 plan by which Fenians, led by John Devoy and Michael Davitt, agreed to cooperate with constitutional nationalists for land reform and Home Rule.
Land League
The Irish National Land League, founded in 1879, which campaigned against rack-renting and eviction and was funded in part by Irish-American donations.
Friends of Irish Freedom
An Irish-American organisation founded in New York in 1916 to press for Irish independence in the United States.
Anti-partition campaign
The campaign after 1945 to end the partition of Ireland, which sought support from the Irish government and Irish-American opinion.
Ulster-Scots identity
The sense of descent from Ulster Presbyterian settlers in North America, often called Scotch-Irish, which kept Ulster Protestant identity alive abroad.
Orange Order
The Protestant fraternal society founded in Co. Armagh in 1795, which had lodges in Canada and Australia that supported the British connection.
Catalpa rescue
The 1876 escape of six Fenian prisoners from Western Australia on an American whaler, organised by Clan na Gael.
Ulster Covenant
The pledge signed on 28 September 1912 by Ulster Unionists to resist Home Rule.
