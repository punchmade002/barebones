Case study: de Valera in America, background and causes

Éamon de Valera went to America in June 1919 as head of the Dáil government, after escaping from prison in England, to win recognition for the Irish Republic.

  • De Valera was born in New York in 1882 to an Irish mother and a Spanish father, and he was raised in Co. Limerick.
  • He commanded the Volunteer garrison at Boland's Mill in Dublin during the 1916 Rising, and his death sentence was commuted to imprisonment.
  • Sinn Féin won 73 of the 105 Irish seats in the general election of December 1918, and its MPs set up Dáil Éireann in January 1919 and declared an Irish Republic.
  • He was arrested in May 1918 in the round-up that followed the so-called German Plot, and he was held in Lincoln Gaol in England.
  • He escaped from Lincoln Gaol on 3 February 1919, in an escape arranged by Michael Collins and Harry Boland.
  • In April 1919 the Dáil elected him head of its ministry, as Príomh Aire, usually translated as President.
  • An Irish Race Convention in Philadelphia in February 1919 sent three Irish-American delegates, Frank P. Walsh, Edward F. Dunne and Michael J. Ryan, to Paris to ask Wilson to hear Ireland's case; they later visited Ireland.
  • The Dáil had sent envoys to the Paris Peace Conference, but President Woodrow Wilson did not press Britain on Irish self-determination, and the Irish delegates, including Seán T. O'Kelly, were not heard.
  • His aims in America were to win US recognition of the Republic, to raise funds for the Dáil government, to influence the 1920 presidential election, and to bring pressure on Britain.

Case study: the arrival and the tour, 1919

De Valera arrived in America in June 1919 and toured cities with large Irish populations, where he was greeted by crowds, receptions and parades.

  • He left Ireland secretly in May 1919 as a stowaway on a ship from Liverpool, and he arrived in New York in June 1919, where Harry Boland had already arrived to prepare the way.
  • His public appearance in New York later in June 1919 launched a tour of the cities with the largest Irish communities, and he addressed a huge rally at Fenway Park in Boston on 29 June 1919.
  • His meetings in cities such as Boston, Chicago and New York drew large Irish-American audiences, who gave him receptions and donations.
  • A few days before his arrival, on 6 June 1919, the US Senate passed a resolution asking the Peace Conference to hear Irish representatives, which showed sympathy but did not commit the government.
  • Irish-American organisations and parishes arranged his meetings, and local branches raised money and sent subscriptions to Dublin.
  • His message was recognition of the Republic, support for the Dáil, and funds for the struggle in Ireland.
  • The reception was enthusiastic among Irish Americans, while the American government and most of the press treated the mission with caution.
  • British officials objected to his tour, which they regarded as propaganda against British rule in Ireland.
  • The tour continued through 1919 and into 1920, and de Valera also visited Irish communities in western states, where Irish-American organisations arranged his meetings and receptions.

Case study: funds and the Dáil bond drive

The bond drive was meant to raise a large sum for the Dáil government, and it was the most successful part of the mission.

  • The Dáil authorised a loan, and bond certificates in the Irish Republic were sold to Irish Americans in a campaign launched in January 1920, so that supporters could invest in the struggle for independence.
  • The aim was to raise five million dollars, and the drive reached roughly that amount by 1921, though sources differ on the exact total.
  • The drive used the networks of Irish-American organisations, parishes and clubs to reach donors in cities across the United States.
  • Disputes arose over who controlled the money, and Irish-American leaders disagreed with de Valera about how the funds should be spent.
  • The bond drive gave the Dáil government a financial base in the diaspora, which was used alongside the National Loan raised in Ireland by Michael Collins as Minister for Finance.
  • Francis M. Carroll's study of the money raised, Money for Ireland (2002), stresses its importance for the Republic's finances.

Case study: the conflict with Irish-American leaders

De Valera's mission divided Irish America, and his clash with Devoy and Judge Cohalan weakened the unity of the movement.

  • John Devoy and Judge Daniel Cohalan led the Friends of Irish Freedom, which had supported Irish independence since 1916.
  • They quarrelled with de Valera over the control of funds and of the American movement, and the conflict was carried out in the press.
  • Devoy and Cohalan saw de Valera as trying to take control of an organisation they had built, while de Valera wanted an organisation that answered to the Dáil.
  • Harry Boland, who had organised the American side of the mission, backed de Valera, and the two camps traded accusations in the Gaelic American and other papers.
  • The split reduced the unity of Irish America, and de Valera turned to a new organisation of his own.
  • The quarrel showed the difficulty of running a movement from America when the leaders in Ireland had their own priorities.

Case study: the Westminster Gazette interview

De Valera's public statements in early 1920 caused controversy among his own supporters.

  • In February 1920 de Valera gave an interview to the Westminster Gazette in London, in which he suggested that Britain's security fears could be met by treating Ireland as the United States treated Cuba.
  • The comparison with Cuba, which accepted American influence over its foreign policy, caused controversy among republicans at home and abroad, who felt it offered a compromise on full independence.
  • Cohalan and Devoy used the interview to attack him.
  • De Valera himself argued that he was only showing that the Irish demand was reasonable to American opinion.

Case study: the 1920 conventions and the presidential election

In 1920 de Valera tried to win a commitment to recognition from both major American parties, and neither convention gave one.

  • The Republican National Convention met in Chicago in June 1920, and the Democratic National Convention met in San Francisco in June and July 1920.
  • De Valera approached delegates and party leaders at both conventions, seeking a plank recognising the Irish Republic.
  • The Republican platform ignored the request, and the Democrats adopted only a resolution of sympathy with Irish aspirations for self-government.
  • The Republican candidate Warren G. Harding won the presidential election in November 1920, and his administration did not recognise the Republic.
  • Many Irish Americans, angered by the Wilson administration's refusal to back Irish self-determination, voted Republican in November 1920.

Case study: the American Association for the Recognition of the Irish Republic

After the failure of the conventions the campaign was reorganised.

  • The American Association for the Recognition of the Irish Republic (AARIR) was founded in November 1920 as a new organisation for campaigning for recognition, which replaced the Friends of Irish Freedom for de Valera's supporters.
  • The new association grew rapidly and claimed several hundred thousand members, and it organised rallies and lobbied members of Congress.
  • An American Commission on Conditions in Ireland, set up in December 1920, investigated British reprisals in Ireland and increased American sympathy.
  • The death on hunger strike of Terence MacSwiney, Lord Mayor of Cork, on 25 October 1920 received wide American press coverage, and it helped the Irish cause.

Case study: events in Ireland while he was away

While de Valera was in America, the Anglo-Irish War intensified, and the British government passed a Government of Ireland Act that divided the island.

  • The Soloheadbeg ambush on 21 January 1919 marked the start of the Anglo-Irish War, which lasted until 1921.
  • The British government banned the Dáil on 12 September 1919, and the Dáil's ministers went underground.
  • Michael Collins, as Minister for Finance and director of IRA intelligence, ran much of the war in Ireland while de Valera was in America.
  • From March 1920 the British government reinforced the Royal Irish Constabulary with the Black and Tans and, from July, the Auxiliaries, and reprisals against civilians were widely reported in American newspapers.
  • On 21 November 1920, known as Bloody Sunday, IRA gunmen killed British intelligence officers in Dublin, and British forces then fired on a crowd at Croke Park, killing 14.
  • Kevin Barry, an 18-year-old IRA Volunteer, was hanged in Dublin on 1 November 1920, and Auxiliaries burned part of Cork city in December 1920.
  • The Government of Ireland Act, passed in December 1920, created separate parliaments for Northern Ireland and Southern Ireland.
  • De Valera returned to Ireland in December 1920, when the Government of Ireland Act was being passed, and Harry Boland stayed on in America.

Case study: outcomes and significance

The mission secured publicity and money for the Republic, but not the recognition its leaders wanted.

  • No American government recognised the Irish Republic during the mission or in the years that followed.
  • The mission raised money for the Dáil government, which helped to pay for the war and the Dáil's administration.
  • The mission raised the profile of the Irish Republic in American newspapers and at public meetings.
  • The split among Irish-American organisations weakened the Friends of Irish Freedom and led to a new body, the AARIR.
  • De Valera's absence left the Dáil government without its president at a time of war, and relations between him and Collins were strained.
  • The mission was one of the first major efforts by the Irish Republic to win international recognition, and it shows both the possibilities and the limits of diaspora politics.
  • Harry Boland remained in the United States after de Valera left, and the AARIR carried on the campaign into 1921.

Assessment: success or failure in America

  • Achievement: the mission brought the Irish Republic's case to large American audiences and raised about five million dollars for the Dáil government.
  • Limit: the American government never recognised the Republic, and the Irish-American movement was divided by the mission.
  • Contemporary view: supporters called the mission a triumph, while the British government and its allies called it dangerous and divisive.
  • Contemporary view in Ireland: some nationalists argued that the time spent in America would have been better used at home.
  • Later view: historians stress that the mission was a propaganda and fundraising success but a diplomatic failure, because the recognition it sought was never granted.

Key terms

Dáil loan
The bond certificates sold to Irish Americans from 1920 to raise money for the Dáil government and the struggle for independence.
Recognition
The formal acceptance by a foreign government of the Irish Republic as an independent state, which the Dáil sought from the United States.
American Association for the Recognition of the Irish Republic
An American campaigning body founded in November 1920 to press for US recognition of the Irish Republic.
Friends of Irish Freedom
An Irish-American organisation founded in 1916 and led by Judge Daniel Cohalan, which split with de Valera during his American mission.
Dáil Éireann
The assembly of elected representatives that claimed to be the parliament of the Irish Republic from 1919.
Anglo-Irish War
The conflict of 1919 to 1921 between the IRA and British forces, which ran alongside de Valera's American mission.
Government of Ireland Act 1920
The Act passed in December 1920 that created separate parliaments for Northern and Southern Ireland.
Self-determination
The principle, associated with President Wilson, that peoples should choose their own governments; Irish republicans invoked it at Paris and in America.
