Background: the siege of Derry, 1688–1689

The siege of Derry was the defining event for the Apprentice Boys, and its memory has shaped Protestant identity in the city ever since.

  • In December 1688, during the Williamite-Jacobite conflict, thirteen apprentice boys shut Derry's gates against a regiment loyal to the Catholic King James II.
  • Governor Robert Lundy favoured negotiating with James and was distrusted by the defenders; he left the city in April 1689, and his name became a byword for betrayal among Protestants.
  • The siege proper began in April 1689 and lasted 105 days, during which the city's population suffered severe hunger and disease; George Walker and Henry Baker were among the defence leaders.
  • The relief ship Mountjoy broke the boom across the River Foyle on 28 July 1689, bringing supplies to the starving garrison.
  • The Jacobite army withdrew at the end of July and the start of August 1689, and the defence of the city became a central Protestant story of resistance; the Battle of the Boyne followed in 1690.
  • The city walls were built between 1613 and 1619, and they gave Derry its distinctive fortified character.
  • Food shortages in the city were severe, and accounts describe the garrison eating horses and other animals in the last weeks of the siege.
  • The defenders flew a crimson flag from the cathedral tower as a sign of defiance, and the cannon Roaring Meg on the walls became a symbol of the siege.
  • The relief ship Mountjoy was accompanied by the Phoenix, and the pair broke the boom across the Foyle on 28 July 1689.

The founding of the Apprentice Boys, 1814

The Apprentice Boys of Derry was founded in 1814 as a club to commemorate the siege, and its members were drawn largely from Protestant tradesmen and businessmen.

  • The Apprentice Boys of Derry Club was established in 1814 by Protestants in Derry to commemorate the siege, and the No Surrender Club followed in 1824.
  • The organisation was named after the apprentices who shut the gates in 1688, and its members took the apprentices as their symbol.
  • The Memorial Hall in Society Street, opened in 1877 in honour of the thirteen apprentice boys, became the centre of the organisation's activities.
  • The Apprentice Boys spread to other places in Northern Ireland and Britain through branches founded by Derry members, and a governing General Committee was set up in 1861.
  • Membership was open to Protestant men, and the organisation was part of the wider network of Protestant loyal and fraternal bodies.
  • Membership grew through the nineteenth century as the commemorative tradition spread among Protestant tradesmen.
  • The Dublin Apprentice Boys of Derry Society formed in December 1813, shortly before the Derry parent club.

Parades and rituals

The Apprentice Boys' parades and commemorations in August and December marked the siege, and their route along the walls gave them a particular meaning.

  • The Relief of Derry commemoration takes place in August, traditionally on 12 August and now the second Saturday, marking the lifting of the siege.
  • The August parade follows a route around the city walls, and it is the largest Apprentice Boys event in the calendar.
  • The December commemoration of the Shutting of the Gates, now on the first Saturday, includes a service at St Columb's Cathedral and the burning of an effigy of Governor Lundy, a custom said to date from 1832.
  • The parades include bands, banners, the firing of a cannon, the hoisting of crimson flags, wreaths for those who died and speeches, and they draw large crowds of visitors and members.
  • The walls and the Diamond at the centre of the city are the main focus of the parade's route and its symbolism.

Symbolism: siege mentality and 'No Surrender'

The Apprentice Boys' symbols and slogans expressed a sense of threat and loyalty, and critics saw them as triumphalist.

  • The siege mentality, a term used by historians, describes a community's sense of being surrounded and under threat, which the siege story reinforced for unionists.
  • The slogan 'No Surrender' came to express defiance of Catholic and nationalist demands, and it was used at parades and in political rhetoric.
  • For unionists the relief of 1689 represented the defence of Protestant civil and religious liberty against a Catholic monarch.
  • For nationalists the parades and their route past the Bogside symbolised a celebration of military conquest and of the Protestant ascendancy.
  • The term triumphalism, used by critics, describes celebrations that display victory over another community, and it was applied to the Apprentice Boys' parades.
  • The Apprentice Boys' banners and slogans linked the siege to loyalty to the Crown and to the Protestant monarchy of 1689.
  • The phrase 'No Surrender' is also the name of the No Surrender Club of 1824 and has been a unionist slogan since the siege.

Derry and the Bogside before 1969

  • The Bogside lies below the city walls on the west bank of the River Foyle, and it was largely Catholic and working class.
  • Derry had a Catholic majority in its population, but unionists controlled the Londonderry Corporation through gerrymandering.
  • Parades past the Bogside were a source of tension, and the route of the August parade was a recurring point of dispute.
  • Civil rights campaigners in Derry in 1968 and 1969 raised housing and voting grievances, and nationalists saw the Apprentice Boys' parades as part of the city's unionist power structure.
  • Derry's Catholic population was concentrated in the Bogside, the Creggan and the Brandywell.

12 August 1969 and the Battle of the Bogside

The Apprentice Boys' parade of 12 August 1969 passed the Bogside, and the clashes that followed led to the Battle of the Bogside and British army deployment.

  • On 12 August 1969 the Apprentice Boys' parade marched along the walls near the Bogside, and stone-throwing and clashes with the RUC began.
  • The Battle of the Bogside lasted from 12 to 14 August 1969, with residents and police in running confrontation.
  • Residents built barricades and the area became known as 'Free Derry', a symbol of nationalist resistance.
  • British troops were deployed in Derry on 14 August 1969, and the army's presence in the city became a long-term feature of life.
  • The events of August 1969 are seen as a turning point in the Troubles, and the parade is associated with the outbreak of violence; unionists point out that the parade itself was legal and traditional.
  • Nationalists said the parade was a provocation, while the Apprentice Boys said they were holding their traditional march and that disorder was started by others.

Parades, restrictions and restoration in the 1970s

  • After 1969 the authorities restricted or rerouted the Apprentice Boys' parades in Derry in some years of the early 1970s, and the parades resumed as security improved.
  • The Apprentice Boys and their supporters argued that parades were a legitimate expression of their culture and loyalty.
  • The IRA blew up the Walker Pillar, a monument to the siege's governor George Walker, on the city walls in 1973.
  • The organisation negotiated parade routes with the authorities and, later, with residents' groups.
  • Derry's population shifted markedly in these years as many Protestants left the west bank for the Waterside, which reduced the Protestant presence within the walls and sharpened the sense of siege.

Case study: significance for unionists and nationalists

The Apprentice Boys were central to contrasting interpretations of Derry's history, and the parades were a symbol of the wider divide.

  • For unionists the Apprentice Boys' parades celebrate the defence of Protestant liberty, and they keep alive the memory of the siege as a foundation of loyalty.
  • For nationalists the parades are associated with the Protestant ascendancy and with the history of exclusion in the city.
  • The dispute over the parades was part of the wider civil rights agenda in Derry, linking cultural symbols to questions of discrimination and power.
  • Historians see the Apprentice Boys' tradition as an example of how a commemoration of a past event can take on contemporary political meaning.
  • Both communities in Derry developed their own traditions, and the tension between them shaped the city's politics from the 1960s onwards.
  • The dispute over parades was one of the issues raised by the Derry civil rights movement, alongside housing and voting.

Outcomes and significance in Derry

  • The 1969 parade and the Battle of the Bogside brought the civil rights crisis in Derry to a violent climax.
  • The events in Derry in 1969 led to British army deployment and to a sustained period of conflict in the city.
  • The Apprentice Boys retained a central place in Derry's civic and cultural life, and the Memorial Hall remained a focus of commemoration.
  • The disputes over parades and routes contributed to the lasting division between unionist and nationalist interpretations of Derry's history.
  • Major Apprentice Boys events continued to need police and security planning, which kept the parades in the public spotlight.
  • Unionists in the city worried about their place within the walls as Protestant numbers on the west bank fell during the Troubles.

Assessment: tradition or triumphalism

  • Achievement: the Apprentice Boys preserved a commemorative tradition that gave Protestant Derry a shared public identity over more than a century.
  • Limit: the parades' route past the Bogside made them a point of conflict, and they became a symbol of division in the city.
  • Contemporary view: unionists regarded the parades as a legitimate expression of heritage and loyalty, and they resisted restrictions as an attack on their culture.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists saw the parades as triumphalist, and they argued that the route past the Bogside was a provocation.
  • Later view: historians stress that the tradition was both a genuine commemoration and a political statement, and that its meaning changed with the political conflict.

Key terms

Cultural traditions
The distinct heritage, customs and public commemorations of the unionist and nationalist communities, which the Apprentice Boys' parades expressed in Derry.
Cultural identity
A community's sense of belonging through shared history, symbols and commemorations, which the Apprentice Boys' memory of the siege reinforced for many unionists.
Sectarianism
Hostility between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance, which the disputes over the Apprentice Boys' parades reflected.
Tolerance and intolerance
Tolerance is willingness to accept differing identities and commemorations, while intolerance is refusal to accept them, a central issue in disputes over the parades.
Siege mentality
A community's sense of being surrounded and under threat, a term used by historians to describe the unionist attitude reinforced by the siege of Derry.
Triumphalism
Celebration that displays victory over another community, a charge levelled by critics against the Apprentice Boys' parades.
Bogside
The largely Catholic working-class area below the walls of Derry, which was central to the parade disputes and the events of 1969.
Relief of Derry
The lifting of the siege of Derry at the end of July and start of August 1689 after the relief ship Mountjoy broke the boom on the River Foyle, commemorated by the August parade.
Lundy
Governor Robert Lundy, who favoured negotiating with James II in 1689 and whose name became a Protestant byword for betrayal.
Memorial Hall
The Apprentice Boys' headquarters in Society Street, Derry, opened in 1877.
