Background: the 1973 White Paper and the border poll

After the suspension of Stormont, the British government held a border poll and published a plan for a devolved Assembly based on power sharing and an Irish dimension.

  • A border poll held on 8 March 1973 asked voters whether Northern Ireland should remain part of the United Kingdom or join a united Ireland, and nationalists boycotted it.
  • About 98.9 per cent of those who voted chose to stay in the United Kingdom, but turnout was only about 59 per cent because of the boycott.
  • The poll did not settle the constitutional question for nationalists, since the boycott left their view unrecorded.
  • The White Paper of March 1973, Northern Ireland Constitutional Proposals, proposed a new Assembly elected by proportional representation, with an executive in which unionists and nationalists shared power.
  • The White Paper also proposed a Council of Ireland linking Belfast and Dublin, which many unionists regarded as a first step towards a united Ireland.
  • The Northern Ireland Constitution Act of July 1973 abolished the old Stormont parliament and provided for the new Assembly, with the Secretary of State keeping control of security.

The Assembly election and the power-sharing executive, 1973–1974

The Assembly election of June 1973 produced a power-sharing executive that took office on 1 January 1974, led by Brian Faulkner.

  • The Assembly election of 28 June 1973 used the single transferable vote, and it returned unionists, nationalists and Alliance members.
  • The Ulster Unionist Party was split between Faulkner's supporters, who accepted power sharing, and unionists who opposed it, including Paisley's Democratic Unionists and William Craig's Vanguard.
  • Brian Faulkner became Chief Executive of the power-sharing executive, with Gerry Fitt of the SDLP as his Deputy Chief Executive.
  • John Hume of the SDLP became Minister of Commerce, and Oliver Napier of the Alliance Party was Head of Legal Affairs; other SDLP members included Austin Currie (Housing) and Paddy Devlin (Health and Social Services).
  • The executive took office on 1 January 1974, and the Assembly began to operate in the same month.
  • The Assembly had 78 members, and the election gave the SDLP 19 seats and Alliance 8, while anti-agreement unionists took about as many seats as Faulkner's supporters.

The Sunningdale conference, December 1973

The Sunningdale conference of 6 to 9 December 1973 produced an agreement on the Council of Ireland, the status of Northern Ireland and policing.

  • The conference was held at Sunningdale Park in Berkshire from 6 to 9 December 1973, attended by the British Prime Minister Edward Heath and the Taoiseach Liam Cosgrave.
  • The Northern Ireland parties present were Faulkner's Unionists, the SDLP represented by Fitt and Hume, and the Alliance Party.
  • The agreement set up a Council of Ireland with a council of ministers, seven from each side, and a consultative assembly of 60 members drawn equally from the Dáil and the Northern Ireland Assembly.
  • The Irish government accepted that Northern Ireland's status would not change without the consent of a majority there, and the British government stated that it would support a united Ireland if a majority wished it.
  • A Law Enforcement Commission was set up to examine how to deal with fugitive offenders and a possible all-Ireland court, and both governments promised policing acceptable to both communities.
  • The agreement was signed on 9 December 1973, and the British government said it was committed to implementing it through the Assembly.
  • Opponents noted that the Irish government did not formally change Articles 2 and 3 of its constitution at Sunningdale, and the Boland case reinforced that concern.
  • Kevin Boland, a former Fianna Fáil minister, challenged the Irish government's declaration on status in the courts, and the Irish government's defence that the declaration did not alter the constitutional claim damaged Faulkner's position among unionists.

Faulkner's party and the executive in office, January–May 1974

  • The Ulster Unionist Council voted by 427 to 374 on 4 January 1974 to reject the Council of Ireland, and Faulkner resigned as party leader on 7 January 1974.
  • Harry West replaced Faulkner as leader of the Ulster Unionist Party, and Faulkner later formed the Unionist Party of Northern Ireland.
  • The executive's work on housing, health and education was overshadowed by the arguments over the Council of Ireland, which Faulkner tried to postpone.
  • The Conservative government of Edward Heath lost the February 1974 election, and Harold Wilson formed a Labour government in March 1974, with Merlyn Rees as Secretary of State.
  • Harold Wilson, the Prime Minister, said in a televised broadcast on 25 May 1974 that the strikers and their backers were 'sponging' on Westminster and British democracy, a remark that became known as the 'spongers' broadcast and angered unionists.
  • Faulkner resigned as Chief Executive on 28 May 1974 after his proposal to delay the Council of Ireland's later stages was rejected by his SDLP and Alliance colleagues, and the executive collapsed.

Opposition: the UUUC, the Westminster election and the strike

Unionist opponents of Sunningdale united against it, and a general strike in May 1974 brought the executive down.

  • The United Ulster Unionist Council (UUUC) was formed in January 1974 by unionist parties and groups opposed to Sunningdale, including Paisley's supporters and the Vanguard movement.
  • In the Westminster general election of 28 February 1974, the UUUC won eleven of the twelve Northern Ireland seats, and Gerry Fitt kept Belfast West for the SDLP.
  • The Ulster Workers' Council, led by Harry Murray and others and backed by loyalist paramilitaries, called a general strike that began on 15 May 1974, and it cut electricity supplies and paralysed services.
  • Merlyn Rees, the Secretary of State in Harold Wilson's Labour government, and the army did not confront the strikers directly, and the power-sharing executive was unable to govern.
  • The Dublin and Monaghan bombings of 17 May 1974, two days after the strike began, killed 33 people in the Republic; see the notes on terrorism and the Republic's response.
  • Faulkner resigned as Chief Executive on 28 May 1974 after the other unionist members of the executive were unwilling to negotiate with the strikers, and the executive collapsed.
  • The strike drew on workers in power generation and essential industries, and it was enforced by roadblocks and intimidation.
  • The Assembly had voted by 44 to 28 on 14 May 1974 to support the Sunningdale policy, the day before the strike began.

Why Sunningdale collapsed

  • Unionists rejected the Council of Ireland as a step towards a united Ireland, which many believed was the aim of nationalists in the executive.
  • The Assembly's power-sharing arrangements were not supported by a majority of unionists, and the Unionist Party's own members split over the agreement.
  • The Ulster Workers' Council strike showed that a determined protest by loyalist workers could paralyse Northern Ireland's economy and government.
  • The British government's reluctance to use force against the strikers signalled to unionists that London would not defend the executive.
  • Republican violence continued during the executive's term, and it made moderate unionists more fearful about security and nationalist intentions.
  • The executive had no mechanism to build consent among the unionist community, which was essential to any settlement that depended on majority support.
  • Unionist opponents of the agreement argued that it was imposed on the majority, and they used this claim to justify the strike.

Key personality: Brian Faulkner (part 2 and overall evaluation)

Faulkner accepted power sharing at Sunningdale and paid the political price, and his record shows both courage and a misreading of unionist opinion.

  • Faulkner served as Chief Executive from January 1974, after leading the Unionist Party into the Sunningdale settlement.
  • He took part directly in the Sunningdale conference and was the main unionist negotiator in the talks with the British and Irish governments.
  • He was a Unionist who accepted a Council of Ireland, which was a deliberate and costly decision that split his party and cost him the support of many unionists.
  • He resigned on 28 May 1974 when the Ulster Workers' Council strike made the executive impossible to run, and he never returned to office.
  • Contemporaries were divided: loyalist opponents denounced him as a traitor, while nationalists and the British government regarded him as a leader who had taken a serious risk for peace.
  • Historians give him credit for courage and realism, but they argue that he underestimated the depth of unionist opposition and that he lacked consistent British support.
  • He died in 1977 after a fall from his horse in a hunting accident, and he had been made Baron Faulkner of Downpatrick earlier that year.
  • He resigned the leadership of the Ulster Unionist Party in January 1974 and later founded the Unionist Party of Northern Ireland, which failed to attract wide support.

Key personality: John Hume (part 2)

Hume was a key architect of the power-sharing executive and argued that cross-community government was the only realistic path to peace.

  • John Hume served as Minister of Commerce in the power-sharing executive from January 1974, and he was a leading negotiator for the SDLP at Sunningdale.
  • He argued for power sharing and an Irish dimension, and he believed that a Council of Ireland would give nationalists a stake in the settlement.
  • He was a participant in the executive, and he defended it against unionist and republican critics during the strike of May 1974.
  • Contemporaries saw him as one of the most capable nationalist negotiators, although his position was attacked by republicans who rejected any settlement within Northern Ireland.
  • Seamus Mallon, Hume's SDLP colleague, later called the Belfast Agreement of 1998 'Sunningdale for slow learners', and historians often make this connection between the two settlements.
  • Hume continued as an SDLP figure after the executive's collapse and became party leader in 1979; see the later notes on the search for a settlement.

The legacy of Sunningdale until 1993

  • The Constitutional Convention, elected on 1 May 1975, tried to restore devolved government, but it collapsed in March 1976 without agreement.
  • The failure of Sunningdale led Westminster to rely on Direct Rule, with security policy and the administration of the province run from London.
  • The Anglo-Irish Agreement of 1985 returned to the principle that the Irish government should have a formal role, but it did not restore power sharing.
  • Sunningdale became the reference point for later debate on whether any settlement could be imposed without majority unionist consent.

Assessment: Sunningdale as an experiment

  • Achievement: Sunningdale produced the first power-sharing executive in Northern Ireland, with unionists and nationalists in government together.
  • Achievement: the agreement set out principles, including consent and an Irish dimension, that later settlements built on.
  • Failure: the executive lasted only five months, from 1 January to 28 May 1974, and it could not survive a general strike.
  • Failure: the agreement lacked the consent of the majority of unionists, and the Council of Ireland was rejected as a threat to the Union.
  • Contemporary view: the British and Irish governments and the SDLP regarded Sunningdale as a sound basis for peace.
  • Contemporary view: the UUUC and the Ulster Workers' Council regarded it as a betrayal of the Union and a surrender to republicans.
  • Later view: historians see Sunningdale as a failure that showed both the possibility and the difficulty of a settlement that needed unionist consent.
  • Later view: the collapse showed that no executive could survive without the support of a majority of unionists, a lesson applied in the Anglo-Irish Agreement and in later talks.

Key terms

Power sharing
A system of government in which unionists and nationalists share executive power, so that neither community can rule the other.
Council of Ireland
The body proposed at Sunningdale to link Northern Ireland and the Republic through a council of ministers and a consultative assembly.
Ulster Workers' Council strike
The general strike of 15 to 28 May 1974 that paralysed essential services and brought down the power-sharing executive.
UUUC
The United Ulster Unionist Council, formed in January 1974 to oppose the Sunningdale settlement, which won eleven of twelve Northern Ireland seats at the 1974 Westminster election.
Single transferable vote
The system of proportional representation used for the 1973 Assembly election, which allowed voters to rank candidates in order of preference.
Border poll
The referendum of 8 March 1973 on whether Northern Ireland should remain in the United Kingdom, which nationalists boycotted.
Sunningdale Agreement
The agreement of December 1973 between the British and Irish governments and the pro-Assembly parties, setting out power sharing, a Council of Ireland and a guarantee of consent.
Executive
The power-sharing government of January to May 1974, with Brian Faulkner as Chief Executive and Gerry Fitt as Deputy Chief Executive.
Boland case
A legal challenge by Kevin Boland to the Irish government's declaration on Northern Ireland's status at Sunningdale, which unionists saw as proof the claim remained.
