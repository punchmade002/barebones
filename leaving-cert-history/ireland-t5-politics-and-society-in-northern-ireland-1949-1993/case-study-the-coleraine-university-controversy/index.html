Background: higher education in Derry

Derry had a college but not a university, and its nationalist majority saw the choice of a new university site as a test of fair treatment.

  • Magee College in Derry had been founded in 1865 by the Presbyterian Church, taught degrees of Trinity College Dublin, and did not have full university status.
  • The Robbins Report of 1963 recommended a large expansion of higher education across the United Kingdom, and Northern Ireland began planning a second university alongside Queen's University Belfast.
  • Derry's population was mostly Catholic, and the city had higher unemployment and more emigration than the Belfast area.
  • Nationalists in Derry argued that the city, the second largest in Northern Ireland, deserved the new university after decades of neglect, and many Derry unionists also wanted it.
  • The question of location was therefore not only academic: it was a test of whether Stormont treated the west of the province fairly.
  • Derry's grievances over the university were part of the wider 'west of the Bann' complaint about public investment.
  • The new university was planned as a single institution, so choosing one town meant that no other town could host it.

The Lockwood Committee and the University for Derry campaign, 1963–1965

The Lockwood Committee was set up to advise on the site, and a cross-community campaign in Derry pressed for the city to be chosen.

  • The Lockwood Committee, chaired by Sir John Lockwood, was appointed in 1963 to advise on the development of higher education in Northern Ireland, including the siting of the new university.
  • The University for Derry Committee was formed to campaign for the city, and it was chaired by John Hume, then a young teacher.
  • The campaign drew support from across the community, including some Protestant and business figures in Derry, as well as nationalists.
  • The campaign argued that Derry had the population, the existing college buildings and the need for jobs that justified a university.
  • The Lockwood Committee reported in February 1965 and recommended Coleraine rather than Derry.
  • After the report, Derry supporters organised a motorcade to Stormont in February 1965 to protest against the decision.
  • The committee heard evidence from the Derry campaign and from other towns competing for the university.

The Stormont decision, February 1965

The government accepted the Lockwood recommendation, and the decision was carried by the unionist majority in the Stormont parliament.

  • The government accepted the Lockwood recommendation that the new university be sited at Coleraine, County Londonderry, a smaller town about 30 miles from Derry.
  • Derry's representatives argued that the choice had been made on political grounds, and the allegation that 'faceless men' had shaped it became a popular slogan.
  • The Stormont debate and vote produced a unionist majority in favour of Coleraine, while nationalist members and some Derry unionists opposed the decision.
  • The government argued that Coleraine offered a central site, good access and room for expansion, and that Derry's case had been fully considered.
  • The Derry campaign continued after the decision, and the controversy remained a live issue in Derry politics for several years.
  • The decision was widely seen in Derry as a sign that Stormont would not give the city equal treatment in public investment.

Key personality: John Hume (part 1, the University for Derry campaign)

Hume was the chair of the University for Derry campaign and became the nationalist leader who pressed for reform through constitutional politics.

  • John Hume was born in Derry in 1937, trained as a teacher at St Patrick's College, Maynooth, and taught in Derry.
  • He chaired the University for Derry Committee, bringing together nationalists and some unionists behind a single campaign for the city.
  • He was a participant in the campaign, and he led deputations and public protests for the university in 1964 and 1965.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a moderate and effective campaigner who could organise across the community, though some unionists saw him as a nationalist agitator.
  • Historians regard the campaign as an early step in his career, and as a sign of his commitment to constitutional and cross-community politics.
  • The campaign shaped his later politics, which combined social grievances with constitutional nationalism, and led to his later role in the SDLP.
  • Hume was elected to Stormont for Foyle in 1969 and to Westminster for Foyle in 1983.
  • He was also a founder of the credit union movement in Derry and a leader of the Derry Citizens' Action Committee formed after 5 October 1968.

The controversy's timeline, 1963–1968

  • 1963: the Robbins Report prompts the search for a site for a second university in Northern Ireland, and the Lockwood Committee is appointed.
  • 1963 to 1965: the Lockwood Committee investigates possible sites, while the University for Derry Committee campaigns for Derry.
  • February 1965: the Lockwood Committee reports in favour of Coleraine, and Derry supporters travel to Stormont by motorcade.
  • 1965: the Stormont government accepts the recommendation after a debate and a unionist majority vote.
  • 1965 to 1968: planning and building of the new university at Coleraine proceed while the Derry campaign continues.
  • 1968: the New University of Ulster opens at Coleraine, with its first students arriving that autumn.
  • October 1968: the Derry civil rights march of 5 October and the formation of the Derry Citizens' Action Committee link the university issue to housing and voting grievances.

Propaganda, politics and the 'west of the Bann' grievance

Both sides used the controversy to make arguments about fairness, and the decision became a symbol of regional and religious inequality.

  • Derry campaigners used posters, pamphlets and public meetings to present the case for the city, and their message was that Derry had been overlooked.
  • The government and its supporters presented Coleraine as a sound academic and economic choice, and they rejected charges of sectarian motives.
  • Critics linked the decision to other public investment decisions, including the choice of Craigavon, a new city between Lurgan and Portadown, designated in 1965.
  • The 'west of the Bann' grievance held that new industry and public bodies were located east of the Bann, leaving Derry and the west with fewer jobs.
  • The University controversy therefore fed a wider belief that discrimination was built into government decisions, not only into local councils.
  • The controversy helped nationalists argue that they had no fair share of public investment, a view that the civil rights movement later made central.

Other west of the Bann grievances in the mid-1960s

The university decision was one of several decisions of the mid-1960s that Derry nationalists saw as favouring the east.

  • The Benson Report of 1963 recommended closing unprofitable railway lines, and the Derry Road line from Portadown through Omagh to Derry was closed in 1965.
  • The closure left Derry without a direct rail link to Belfast and, nationalists argued, reinforced its isolation.
  • The Matthew Report of 1963 proposed new growth centres around Belfast, and the new city of Craigavon in County Armagh was seen in Derry as drawing investment east.
  • Derry had traditional industries such as shirt-making, which employed mainly women, while male unemployment was high.
  • The Londonderry Corporation boundaries drawn in 1936 gave unionists control of a council in a majority Catholic city, so the city's own representatives could not influence these decisions.
  • Housing in Derry was overcrowded, which in 1968 produced direct action by housing protesters.
  • These grievances together created the sense that the university decision was one in a series of decisions against the west.

Significance for Derry, Hume and the civil rights movement

  • For Derry, the decision confirmed a sense of neglect and gave the city's nationalists a clear example of unfair treatment by Stormont.
  • The university campaign gave Hume experience of cross-community organisation, which he later used in the SDLP and in the power-sharing debates.
  • Derry's grievances over the university fed directly into the demands of the civil rights movement in Derry in 1968 and 1969.
  • The controversy showed that economic and educational investment could become a political issue in its own right.
  • Unionists regarded the decision as a rational choice of site and an example of the government's modernising programme.
  • Historians view the decision as one of several episodes that deepened nationalist distrust of Stormont in the mid-1960s.
  • Magee became part of the University of Ulster formed in 1984, giving Derry a university presence that the campaign had sought.

Assessment: the Coleraine decision and discrimination

  • Strength of the nationalist case: Derry had the larger population, existing college buildings, and high unemployment, which gave a strong economic argument.
  • Strength of the government's case: Coleraine offered a central site and room to grow, and the Lockwood Committee had made its recommendation on those grounds.
  • Limit of the nationalist case: the 'faceless men' allegation was not backed by proof, and the decision followed a formal committee process.
  • Limit of the government's case: the choice of Coleraine, with a small population, weakened the argument that public investment was evenly spread.
  • Contemporary view: Derry nationalists saw the decision as proof of discrimination, while unionists saw it as a planning matter.
  • Later view: historians tend to see the controversy as a symbol of the wider inequality in public investment, rather than as proof of a single plot.
  • Both sides accepted that Derry needed higher education, so the dispute was over the site rather than the principle.

Key terms

Discrimination
Unequal treatment of people because of their religion or background; nationalists saw the siting of the new university away from Derry as an example.
Propaganda
Materials and messages used to shape public opinion, which both the University for Derry campaign and the government used to argue their case.
Lockwood Committee
The committee appointed in 1963 to examine possible sites for a new university in Northern Ireland and to recommend one.
University for Derry
The cross-community campaign of 1964 to 1965 that pressed for the new university to be sited in Derry, chaired by John Hume.
New University of Ulster
The university opened at Coleraine in 1968 as a result of the Lockwood recommendation.
West of the Bann
The nationalist grievance that public investment and new industry were concentrated east of the River Bann, leaving the west and Derry with fewer jobs.
Magee College
The Presbyterian college in Derry, founded in 1865, whose lack of university status was central to the case for a university in the city.
Robbins Report
The 1963 report that recommended expanding higher education across the United Kingdom and prompted Northern Ireland's plan for a second university.
Benson Report
The 1963 transport report that recommended railway closures, including the Derry Road line, closed in 1965.
