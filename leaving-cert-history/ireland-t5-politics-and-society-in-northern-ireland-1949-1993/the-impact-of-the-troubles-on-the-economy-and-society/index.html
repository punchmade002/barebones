Deaths and injuries

The Troubles caused thousands of deaths and injuries, and the violence fell most heavily on the working-class areas of Belfast and Derry.

  • Between 1969 and 1993 the Troubles caused about 3,000 deaths, and the total for the whole conflict to 2001 is about 3,500; figures vary by source.
  • 1972 was the deadliest year of the Troubles, with nearly 500 deaths.
  • About half of the dead were civilians, and members of the security forces, republican paramilitaries and loyalist paramilitaries also died in large numbers.
  • Tens of thousands of people were injured, and hospitals in Belfast developed specialist expertise in treating gunshot and blast injuries.
  • The violence was concentrated in Belfast, Derry and the border counties, and in working-class areas where both communities lived side by side.
  • Most deaths took place in Northern Ireland, though bombings in Britain and the Republic also caused deaths, including the Dublin and Monaghan bombings of 1974.
  • Deaths were concentrated in north and west Belfast, and working-class districts such as Ardoyne, the Falls and the Shankill suffered most.

Loss of investment and tourism

The violence deterred investment and tourism, and the cost of insurance and security rose for businesses across the province.

  • Foreign investors were reluctant to build factories in Northern Ireland because of the risk of bombing and disruption.
  • Tourism fell sharply, as visitors avoided the province, and bomb attacks and the security cordon around central Belfast, the so-called ring of steel, harmed the city's trade.
  • Businesses faced high insurance premiums to cover damage from bombs, and some companies moved their operations to Britain or the Republic.
  • The security measures, including checkpoints and searches, increased the time and cost of moving goods and people.
  • Business closures and relocations put further pressure on local jobs.
  • Compensation for criminal damage was paid under government schemes, which added to public spending.

Decline of manufacturing and growth of public-sector employment

Traditional manufacturing kept shrinking, while the public sector and security jobs grew, and the province became more dependent on the British state.

  • Manufacturing employment continued to decline, with the shipyards, linen and engineering losing jobs through the 1970s and 1980s.
  • Public-sector employment grew, with jobs in health, education, local administration and the security services increasing as a share of the workforce.
  • The Ulster Defence Regiment, the RUC and the prison service provided large numbers of jobs in the security sector.
  • British subvention, the public money transferred from the Treasury, rose and kept the public sector and living standards above what local output alone could support.
  • Dependence on the public sector meant that Northern Ireland's economy was exposed to changes in British government spending.
  • Harland and Wolff was taken into public ownership in 1975 and Short Brothers in 1977, because the government wished to protect jobs.
  • Several man-made fibre plants promoted in the 1960s cut back or closed in the 1970s and 1980s.

The De Lorean episode

The De Lorean factory at Dunmurry, Belfast, was a government-backed investment that collapsed in 1982 and cost the taxpayer heavily.

  • The De Lorean Motor Company built a car factory at Dunmurry, near Belfast, which began production in 1981, with large grants and loans from the British government.
  • The factory was intended to create about 2,000 jobs in an area of high unemployment, and it was sited between Catholic and Protestant districts of west Belfast.
  • The De Lorean car's production was limited and its sales were poor, and the company went into receivership in 1982.
  • John De Lorean was arrested in the United States in October 1982 and charged with drug trafficking, and he was acquitted in 1984.
  • The collapse left the British government with heavy losses, and the episode became a symbol of the difficulty of attracting investment to Northern Ireland.

Unemployment and deprivation

Unemployment stayed well above the UK average, and Catholic unemployment was consistently higher than Protestant unemployment.

  • Unemployment in Northern Ireland was the highest of any UK region for much of the period, and it stayed above the UK average through the 1970s and 1980s.
  • Catholic unemployment was consistently higher than Protestant unemployment, and in some surveys it was roughly twice as high.
  • The Fair Employment (Northern Ireland) Act 1976 set up a Fair Employment Agency to investigate discrimination in employment.
  • The Fair Employment Act of 1989 strengthened the law, created a Fair Employment Commission and required employers to monitor their workforce.
  • Areas of west Belfast and Derry suffered the highest levels of deprivation, with families who had lost jobs and had few alternative sources of income.
  • Unemployment among young Catholic men was especially high in the 1980s, and was a factor in recruitment to paramilitaries.
  • The MacBride Principles of 1984 were a code of fair employment practice promoted by Irish-American campaigners for US firms operating in Northern Ireland.

Housing: the Housing Executive, population movement and peace walls

The Northern Ireland Housing Executive took control of public housing, and the violence accelerated the segregation of Protestant and Catholic communities.

  • The Northern Ireland Housing Executive was established in 1971 to take over public housing from the local councils and the Housing Trust.
  • The Executive introduced allocation procedures based on a points system, intended to end the discrimination that the civil rights movement had criticised.
  • Intimidation drove families to move to areas where their own community was the majority, and an estimated tens of thousands of people left their homes in Belfast between 1969 and 1973.
  • Peace walls, barriers built between Protestant and Catholic streets from 1969 onwards, separated some neighbourhoods in Belfast and later in Derry.
  • Segregation of housing made communities more distinct and it made it harder for people from different backgrounds to meet.
  • Some families moved from mixed areas into segregated estates, despite the Housing Executive's allocation policy.

Education: segregation and integrated schools

Schools remained largely segregated by religion, and a small integrated movement began in the 1970s and 1980s.

  • Most children continued to be educated in schools that were either Catholic or Protestant, and the system reflected the religious divisions of the province.
  • The integrated education movement began in the 1970s, when parents in both communities formed All Children Together in 1974 to campaign for mixed schools.
  • Lagan College, opened in Belfast in 1981 with a small intake, was the first integrated secondary school in Northern Ireland.
  • The Education Reform (Northern Ireland) Order of 1989 gave statutory support to integrated schools and placed a duty on the government to encourage them.
  • Integrated schools were few at first and depended on parental campaigns and charitable funding.
  • Teacher training was also separated, with Catholic teachers trained mainly at St Mary's College and Protestants at Stranmillis College.
  • Catholic voluntary schools continued to get state support, and the Catholic Church defended its right to control schools.

Health: trauma care and mental health

The health service coped with trauma and gunshot injuries, and the violence caused long-term stress that affected the population.

  • The Royal Victoria Hospital in Belfast developed expertise in treating gunshot wounds and blast injuries, skills that many hospitals in Britain had little need for.
  • The health service had to treat large numbers of injured civilians and members of the security forces, and this placed heavy demands on staff.
  • Mental health problems, including stress and trauma, were widespread among people who had lived through bombings, shootings and displacement.
  • The security measures and fear of attack affected daily life, including health care, through delays and restrictions on movement.
  • Ambulance crews and hospital staff faced danger when responding to bomb incidents.
  • The health service was reorganised in 1973 into four Health and Social Services Boards.

Everyday life: security and division

  • Security checkpoints, searches and vehicle checks were a feature of daily life in Belfast and Derry, and they changed the routines of shopping and travel.
  • Some town centres were closed to traffic or sealed off after bombings, and shoppers had to pass through checks to enter them.
  • Divided towns and estates meant that some people avoided areas where the other community lived, which limited social contact.
  • Children grew up with the sound and presence of security forces and with memories of violence in their local areas.
  • Car bombs prompted security checks and car-park controls in city-centre shopping areas.

Assessment: economic and social effects

  • Cost: the violence deterred investment and tourism, and the economy became dependent on public money and the security sector.
  • Cost: segregation of housing and schooling deepened the social divisions between the communities.
  • Achievement: the Fair Employment Acts and the Housing Executive were steps towards equal treatment in jobs and housing.
  • Limit: unemployment and deprivation remained high, and the reforms did not close the gap between Catholics and Protestants.
  • Contemporary view: unionists pointed to the public investment and the British subvention as evidence that the state supported the province.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists argued that discrimination and deprivation were rooted in the state's policies and that the Troubles had made them worse.
  • Later view: historians stress that the Troubles were both a cause and a consequence of economic and social inequality, and that the economic costs were heavy for both communities.

Key terms

Subvention
The public money transferred from the British Treasury to Northern Ireland to fund services and support living standards beyond what local output could pay for.
Northern Ireland Housing Executive
The public body established in 1971 to take over public housing from the local councils and allocate housing.
Peace walls
Barriers built from 1969 onwards between Protestant and Catholic streets in Belfast to reduce violence between neighbourhoods.
Fair employment
Legislation and agencies introduced from 1976 to investigate and prevent discrimination in employment on religious grounds.
Integrated education
Schooling in which Protestant and Catholic children are taught together, promoted from the 1970s as an alternative to segregated schools.
Intimidation
Threats and violence used to force people to leave their homes or areas, which drove population movement and housing segregation.
Public-sector employment
Jobs in government services such as health, education, administration and security, which grew as a share of the workforce.
Deprivation
Lack of income, jobs, housing and services, which was most severe in the working-class areas most affected by the Troubles.
MacBride Principles
A 1984 code of fair employment practice promoted by Irish-American campaigners for US companies operating in Northern Ireland.
Lagan College
The first integrated secondary school in Northern Ireland, opened in Belfast in 1981.
De Lorean
The government-funded car factory at Dunmurry that opened in 1981 and collapsed into receivership in 1982.
