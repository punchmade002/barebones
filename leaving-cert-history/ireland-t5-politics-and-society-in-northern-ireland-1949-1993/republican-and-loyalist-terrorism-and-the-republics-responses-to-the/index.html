The Provisional IRA campaign: aims and main attacks

The Provisional IRA aimed to force British withdrawal and a united Ireland, and its campaign included bombings in Northern Ireland and Britain.

  • The Provisional IRA's aim was to end British rule in Northern Ireland and to bring about a united Ireland through a campaign of armed attacks.
  • Its attacks targeted British soldiers, members of the RUC and UDR, and civilians in bombings, shootings and car bombs.
  • Bloody Friday on 21 July 1972 saw more than twenty bombs in Belfast city centre, killing nine people.
  • The Birmingham pub bombings of 21 November 1974 killed 21 people; they were attributed to the Provisional IRA, which never claimed them, and the Birmingham Six convicted of them were freed in 1991 when their convictions were quashed.
  • The Guildford pub bombings of October 1974 killed five people and were attributed to the Provisional IRA; the Guildford Four convicted of them were freed in 1989.
  • Lord Mountbatten was killed with three others by an IRA bomb on his boat off Mullaghmore, County Sligo, on 27 August 1979, the same day that eighteen British soldiers were killed in an ambush at Warrenpoint, County Down.
  • The Brighton bombing of 12 October 1984 killed five people and targeted the Conservative government.
  • The Enniskillen bombing of 8 November 1987 killed eleven people at a Remembrance Day ceremony.
  • The Warrington bombing of 20 March 1993 killed two boys, Johnathan Ball and Tim Parry, and injured many more.
  • The IRA bombed Hyde Park and Regent's Park in London on 20 July 1982, killing eleven soldiers and bandsmen, and Harrods in December 1983, killing six people.
  • Republican attacks on Protestants in Northern Ireland included the La Mon House bombing of 1978, which killed twelve people, and Teebane in January 1992, where eight Protestant workmen were killed.
  • The Irish National Liberation Army, formed in 1975, killed 17 people in the Droppin' Well bar bombing in Ballykelly in December 1982, 11 of them soldiers.

Casualties and responsibility

Responsibility for deaths during the Troubles was spread across republican and loyalist paramilitaries and the security forces.

  • About 3,500 people were killed between 1969 and 2001, and about half of them were civilians.
  • Republican paramilitaries were responsible for about 60 per cent of the deaths, loyalist paramilitaries for about 30 per cent and the security forces for about 10 per cent.
  • The Provisional IRA was responsible for the largest number of killings, followed by loyalists of the UDA and UVF.
  • The worst year was 1972, with nearly 500 deaths, and the number of deaths fell sharply in the 1980s.
  • In 1992 to 1994 loyalist groups killed more people than republican groups, many in sectarian attacks on Catholics.
  • On 5 February 1992 the UFF killed five Catholic civilians at Sean Graham's bookmakers on the Ormeau Road, Belfast.
  • The Miami Showband massacre of 31 July 1975 saw UVF members kill three members of a popular band at a fake checkpoint in County Down.
  • On 23 October 1993 an IRA bomb in a fish shop on the Shankill Road killed ten people, including one of the bombers, and loyalists responded a week later at Greysteel.
  • The Kingsmill massacre of January 1976 saw ten Protestant workmen shot dead in South Armagh by republicans, who claimed it as a response to earlier loyalist killings of Catholics.

Security force operations and republican losses

  • The Loughgall ambush of 8 May 1987 saw the SAS kill eight IRA members and a civilian passer-by when the IRA attacked a police station, and it became central to allegations of a 'shoot to kill' policy.
  • The Gibraltar shootings of 6 March 1988 saw the SAS kill three unarmed IRA members who were planning an attack, and the incident raised questions about the use of lethal force by the security forces.
  • The British army and RUC carried out searches, arrests and surveillance operations that disrupted IRA units, but the IRA continued to recruit and attack.
  • Republican prisoners in the 1970s and 1980s were held in special category and later in the H-Blocks, where their protests kept the issue of political status in the public eye.
  • Republicans argued that the army's arrests and searches in Catholic areas increased recruitment to the IRA.
  • The Stalker and Sampson inquiries of 1984 to 1986 investigated allegations that the RUC had shot unarmed republicans in 1982, and the claims fed the 'shoot to kill' controversy.
  • A cargo of arms from Libya on the ship Eksund was seized by French customs in November 1987, showing how the IRA was supplied for its campaign.

Loyalist paramilitaries: UVF, UDA and UFF

Loyalist paramilitaries opposed British withdrawal and the power-sharing settlements, and they attacked Catholic civilians as well as republicans.

  • The Ulster Volunteer Force was revived in 1966 and carried out attacks in the early years of the Troubles, including bombings and shootings.
  • The Ulster Defence Association was formed in 1971 as an umbrella for local loyalist groups, and its killing wing, the Ulster Freedom Fighters, was formed in 1973.
  • The bombing of McGurk's Bar in Belfast on 4 December 1971 killed 15 Catholic civilians; police first blamed the IRA, but the Police Ombudsman concluded in 2011 that the UVF was responsible.
  • The UVF carried out the bombings in Dublin and Monaghan on 17 May 1974, which killed 33 people and an unborn child and are the largest loss of life in a single day of the Troubles; claims of security force collusion were never proven.
  • The Greysteel massacre of 30 October 1993 saw the Ulster Freedom Fighters kill eight people at a Halloween dance in the Rising Sun bar, County Londonderry, a week after the Shankill bomb.
  • Loyalist paramilitaries claimed to be defending the Union, but most of their victims were Catholic civilians with no paramilitary links.
  • UVF and UDA gunmen carried out attacks on Catholic bars and homes in Belfast during the 1970s, and attacks on Catholic workers on the roads.

Sectarian killing: the Shankill Butchers

Some loyalist groups carried out sectarian murders of Catholics that were marked by torture and brutality, and the Shankill Butchers were the most notorious.

  • The Shankill Butchers were a UVF gang based on the Shankill Road in west Belfast, who abducted and killed Catholics at night between 1975 and 1977.
  • The gang was led by Lenny Murphy, and eleven members were convicted in 1979 of a series of murders, receiving many life sentences.
  • Many victims were killed with knives and hatchets, which led to widespread revulsion in both communities.
  • The gang's activities showed how sectarian violence could develop outside the formal paramilitary chains of command, and Murphy himself was shot dead in 1982.

Propaganda and the battle for public opinion

Both republicans and loyalists used murals, publications and political statements to shape public opinion, and the British government sought to restrict broadcasts.

  • Republican murals in the Falls and Bogside celebrated IRA volunteers and the hunger strikers, and they became a central feature of the political landscape.
  • Loyalist murals in Protestant areas celebrated the UVF and UDA and presented their violence as a defence of loyal communities.
  • Republicans used publications such as An Phoblacht to present their case, and their statements were sought by international media.
  • In October 1988 the British government banned the broadcast of the voices of spokesmen for Sinn Féin and ten other organisations, including loyalist groups, and broadcasters used actors' voices; the ban was lifted in 1994.
  • The ban was intended to deny publicity to terrorist groups, and critics said it restricted freedom of expression.
  • Irish-American support for republicans provided funds and publicity for the republican campaign.
  • In the Republic, Section 31 of the Broadcasting Authority Act barred broadcasters from interviewing spokesmen for the IRA and, from 1977, Sinn Féin, and it was allowed to lapse in 1994.
  • The republican movement published An Phoblacht, and its propaganda stressed the British occupation and the hunger strikers as martyrs.

The Republic's response, 1969–1972

The Republic's government responded to the violence of August 1969 with a broadcast, emergency aid and a border presence, but the Arms Crisis of 1970 divided the Fianna Fáil government.

  • On 13 August 1969 the Taoiseach Jack Lynch broadcast that the Irish government 'can no longer stand by' while Catholics were attacked in Belfast and Derry, and he called for a United Nations peacekeeping force.
  • The Irish army set up field hospitals at the border and the government provided relief for refugees fleeing Belfast and Derry, with camps at Gormanston and elsewhere.
  • The Arms Crisis of May 1970 followed allegations that ministers had planned to import arms for Northern nationalists using money voted for relief, and Lynch dismissed Charles Haughey and Neil Blaney from the government on 6 May 1970.
  • Haughey, Captain James Kelly, John Kelly and Albert Luykx were tried for conspiracy to import arms and acquitted in October 1970.
  • After Bloody Sunday the Irish government declared a day of mourning and recalled its ambassador from London, and the British embassy in Dublin was burned on 2 February 1972 by a crowd, which the government condemned.

The Republic's security laws, 1972–1976

  • The IRA was declared an unlawful organisation in the Republic in 1936, and membership remained illegal under the Offences against the State Act 1939.
  • The Offences against the State (Amendment) Act 1972 allowed a Garda chief superintendent's belief to be evidence of IRA membership, and it was passed in December 1972 after bombs in Dublin.
  • The Special Criminal Court was reactivated in 1972 to try offences connected with paramilitary violence without a jury.
  • The Republic's government under Liam Cosgrave supported the Sunningdale settlement and its Council of Ireland, which was intended to show Dublin's commitment to cross-border cooperation.
  • Garda cooperation with the RUC increased, but extradition from the Republic of people wanted in Northern Ireland remained a point of tension between Dublin and London.
  • The Criminal Law (Jurisdiction) Act 1976 allowed some cross-border offences to be tried in the Republic.
  • The Emergency Powers Act 1976 followed the IRA killing of the British ambassador, Christopher Ewart-Biggs, and led President Cearbhall Ó Dálaigh to refer the bill to the Supreme Court and then resign.
  • The Extradition (European Convention on the Suppression of Terrorism) Act 1987 was passed after the Enniskillen bombing.

The Republic and the Anglo-Irish process, 1980s

  • Garret FitzGerald, Taoiseach from 1981 to 1982 and from 1982 to 1987, pursued the New Ireland Forum and signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement with Margaret Thatcher in 1985.
  • Charles Haughey, Taoiseach from 1987 to 1992, returned Fianna Fáil to power and kept the Anglo-Irish Agreement's framework in place.
  • Unionists complained that the Republic had too much influence through the Agreement's Intergovernmental Conference.
  • Extradition and cross-border security cooperation were recurring issues, and the Anglo-Irish Agreement put security cooperation on a formal footing.
  • Haughey and Thatcher held a summit in Dublin in December 1980 that agreed to study the 'totality of relationships' between the two islands.
  • Fianna Fáil under Haughey opposed the Anglo-Irish Agreement in the Dáil in 1985, but kept its framework when he returned to government in 1987.

Assessment: the impact of terrorism

  • Impact: terrorism killed large numbers of civilians and security force members, and it made political compromise harder by hardening attitudes on all sides.
  • Impact: republican and loyalist campaigns shaped British policy, with security measures and political initiatives both shaped by the need to contain violence.
  • Failure: neither the IRA nor the loyalist groups achieved their stated aims, and the main ceasefires came in 1994, after the period covered here.
  • Failure: terrorism deepened sectarian divisions, and its victims in both communities created lasting grievances.
  • Contemporary view: the British and Irish governments condemned terrorism and sought to isolate paramilitaries from political influence.
  • Contemporary view: republicans argued that the IRA was defending the Catholic community and fighting an occupying power, while loyalists claimed to defend the Union.
  • Later view: historians point out that most of the killing was done by paramilitary groups, not the state, and that the long campaign persuaded many in each group that violence could not win.

Key terms

Terrorism
The use of violence against civilians and the state to achieve political aims, which both republican and loyalist paramilitaries used between 1969 and 1993.
Sectarianism
Hostility and violence between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance, shown most clearly in loyalist sectarian murders.
Propaganda
The use of murals, publications, broadcasts and statements to shape public opinion, and in the 1988 case the restriction of broadcast voices.
Provisional IRA
The republican paramilitary organisation formed in December 1969 that carried out the main armed campaign against British rule in Northern Ireland, responsible for the largest number of Troubles deaths.
Ulster Volunteer Force
The loyalist paramilitary organisation revived in 1966, responsible for many attacks on Catholic civilians including the 1974 Dublin and Monaghan bombings.
Arms Crisis
The May 1970 affair in which ministers in the Irish government were accused of approving arms imports for Northern nationalists, leading to the dismissal of Charles Haughey and Neil Blaney.
Special Criminal Court
A court in the Republic, re-established in 1972, that tried offences linked to paramilitary violence without a jury.
Shoot to kill
The contested allegation that British security forces deliberately killed suspects rather than arresting them, which became an issue after incidents such as Loughgall in 1987.
Section 31
The provision in Irish broadcasting law that barred broadcasters from interviewing spokesmen for the IRA and later Sinn Féin, allowed to lapse in 1994.
Ulster Freedom Fighters
The cover name used by the UDA for sectarian killings from 1973.
Irish National Liberation Army
A republican paramilitary group formed in 1975, responsible for the Ballykelly bombing of 1982.
