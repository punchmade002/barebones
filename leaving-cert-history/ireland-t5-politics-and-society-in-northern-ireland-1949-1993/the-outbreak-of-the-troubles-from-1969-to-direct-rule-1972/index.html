August 1969: Derry and Belfast erupt, and the police reforms that followed

The Apprentice Boys' parade in Derry in August 1969 led to street fighting, and British troops were sent in to restore order.

  • On 12 August 1969 the Apprentice Boys' parade passed close to the Bogside, and stone-throwing and clashes with the RUC began.
  • The Battle of the Bogside lasted from 12 to 14 August 1969, and residents erected barricades that became the 'Free Derry' area.
  • British troops were deployed in Derry on 14 August 1969, the start of the army's long involvement in internal security.
  • Rioting in Belfast on 14 and 15 August 1969 saw Catholic streets such as Bombay Street burned and several people killed, most of them Catholics.
  • Thousands of families, most of them Catholic, fled their homes in Belfast, and refugee centres were set up in the Republic and in Catholic parishes.
  • The events of August 1969 are often treated as the start of the Troubles in their most violent form.
  • The Hunt Report, published on 10 October 1969 by a committee led by Lord Hunt, recommended a reformed, unarmed RUC under a police authority.
  • It recommended that the B Specials be disbanded, and the Ulster Special Constabulary was stood down in April 1970.
  • The Ulster Defence Regiment, formed in 1970 as a part-time regiment of the British army, replaced the B Specials in local security duties.
  • After the August rioting the British government issued the Downing Street Declaration of 19 August 1969, which reaffirmed that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of its people, and promised reforms.

Chichester-Clark's government, 1969–1971

Chichester-Clark's two years as Prime Minister saw reform under pressure from London and a rising tide of violence.

  • James Chichester-Clark became Prime Minister on 1 May 1969, defeating Brian Faulkner in the party vote by 17 to 16.
  • He presided over the Battle of the Bogside and the disbanding of the B Specials, and was pressed by London to carry out the reforms of the Hunt Report.
  • His government announced a points system for housing allocation, the creation of a Housing Executive (established in 1971) and reform of local government.
  • Unionist hardliners saw these steps as surrender, while nationalists said they came too late.
  • He resigned on 20 March 1971 after the British government refused to send more troops and reinforce the security policy he wanted.
  • Brian Faulkner succeeded him as Prime Minister on 23 March 1971.

The Provisional IRA emerges, 1969–1970

The split in the IRA at the end of 1969 created the Provisional IRA, which argued that the Catholic community needed armed defence.

  • The IRA split in December 1969 into the Provisional IRA, led by figures such as Seán Mac Stíofáin, and the Official IRA, led by Cathal Goulding.
  • The Provisionals argued that the IRA must defend Catholic areas and that British forces should be driven out, a position that gained support after August 1969.
  • Provisional Sinn Féin was set up in January 1970 to match the new military wing.
  • The Falls Road curfew of 3 to 5 July 1970 saw troops search houses for weapons, and four civilians were killed during the operation.
  • The Social Democratic and Labour Party was founded on 21 August 1970 by nationalist politicians including Gerry Fitt, John Hume, Austin Currie and Paddy Devlin, with Fitt as leader.
  • The Alliance Party was founded in April 1970 as a cross-community party, and it later chose Oliver Napier as leader.
  • The Official IRA, which stressed a left-wing political strategy, declared a ceasefire in May 1972, and the Provisionals became the main republican force.
  • The SDLP withdrew from Stormont in July 1971 after the army shot dead two men in Derry, Séamus Cusack and Desmond Beattie.

Loyalist paramilitaries emerge, 1966–1972

Loyalist paramilitary groups grew in response to the republican campaign, and they attacked Catholic civilians and republicans.

  • The Ulster Volunteer Force was revived in 1966, and in the early years of the Troubles it carried out attacks on Catholic targets.
  • The Ulster Volunteer Force was revived in 1966 and murdered three people that year, and in the early years of the Troubles it carried out attacks on Catholic targets.
  • The Ulster Defence Association, a loose umbrella of loyalist groups, was formed in 1971 and grew rapidly in working-class Protestant areas; it was a legal organisation until 1992.
  • The bombing of McGurk's Bar in Belfast on 4 December 1971 killed 15 Catholic civilians; the police first blamed the IRA, but the Police Ombudsman concluded in 2011 that the Ulster Volunteer Force was responsible.
  • Loyalist groups claimed that their violence defended the Union and Protestant areas, but most of their victims were Catholic civilians.
  • William Craig founded the Ulster Vanguard movement in 1972, which held large rallies against the loss of Stormont's powers.
  • Loyalist paramilitaries killed hundreds of people between 1969 and 1993, the great majority of them civilians, mostly Catholics.

Key personality: Brian Faulkner (part 1, as Prime Minister)

Faulkner was the Prime Minister who introduced internment and then saw the government collapse under the pressure of violence and British policy.

  • Brian Faulkner, born in 1921, had been Minister of Home Affairs in 1959–63 and Minister of Commerce under O'Neill until he resigned in 1969; he became Prime Minister in March 1971.
  • He succeeded James Chichester-Clark on 23 March 1971, winning a party vote against William Craig.
  • He asked London to authorise internment, which began on 9 August 1971, and he defended it as necessary to stop the IRA.
  • He participated in the talks with Westminster on the control of security, and he insisted that Stormont should keep its powers over policing and order.
  • Contemporary unionists praised his firmness on security, while nationalists saw internment as a deliberate attack on the Catholic community.
  • He resigned on 24 March 1972 when Westminster took over security, and historians generally see him as a pragmatic, able politician whose attempts at reform were swamped by violence he could not contain.
  • He offered the opposition committee chairmanships at Stormont in 1971, and the SDLP rejected the offer and left Stormont after the July 1971 shootings in Derry.

Internment and the Ballymurphy killings, August 1971

Internment was introduced on 9 August 1971 and was applied mainly to republicans, and the violence that followed killed civilians.

  • Internment, the detention of suspects without trial, began on 9 August 1971 in Operation Demetrius, which arrested about 340 people in its first sweep.
  • Those arrested were almost entirely Catholic, and many had no paramilitary links; no loyalist was interned until 1973.
  • Interrogation methods known as the five techniques, which included hooding and sleep deprivation, were used on fourteen internees, known as the Hooded Men.
  • The Compton and Parker inquiries examined the methods, and in 1978 the European Court of Human Rights found that the techniques were inhuman and degrading, but not torture.
  • At Ballymurphy in west Belfast, eleven civilians died during army operations of 9 to 11 August 1971, including a priest, Father Hugh Mullan; a coroner's inquest in 2021 found that those killed were innocent.
  • Nationalists saw internment as proof of bias, and it increased recruitment to the Provisional IRA; 1971 was the first year in which Troubles deaths passed 100.

Bloody Sunday, 30 January 1972

A civil rights march in Derry against internment ended with British paratroopers killing thirteen civilians, an event that deepened the conflict.

  • The NICRA march of 30 January 1972 in Derry protested against internment and was banned by the authorities.
  • Soldiers of the 1st Battalion, Parachute Regiment, entered the Bogside, and they fired on marchers and bystanders, killing thirteen people on the day.
  • A fourteenth man who was wounded died some months later, and most of the dead were shot while unarmed or while fleeing.
  • The Widgery Report of 19 April 1972, by the Lord Chief Justice, largely accepted the army's account, and nationalists widely rejected it.
  • The Saville Inquiry, reporting in June 2010, found that the soldiers' firing was unjustified and unjustifiable and that none of the casualties had posed a threat; the Prime Minister, David Cameron, apologised.
  • The killings led to the burning of the British embassy in Dublin on 2 February 1972, and they increased recruitment to the Provisional IRA.

Stormont suspended and Direct Rule, March 1972

Stormont was suspended and government was taken over by Westminster after the British government refused to hand security powers to Stormont.

  • Faulkner demanded control of security from Westminster, but the British government under Edward Heath refused.
  • Faulkner and his ministers resigned on 24 March 1972 in protest, and Westminster suspended the Stormont parliament from 30 March.
  • The Northern Ireland (Temporary Provisions) Act 1972 introduced Direct Rule from 30 March 1972, placing the province under a Secretary of State.
  • William Whitelaw was the first Secretary of State for Northern Ireland and governed with civil servants, two ministers of state and the army.
  • Direct Rule was meant to be temporary, but apart from the brief power-sharing executive of 1974 it lasted throughout the period to 1993, because the parties could not agree a power-sharing settlement acceptable to both communities.
  • The Stormont parliament was formally abolished in 1973 by the Northern Ireland Constitution Act.
  • Whitelaw granted special category status to paramilitary prisoners in June 1972, and he met leaders of the Provisional IRA in London on 7 July 1972 during a short ceasefire, talks which failed.
  • Direct Rule lasted because unionists would not accept an assembly with nationalist participation, nationalists would not accept a return to majority rule, and London wished to avoid a return to the old system.

Bloody Friday and Operation Motorman, July 1972

The IRA bombings of 21 July 1972 killed nine people in Belfast, and the army's Operation Motorman broke the IRA's no-go areas.

  • On 21 July 1972, the Provisional IRA set off about twenty bombs in Belfast in the space of about an hour and a half, a day that became known as Bloody Friday.
  • Nine people died in the bombings, and about 130 were injured, and the attacks shocked public opinion in Britain and Northern Ireland.
  • Operation Motorman, launched on 31 July 1972 with more than 12,000 soldiers, moved into the no-go areas in Belfast and Derry, including the Creggan and the Bogside.
  • The operation dismantled the barricades and restored government control over areas that the IRA had controlled.
  • The IRA's campaign continued after Motorman, and 1972 was the deadliest year of the Troubles, with nearly 500 deaths.
  • By the end of 1972 about 700 people had died in the conflict since 1969.

Why the Troubles erupted and lasted

  • Discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting gave the civil rights movement a sustained base of grievance.
  • The state's security forces, the B Specials and the RUC, were seen by Catholics as partisan, which turned protest into a security issue.
  • The Provisional IRA gave the Catholic community a military organisation, and its campaign drew the British army into the conflict.
  • Loyalist paramilitaries defended Protestant areas and attacked Catholic civilians, which increased the cycle of reprisal.
  • Internment and Bloody Sunday hardened attitudes on both sides and made political compromise harder.
  • Stormont's inability to share power with nationalists meant there was no political institution capable of containing the violence.
  • The decline of Stormont had many causes: civil rights protest, the failure to reform fast enough, the IRA campaign, and the British government's decision to take over security.

Assessment: Direct Rule and the failure of Stormont

  • Achievement: the end of Stormont removed an institution that nationalists had rejected since 1921, and it opened the way for new political settlements.
  • Failure: the Stormont government could not contain the violence, and its reliance on internment alienated the Catholic community.
  • Contemporary view: unionists saw the suspension of Stormont as a betrayal by Westminster, while nationalists welcomed it as the end of an unfair system.
  • Later view: historians argue that the 1972 settlement was forced by violence, and that the Unionist government's decline was a product of its own policies as well as republican action.
  • Contemporary view: the British government saw Direct Rule as a temporary measure, but it proved to be a long-term arrangement.

Key terms

Terrorism
The use of violence against civilians and the state to achieve political aims, used by both republican and loyalist paramilitaries in this period.
Sectarianism
Hostility and violence between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance, which intensified during the early Troubles.
Direct Rule
The government of Northern Ireland by Westminster through a Secretary of State, introduced in March 1972 after Stormont was suspended.
Internment
The detention of suspects without trial, introduced in Northern Ireland on 9 August 1971 and applied mainly to republicans.
Provisional IRA
The republican paramilitary organisation that split from the IRA in December 1969 and conducted the main republican campaign of the Troubles.
Bloody Sunday
The killing of thirteen civilians by British paratroopers during a civil rights march in Derry on 30 January 1972.
Operation Motorman
The British army operation of 31 July 1972 that removed the IRA's barricades and no-go areas in Belfast and Derry.
Ulster Defence Regiment
The part-time army regiment formed in 1970 to replace the B Specials as the local security force.
Official IRA
The wing of the IRA that stayed with Cathal Goulding after the 1969 split, favouring a political and socialist strategy, and which declared a ceasefire in May 1972.
SDLP
The Social Democratic and Labour Party, founded in August 1970 by Gerry Fitt, John Hume and others as a constitutional nationalist party.
Ulster Defence Association
A loyalist paramilitary umbrella formed in 1971, legal until 1992.
B Specials
The part-time Ulster Special Constabulary reserve, mostly Protestant, disbanded in 1970 on the recommendation of the Hunt Report.
