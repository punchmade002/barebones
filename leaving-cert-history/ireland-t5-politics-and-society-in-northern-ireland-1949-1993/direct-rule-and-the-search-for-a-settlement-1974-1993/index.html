Direct Rule, the Constitutional Convention and the Peace People

Direct Rule continued after 1974 except for the brief power-sharing executive, and the Peace People brought a popular peace movement onto the streets in 1976.

  • Direct Rule was restored after the collapse of the executive in 1974, with the Secretary of State and the Northern Ireland Office responsible for government.
  • The Secretaries of State included Merlyn Rees (1974 to 1976), Roy Mason (1976 to 1979), Humphrey Atkins (1979 to 1981), Jim Prior (1981 to 1984), Douglas Hurd (1984 to 1985), Tom King (1985 to 1989), Peter Brooke (1989 to 1992) and Patrick Mayhew (1992 to 1997).
  • The Constitutional Convention, elected on 1 May 1975, was meant to find a devolved settlement; its unionist majority recommended a return to majority rule, Westminster rejected this for lacking cross-community support, and it was dissolved in March 1976.
  • Under 'Ulsterisation' from 1976, the security role of the British army was reduced in favour of the Royal Ulster Constabulary and the Ulster Defence Regiment.
  • On 10 August 1976 three children of Anne Maguire were killed in Belfast when a car driven by a Provisional IRA volunteer, Danny Lennon, who had been shot by soldiers, crashed into them.
  • Betty Williams, a Belfast woman who witnessed the crash, and Mairead Corrigan, the children's aunt, led the first peace march, with the journalist Ciaran McKeown as a third founder.
  • The movement organised large peace rallies in Belfast and across Northern Ireland, and it appealed to both communities to end the violence.
  • The Peace People were a movement of ordinary people, and they called for an end to paramilitary violence on all sides.
  • Williams and Corrigan were awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for 1976, which was announced in 1977.
  • Contemporaries praised the movement, but some republicans and loyalists dismissed it as naive, and its support faded after 1977 as the violence continued.
  • Criminalisation was the policy of treating paramilitary offenders as ordinary criminals: special category status for prisoners was ended on 1 March 1976, and Diplock courts, trials without a jury, had been introduced in 1973.

The H-Blocks, hunger strikes and Sinn Féin's electoral turn, 1976–1983

The prison protests over political status at the Maze prison, known as the H-Blocks, led to the 1981 hunger strikes, which made Bobby Sands a symbol across Ireland.

  • After special category status ended on 1 March 1976, republican prisoners refused to wear prison uniform, beginning the blanket protest, and in 1978 the protest escalated into the dirty protest.
  • Bobby Sands began a hunger strike on 1 March 1981 to demand the restoration of political status, and he died on 5 May 1981 after 66 days.
  • Sands was elected MP for Fermanagh and South Tyrone in April 1981, while he was on hunger strike, and his election gave the protest wide publicity.
  • Nine further hunger strikers died between May and August 1981, including Francis Hughes, Patsy O'Hara and Kieran Doherty, making ten in all.
  • The hunger strike ended on 3 October 1981 without political status, although concessions were later made on clothing and remission.
  • Sinn Féin adopted an electoral strategy in 1981, summed up in the phrase 'the armalite and the ballot box', used by Danny Morrison at the party's October 1981 Ard Fheis.
  • Owen Carron, Sands's election agent, won the Fermanagh and South Tyrone by-election in August 1981 as an Anti H-Block candidate.
  • Sinn Féin won five seats in the October 1982 Northern Ireland Assembly election, and Gerry Adams was among those elected, though the members abstained.
  • In June 1983 Gerry Adams won the West Belfast seat at Westminster, which Gerry Fitt had held since 1966, and Sinn Féin members refused to take their seats there.
  • The strategy was a deliberate move to build political support, and critics saw it as a cover for continued IRA violence.
  • Margaret Thatcher declared that 'crime is crime is crime; it is not political', and her government refused to concede political status.
  • Prisoners had begun an earlier hunger strike in October 1980, which ended on 18 December 1980, and two prisoners were elected to the Dáil in June 1981, including Kieran Doherty.

Key personality: Gerry Adams

Adams was the Sinn Féin leader who turned the party into an electoral force, and he became the main republican interlocutor with the British and Irish governments.

  • Gerry Adams was born in 1948 in Belfast and grew up in a republican family in West Belfast.
  • He was interned in the early 1970s and in July 1972 was released to take part in the secret talks between the Provisional IRA and William Whitelaw in London.
  • He was elected president of Sinn Féin in November 1983, a post he held until 2018, and he led the party's move into electoral politics.
  • He was a participant in the republican movement's talks with the SDLP's John Hume from 1988 and again from 1991 to 1993.
  • Contemporaries saw him as the political face of the republican movement; in October 1988 the Home Secretary, Douglas Hurd, banned the broadcast of the voices of spokespeople for Sinn Féin and other groups, including Adams, and broadcasters used actors' voices; the ban ended in 1994.
  • Supporters credit him with leading republicans from armed struggle towards negotiation; critics, including unionists, argued that he used Sinn Féin's electoral strategy to complement the IRA campaign.
  • Adams has always denied membership of the IRA, though many historians and security sources identify him as a senior republican figure.
  • He was shot and wounded by loyalist gunmen in Belfast in March 1984, lost his Westminster seat to Joe Hendron of the SDLP in 1992, and regained it in 1997.

The New Ireland Forum, Thatcher's response and the Brighton bombing

The New Ireland Forum brought together the constitutional nationalist parties of Ireland, and its three options were rejected by the British Prime Minister.

  • The New Ireland Forum was set up in 1983 by the Dublin government, and it included Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael, Labour and the SDLP, while Sinn Féin was excluded.
  • The unionist parties declined to take part, which meant that its report reflected the views of nationalist parties and the Dublin government.
  • The Forum's report, published on 2 May 1984, set out three options: a unitary Irish state, a federal or confederal state, and joint authority by Britain and Ireland, and it said that the unitary state was the preferred option.
  • At a press conference on 19 November 1984, Margaret Thatcher rejected all three options, saying 'Out, out, out'.
  • Thatcher's rejection of the Forum's options marked the limits of British willingness to change Northern Ireland's constitutional position in the mid-1980s.
  • On 12 October 1984 the IRA bombed the Grand Hotel in Brighton, where the Conservative Party conference was being held.
  • Five people were killed in the bombing, and Margaret Thatcher escaped; the IRA said it would try again, and Patrick Magee was later convicted of planting the bomb.
  • The Hume–Adams dialogue began in 1988, when the SDLP leader and the Sinn Féin president held talks that were publicly acknowledged.
  • The talks were controversial, with unionists and some nationalists objecting to contact between the SDLP and Sinn Féin while the IRA campaign continued.
  • The dialogue resumed in 1991 and produced joint statements in 1993, and historians see it as an important precursor to the peace process.

Key personality: Margaret Thatcher

Thatcher was the British Prime Minister who led the government through the hunger strikes and signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement, a shift in British policy.

  • Margaret Thatcher was Prime Minister from May 1979 to November 1990, and she made security and the defeat of terrorism central to her policy in Northern Ireland.
  • She refused to grant political status to the hunger strikers, saying 'crime is crime is crime', and her government held firm during the 1981 protest.
  • She signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement with the Taoiseach Garret FitzGerald at Hillsborough Castle on 15 November 1985, giving the Irish government a formal consultative role.
  • She took part in the negotiations at Chequers and Hillsborough, and she signed the Agreement despite strong opposition from unionists.
  • Contemporary unionists regarded her as having betrayed the Union, while nationalists welcomed the Agreement as a sign of British recognition of an Irish interest.
  • Historians differ on her motives: some stress the hope of improved security cooperation with Dublin, others the aim of preventing Sinn Féin's growth after 1981, and in her memoirs she acknowledged that security cooperation did not improve as she had hoped.
  • Her Northern Ireland years saw the IRA killing of Lord Mountbatten on 27 August 1979, the same day as the Warrenpoint ambush that killed eighteen soldiers, and the Brighton bombing.

Key personality: James Molyneaux

Molyneaux led the Ulster Unionist Party through the period of the Anglo-Irish Agreement and was the main voice of mainstream unionist opposition.

  • James Molyneaux was born in 1920, was MP for South Antrim from 1970 and for Lagan Valley from 1983, and he became leader of the Ulster Unionist Party in 1979.
  • He led the unionist opposition to the Anglo-Irish Agreement, and he worked with Ian Paisley on the 'Ulster says No' campaign.
  • He took part in the unionist campaign of 1985 and 1986, which included the 'Ulster says No' rally at Belfast City Hall on 23 November 1985 and the resignation of fifteen unionist MPs from Westminster in January 1986.
  • Contemporaries valued his caution and his commitment to the Union, although some unionists felt he was too passive in opposing the Agreement.
  • Historians regard him as a steady leader of mainstream unionism who opposed the Agreement without the confrontational style of Paisley.
  • He favoured the integration of Northern Ireland into the United Kingdom's system of government rather than devolution, and he led the party until 1995.
  • He cultivated good relations with British ministers in the 1990s, and John Major's government at times relied on Ulster Unionist votes at Westminster.

Key personality: John Hume (part 3, SDLP leader and the Hume–Adams talks)

Hume led the SDLP from 1979 and became the leading constitutional nationalist voice, with his dialogue with Sinn Féin reshaping the search for peace.

  • John Hume became leader of the SDLP in 1979 and was elected to the European Parliament the same year.
  • He supported the Anglo-Irish Agreement, arguing that it gave nationalists a role in Northern Ireland's government.
  • He began the dialogue with Gerry Adams in 1988 and argued that the IRA campaign must end if nationalists were to achieve their goals by dialogue.
  • He took part in the Brooke–Mayhew talks of 1991 and 1992, which tried to set up a new devolved settlement.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a moderate and effective nationalist leader, while unionists criticised him for talking to Sinn Féin.
  • Historians credit him with shaping the nationalist case for a peace process based on consent and dialogue, and his work with Adams and with the Irish government influenced the Downing Street Declaration.
  • He described the conflict as a problem of three relationships, within Northern Ireland, between North and South and between Ireland and Britain.

The Anglo-Irish Agreement, 15 November 1985

The Anglo-Irish Agreement gave the Republic a formal consultative role in Northern Ireland, and it was the biggest British policy shift since Sunningdale.

  • The Agreement was signed on 15 November 1985 at Hillsborough Castle by Margaret Thatcher and Garret FitzGerald.
  • It set up an Intergovernmental Conference of ministers, with a permanent joint secretariat at Maryfield near Belfast, through which the Irish government could put forward views on Northern Ireland.
  • Article 1 affirmed that there would be no change in the status of Northern Ireland without the consent of a majority of its people, and the Irish government's role was consultative only.
  • Unionists responded with the slogan 'Ulster says No', a large rally at Belfast City Hall on 23 November 1985 and a day of action in March 1986.
  • Fifteen unionist MPs resigned their Westminster seats in January 1986 to force by-elections, but the Agreement remained in force.
  • Republicans rejected the Agreement because it did not end British jurisdiction, and Fianna Fáil under Charles Haughey opposed it in the Dáil, but nationalists in the SDLP saw it as a limited but real step towards Irish involvement.
  • Thatcher's motives included improving cross-border security and winning back nationalist support from Sinn Féin, while the Irish government wanted to protect the SDLP.

Enniskillen, 1987

The Enniskillen bombing of 1987 became one of the best-known atrocities of the Troubles and increased pressure for peace.

  • On 8 November 1987, Remembrance Sunday, an IRA bomb exploded at the war memorial in Enniskillen, County Fermanagh, killing eleven people.
  • The victims were civilians, mostly elderly, who had gathered for the ceremony, and a twelfth person died in 2000 after a long coma.
  • The IRA said it had intended to attack soldiers, but it admitted that the bombing was a mistake and was widely condemned.
  • Gordon Wilson, whose daughter Marie was killed, forgave the bombers in a famous BBC interview and called for an end to violence.
  • The bombing was condemned in Britain, in Ireland and in the United States, and it damaged the IRA's standing.

The Brooke-Mayhew talks and the Downing Street Declaration

The talks of 1991 and 1992 failed to produce a settlement, and the Downing Street Declaration of 1993 set out the principles on which later peace efforts were built.

  • Peter Brooke, as Secretary of State, said in November 1990 that Britain had no selfish strategic or economic interest in Northern Ireland.
  • The Brooke initiative of 1991 and the Mayhew talks of 1992 brought the parties together in multi-party talks, but they did not produce agreement.
  • The Downing Street Declaration was issued on 15 December 1993 by Prime Minister John Major and Taoiseach Albert Reynolds.
  • The declaration said that the Irish people as a whole had the right to self-determination, which could be exercised by agreement between North and South, and it said that a united Ireland would require the consent of a majority in Northern Ireland.
  • It said that the British government had no selfish strategic or economic interest in Northern Ireland, would legislate for a united Ireland if a majority wished it, and invited republicans to renounce violence and join talks.
  • It also contained a commitment by the Irish government to establish a forum for peace and reconciliation, and to examine Articles 2 and 3 of its constitution.

Assessment: the search for a settlement, 1974–1993

  • Achievement: the Anglo-Irish Agreement gave the Irish government a formal consultative role in Northern Ireland's affairs, a larger role than Sunningdale had given it.
  • Achievement: the Downing Street Declaration set out principles of consent and self-determination that became the basis for later agreements.
  • Limit: between 1974 and 1993 no devolved government was restored, and Direct Rule remained the basis of British policy.
  • Limit: the Hume-Adams dialogue and the Brooke and Mayhew talks did not end the IRA campaign, and violence continued throughout the period.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists and the Irish government saw the Anglo-Irish Agreement and the Declaration as steps towards justice and peace.
  • Contemporary view: unionists regarded the Agreement as an infringement of their constitutional rights and denounced the Declaration as a sign that Britain was moving towards a united Ireland.
  • Later view: historians see the period as one in which the framework for peace was built gradually, through dialogue, agreements and the acceptance of consent as a principle.

Key terms

Direct Rule
The government of Northern Ireland by Westminster through a Secretary of State, in place from 1972 apart from the brief executive of 1974, and the framework for policy from 1974 to 1993.
Terrorism
The use of violence against civilians and the state for political ends, which the British government sought to defeat through security policy and political negotiation.
Power sharing
A system of government in which unionists and nationalists share power, which British governments sought but failed to restore between 1974 and 1993.
Propaganda
Materials and messages used to shape public opinion, which the British government and republicans both used during the hunger strikes and the Anglo-Irish negotiations.
Anglo-Irish Agreement
The 1985 agreement between Thatcher and FitzGerald that gave the Irish government a consultative role in Northern Ireland through an Intergovernmental Conference.
Downing Street Declaration
The 15 December 1993 declaration by Major and Reynolds that accepted the principle of self-determination and majority consent for any change in Northern Ireland's status.
Ulsterisation
The policy from 1976 of transferring the security role of the British army to the Royal Ulster Constabulary.
Hunger strike
A refusal of food by prisoners to press for political status, which in 1981 led to ten deaths including Bobby Sands.
Criminalisation
The policy from 1976 of treating paramilitary prisoners as ordinary criminals and ending special category status.
Peace People
The movement founded in August 1976 by Betty Williams and Mairead Corrigan after the deaths of three children in Belfast.
New Ireland Forum
The 1983 to 1984 Dublin conference of the main nationalist parties, whose report offered three options for Irish unity that Thatcher rejected.
Consent principle
The principle that Northern Ireland's constitutional status could change only with the agreement of a majority of its people, restated in 1973, 1985 and 1993.
