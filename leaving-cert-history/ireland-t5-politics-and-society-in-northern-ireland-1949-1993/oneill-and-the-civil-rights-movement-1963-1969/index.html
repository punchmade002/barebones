O'Neill's modernisation, 1963–1965

Terence O'Neill came to power in 1963 with a programme of economic modernisation and gestures towards Catholics and the Republic.

  • Terence O'Neill succeeded Brookeborough as Prime Minister in March 1963, having been Minister of Finance since 1956.
  • He promoted economic planning, drawing on the Matthew Report (1963) on regional planning around Belfast and the Wilson Plan (1965) on economic development, which led to the new city of Craigavon in County Armagh.
  • He made symbolic visits to Catholic schools and hospitals, which unionist hardliners resented as concessions.
  • On 14 January 1965 he received the Taoiseach, Seán Lemass, at Stormont, a meeting widely described as the first between the heads of the two governments since partition; O'Neill had not told most of his cabinet in advance.
  • O'Neill returned the visit to Dublin in February 1965, and his policy of improving relations with Catholics and the Republic became known as bridge-building.
  • Government-backed projects such as Craigavon and the new university at Coleraine were part of a wider plan to show that Northern Ireland could be modern and prosperous.
  • Catholics welcomed the gestures but judged that real change in housing, jobs and voting was too slow.
  • Nationalists responded in 1965 when the Nationalist Party under Eddie McAteer agreed to become the official opposition at Stormont for the first time.

Unionist opposition to O'Neill

O'Neill's reforms split his own party, and hardline unionists saw the Lemass meeting as the first step towards a united Ireland.

  • Ian Paisley founded the Free Presbyterian Church in 1951, and by the mid-1960s he was the leading Protestant critic of O'Neill's policies.
  • Paisley and other hardliners opposed the Lemass meeting, arguing that it undermined the Union and Protestant identity.
  • William Craig, Minister of Home Affairs, was a hardline figure in the cabinet who banned the Derry march of 5 October 1968; O'Neill dismissed him in December 1968 after he spoke in favour of a unilateral declaration of independence.
  • Unionist MPs in the Stormont parliament and in the party organisation argued that O'Neill was giving away too much to nationalists.
  • The 'O'Neill must go' campaign, led by Paisley and others from 1966, sought the Prime Minister's removal from office.
  • O'Neill's position weakened as unionist opposition grew and as violence on both sides increased in 1968 and 1969.
  • In 1966 Paisley set up the Ulster Constitution Defence Committee and the Ulster Protestant Volunteers, and he was jailed for three months in 1966 for refusing to be bound over after a protest at the Presbyterian General Assembly.
  • The Ulster Volunteer Force, a loyalist paramilitary group, murdered three people in 1966, including Peter Ward, a Catholic barman, and its leader Gusty Spence was convicted of murder.

Key personality: Terence O'Neill

O'Neill was the reforming Prime Minister whose policies drove the civil rights movement and whose authority collapsed under its pressure.

  • He was born in London in 1914 into a landed family with its seat at Shane's Castle, County Antrim, was educated at Eton and became Prime Minister in 1963 after a career in the Stormont cabinet.
  • He held the post of Minister of Finance from 1956 to 1963, where he developed the economic planning that later shaped his premiership.
  • He made modernisation and reconciliation the centre of his programme, and he tried to build contact with Catholics and with Dublin.
  • In 1968 and 1969 he came under pressure from London, from the civil rights movement and from within his own party, and he conceded reforms step by step.
  • He announced a five-point reform package on 22 November 1968 and explained his policy in the 'Ulster at the Crossroads' broadcast on 9 December 1968.
  • He resigned as Prime Minister on 28 April 1969 after a crisis in his party, a poor showing in the February election and a series of bomb attacks on water and electricity installations.
  • He was made a life peer in 1970 as Baron O'Neill of the Maine.
  • Contemporaries were divided: some hardline unionists accused him of betraying the Union, while nationalists welcomed the gestures but judged them too slow.
  • Historians generally judge him a modernising economic planner who wished to reform on his own terms; they note that he did not see Catholic grievances as primarily political and that his authority was constrained by his own party.

Key personality: Conn and Patricia McCluskey

The McCluskeys founded the Campaign for Social Justice, which turned evidence of discrimination in Dungannon into a national campaign.

  • Conn and Patricia McCluskey founded the Campaign for Social Justice in Northern Ireland in January 1964 in Dungannon, County Tyrone; it grew out of the Dungannon Homeless Citizens League which they set up in 1963.
  • Conn McCluskey was a local doctor, and the campaign gathered evidence on discrimination in housing allocation and local jobs, especially by councils in Tyrone and Fermanagh.
  • It published its findings and lobbied British MPs, which helped to lead to the formation of the Campaign for Democracy in Ulster at Westminster in 1965.
  • The McCluskeys were participants in the campaign, and their work provided models and evidence for the civil rights movement that followed.
  • Some unionists dismissed the campaign as a front for republicans, a charge the McCluskeys rejected; they described their aim as equal treatment of citizens.
  • Historians credit the Campaign for Social Justice with turning discrimination into a public issue before the Civil Rights Association was formed.

The Civil Rights Association and its demands

The Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association was founded in 1967 to campaign for equal rights for Catholics on the British model.

  • NICRA was founded in January 1967 and drew together nationalists, socialists, trade unionists and some liberal Protestants.
  • Its demands included one man, one vote in local elections, an end to gerrymandering, fair allocation of council houses on a points system and laws against discrimination by public bodies.
  • It also demanded the repeal of the Special Powers Act and the disbanding of the B Specials.
  • Its first march was from Coalisland to Dungannon on 24 August 1968, and it was followed by marches in Derry, Newry and Belfast.
  • The movement used peaceful marches and sit-ins to draw attention to discrimination, and it sought British support for reform.
  • The authorities banned or obstructed many marches, which led to confrontations with the police and loyalist counter-demonstrators, as when supporters of Paisley blocked a march in Armagh on 30 November 1968.
  • Westminster elections already used one man, one vote, so the demand applied to local elections, where the franchise was restricted to ratepayers and their spouses.

Derry, 5 October 1968, and the five-point response

The banned civil rights march in Derry was broken up by police, and the footage shown on television turned the movement into an international story.

  • The Derry march of 5 October 1968 was organised by NICRA and was banned by the Minister of Home Affairs, William Craig.
  • Marchers who defied the ban were confronted by RUC officers, who used batons on the crowd in the Duke Street area.
  • Television footage of the violence, in which the Westminster MP Gerry Fitt was among those struck, was broadcast widely and shocked opinion in Britain and Ireland.
  • The event is seen as the start of the civil rights crisis of 1968 and 1969, as it moved the movement from marches to confrontation.
  • Nationalists in Derry saw the police action as proof of discrimination, while unionists blamed the civil rights organisers for provoking disorder.
  • The Derry disturbances led to the formation of the Derry Citizens' Action Committee, with John Hume as vice-chairman, and to pressure from the British government on O'Neill to introduce reforms.
  • The five-point package of 22 November 1968 promised a points system for allocating council houses.
  • It promised an ombudsman, a Parliamentary Commissioner, to investigate complaints against government departments.
  • It promised that Londonderry Corporation would be replaced by a development commission.
  • It promised to withdraw the Special Powers Act when the situation allowed, and to end the company vote in local elections while reviewing the wider franchise.
  • One man, one vote in local elections was not in the package, and O'Neill's party accepted it only in April 1969.

People's Democracy, Burntollet and the O'Neill crisis, 1969

The People's Democracy march to Derry was attacked at Burntollet, and the violence that followed brought O'Neill's government to its end.

  • People's Democracy was formed in October 1968 by students at Queen's University Belfast, including Bernadette Devlin, in the wake of the Derry march.
  • The People's Democracy march from Belfast to Derry began on 1 January 1969, a few dozen marchers aiming to protest against discrimination and to press for one man, one vote.
  • On 4 January 1969 the marchers were attacked by a loyalist crowd near Burntollet Bridge, among whom were off-duty B Specials, and many marchers were injured.
  • That night RUC officers entered the Catholic Bogside, and residents built barricades and declared the area Free Derry.
  • Marchers and civil rights supporters accused the RUC of failing to protect the march, which damaged confidence in the police among Catholics.
  • The Stormont general election of 24 February 1969 left the Unionist Party divided, and O'Neill held his own seat at Bannside against Paisley by 1,414 votes.
  • The Mid Ulster by-election of 17 April 1969 returned Bernadette Devlin, and in the same month O'Neill's party agreed to accept one man, one vote in local elections.
  • O'Neill resigned on 28 April 1969 and was succeeded by James Chichester-Clark, who defeated Brian Faulkner in the party vote by a single vote.
  • Bombs at water and electricity installations in March and April 1969 were first blamed on the IRA, but were later attributed to loyalists of the Ulster Volunteer Force, and they undermined O'Neill's position.

Key personality: Bernadette Devlin

Devlin was a student leader and civil rights activist who became the youngest woman elected to Westminster at the time.

  • Bernadette Devlin was born in 1947 in Cookstown, County Tyrone, and studied psychology at Queen's University Belfast.
  • She became a leader of People's Democracy and a prominent speaker in the civil rights campaign in 1968 and 1969.
  • She was elected MP for Mid Ulster at a by-election in April 1969, aged twenty-one, as a candidate of the unity and civil rights movement.
  • She was a participant in the People's Democracy campaign that organised the Belfast to Derry march of January 1969.
  • She wrote a memoir, The Price of My Soul, published in 1969, which presented the civil rights struggle from her own perspective.
  • Contemporary nationalists saw her as a symbol of youth and defiance, while unionists regarded her as a dangerous agitator.
  • Historians tend to view her as a key figure who brought the civil rights movement into Westminster politics.
  • She was convicted of incitement to riot after the Battle of the Bogside in August 1969 and served a prison sentence in 1970.
  • On 31 January 1972, the day after Bloody Sunday, she struck the Home Secretary Reginald Maudling in the House of Commons.
  • She lost her Westminster seat in February 1974, and in January 1981 she and her husband were seriously wounded by loyalist gunmen at their home.

Key personality: Ian Paisley (part 1, to 1969)

Paisley's opposition to O'Neill made him the most visible unionist critic of reform before 1969.

  • Ian Paisley was born in 1926 in Armagh and founded the Free Presbyterian Church in 1951.
  • Before 1969 he was a minister and public campaigner, who organised rallies and protests against O'Neill's policies.
  • He opposed the Lemass meeting and argued that the Prime Minister was undermining Protestant identity and the Union.
  • He was a participant in the 'O'Neill must go' campaign, and his rallies mobilised grassroots Protestant opinion.
  • Contemporaries saw him as the voice of hardline unionism, and many moderate unionists were uneasy about his methods.
  • Historians argue that his campaign against O'Neill helped to shift unionist politics towards the hardline position of the 1970s.
  • In 1966 he was jailed for three months, and in April 1970 he won the Bannside seat at Stormont that O'Neill had vacated.

Assessment: O'Neill and the civil rights movement

  • Success: O'Neill's reforms conceded much of the civil rights programme, including a points system for housing, an ombudsman and, by April 1969, one man, one vote in local elections.
  • Limit: the reforms came late, after the Derry march of October 1968 and British pressure, and many civil rights leaders judged them concessions forced by protest.
  • Success: the movement put discrimination on the British political agenda and brought the question of Northern Ireland's government into the open.
  • Failure: O'Neill could not carry his party or the unionist community, and his resignation in April 1969 showed the limits of reform from above.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists welcomed the movement's gains but argued that the state had not yet ended discrimination.
  • Contemporary view: unionists were divided, with hardliners blaming the civil rights movement for provoking violence.
  • Later view: historians differ on whether O'Neill was a sincere reformer who was undermined, or a leader whose reforms were too limited and too late.

Key terms

Civil Rights
The movement of the 1960s that campaigned for equal treatment of Catholics in voting, housing and employment, using peaceful marches and sit-ins.
Gerrymandering
The drawing of electoral boundaries to give one group an advantage, which the civil rights movement targeted in local government in Derry and elsewhere.
Tolerance and intolerance
Tolerance is willingness to accept differing beliefs and identities, while intolerance is refusal to accept them, a key divide between unionists and nationalists in this period.
Bridge-building
O'Neill's policy of improving relations with Catholics and with the Republic of Ireland through gestures and meetings.
NICRA
The Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association, founded in 1967 to campaign for equal rights for Catholics.
Five-point reform package
O'Neill's reforms of 22 November 1968: a points system for housing, an ombudsman, replacement of Londonderry Corporation by a commission, withdrawal of the Special Powers Act when possible, and an end to the company vote.
People's Democracy
A student-led civil rights organisation formed at Queen's University Belfast in October 1968, which organised the Belfast to Derry march.
Burntollet
The ambush on 4 January 1969 by loyalists at Burntollet Bridge on the People's Democracy march to Derry.
Free Presbyterian Church
The church Ian Paisley founded in 1951, which combined strict Protestant doctrine with opposition to ecumenism and to concessions to nationalists.
One man, one vote
The demand for a single vote for each adult in local elections, replacing the ratepayer franchise and company votes; accepted by O'Neill's party in April 1969.
Campaign for Social Justice
The body founded by Conn and Patricia McCluskey in 1964 to collect evidence of discrimination and publicise it at Westminster.
