Religion and identity in Northern Ireland

Religion and political allegiance overlapped, so that most unionists were Protestant and most nationalists were Catholic, though the boundaries were not absolute.

  • Catholics made up about a third of the population of Northern Ireland, 35 per cent in the 1961 census, and Protestants made up the majority.
  • The Presbyterian Church in Ireland was the largest Protestant denomination, with the Church of Ireland and the Methodist Church also significant.
  • Unionist identity combined Protestantism, loyalty to the British Crown and attachment to the Union with Great Britain.
  • Nationalist identity combined Catholicism, Irish cultural heritage and support for a united Ireland, though many nationalists were not practising Catholics.
  • Religious affiliation shaped schooling, marriage patterns and social networks, and many people had little regular contact across the divide.
  • Denominational identity was passed on through baptism and schooling, so divisions were reinforced across generations.

The Orange Order and Protestant tradition

The Orange Order was the largest Protestant fraternal organisation, and its parades became a focus of unionist identity and of conflict.

  • The Orange Order was founded in 1795 after the Battle of the Diamond, near Loughgall in County Armagh, between Protestant and Catholic groups.
  • The Order's annual parades on 12 July, marking the Battle of the Boyne of 1690, are its central public ritual.
  • Members of the Order were required to be Protestant, and its lodges were organised in local communities across the province.
  • The Order was closely linked to the Ulster Unionist Party through its delegates to the Ulster Unionist Council, and many unionist politicians were members.
  • Parades through Catholic areas were a source of tension, and the Order's marches were often restricted or rerouted to avoid confrontation.
  • Orangemen were forbidden to marry Catholics or attend Catholic services, which reinforced the separation of the communities.
  • The Twelfth of July, the main Orange parade day, is a public holiday in Northern Ireland, and bands, banners and bonfires on the eleventh night are part of the tradition.
  • The Black Preceptory and the Apprentice Boys are related Protestant institutions with their own parades.

Catholic tradition and the Gaelic revival

Catholic identity drew on the Church, the Irish language and the Gaelic Athletic Association, and the GAA ban on British security forces was a key symbol of separateness.

  • The Gaelic Athletic Association was founded in 1884 and promoted Gaelic games as an expression of Irish identity.
  • The GAA's Rule 21 banned members of the British security forces, the RUC and the British army, from membership, and it remained in force until 2001, a point of contention with unionists.
  • The Ancient Order of Hibernians was a Catholic fraternal body that paralleled the Orange Order in its social and political role.
  • The Irish language had a small but active community in Northern Ireland, and in 1971 the first Irish-medium primary school opened at Shaw's Road in Belfast.
  • Catholic churches and schools were central to community life in nationalist areas, and the Catholic Church was a major provider of education.
  • In Catholic areas the GAA clubs, parish halls and the Church were the main centres of social life.
  • Nationalists regarded the Irish language and Gaelic games as expressions of an Irish identity that the Stormont state had not respected, for example in the Flags and Emblems Act.

Ecumenism: early initiatives

Ecumenism is the movement for dialogue and cooperation between churches, and it gained momentum from the Second Vatican Council and from local initiatives in Northern Ireland.

  • The Second Vatican Council, held from 1962 to 1965, encouraged dialogue between the Catholic Church and other Christian churches.
  • The Corrymeela Community was founded in 1965 by the Presbyterian minister Ray Davey, who had been chaplain at Queen's University, at Ballycastle, County Antrim, as a centre for reconciliation.
  • The Irish School of Ecumenics was founded in 1970 in Dublin by the Jesuit priest Michael Hurley to promote reconciliation and interchurch dialogue.
  • Leaders of the Catholic and main Protestant churches held their first formal inter-church meeting at Ballymascanlon, County Louth, in 1973.
  • Ecumenism was supported by moderate church leaders, but it faced hostility from hardline Protestant and Catholic traditions.
  • In December 1974 a group of Protestant clergy met IRA leaders at Feakle, County Clare, and the IRA declared a Christmas ceasefire soon afterwards.
  • Pope John Paul II appealed for an end to violence during his visit to Drogheda in September 1979.

Ecumenism's problems and limits

  • Hardline Protestant opposition to ecumenism, led by Ian Paisley and the Free Presbyterian Church, portrayed ecumenism as a betrayal of the Protestant faith and the Reformation.
  • Catholic and Protestant clergy who joined joint initiatives were sometimes denounced by their own congregations or hierarchies.
  • Mixed marriages and joint worship faced disapproval in both communities, which limited the practical impact of ecumenism.
  • Violence and sectarian killing undermined trust between the churches, and clergy were themselves targets of attacks.
  • The ecumenical movement succeeded in building contacts and dialogue, but it did not change the political division between unionists and nationalists.
  • The Catholic Church's rules on mixed marriages, which required the children to be brought up as Catholics, caused resentment among Protestants until they were relaxed in 1970.
  • Paisley protested when the Presbyterian General Assembly considered links with the World Council of Churches in 1966.

Key personality: Ian Paisley (part 2, religion and politics)

Paisley combined religious and political leadership, and his opposition to ecumenism and to Catholicism shaped the hardline unionist position of the 1970s and 1980s.

  • Ian Paisley was the founder and moderator of the Free Presbyterian Church, and he combined his ministry with a political career from 1970.
  • He founded the Democratic Unionist Party in 1971 and led it until 2008; he won the Bannside seat at Stormont in April 1970 and the North Antrim seat at Westminster in June 1970.
  • He opposed ecumenism and any dialogue with the Catholic Church, and he attacked the Papacy and the Catholic Church in public speeches.
  • He took part in the unionist opposition to Sunningdale, supporting the 1974 strike, and he led protests against the Anglo-Irish Agreement.
  • Contemporaries saw him as a powerful and divisive figure, admired by his supporters and condemned by his opponents for his rhetoric.
  • The sociologist Steve Bruce argued that evangelical Protestant belief was central to Paisley's politics, and historians note that his religious politics made ecumenism more difficult.
  • In October 1988 he shouted at Pope John Paul II in the European Parliament in Strasbourg, calling him the Antichrist, and was removed from the chamber.
  • He was elected to the European Parliament in 1979, topping the poll in Northern Ireland.

Key personality: Seamus Heaney

Seamus Heaney was the leading poet of the Troubles, and his work explored violence, identity and the inheritance of the past.

  • Seamus Heaney was born in 1939 at Mossbawn, near Castledawson in County Derry, the eldest of nine children of a Catholic farming family, and he was educated at St Columb's College, Derry, and Queen's University Belfast.
  • His early collections included Death of a Naturalist (1966), Door into the Dark (1969) and Wintering Out (1972).
  • North, published in 1975, drew on Iron Age bog bodies, including 'Punishment', to reflect on the violence in Northern Ireland, and its poems made the bog body a symbol of ritual killing.
  • He moved to County Wicklow in the Republic in 1972, and later taught at Harvard and Oxford; he was awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1995.
  • Field Work (1979) and Station Island (1984) continued his engagement with violence, memory and the Catholic tradition.
  • Critics such as Ciaran Carson argued that North mythologised violence and made it seem inevitable, while others praised the poems as a major contribution to literature.
  • Historians see his work as a key cultural response to the Troubles, one that gave expression to grief, guilt and the search for an identity beyond sectarianism.
  • In his 1983 poem 'An Open Letter' he objected to being described as British in an anthology, writing that his 'passport's green'.

Cultural responses: drama, poetry and the arts

Writers, playwrights and artists responded to the Troubles in many forms, and the Field Day Theatre Company was a leading cultural initiative.

  • Field Day Theatre Company was founded in 1980 by the playwright Brian Friel and the actor Stephen Rea, with Seamus Heaney, Seamus Deane and Tom Paulin among its directors.
  • Friel's play Translations, the company's first production, opened at the Guildhall in Derry in September 1980, and it dealt with the English renaming of Irish placenames and the loss of the Irish language.
  • Other Northern poets, including Derek Mahon, Michael Longley and John Montague, explored identity, landscape and violence in their work.
  • Novels such as Bernard MacLaverty's Cal (1983) and Brian Moore's Lies of Silence (1990), plays such as Frank McGuinness's Observe the Sons of Ulster Marching Towards the Somme (1985), and songs such as Stiff Little Fingers' Alternative Ulster (1978) dealt with life in the Troubles.
  • Cultural production in both communities reflected a desire to express identity, but it also reinforced the sense of separate traditions.
  • Field Day's critics, such as the critic Edna Longley, argued that its emphasis on a shared Irish culture downplayed the unionist tradition.
  • Murals in Catholic and Protestant areas of Belfast and Derry became popular forms of political expression, as did the painted gable end in the Bogside reading 'You are now entering Free Derry'.

The Cultural Traditions Group, 1988

The Cultural Traditions Group was set up in 1988 to promote mutual understanding of the different cultural traditions in Northern Ireland.

  • The Cultural Traditions Group was set up in 1988 by the government's Central Community Relations Unit, and was later part of the Community Relations Council formed in 1990.
  • Its purpose was to recognise both unionist and nationalist traditions, and to encourage sharing of culture across the community divide.
  • Critics on both sides questioned whether equal recognition of traditions could replace political agreement on the constitutional question.
  • The Group's work centred on cultural events, education and shared activity across the community.
  • The Education Reform Order of 1989 made Education for Mutual Understanding and Cultural Heritage cross-curricular themes in schools.

Assessment: religion, identity and culture

  • Strength: ecumenism created channels of dialogue between churches and clergy, which gave some moderates a voice during the Troubles.
  • Limit: hardline religious opposition, led by Paisley, restricted the impact of ecumenism and made dialogue politically costly.
  • Strength: Heaney's poetry and the Field Day movement gave a shared cultural language to the violence and to the search for identity.
  • Limit: cultural initiatives reached mainly educated and middle-class audiences, and they did not change the political divisions between the communities.
  • Contemporary view: unionists saw religious and cultural traditions as central to their British identity and resisted anything that appeared to dilute them.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists saw their culture and religion as the basis of a distinct Irish identity, which they wanted recognised within a united Ireland.
  • Later view: historians argue that cultural responses to the Troubles were genuine and productive, but that they were not sufficient on their own to resolve the political conflict.

Key terms

Cultural traditions
The distinct heritage, customs and practices of the unionist and nationalist communities in Northern Ireland, which the Cultural Traditions Group sought to recognise.
Cultural identity
A person's sense of belonging to a community through shared language, religion, history and customs, which in Northern Ireland was closely tied to unionist or nationalist allegiance.
Ecumenism
The movement for dialogue and cooperation between Christian churches, which in Northern Ireland included the Corrymeela Community and the Irish School of Ecumenics.
Bigotry
Obstinate intolerance of people with different beliefs or identities, which both sides in the Troubles were accused of showing.
Tolerance and intolerance
Tolerance is willingness to accept differing beliefs and identities, while intolerance is refusal to accept them, a central divide in Northern Irish society.
Orange Order
The Protestant fraternal organisation founded in 1795 whose parades on 12 July became a focus of unionist identity.
Field Day Theatre Company
The cultural company founded in Derry in 1980 by Brian Friel and Stephen Rea, which staged Translations and produced pamphlets on Irish identity.
Corrymeela Community
The Christian reconciliation community founded in 1965 near Ballycastle, County Antrim, by the Presbyterian minister Ray Davey.
Democratic Unionist Party
The party Ian Paisley founded in 1971 and led until 2008, opposed to power sharing with nationalists and to the Anglo-Irish Agreement.
Gaelic Athletic Association
The sporting body founded in 1884 to promote Gaelic games, central to nationalist cultural identity.
Irish School of Ecumenics
The institute founded in Dublin in 1970 by Michael Hurley to promote interchurch dialogue and reconciliation.
