Northern Ireland's constitutional position, 1949

Northern Ireland had its own parliament at Stormont from 1921, but its place in the United Kingdom was the central question for both communities.

  • The Government of Ireland Act 1920 created Northern Ireland from six counties, with a devolved parliament at Stormont in Belfast; the other twenty-six counties got a separate settlement.
  • The Republic of Ireland Act 1948 took effect on 18 April 1949, ending Ireland's membership of the Commonwealth and formally declaring the twenty-six counties a republic.
  • The Ireland Act 1949, passed at Westminster in response, declared that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of its parliament.
  • This guarantee gave unionists a legal safeguard against a united Ireland imposed by Westminster without their consent.
  • Unionists held a majority in the Stormont parliament at every election from 1921 to 1972, so nationalists formed a permanent opposition.
  • Local government elections used first-past-the-post after the 1922 abolition of proportional representation, which helped unionists win control of councils in mixed areas.
  • Local election voting was restricted to ratepayers and their spouses, and company directors had extra votes, until the franchise was reformed in the late 1960s; nationalists called this the business vote.
  • The Stormont general election of 10 February 1949, sometimes called the chapel-gate election, was fought on the border question after the declaration of the Republic, and the Unionist Party won 37 of the 52 seats.
  • The Flags and Emblems (Display) Act (Northern Ireland) 1954 allowed the police to remove an Irish tricolour if its display was thought likely to cause a breach of the peace, which nationalists resented.

Brookeborough's government, 1943–1963

Sir Basil Brooke, later Lord Brookeborough, led the Unionist government for twenty years and put stability and loyalty to the Crown first.

  • Basil Brooke became Prime Minister of Northern Ireland in 1943 and was succeeded by Terence O'Neill in 1963.
  • His government resisted reform of local government and the franchise, and it saw no need to change a system that kept unionists in power.
  • As Minister of Agriculture in the 1930s Brooke urged loyalists to employ Protestants rather than Catholics, speeches that nationalists cited for decades as evidence of sectarian attitudes at the top of the Unionist Party.
  • The Civil Authorities (Special Powers) Act (Northern Ireland) 1922 stayed in force, allowing detention without trial and the banning of organisations.
  • The Ulster Special Constabulary, including the part-time B Specials, was kept as an armed reserve alongside the Royal Ulster Constabulary, and it was almost wholly Protestant.
  • The Royal Ulster Constabulary was the main police force, and Catholics made up only a small minority of its ranks.
  • The Nationalist Party abstained from Stormont for long periods and accepted the role of official opposition only in 1965, so there was little effective challenge to the government in the 1950s.
  • Brooke retired in March 1963 at the age of 74 and Terence O'Neill succeeded him.

Education: the Education Act (Northern Ireland) 1947

Stormont adopted the British welfare settlement for schooling, but the system kept religious divisions and set a selection test at age eleven.

  • The Education Act (Northern Ireland) 1947 provided free secondary education for all pupils, extending the opportunities available to children from poorer families.
  • It set up a tripartite system of grammar, technical and secondary intermediate schools.
  • Pupils moved on at about age eleven after the eleven-plus examination, which decided who went to a grammar school.
  • Voluntary schools, mostly Catholic, kept their own management and received a state capital grant towards building costs of 65 per cent, while controlled schools were run by the education authorities.
  • The Act also extended state scholarships and grants for university study, so more Catholic students reached Queen's University Belfast from the 1950s.
  • Free secondary education gave Catholic children access to a wider range of qualifications, and by the 1960s a new educated Catholic middle class was emerging.
  • Education remained segregated by religion, so Protestant and Catholic children grew up in separate schools.

Health, housing and the Housing Trust

The National Health Service and the state housing programme reached Northern Ireland, but local councils controlled the allocation of council houses.

  • The National Health Service began in Northern Ireland on 5 July 1948, the same day as in England, Scotland and Wales.
  • The Northern Ireland Housing Trust, set up in 1945, built houses and estates in towns and cities across the province.
  • The Housing Trust allocated its houses on a points system and was regarded as fair, but most council houses were allocated by local councils, and many unionist-controlled councils allocated houses in ways that favoured Protestant families.
  • Londonderry Corporation was controlled by unionists even though Catholics formed a majority of the city's population, because the ward boundaries were drawn in 1936 to place most Catholic voters in one ward.
  • Gerrymandering was the drawing of electoral boundaries so that one group's voters were concentrated in a few wards, and unionists won more seats than their share of the vote.
  • Catholic and Protestant families were increasingly housed in separate estates, which later made segregated housing a feature of Belfast and Derry.
  • In Fermanagh and Tyrone, where Catholics were roughly half the population, unionist-controlled councils also held power, a pattern nationalists cited in the 1960s.

Welfare parity with Britain

The welfare reforms of the 1940s were extended to Northern Ireland on a basis of parity with Britain.

  • National Insurance, unemployment benefit and pensions were run on a UK-wide basis, so benefits in Northern Ireland matched those in Britain.
  • The welfare reforms of the 1940s followed the Beveridge Report (1942) and the post-war Labour government's legislation of 1945 to 1948.
  • The principle of parity meant Stormont was expected to follow British social policy, which limited its freedom to set different rules.
  • The welfare state was therefore a shared British settlement, and its cost in Northern Ireland was met largely by the British Treasury.
  • The Social Services Agreement of 1949 formalised parity, and the British subsidy paid for public services that Northern Ireland could not fund from its own tax revenue.
  • Welfare parity meant that the Catholic minority benefited from the same services as Protestants, but it did not remove discrimination in allocation and jobs.

Economic decline and new industry

Northern Ireland's economy depended on shipbuilding and linen, which declined from the 1950s, while new industry was concentrated in unionist areas.

  • Harland and Wolff, Belfast's main shipyard, had built the Titanic, completed in 1912, but its workforce shrank in the post-war decades as shipbuilding declined.
  • Linen manufacturing, once a major employer in County Antrim and the Lagan valley, declined as cheaper foreign competition and synthetic fibres spread.
  • Unemployment in Northern Ireland stayed above the UK average and was the highest of any UK region for much of the period.
  • The Stormont government offered grants and incentives to attract new industry, including man-made fibre plants and aircraft production at Short Brothers.
  • Most new industrial investment was placed in the Belfast area and towns east of the River Bann, leaving the largely Catholic west, including Derry, with fewer jobs.
  • Emigration drained the province of young people, and the economy lagged behind Britain throughout the Brookeborough years.
  • Agriculture remained a large employer, and the farming economy was supported by British agricultural subsidies.

Discrimination in employment and public life

Catholics made up about a third of the population but were under-represented in public employment, policing and senior posts.

  • Catholics made up about a third of the population of Northern Ireland, 35 per cent in the 1961 census.
  • Catholics were under-represented in the Royal Ulster Constabulary and in senior civil service posts.
  • Some large private employers, especially in Belfast's shipyards and engineering works, employed very few Catholics.
  • Nationalists argued that Stormont treated the minority as second-class citizens and that discrimination was systematic.
  • Unionists argued that discrimination was exaggerated and that Catholics had equal access to state services and to the vote.
  • Unionist governments saw the Specials and the RUC as essential defences against republican violence, not as sectarian forces.
  • Local councils in Unionist hands were also major employers, and nationalists complained that they too hired few Catholics for senior posts.

The IRA border campaign, 1956–1962

The IRA's Border Campaign was an attempt to end partition by force, and it failed to win popular support.

  • The IRA launched its campaign, codenamed Operation Harvest, in December 1956, with attacks on RUC barracks, customs posts and border installations.
  • The campaign aimed to end partition by force, but it attracted little support from the nationalist community.
  • Stormont introduced internment without trial in 1957, and the Dublin government also acted against IRA activity in the Republic.
  • The campaign began with raids such as the attack on Brookeborough RUC barracks on 1 January 1957, in which the IRA men Seán South and Feargal O'Hanlon were killed; both became republican martyrs and South was remembered in song.
  • The IRA Army Council called off the campaign in February 1962, after which the movement was weak for several years.
  • The failure led some republicans to rethink their strategy, and in the 1960s a faction turned towards left-wing politics and social campaigns.
  • The campaign was mostly confined to the border counties, and its members were largely rounded up under internment north and south of the border.

Sectarianism in everyday life

Sectarianism was the hostility between Protestants and Catholics that shaped jobs, housing, marriage and social life in Northern Ireland.

  • The Belfast riots of 1920 to 1922 killed hundreds of people and left lasting memories of violence on both sides.
  • The Orange Order and the Ancient Order of Hibernians were the main Protestant and Catholic fraternal bodies, each with a political dimension.
  • Religious segregation in schools, housing and workplaces meant that most people had little regular contact with the other community.
  • Mixed marriages were uncommon, and religious differences were a factor in many social and personal decisions.
  • Sectarian prejudice was strongest in working-class areas where jobs and houses were scarce and rival communities lived side by side.
  • Parades and the display of flags marked out territory, so symbols of identity were also symbols of division.

Assessment: Brookeborough's achievements and failures

  • Achievement: the welfare reforms in health, education and housing raised living standards for citizens across Northern Ireland.
  • Achievement: Stormont kept political stability through the 1950s, with no sustained challenge to the Union, and it defeated the IRA border campaign.
  • Failure: the Catholic minority continued to face discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting, and this grievance was later taken up by the civil rights movement.
  • Failure: the economy stayed weak, with unemployment and emigration above the UK level, and new industry was poorly distributed between east and west.
  • Contemporary view: unionists praised Brooke's stability and his loyalty to the Crown, and his supporters saw his government as the guardian of the Union.
  • Contemporary view: nationalists condemned the Special Powers Act and discrimination as proof that the state was sectarian.
  • Later view: historians stress that the welfare reforms were real, but that the failure to share power meant they could not satisfy the Catholic community.
  • Achievement: the 1947 education reforms and the NHS gave Catholic and Protestant families alike better health and wider opportunities.

Key terms

Gerrymandering
The drawing of electoral boundaries to give one group an electoral advantage, used in Northern Ireland to concentrate Catholic voters and keep unionists in control of councils.
Sectarianism
Hostility and discrimination between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance.
Discrimination
Unequal treatment of people, in Northern Ireland especially of Catholics in housing allocation, employment and local voting.
Welfare state
A system of state-funded health, education, housing and social security, extended to Northern Ireland after the Second World War on the British model.
Eleven-plus
The examination taken at about age eleven that decided whether a pupil went to a grammar school or a secondary school.
Ireland Act 1949
The Westminster act guaranteeing that Northern Ireland would not leave the United Kingdom without the consent of its parliament at Stormont.
Stormont
The Northern Ireland parliament and seat of government in Belfast, dominated by unionists from 1921 to 1972.
Border campaign
The IRA campaign of 1956 to 1962, codenamed Operation Harvest, which attacked border installations and was called off in February 1962.
Parity
The principle, formalised in the Social Services Agreement of 1949, that welfare benefits in Northern Ireland matched British levels, with Britain meeting the cost.
Business vote
The extra local-election votes held by company directors and ratepayers, which nationalists said favoured unionists and which was ended in the late 1960s.
