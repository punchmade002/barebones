What the business economy covers

The business economy is the part of the economy in which firms produce goods and services for sale, and the CSO uses it to measure business activity.

  • It generally covers market-based activity such as industry, construction, distribution, transport, accommodation and food, information and communication, and professional services.
  • It generally leaves out the public administration, health and education sectors, which are funded mainly through the State rather than by sales.
  • The exact sector list is set by the CSO, so the latest CSO release should be checked when describing the business economy in an exam.

Headline measures

Four measures describe the size and output of the business economy.

  • The number of enterprises counts the businesses operating in the economy, and it is dominated by very small firms.
  • Turnover is the total sales revenue earned by enterprises from trading in a period, and it shows the scale of commercial activity.
  • Gross value added, GVA, is the value an enterprise adds to the materials and services it buys from others, and it shows the contribution to national output.
  • Persons employed counts the people working in enterprises, and it shows how many jobs the business economy supports.

Size pattern: SMEs and large firms

A small share of large firms accounts for a disproportionate share of turnover and value added, while small firms employ a large share of the workforce.

  • The EU classifies small and medium enterprises, SMEs, by staff numbers, with micro, small and medium categories, and they make up the vast majority of enterprises.
  • Large firms are few in number but generate a large share of turnover and GVA, because they operate at a much bigger scale.
  • SMEs employ a large share of the workforce, especially in retail, hospitality and construction, so they are central to local employment.
  • Productivity, measured as GVA per person employed, is generally lower in SMEs than in large firms, which points to a productivity gap that policy tries to close.

Ownership pattern: Irish-owned and foreign-owned firms

Foreign-owned multinationals and Irish-owned firms contribute to the economy in different ways.

  • Foreign-owned firms account for a large share of turnover, exports and GVA, because many are large multinationals operating in high-value sectors.
  • Irish-owned firms, which are mostly SMEs, account for a large share of employment, and they tend to serve the domestic market.
  • The gap between the two groups means the share of value added can be far higher than the share of jobs for foreign-owned firms.
  • Foreign-owned firms have stronger links to global supply chains, while Irish-owned firms have more links to local suppliers and customers.

Sector pattern

Sectors differ widely in how much value each worker produces and in how many people they employ.

  • High-value manufacturing, such as pharmaceuticals, and information and communication technology produce a lot of GVA per worker.
  • Retail, hospitality and construction employ large numbers of people, but generally produce less GVA per worker than high-value manufacturing.
  • Food processing is an important Irish sector, with companies such as Kerry Group and Glanbia having a large presence in global markets.
  • Professional services, such as legal and accounting firms, are heavily concentrated in urban areas and serve both domestic and international clients.

Regional pattern

Business activity is unevenly spread across Ireland, with Dublin and the east holding the largest share.

  • Dublin and the eastern region hold a larger share of turnover and employment than any other region.
  • Regions outside Dublin rely more heavily on SMEs, agriculture-related food businesses and tourism, which makes local economies more exposed to changes in those sectors.
  • Rural areas depend on small firms for much of their employment, so the loss of a single local business can have a large local effect.

Recent trends

Recent years have brought several trends that shape the business economy and its future.

  • The shift towards digital services and online trade is changing how many firms sell, and it creates demand for digital skills.
  • Labour shortages in sectors such as hospitality and construction have increased wages and pushed some firms to automate or change their services.
  • The green transition creates demand for investment in energy efficiency and low-carbon technologies, which affects firms of every size.

What the patterns mean for Irish business

The patterns show both the strengths and the vulnerabilities of the Irish business economy.

  • Dependence on a few large foreign firms exposes the economy to decisions made abroad about investment, location and tax, and a small number of firms pay a large share of corporation tax.
  • SMEs provide resilient local employment and a base for entrepreneurship, but their lower productivity limits their contribution to national output growth.
  • Closing the productivity gap through digital transformation, skills and access to finance is a key challenge for SMEs if they are to grow.
  • Ethics and sustainability also shape the outlook, because customers, investors and regulators increasingly expect firms of every size to manage their environmental and social impact.

Key terms

Business economy
The market-based part of the economy in which firms produce goods and services for sale, as measured by the CSO.
Enterprise
A business organisation that produces goods or services for sale and is counted as a single unit in business statistics.
Turnover
The total sales revenue a business earns from its trading activity in a period.
Gross value added
The value a business adds to its inputs, found as its output minus the cost of materials and services bought from other firms.
Persons employed
The number of people who work in a business, as counted in business statistics.
SME
A micro, small or medium enterprise, classified by the EU mainly on the basis of staff numbers, with limits on turnover and balance sheet total.
Foreign-owned firm
A business whose ultimate owners are based outside Ireland, often a multinational company with a subsidiary here.
Multinational company
A company that owns or controls operations in more than one country, often through subsidiaries.
