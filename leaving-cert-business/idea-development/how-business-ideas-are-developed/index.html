Internal sources of ideas

Ideas from inside a firm come from people and records the firm already has.

  • Staff are a strong internal source because they see customer problems and work practices first-hand.
  • Intrapreneurs inside a firm can propose new products, which gives the firm innovation without setting up a new start-up.
  • Research and development produces new products or processes through structured testing and experiment.
  • Brainstorming sessions bring staff together to generate many ideas quickly, to be screened later.
  • Existing products suggest extensions, such as a new size or flavour of a popular line.
  • Sales data shows which products sell, to whom and when, and can reveal gaps or changing demand.

External sources of ideas

Ideas from outside the firm show what customers want, what competitors offer and where the wider world is moving.

  • Customers reveal ideas through complaints, reviews and direct requests, which show needs the firm is not meeting.
  • Competitors show what the market already offers and where it is weak or overcrowded.
  • Market research, such as surveys and focus groups, tests whether an idea has demand before the firm invests in it.
  • Trends in lifestyle, population and spending, such as growing interest in plant-based food, point to new markets.
  • New technology can make a product possible or cheaper to produce, such as mobile payment apps.
  • Travel exposes entrepreneurs to products and services that are not yet available at home.
  • Hobbies and personal interests give ideas that the founder understands well enough to judge their quality.
  • Government and university research produce findings that firms can commercialise, often through partnership or licensing.

Idea generation and screening

Generation produces many ideas, and screening narrows the list to those worth developing.

  • Idea generation aims for quantity first, because early judgement can stop creative ideas before they are properly considered.
  • Screening tests each idea against criteria such as fit with the firm's goals, likely cost and likely demand.
  • A simple screening matrix scores each idea on each criterion and gives the most important criteria the highest weighting.
  • Ideas that pass screening move to development, where the firm checks their feasibility in more detail.

Factors that help an idea develop

Several factors decide whether an idea grows into a viable business.

  • Market demand: a strong and growing need for the product improves its chance of success.
  • Competition: the number and strength of rivals affects how easily a new idea can win customers.
  • Finance: an idea needs money for development, production and marketing before it earns revenue.
  • Skills and experience: a founder or team with the right skills reduces the risk of poor execution.
  • Technology: available technology determines whether a product can be made at a reasonable cost.

Factors that hinder an idea

The same kinds of factor can also block an idea or delay it.

  • Legal and regulatory constraints, such as food safety rules or planning permission, can delay or stop a product.
  • Economic conditions, such as high interest rates or weak consumer spending, reduce demand and make borrowing costly.
  • Ethics and sustainability: an idea that harms the environment or breaks ethical standards may be rejected or lose customers.
  • Timing: an idea can arrive before its market is ready, or the market can move on before the firm launches.

Illustrative examples

These examples are illustrative, showing how an idea can start from a particular source. They are not historical claims.

  • A Cork food producer might develop a new snack from sales data showing growing demand for healthy snacks in supermarkets.
  • A Dublin software firm might develop a booking app from a hobby or personal frustration of its founder that other customers also share.
  • Stripe, founded by two Irish brothers, grew from the problem of how online businesses take payments, an example of an idea shaped by digital trade.

Key terms

Internal sources of ideas
Ideas that come from within the firm, such as staff, existing products, research and development, and sales records.
External sources of ideas
Ideas that come from outside the firm, such as customers, competitors, trends, technology and research bodies.
Idea generation
The stage of producing many possible business ideas before any of them are judged.
Screening
Testing a list of ideas against set criteria to decide which should go forward for development.
Brainstorming
A group technique in which people suggest ideas freely, with judgement postponed until later.
Market research
The systematic gathering of information about customers, competitors and demand, using surveys, interviews or focus groups.
Research and development
Systematic work that tests new ideas to create new or improved products and processes.
Sales data
Records of what products sold, when, to whom and at what price, used to spot patterns in demand.
