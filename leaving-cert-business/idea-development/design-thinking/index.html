Design thinking as an approach

Design thinking is an innovative approach to problem solving that starts from the needs of the end user.

  • It is innovative because it treats problem solving as a creative process that generates new ideas, not only as analysis of existing data.
  • It is non-linear: teams move back and forth between phases rather than completing each step once in order.
  • It is solution-centred because it aims to produce a practical answer to the user's problem.
  • It is person-centred because the user's needs, feelings and behaviour drive each decision.
  • Design thinking supports idea development by reducing the risk of building something people do not want.

Phase one: clarify

Clarify means understanding the user and defining the real problem before any solution is chosen.

  • Empathy and user research: observe users, interview them and learn their needs, frustrations and habits.
  • A problem statement sums up what the user needs and why, and guides every later phase.
  • Clarifying prevents a firm from solving the wrong problem, which is a common cause of product failure.

Phase two: ideate

Ideate means generating a wide range of possible solutions before choosing one.

  • Brainstorming produces many ideas quickly, with judgement held back until the list is complete.
  • Ideas are sorted using criteria such as user need and feasibility, so that the team chooses which ones to prototype.
  • Ideation works best when the group includes different perspectives, such as sales, design and users.

Phase three: develop

Develop turns the chosen idea into a prototype and tests it with real users.

  • Prototyping builds a simple, low-cost version of the product, such as a paper mock-up or clickable app screens.
  • Testing shows how real users respond to the prototype and reveals problems the team did not expect.
  • Feedback from users is used to improve the design, and the cycle repeats until the design works well.
  • Developing is iterative because each test can send the team back to clarify the problem or to ideate again.

Phase four: implement

Implement means launching the solution to customers and checking its results.

  • Launch brings the product to customers through a planned marketing mix, including promotion, pricing and place.
  • After launch the firm monitors sales and customer feedback, which feeds into future improvements.
  • Implementation can reveal new problems, which send the team back to earlier phases.

Design thinking as a loop

The four phases form a loop, so each cycle improves the solution.

  • The process is iterative: it repeats, and each cycle refines the solution from the last.
  • For example, a café chain notices long lunchtime queues. It clarifies the problem by observing customers, ideates ordering apps and counter layouts, develops a prototype app and tests it with regular customers.
  • If the app test shows that customers want to pay at a table, the team returns to ideate, and only then does it implement a rollout across the outlets.

Benefits and drawbacks

Design thinking has clear advantages and some costs.

  • Benefit: products that meet real user needs are more likely to succeed in the market.
  • Benefit: testing early reduces the cost of fixing faults after launch.
  • Drawback: the process takes time, and repeated testing can delay the launch.
  • Drawback: research with a small group of users may not represent the whole market.
  • Drawback: the firm needs staff with the right skills, and running the process may require training.

Key terms

Design thinking
An innovative, non-linear approach to solving problems that focuses on solutions for the end user through four phases.
Iterative
Repeating a process in cycles, with each cycle refining the result of the one before.
Person-centred
An approach in which the needs, feelings and behaviour of the user guide each decision.
Solution-centred
An approach that aims to deliver a practical answer to the user's problem, rather than an abstract analysis.
Empathy
The ability to understand another person's needs, feelings and experiences, built in design thinking through observation and interviews.
Problem statement
A short, clear description of the user's need and the problem to be solved, which guides the rest of the process.
Prototype
A simple, low-cost early version of a product used to test ideas before full production.
Non-linear process
A process in which the steps can be revisited in any order, rather than completed once in sequence.
