What a feasibility study is

A feasibility study tests whether a business idea is likely to succeed before the firm commits significant money.

  • A feasibility study assesses the potential future viability of an idea, meaning whether it can survive and earn a profit over time.
  • It is more detailed than screening, which only compares ideas against criteria, because it gathers evidence on each part of the business.
  • It is carried out after ideas have passed screening and before the firm invests in launch.
  • It usually ends in a written report that sets out the evidence and gives a recommendation.

Market feasibility

The market part of the study checks whether customers want the product and can pay for it.

  • It examines the size of the target market, customer needs and the amount of competition.
  • It estimates likely sales volume and price, using market research data and comparison with similar products.
  • A market study that finds no clear customer group signals that the idea needs redesign or should be abandoned.

Financial feasibility

The financial part estimates costs and revenue to check that the business can pay its way.

  • Start-up costs are one-off costs incurred before trading begins, such as equipment, premises fit-out, licences and initial stock.
  • Running costs recur, such as wages, rent, utilities, marketing and raw materials.
  • Projected revenue is estimated by multiplying expected sales volume by the selling price.
  • Contribution per unit is the selling price minus the variable cost of one unit, and it pays towards fixed costs and profit.
  • The break-even point is the sales level at which total revenue equals total costs, found by dividing fixed costs by the contribution per unit.
  • Cash flow forecasting shows whether the firm can pay its bills in the early months, when sales are usually low.

Technical, organisational, legal, environmental and ethical parts

These parts check whether the idea can be made, staffed, legally run and accepted by society.

  • Technical or production feasibility asks whether the firm has the equipment, technology and processes to make the product to the required standard.
  • Organisational and staffing feasibility asks whether the firm has, or can hire, the people and management skills it needs.
  • Legal and regulatory feasibility checks licences, permits, safety standards and planning requirements before launch.
  • Environmental and ethical feasibility assesses the impact on the environment and whether the idea is acceptable to customers and society.

Outcomes of the study

The study ends with one of three decisions.

  • Proceed: the evidence shows the idea is viable, so the firm moves on to launch.
  • Modify: the idea has potential, but one part, such as price or product design, must change before the firm proceeds.
  • Abandon: the study shows the idea is not viable, which saves resources for better ideas.

Benefits and limitations

The study has real value, but its results are only as good as its assumptions.

  • Benefit: it reduces risk by finding problems before money is spent.
  • Benefit: a clear study supports applications for funding from banks, investors and state agencies.
  • Benefit: it identifies problems early, such as an unexpected legal requirement, when they are cheaper to fix.
  • Limitation: carrying out the study costs time and money, which can be a burden for a very small firm.
  • Limitation: forecasts of sales and costs are uncertain, so a positive study may still prove wrong.
  • Evaluation: the value of a study depends on the quality of its research, and key assumptions should be tested with real customers.

Key terms

Feasibility study
A detailed assessment of whether a business idea is likely to be viable, covering market, financial and practical factors.
Viability
The ability of a business idea to survive and earn a sustainable profit over time.
Start-up costs
One-off costs incurred before a business begins trading, such as equipment, premises fit-out and licences.
Running costs
Recurring costs of operating a business, such as wages, rent, utilities and raw materials.
Break-even point
The level of sales at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither profit nor loss.
Contribution per unit
The selling price of one unit minus its variable cost, which pays towards fixed costs and profit.
Cash flow forecast
An estimate of money coming into and going out of a business over time, showing when cash shortages may occur.
Projected revenue
The expected income from sales over a period, calculated from forecast volume and selling price.
