What a unique selling point is

A unique selling point is the feature or benefit that sets a product or service apart from competitors and gives customers a reason to choose it.

  • A USP must be something customers value, which is why the target market matters: a feature no one wants is not a useful USP.
  • A strong USP is distinctive, relevant to the target market and difficult for competitors to imitate quickly.
  • A USP is not the same as a slogan: the slogan communicates the USP, but the USP itself must be grounded in what the offer actually delivers.

How to analyse a USP

A USP analysis tests a claimed advantage against three questions before the business builds its marketing around it.

  • Uniqueness: identify exactly what is different about the offer and check it against the main competitors' offers.
  • Customer value: confirm that the difference matters to the target market, for example by testing it in field research.
  • Defensibility: assess whether competitors could copy the feature, price or service quickly, and what barriers protect it, such as patents or brand loyalty.
  • Conclusion: a USP that is unique and valued but easily copied needs supporting advantages, such as strong service or brand reputation, to hold its position.

Product: a 7-P mix for a porridge pot (hypothetical)

This mix is for a hypothetical Irish firm, Driftwood Oats, which sells a premium ready-to-eat porridge pot made from traceable Irish oats.

  • Product: a single-serve porridge pot in three flavours, with no added sugar and a clear list of ingredients, aimed at busy adults who want a healthy breakfast.
  • Pricing: premium pricing set above mainstream porridge pots, justified by the traceable sourcing and quality of the oats.
  • Promotion: social media posts that show the farms the oats come from, tasting events at Irish gyms and cafes, and sponsorship of a local running club.
  • People: a small team of brand ambassadors who run tastings at farmers' markets and answer questions about sourcing with confidence.
  • Packaging: a recyclable cardboard sleeve with a tear-off lid, labelled with ingredients, allergens and the named farm of origin.
  • Process: online pre-orders for weekly delivery to offices by a local courier, with reminders sent before each delivery.
  • Place: sold through independent Irish grocers and cafes, plus the brand's own website, combining indirect and direct channels.

USP analysis: porridge pot

The porridge pot's claimed advantage is traceable Irish oats from named farms, and the analysis tests it.

  • Uniqueness: most mainstream porridge pots state Irish origin in general terms, while Driftwood Oats names the farms that supply each batch.
  • Customer value: busy adults who care about local food and transparency are willing to pay a premium for a verifiable origin.
  • Defensibility: competitors can claim Irish origin, but naming farms and publishing verifiable supply contracts is harder to copy quickly.
  • Weakness: the claim is only as strong as its evidence, so the firm must keep supply records accurate or the USP becomes a liability.

Service: a 7-P mix for mobile bicycle repair (hypothetical)

This mix is for a hypothetical mobile bicycle repair service in Galway, Spokes at Your Door, which visits customers' homes and offices.

  • Service: mobile servicing and repair of bicycles at the customer's door, including safety checks, tune-ups, puncture repair and brake and gear adjustment.
  • Pricing: fixed prices for standard jobs shown online, set slightly above a workshop's rates to reflect convenience, with a volume discount for corporate contracts.
  • Promotion: local social media and search advertising, referrals from cycling clubs and a partnership with employers' wellbeing schemes.
  • People: qualified mechanics trained in customer service, who explain the work and the price before starting any job.
  • Packaging: for a service, packaging means presentation, so the business uses a branded van, a printed job card and a clean finish on every bike.
  • Process: online booking for a chosen time slot, a reminder message before the visit, a job card recording the work done and card payment on completion.
  • Place: the van travels to the customer, so the service is delivered where the customer is, and the base workshop is used only for parts and storage.

USP analysis: bicycle repair service

The service's claimed advantage is that it comes to the customer, and the analysis tests whether this can be sustained.

  • Uniqueness: the business repairs bikes at the customer's door, so the customer does not lose time travelling to a shop or carrying the bike.
  • Customer value: commuters and busy families value the saved time and are willing to pay for it.
  • Defensibility: some shops now offer collection, so the advantage can be copied; the business must build a reputation for speed and reliability to hold it.
  • Suggested extension: a same-day service would strengthen the proposition, though it would need more mechanics and a tighter route plan.

Evaluating a marketing mix

A marketing mix is evaluated against five criteria to see whether it serves the target market and supports profit.

  • Fit with the target market: each P should match the needs, budget and media habits of the customers it aims to reach.
  • Consistency between the Ps: a premium price combined with heavy discounting sends mixed signals and weakens the brand.
  • Competitiveness: the mix should give customers a reason to choose the business over rivals, built on its USP.
  • Profitability: revenue must exceed the costs of the mix, including promotion, packaging and delivery.
  • Ethics and sustainability: the mix should avoid misleading claims and wasteful packaging, because these damage trust over time.

Improving an existing mix

Improvements target the weakest element or the mismatch that the evaluation reveals.

  • Pricing: when sales are weak against cheaper rivals, a bundle or an introductory offer can widen the appeal without cutting the core price.
  • Promotion: spending can move to the channels the target market actually uses, with results measured through response rates.
  • Process: slow or manual steps, such as phone-only booking, can be replaced with online ordering to save time for both sides.
  • Packaging: a design that looks dated, or that customers see as wasteful, can be refreshed with lighter or more recyclable materials.
  • People: when customers report poor experiences, training and clearer service standards address the cause, since service depends on staff.

Modifying a mix over time

A marketing mix is modified as the product moves through its life cycle and as customers, competitors and costs change.

  • In the introduction stage the mix emphasises awareness and may use penetration pricing and heavy promotion.
  • In maturity the mix often shifts towards loyalty, price competition and cost control.
  • In decline the business may reduce spending and drop weak channels, or it may reposition the product for a new group of customers.
  • Competitor moves and new technology, such as online ordering, can force changes to place and process at any stage.

Key terms

unique selling point
The distinctive feature or benefit that sets a product or service apart from competitors and gives customers a reason to choose it.
USP analysis
Testing a claimed advantage for its uniqueness, its value to customers and whether competitors could copy it.
defensibility
The extent to which a competitive advantage can be protected from being copied by rivals.
patent
A legal right that gives an inventor exclusive use of an invention for a set period of time.
brand loyalty
Repeat buying and commitment to a brand that make customers less likely to switch to competitors.
mix consistency
The extent to which all elements of the marketing mix send the same message about the brand and its target market.
modifying the marketing mix
Changing the elements of the mix as a product moves through its life cycle or as market conditions change.
mark-up
The amount added to the cost of a product to set its selling price, usually expressed as a proportion of that cost.
