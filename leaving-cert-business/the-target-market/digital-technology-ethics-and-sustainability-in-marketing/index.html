Disruptive impact of digital technology

Digital technology is disruptive because it changes established ways of researching and marketing products, and its effects can be positive or negative.

  • Positive impact: it lowers the cost of reaching customers, speeds up feedback and opens markets that were too expensive to serve before.
  • Negative impact for firms: established businesses using traditional methods can lose market share to digital entrants that offer lower prices or greater convenience.
  • Negative impact for consumers: information overload, privacy risks and manipulative targeting can harm customers who do not understand them.
  • Disruption also changes skills, because marketing teams now need data analysis and content skills that were rare in the past.

Digital market research

Digital research collects consumer information online, using methods that are faster and cheaper than traditional fieldwork.

  • Online surveys reach large samples quickly and cheaply, though they may under-represent people who do not use the internet.
  • Web analytics track visits, clicks and time spent on pages, showing how visitors behave on a website.
  • Social listening monitors public posts and comments to reveal what customers say about a brand or a market.
  • Big data combines very large volumes of transaction and behaviour records to find patterns that a single survey would miss.
  • Artificial intelligence processes this data to predict trends and group customers, but the results are only as good as the data behind them.

Digital marketing methods

Digital marketing uses online channels to reach and persuade customers, and each method has benefits and drawbacks for firms and consumers.

  • Social media marketing builds community and engagement, but a firm loses control of conversations that can turn negative quickly.
  • Influencer marketing reaches niche audiences through trusted voices, though audiences may distrust paid endorsements.
  • Search and targeted advertising reach people who are already looking for products, but costs rise where many firms compete for the same search terms.
  • E-commerce and personalisation make buying easier and recommendations more relevant, but they can encourage overspending and raise privacy concerns.

Benefits and drawbacks for firms and consumers

Digital marketing offers consumers convenience and information, while firms gain reach and data, and both sides face risks.

  • For firms: lower cost per contact, measurable results and the ability to test campaigns quickly and change them.
  • For firms: heavy dependence on platforms controlled by others, which can change their rules or fees without notice.
  • For consumers: easier comparison of prices and a wider choice of suppliers.
  • For consumers: exposure to tracking, targeted pressure and misleading content that is hard to identify as advertising.

Ethical marketing

Ethical marketing treats customers honestly and fairly, and it matters most where personal data is used or vulnerable groups are targeted.

  • Honest claims: product benefits must be accurate and supported by evidence, not exaggerated or misleading.
  • No exploitation of children or vulnerable groups: marketing aimed at children, or at people in financial or emotional difficulty, must be restrained.
  • Data privacy: personal data must be collected lawfully and used only for the purposes stated, as required by the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).
  • Influencer disclosure: paid or gifted endorsements must be clearly labelled, so audiences know that the content is commercial.

Sustainability in marketing

Sustainable marketing promotes products and packaging that reduce environmental and social harm, and every claim must be backed by evidence.

  • Green products: products designed to use less energy, last longer or contain recycled materials can be marketed as genuinely sustainable.
  • Sustainable packaging: less packaging, recyclable materials and clear disposal guidance support environmental claims.
  • Greenwashing means presenting a product or business as more environmentally friendly than it really is, which misleads customers.
  • The risks of greenwashing include legal penalties, reputational damage and lost customer trust once the claim is exposed.
  • Regulation: the EU has rules on environmental claims that require them to be specific and substantiated.

How ethics and sustainability shape strategy and reputation

Ethical and sustainability commitments influence the choice of target market, the marketing mix and the way a company is judged.

  • Strategy: a business may choose a target market, such as conscious consumers, that values sustainability, and build its mix around that choice.
  • Reputation: consistent ethical conduct builds the trust that supports loyalty and gives a brand room to charge a fair price.
  • Influence on marketing agencies and brands: firms may refuse work, or change a campaign, to avoid practices that are unethical or misleading.
  • Evaluation: ethical marketing can cost more in the short term, but it reduces the risk of regulatory action and consumer boycotts.

Key terms

disruptive impact
The effect of a new technology or business model that reshapes an established market, sometimes displacing existing firms.
digital market research
Collecting information about consumers online, using tools such as online surveys, web analytics and social listening.
web analytics
Software and data that record and report how visitors use a website, including the pages viewed and the actions taken.
social listening
Monitoring online conversations about a brand, product or market to understand what people say about it.
big data
Very large volumes of varied data, from transactions and online behaviour, analysed to find patterns.
personalisation
Tailoring products, content or offers to individual customers using information about their preferences and behaviour.
greenwashing
Presenting a product or business as more environmentally friendly than it really is, which misleads customers.
influencer marketing
Partnering with people who have a loyal online audience so they promote products to that audience.
sustainable packaging
Packaging designed to reduce environmental harm through lower material use, recyclability or renewable inputs.
