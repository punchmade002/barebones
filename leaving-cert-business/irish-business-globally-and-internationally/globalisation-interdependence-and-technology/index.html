Globalisation and interdependence

Globalisation is the growing integration of economies, markets and cultures across borders. Interdependence is the reliance that countries and firms have on one another as a result.

  • Globalisation is driven by the removal of trade barriers, falling transport and communication costs, and the growth of multinational firms.
  • The World Trade Organisation, established in 1995, sets rules for international trade and handles disputes between its members.
  • Interdependence means a downturn in one major economy spreads to trading partners through lower demand, prices and finance.
  • Irish firms depend on imported components, energy and raw materials, so global supply disruption raises their costs.
  • For consumers, interdependence brings access to a wider range of goods, but also greater exposure to shortages and price shocks.

Positive and negative effects of globalisation

Globalisation creates benefits and costs, and a good answer shows which stakeholders gain or lose.

  • Positive: foreign investment and exports can create jobs and add to national output.
  • Positive: transfer of technology and management practices from multinationals can raise productivity in host economies.
  • Negative: job relocation, as firms move production to lower-cost countries, can cause job losses in the home economy.
  • Negative: workers in low-cost countries can be exploited through low pay and unsafe conditions, as the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh showed.
  • Negative: carbon emissions rise from long-distance shipping and air freight, and from production in carbon-intensive economies.
  • Negative: cultural homogenisation occurs when global brands and media reduce local traditions and products.

Political, financial and cultural risks

Firms trading internationally face risks that domestic firms rarely meet, and each type needs a different response.

  • Political risk: changes of government, trade wars, sanctions or the seizure of assets can close a market or reduce the value of an investment.
  • Responses to political risk include spreading sales across several markets and monitoring policy changes in each country.
  • Financial risk: exchange-rate movements change the euro value of earnings and costs, and firms may use hedging to manage this.
  • Financial risk: overseas customers may fail to pay, so firms use credit insurance or secure payment methods to reduce bad debts.
  • Cultural risk: differences in language, religion, customs and business etiquette can cause misunderstandings in negotiation and marketing.
  • Cultural risk: consumer tastes vary, so a product that sells well at home may fail abroad without adaptation.

Supply chain interdependence and recent disruptions

Global supply chains spread production across countries, which lowers costs but leaves firms exposed when one link fails.

  • A supply chain links suppliers, manufacturers, logistics providers and retailers that can sit in several countries.
  • The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 closed factories and ports, causing shortages of components such as semiconductors.
  • The blocking of the Suez Canal by a container ship in March 2021 delayed shipments between Asia and Europe for several days.
  • Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 pushed up energy and food prices, raising input costs for firms that rely on imports.
  • Firms respond by diversifying suppliers, holding more stock or moving production closer to home, a move often called nearshoring.
  • Shorter supply chains can cut transport emissions and risk but may raise unit costs.

Global marketing: standardise or adapt

A firm must decide whether to offer the same marketing mix worldwide or adjust it for each market.

  • Standardisation keeps the same product, brand and promotion in every market, which reduces costs and builds a consistent global brand.
  • Adaptation changes the product, price, promotion or packaging to suit local tastes, laws or incomes.
  • McDonald's in India does not serve beef, an example of adaptation to local religious and cultural preferences.
  • A firm can standardise its core product while adapting elements such as packaging or language, which is often the most practical approach.

Technology's role in globalisation

Technology has changed how firms trade, how work is organised and how operations run across borders.

  • E-commerce lets small firms sell to overseas customers without a physical presence, through online marketplaces or their own websites.
  • Digital payment providers, such as Stripe, which was founded by Irish-born brothers, process payments in many currencies.
  • Logistics technology, such as shipment tracking and digital customs documentation, makes international deliveries faster and more predictable.
  • Communication tools, such as video conferencing and cloud collaboration, let firms run remote teams across time zones.
  • Cross-border data flows support cloud services and global operations, but they can conflict with local rules on where data must be stored.
  • Automation and robotics can bring production back to high-wage countries, which changes where and how work is done.

Evaluating technology and globalisation

Technology widens access and efficiency but creates new risks, and an evaluation should weigh both.

  • Benefit: lower barriers to entry let small Irish firms reach global customers quickly.
  • Benefit: real-time data helps firms manage stock, suppliers and demand across several countries.
  • Risk: cyber-attacks and data breaches can disrupt operations and damage customer trust across several jurisdictions.
  • Risk: a digital divide excludes firms and consumers who lack access to technology or the skills to use it.
  • Risk: automation can displace workers in some sectors while creating demand for new digital skills.
  • Ethical concern: personal data collected across borders must comply with rules such as the GDPR, which protects personal data in the EU.

Consequences for Irish consumers

Irish consumers gain from globalisation through lower prices and wider choice, but they are exposed to shocks from abroad.

  • Benefit: a wide range of imported goods and services at competitive prices supports household budgets.
  • Challenge: Ireland imports most of its fuel and many consumer goods, so household prices depend on global prices and shipping networks.
  • Challenge: supply disruptions can cause shortages or price spikes for food, fuel and electronics.
  • Challenge: goods made in countries with weaker product inspection can carry safety risks for consumers.
  • Challenge: competition from global online retailers can put pressure on local shops and producers.

Evaluating an Irish organisation's international impact

An Irish firm that trades abroad has social and environmental effects in other countries as well as at home, and an evaluation should cover both.

  • Social positive: Irish firms that build overseas facilities or supply networks can create jobs in host countries and transfer skills.
  • Social negative: poor labour standards in overseas supplier factories can harm workers and damage the reputation of an Irish brand.
  • Environmental negative: long supply chains and freight add emissions, and overseas production may draw on resources that are scarce locally.
  • Environmental positive: an Irish firm can improve standards at overseas suppliers through audits and sustainability requirements.
  • Evaluation test: a firm should be judged on whether its overseas operations meet the standards it applies at home, which links to ethics and sustainability.

Key terms

Globalisation
The growing integration of national economies, markets and cultures through trade, investment and technology.
Interdependence
A situation in which countries and firms rely on one another for trade, inputs, markets and finance.
Standardisation
Using the same product, brand and marketing approach in every country.
Adaptation
Changing a product or marketing approach to suit local tastes, laws or incomes.
Supply chain
The network of suppliers, producers, transport firms and distributors that moves a product from raw material to customer.
Political risk
The chance that government action, instability or conflict damages a firm's operations or investments abroad.
Cultural barrier
A difference in language, customs or values that makes doing business in another country harder.
E-commerce
Buying and selling goods or services over the internet.
