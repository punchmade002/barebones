Ireland as a small open economy

Ireland has a small home market and depends heavily on trade, so international markets matter for businesses and for national income.

  • A small open economy has a small domestic market and relies on exports and imports to sustain growth and living standards.
  • Because the home market is small, Irish firms often need overseas customers before they can grow beyond a certain size.
  • Trade dependence means that a fall in demand from a major trading partner can quickly reach Irish businesses and jobs.
  • Ireland exports large volumes of pharmaceuticals, ICT services and food, much of it produced by multinational firms based here.
  • Because foreign-owned exporters send a share of their profits abroad, export earnings do not all stay in the Irish economy.

Why Irish businesses trade internationally

Firms expand abroad to grow, spread risk and access resources that the home market cannot provide.

  • Growth: overseas markets offer more customers than a small home market, so sales can keep growing.
  • Economies of scale: higher output for export markets can lower average costs per unit and improve competitiveness.
  • Spreading risk: sales in several countries reduce dependence on one economy or one large customer.
  • Access to inputs: firms may source raw materials, components or skilled staff abroad where they are cheaper or better.

Factors to consider before entering an international market

Six factors shape the cost, risk and feasibility of trading in a foreign market.

  • Taxes and tariffs: import duties and local taxes can make a product uncompetitive, so a firm must compare the duty payable with local rivals' costs.
  • Costs: labour, transport, distribution and rent differ between countries, which changes the profitability of entering a market.
  • Exchange rates: a stronger euro makes Irish exports more expensive for overseas buyers, and currency movements change the euro value of earnings.
  • Regulation: product standards, labelling and licensing rules differ between countries, and compliance can be costly.
  • Competition: the number and strength of local rivals determines how easily a new entrant can win customers.
  • Language and culture: differences in language, customs and buying habits affect marketing, and a poor fit can damage a brand.

Methods of entering a foreign market

Firms choose an entry method according to how much control, cost and risk they are willing to accept.

  • Exporting sends goods from the home country and is the least costly and least committed method, though it gives limited control.
  • Agents and distributors sell on the firm's behalf in the foreign market, which brings local knowledge but less control over pricing.
  • Licensing lets a foreign firm use the company's patents, brand or technology in return for royalties.
  • Franchising lets a foreign operator use the brand and business model in return for fees.
  • A joint venture shares ownership and risk with a local partner, which helps with local knowledge and regulation.
  • Direct investment, such as a subsidiary or factory, offers the most control and commitment but carries the highest risk.

Benefits and challenges of trading internationally

Trade can raise sales and profits, but it exposes firms to new costs and risks.

  • Benefit: a larger customer base can raise sales and profits, which can make investment in products and jobs at home more viable.
  • Benefit: exposure to international competition can push firms to improve quality, productivity and innovation.
  • Benefit: firms with diversified markets are less affected when one market slows down.
  • Challenge: foreign languages, legal systems and business customs add time and cost to deals.
  • Challenge: currency movements can change the euro value of overseas earnings and costs.
  • Challenge: trade barriers, sanctions or political change can close a market quickly.
  • Challenge: distance raises transport costs and delivery times, especially for perishable goods.

Balance of trade and balance of payments

The balance of trade counts only visible goods, while the balance of payments records all transactions between Ireland and the rest of the world.

  • Visible trade means physical goods that cross borders, such as machinery, food and pharmaceuticals.
  • Balance of trade equals the value of visible exports minus the value of visible imports over a period.
  • A surplus means exports exceed imports; a deficit means imports exceed exports.
  • The current account records goods, services, primary income such as profits and interest, and secondary income such as transfers.
  • The capital account records capital transfers, and the financial account records investment flows such as foreign direct investment.
  • Balance of payments is the combined total of the current, capital and financial accounts, and the accounts are reconciled with an errors and omissions item.
  • Because the current account includes profit flows, Irish current account figures are heavily influenced by multinational companies.

Worked calculation with sample figures

The figures below are invented for illustration and are labelled sample figures. They are not Irish statistics.

  • Sample figures, goods: exports €60 billion and imports €45 billion. Balance of trade = €60 billion minus €45 billion = €15 billion surplus.
  • Sample figures, services: exports €35 billion and imports €25 billion, so the net services balance = €10 billion surplus.
  • Sample figures, income and transfers: net primary income = minus €20 billion (profit outflows exceed inflows) and net secondary income = minus €2 billion.
  • Sample figures, current account = goods balance + services balance + primary income + secondary income = €15 billion + €10 billion minus €20 billion minus €2 billion = €3 billion surplus.
  • Sample figures, deficit case: goods exports €30 billion and imports €38 billion give a balance of trade of minus €8 billion, a deficit.
  • The current account (€3 billion surplus) and the balance of trade (€15 billion surplus) differ because the current account also includes services and income flows.

Key terms

Small open economy
An economy with a small domestic market that depends heavily on international trade for growth.
Visible trade
The trade in physical goods that cross national borders and are recorded by customs.
Balance of trade
The value of a country's visible exports minus the value of its visible imports over a period.
Balance of payments
A record of all economic transactions between a country and the rest of the world over a period.
Current account
The part of the balance of payments that records goods, services, income and transfers.
Trade surplus
A situation in which the value of goods exported over a period is greater than the value of goods imported.
Trade deficit
A situation in which the value of goods imported over a period is greater than the value of goods exported.
Economies of scale
Falling average costs per unit as a firm increases its output.
