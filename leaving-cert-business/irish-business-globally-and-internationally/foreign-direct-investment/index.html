What foreign direct investment is

Foreign direct investment means a foreign company taking a lasting interest in, and a degree of control over, a business or asset in another country.

  • FDI differs from portfolio investment, which means buying shares or bonds without seeking managerial control.
  • Greenfield investment builds new facilities from the ground up, such as a factory or office, which adds new productive capacity.
  • Acquisition, sometimes called brownfield investment, buys an existing business or site, which transfers ownership without adding new capacity at once.
  • A multinational enterprise owns or controls operations in more than one country, and most FDI is made by multinationals.
  • FDI is usually a long-term commitment, so it tends to be less volatile than portfolio flows.

How Ireland attracts FDI

Ireland's FDI policy works through IDA Ireland and through conditions that make the country attractive to investors.

  • IDA Ireland is the state agency responsible for attracting and supporting foreign investment in manufacturing and internationally traded services.
  • IDA Ireland promotes Ireland overseas, offers grants and supports, helps firms find sites and property, and connects them with skills providers.
  • Human capital development: an educated, English-speaking workforce, built through third-level education and training bodies such as SOLAS.
  • Pro-enterprise policy: a stable legal and regulatory framework that gives foreign firms confidence to invest for the long term.
  • Access to markets: EU membership gives tariff-free access to the single market, and Ireland has a network of trade agreements.
  • Research: universities and research centres, supported by Science Foundation Ireland, attract research and development investment.
  • Clusters: concentrations of related firms, such as ICT firms in Dublin and pharmaceutical and medical technology firms in Cork, Limerick and Galway, attract further investment and specialist suppliers.

Corporation tax and the global minimum tax

Corporation tax is a central part of Ireland's FDI offer, and it is changing under international agreement.

  • Corporation tax is a tax on company profits, and Ireland's 12.5 per cent rate applies to trading income.
  • The OECD global minimum tax, known as Pillar Two, sets a 15 per cent minimum effective tax rate for very large multinational groups.
  • Pillar Two reduces the advantage of a low tax rate for large groups, so Ireland's appeal depends more on non-tax factors such as skills and infrastructure.
  • Tax policy for multinationals is shaped by OECD and EU agreements as well as by Irish law, so changes can come from international agreement.

Opportunities of FDI for Ireland

FDI brings direct and indirect benefits to the Irish economy, although their size and spread vary.

  • Employment: foreign firms employ many workers directly, and their suppliers and service providers create further indirect jobs.
  • Tax revenue: corporation tax paid by multinationals funds public services, and their employees pay income tax and the Universal Social Charge.
  • Research and development: multinationals invest in R&D centres in Ireland, supporting innovation and high-skilled jobs.
  • Skills transfer: employees trained by multinationals can move to Irish firms and spread technical and management skills.
  • Supplier linkages: multinationals buy goods and services from local firms, which can help Irish SMEs grow.
  • Regional development: FDI in regional towns and cities outside Dublin creates jobs beyond the capital.

Challenges of FDI for Ireland

The benefits of FDI depend on how Ireland manages dependence, housing, infrastructure and the links to the domestic economy.

  • Concentration: a small number of large multinationals provide a large share of corporation tax receipts, which leaves public finances exposed if one firm leaves or its profits fall.
  • Footloose investment: firms can relocate if taxes, costs or regulation change, so Ireland must keep its offer competitive.
  • Pressure on housing and infrastructure: rapid job growth in Dublin increases demand for housing, transport and water, raising rents and congestion.
  • Profit repatriation: profits paid to overseas parent companies leave the economy, which is why GNI* is used as a measure of income alongside GDP.
  • Effect on Irish SMEs: multinationals compete for skilled staff and can push up wages, making it harder for SMEs to recruit.
  • Limited linkages: some multinationals source most of their inputs from abroad, so the benefit to local suppliers can be small.

Analysing business news and forming an opinion

The exam asks for an informed opinion, which needs a clear method rather than a general view.

  • Identify the central claim in the news story, such as a new investment announcement or a change in tax policy.
  • Check the evidence: the number of jobs, the timescale, the amount invested, and whether the figures are announced or confirmed.
  • Identify the stakeholders affected, such as workers, local communities, suppliers, government and shareholders.
  • Weigh short-term and long-term effects: a new plant may create jobs quickly but may depend on future policy and tax rules.
  • Pick a recent FDI story, such as a pharmaceutical or technology investment announcement, and check its details on a reputable news source before you use it.
  • Form a judgement, support it with at least two pieces of evidence, and state a limitation of your view.

Key terms

Foreign direct investment
Investment by a company in one country that creates a lasting interest and some control over a business or asset in another country.
Greenfield investment
Investment that builds new facilities from the ground up in another country.
Portfolio investment
Buying shares or bonds in a foreign company without seeking managerial control.
IDA Ireland
The state agency that attracts and supports foreign investment in manufacturing and internationally traded services.
Human capital
The skills, knowledge and education of a country's workforce, which affects its productivity.
Corporation tax
A tax levied on the profits of companies.
Profit repatriation
The transfer of profits earned in one country back to owners in another country.
Footloose investment
Investment that can move to another country easily if costs, taxes or regulation change.
