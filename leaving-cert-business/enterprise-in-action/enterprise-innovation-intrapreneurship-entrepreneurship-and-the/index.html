Enterprise and types of innovation

Enterprise is wider than starting a company, and innovation can change what a firm sells, how it works or how it earns money.

  • Enterprise is the process of spotting an opportunity and organising people, money and ideas to pursue it, so it applies to charities, community groups and public bodies as well as new companies.
  • Innovation is the introduction of something new or significantly improved, and it can take the form of a product, a process or a business model.
  • Product innovation changes what a firm sells, such as a new flavour of crisp or a redesigned phone.
  • Process innovation changes how a firm makes or delivers its output, such as automated stock control or online ordering.
  • Business-model innovation changes how a firm earns money, such as a monthly subscription replacing one-off sales.

Entrepreneurs and intrapreneurs

Both take new ideas forward, but they work in different settings and carry risk differently.

  • An entrepreneur starts and runs a new venture and carries the financial risk of its success or failure personally.
  • An intrapreneur develops new products or processes inside an existing organisation, using the firm's resources, so the firm carries most of the financial risk.
  • Intrapreneurship lets an established firm innovate while keeping the stability of its existing business.
  • Entrepreneurship and innovation are linked but not identical: an entrepreneur may run a business with no new product, and an established firm may produce an innovation.

Reasons for starting an enterprise

Reasons are financial and non-financial, and most people who start a business have more than one.

  • Financial reasons include the wish to earn more than paid employment offers and to build personal wealth.
  • Independence means being your own boss and controlling how, when and where you work.
  • A gap in the market is an unmet customer need that existing firms are not serving, which the entrepreneur sees as an opportunity.
  • A social goal drives people to solve a community problem, with the business run to achieve that aim.
  • Redundancy can push a worker to start a business when no suitable job is available.
  • Passion for a hobby or skill can be turned into a business, such as a baker who starts by selling from home.
  • Recognition, or the wish to prove an idea works, also motivates many founders.

Enterprise in society and personal life

Enterprise appears in government, communities, social enterprises and everyday life, not only in private companies.

  • Government bodies support enterprise through agencies that give advice and funding to business.
  • Community enterprise is run by local people for the benefit of their area, often using surplus income to fund local services.
  • A social enterprise trades to achieve a social or environmental purpose and reinvests most of its surplus in that aim.
  • Enterprise skills matter in personal life too, such as planning a school fundraiser or managing the budget for a part-time job.
  • Enterprise education in schools builds the planning, teamwork and decision-making skills that students need to run their own projects.

Why innovation matters

Innovation is important to the firm, the economy and society, and it also raises ethical questions.

  • For a business, innovation creates competitive advantage, because a new product or process is hard for rivals to copy quickly.
  • Innovation supports growth by opening new markets and increasing sales to existing customers.
  • For the economy, innovation creates jobs in new sectors and helps exporters win sales abroad.
  • Successful innovative firms grow and pay more tax on profits and wages, which raises revenue for the state.
  • For society, innovation provides solutions to problems in health, energy use and waste, and can make production more sustainable.
  • Innovation can carry costs, such as job losses from automation or privacy risks from new digital services, which firms must weigh up ethically.

Competencies of innovators

Competencies are the knowledge, skills, values and dispositions that help people turn ideas into working businesses.

  • Creativity lets an innovator generate many ideas and look at problems from new angles.
  • Risk-taking means accepting uncertain outcomes in return for possible rewards, and it is needed to launch a product with no proven market.
  • Resilience is the ability to recover from setbacks, which matters because many start-ups fail or stumble early.
  • Self-confidence and decisiveness allow a founder to make choices quickly when information is incomplete.
  • Vision gives a clear picture of where the business is going, which helps attract staff, investors and customers.
  • Initiative means acting without waiting to be told, such as finding a second supplier before a shortage happens.
  • Networking builds contacts with mentors, suppliers and customers, which helps a new business get advice and early sales.
  • Adaptability means changing a plan when customer feedback or market conditions change, which is essential at start-up.

Examples and reading enterprise news

Examples show how enterprise and innovation appear in practice, from local firms to global ones.

  • Kerry Group, an Irish food company, competes internationally by developing food ingredients that it adapts for its customers.
  • Stripe was founded by two Irish brothers and provides online payment tools, an example of Irish-founded digital innovation with a global market.
  • Supermac's, an Irish fast food chain, has grown across Ireland by building a brand around its own menu and outlet network.
  • An intrapreneurship example is a bank's internal team developing a mobile app for a new savings service, rather than a founder starting a new bank.
  • When analysing news on enterprise, ask what problem the firm solves, who benefits, and what risks it faces.

Key terms

Enterprise
The process of spotting an opportunity and organising people, money and ideas to pursue it.
Innovation
Introducing a new or significantly improved product, process or business model.
Entrepreneur
A person who starts and runs a new venture and carries the financial risk of its success or failure.
Intrapreneur
An employee who develops new products or processes inside an existing organisation, using its resources.
Social enterprise
A business that trades to achieve a social or environmental purpose and reinvests most of its surplus in that aim.
Competencies of innovators
The knowledge, skills, values and dispositions that help innovators turn ideas into viable businesses.
Competitive advantage
A feature, such as a unique product or a lower cost, that lets a firm outperform rivals and that they find hard to copy.
Gap in the market
An unmet customer need that existing firms are not serving, which a new business can exploit.
