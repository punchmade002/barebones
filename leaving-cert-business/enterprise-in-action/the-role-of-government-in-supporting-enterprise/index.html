Why government supports enterprise

Governments support enterprise because small firms create jobs and exports, and private markets may not fund some good ideas.

  • Market failure occurs when private finance is not available for good ideas that are too risky or too small for banks to lend to.
  • Support helps firms grow into exporters, which brings foreign earnings into the economy.
  • Support for enterprise outside Dublin and the other large cities spreads jobs across regions.
  • Support can also encourage firms to adopt sustainable and digital practices, which benefits society as well as the firm.

Local Enterprise Offices

Local Enterprise Offices are the main first point of contact for small firms and are based within local authorities.

  • Local Enterprise Offices focus on micro and small firms, including start-ups and firms that want to grow.
  • They offer grants for start-up and expansion costs, such as equipment and technology.
  • They provide mentoring, training and advice on business planning, financial management and marketing.
  • The Trading Online Voucher scheme is a voucher scheme that helps small firms build an online presence and sell online.

Enterprise Ireland and IDA Ireland

These two agencies support different kinds of firms, Irish-owned and foreign-owned.

  • Enterprise Ireland supports Irish-owned firms, particularly high-potential start-ups and firms that export or want to.
  • Enterprise Ireland helps firms with market research, export marketing and introductions to overseas buyers.
  • IDA Ireland attracts foreign direct investment, meaning investment by multinational firms that set up factories, offices or research centres in Ireland.
  • Foreign investment brings jobs and skills, and Irish suppliers can benefit by selling to the foreign firms that set up here.

Finance and state-backed lending

Finance is often the main barrier to a growing firm, and the state supports lending where banks are cautious.

  • MicroFinance Ireland provides small loans to people who cannot get a bank loan for their business, usually with mentoring attached.
  • The Strategic Banking Corporation of Ireland, SBCI, funds loan schemes that partner banks offer to small and medium-sized firms, which lowers the cost of borrowing.
  • State-backed lending reduces the risk for banks, which makes them willing to lend to firms they would otherwise refuse.
  • Údarás na Gaeltachta supports enterprise in Irish-speaking areas through grants, advice and property for firms that locate there.

Tax measures

Tax measures reduce what firms pay or reward specific activity, but they cost the state revenue.

  • A low headline rate of corporation tax makes Ireland attractive for profitable firms and for foreign investors.
  • The research and development tax credit reduces tax for firms that spend money on research and development work.
  • Start-up tax reliefs reduce tax for new firms in their early years, easing cash flow when money is tight.
  • The Employment and Investment Incentive Scheme gives income tax relief to investors who buy shares in qualifying small firms, which makes equity easier to raise.
  • Tax measures cost the state revenue, so their value must be weighed against the activity they encourage.

Education, training and infrastructure

Skills and infrastructure remove barriers to growth, especially for small firms and rural firms.

  • Skillnet Ireland supports business networks that fund training for groups of firms, so small firms can access training they could not afford alone.
  • Education and Training Boards, ETBs, offer courses in business, technology and craft skills that firms need.
  • Good broadband and transport infrastructure allow rural firms to sell online and reach customers across the country.
  • Regional and rural supports, including grants and place-based programmes, help firms in areas with weaker markets.

Government as regulator and protector

Government also sets the rules firms must follow, and those rules protect both consumers and firms.

  • Regulation sets standards such as food safety and employment rights that firms must meet, which protects consumers and workers.
  • Clear rules protect fair competition, so that firms with honest practices are not undercut by firms that break the law.
  • Government can reduce red tape, which lowers the time and cost of meeting legal requirements for small firms.
  • National enterprise strategy sets priorities for support over several years, which gives firms some certainty about what help is available.

Benefits and limits of state support

Support helps firms but has costs and limits, and its value depends on how well it targets real gaps.

  • Benefits include access to finance, advice, training and export contacts that a small firm would struggle to find alone.
  • A limit is dependence: firms may rely on grants and fail to build a business that survives when the support ends.
  • Support can favour firms that are good at applying for it, not necessarily the firms with the best ideas.
  • The state funds support from taxes, so schemes compete with other public spending on health and education.
  • Evaluation: support works best where it addresses a real gap, such as finance for exporters, rather than subsidising firms that would succeed anyway.

Key terms

Market failure
A situation in which markets left alone do not supply something useful, such as finance for risky start-ups.
Local Enterprise Office
A local support centre, based within local authorities, that gives grants, training and advice to small firms.
Enterprise Ireland
The state agency that supports Irish-owned firms with high growth potential, especially those that export.
IDA Ireland
The state agency that attracts multinational firms to invest in Ireland.
Foreign direct investment
Money invested by a foreign company to set up or expand operations, such as a factory or office, in another country.
MicroFinance Ireland
A body that provides small loans, with mentoring, to people who cannot get a bank loan for their business.
Strategic Banking Corporation of Ireland (SBCI)
A state-backed body that funds loan schemes which partner banks offer to small and medium-sized firms.
Employment and Investment Incentive Scheme (EIIS)
A tax relief that gives income tax relief to individuals who invest in qualifying small firms by buying their shares.
