Purpose of regulation

Regulation is the set of rules, set by the State or an independent body, that controls how businesses operate.

  • Regulation protects stakeholders such as customers, employees and the public from harm, for example from unsafe products or misleading advertising.
  • It promotes fair competition, so that no firm gains an advantage by breaking the rules, by colluding with rivals or by abusing a dominant position.
  • It protects safety at work and in the environment, by setting standards that businesses must meet regardless of their profit motive.
  • It builds trust in markets, which encourages customers, employees and investors to take part, for example by keeping customer deposits in banks secure.

Internal regulation

Internal regulation is the set of controls a business puts in place itself, beyond what the law requires.

  • The board of directors has a legal duty to act in the best interests of the company, to act with care and skill, and to avoid conflicts of interest.
  • Internal audit is an independent review function that checks whether controls are working and reports its findings to the audit committee or board.
  • Risk management means identifying, assessing and controlling events that could stop the business meeting its objectives, such as fraud, a cyber attack or a supply disruption, usually recorded in a risk register.
  • A compliance function checks that the business obeys laws, regulations and its own policies, and it reports any breaches to senior management.
  • A code of conduct sets out expected ethical behaviour for employees and directors, and protected reporting channels let staff raise concerns about wrongdoing.

External regulation

External regulation is imposed on a business by law, independent statutory bodies and independent auditors.

  • A statutory external audit is required by the Companies Act 2014 for many companies, and an independent auditor reports to shareholders on whether the accounts give a true and fair view.
  • The Central Bank of Ireland supervises banks, insurers and investment firms, and it has powers to investigate and penalise firms that breach its rules.
  • The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission enforces competition and consumer law and reviews proposed mergers that could reduce competition.
  • The Corporate Enforcement Authority enforces company law, investigates breaches by companies and directors, and can bring prosecutions.
  • Revenue enforces tax law, the Health and Safety Authority enforces workplace safety, and the Environmental Protection Agency enforces environmental law.
  • The Data Protection Commission enforces the GDPR, which governs how businesses collect, store and use personal data.

Corporate governance

Corporate governance is the system of rules, practices and processes by which a company is directed and controlled.

  • Accountability means directors answer to shareholders and other stakeholders for their decisions and results, and transparency means disclosing accurate information on time.
  • A board usually combines executive directors, who run the business, with non-executive directors, who bring independent scrutiny to decisions.
  • Good governance often separates the roles of chair and chief executive, so that no single person holds unchecked power over the business.
  • Board committees do specialist work: an audit committee oversees financial reporting and internal audit, and a remuneration committee sets executive pay.
  • Shareholders exercise governance rights by voting at the annual general meeting, including on the appointment and removal of directors.
  • Strong governance is a form of ethics in practice, because it sets the culture that decides whether controls are respected or ignored.

The three ESG pillars

ESG stands for environmental, social and governance factors, which investors and other stakeholders use to judge a company's sustainability and ethics.

  • Environmental factors cover carbon emissions, energy use, water use, waste and pollution, for example a food company cutting the packaging waste it sends to landfill.
  • Social factors cover employees' health and safety, pay and diversity, fair treatment of customers and suppliers, and the impact of the business on its local community.
  • Governance factors cover board structure, executive pay, anti-bribery controls, tax transparency and the protection of shareholder rights.
  • Environmental and social harm can cost a business customers, staff and access to finance, so managing ESG risk is a practical form of business sustainability.

ESG reporting

An ESG report discloses how a company performs on environmental, social and governance factors, usually alongside its financial results.

  • Investors and lenders use ESG reports to assess long-term risks, such as climate-related disruption or abuse in the supply chain, which can affect future profits.
  • Reporting builds stakeholder trust and can help a business win contracts from customers that require suppliers to meet sustainability standards.
  • The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive requires larger companies to publish standardised sustainability reports, and Ireland has transposed it into Irish law.
  • Reports under the Directive must be checked by an independent assurance provider, so the information is not left entirely to the company's own judgement.

Evaluating regulation and governance

Regulation and governance bring benefits and costs, and their effectiveness depends on how consistently they are applied.

  • The benefits are protection for stakeholders, greater trust and a level playing field between firms that obey the rules.
  • The costs are compliance expenses and staff time, which weigh more heavily on small and medium enterprises that have few staff to handle reporting.
  • Over-regulation can slow innovation and deter entrepreneurs, while under-regulation allows fraud, unsafe practices and environmental damage to go unchecked.
  • Regulation works best when it is enforced consistently and when businesses adopt internal controls that go beyond the legal minimum.

Key terms

Regulation
Rules set and enforced by the State or an independent body that control how businesses operate.
Corporate governance
The system of rules, practices and processes by which a company is directed and controlled.
Accountability
The duty to explain and answer for decisions and results to those with a legitimate interest, such as shareholders.
Transparency
Openly disclosing accurate and timely information so that stakeholders can understand a business's decisions and performance.
Internal audit
A review function inside an organisation that independently checks whether its controls, risk management and governance are working.
Risk management
The process of identifying, assessing and controlling events that could stop a business meeting its objectives.
Compliance
Ensuring that a business follows laws, regulations and its own policies, and reporting any breaches.
Statutory audit
An independent audit of a company's accounts required by law, reporting to shareholders on whether the accounts give a true and fair view.
ESG
Environmental, social and governance factors used to assess a company's sustainability and ethical performance.
