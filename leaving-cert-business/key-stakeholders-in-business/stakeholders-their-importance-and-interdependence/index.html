What a stakeholder is

A stakeholder is any person or group affected by a business or able to affect it. Stakeholders are classed as internal or external according to their relationship with the business.

  • Internal stakeholders work within the business or own it, such as owners, managers and employees.
  • External stakeholders sit outside the business but are affected by it or can affect it, such as consumers, suppliers, the local community and government.
  • Investors can be internal, when they are also owners, or external, when they provide money through shares or loans.
  • Managers run the business day to day, and interest groups, such as trade unions or lobby organisations, press particular concerns on the business.

Owners and investors

Owners and investors provide finance and carry the risk of losing it, so they expect a return on their money.

  • Business owners take the main decisions and bear the loss if the business fails, so they want profit, growth and control.
  • Investors provide money through share purchases or loans, and want the security of their capital together with a return such as dividends or interest.
  • Irish example: a founder of a small food manufacturer who funds the business from savings is both owner and investor, so she carries the full financial risk.

Employees and managers

Employees supply the labour that produces the business's goods and services, so their wellbeing and performance affect every other stakeholder.

  • Employees need fair wages, safe working conditions, job security and opportunities for training and progression.
  • Employees' skills and motivation shape productivity, customer service and quality, which in turn affect sales and reputation.
  • Managers coordinate employees and carry out the owners' strategy, so they must balance the needs of both groups.
  • Irish example: a hotel in Galway relies on its reception and housekeeping staff, and keeping them affects service quality and repeat bookings.

Consumers and suppliers

Consumers buy the business's goods and services, and suppliers provide the inputs the business needs to operate.

  • Consumers expect good quality, a fair price, safety, reliability and, increasingly, products that are produced ethically and sustainably.
  • Suppliers want timely payment, reliable orders and a fair price, and many want a long-term relationship with the business.
  • Consumers provide the revenue a business needs, so a fall in customer trust can reduce sales quickly.
  • Irish example: a bakery that buys flour from an Irish mill depends on that supplier for consistent quality and delivery, while the mill depends on the bakery's regular orders.

Local community and government

The local community and government are external stakeholders whose interests cover jobs, the environment, taxes and regulation.

  • The local community wants employment, a clean environment, safe roads and local spending, and may object to noise, pollution or heavy traffic.
  • Government collects taxes from businesses, such as corporation tax and VAT, sets laws on employment and safety, and funds public services.
  • Agencies such as Enterprise Ireland and the Local Enterprise Offices support businesses with funding and advice, so a firm's relationship with them can open practical help.
  • Irish example: a new data centre in a rural area creates local jobs, and brings scrutiny from the local authority on planning and the environment.

How needs change from start-up to maturity

Stakeholder needs shift as a business develops, and the same stakeholder may move from supporting the business to demanding more from it.

  • At start-up, owners and investors prioritise survival and security, while employees accept uncertainty in exchange for growth potential.
  • At growth, owners and investors want funding for expansion and rising returns, and employees seek progression and new roles.
  • At maturity, investors look for steady dividends and stability, while consumers and suppliers value consistency and reliability over novelty.
  • As a business grows, government and community interests tend to intensify, because a larger firm has more effect on tax revenue, employment and the environment.

Interdependence: one change ripples through

Stakeholders depend on one another, so a change affecting one group usually affects others.

  • A rise in supplier prices can squeeze owners' profits, which may lead to lower staff pay or higher prices for consumers.
  • A new environmental regulation raises the business's costs, which may reduce investor returns and change how the local community views the firm.
  • A staff strike can cut output, delay consumer orders and cause missed deliveries that strain supplier relationships.
  • Because of interdependence, a business that focuses on one stakeholder's needs while ignoring another's risks losing the support of both.

Key terms

Stakeholder
A person or group affected by a business's activities or able to influence its decisions.
Internal stakeholder
A stakeholder who works within or owns the business, such as an owner, a manager or an employee.
External stakeholder
A stakeholder outside the business who is affected by it or can affect it, such as a supplier or the government.
Interdependence
The reliance of stakeholders on one another, so that a change affecting one group flows on to the others.
Investor
A person or institution that provides money to a business, through shares or loans, in the expectation of a return.
Dividend
A payment of part of a company's profit to its shareholders.
Supplier
A business or person that provides goods, services or materials to another business.
Interest group
A group that represents a particular concern, such as a trade union or a lobby organisation, and seeks to influence business or government decisions.
Start-up stage
The early stage of a business when it is new, often unprofitable and dependent on the money of its founders and investors.
