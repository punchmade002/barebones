Business models and value

A business model describes how a business creates value, delivers it to customers and captures part of that value as income.

  • Value creation: the business transforms inputs such as materials, skills and knowledge into products or services that customers want.
  • Value delivery: the business gets the product or service to customers through chosen channels and relationships.
  • Value capture: the business earns income from customers that is enough to cover its costs and leave a profit.
  • The model generates the income that sustains the enterprise, so a weak model cannot succeed even when the product itself is good.

Role of the model in the business plan

The business model is the core logic that the business plan turns into operational detail and financial forecasts.

  • The business plan explains the model in full, and the financial plan tests whether its revenue streams cover its cost structure.
  • The canvas sketches the model on one page, which makes it easier to discuss with investors, staff and advisers.
  • Changing one element of the model, such as the sales channel, affects costs, customer relationships and revenue, so the plan must be updated to match.

The canvas as a tool

The business model canvas is a one-page tool with nine linked blocks that describe how an enterprise creates, delivers and captures value.

  • The canvas is built around the customer and the offer, with the blocks grouped into the customer side, the infrastructure that supports the offer, and the financial side.
  • Because the blocks are linked, a change in one block, such as a new supplier, is traced through the others to see its full effect.
  • The canvas is a starting point for analysis rather than a finished plan, so it is revised as the business learns from customers.

Customer side: four blocks

The customer-side blocks describe who the business serves, what it offers them and how it reaches and keeps them.

  • Customer segments: the distinct groups of people or organisations the business aims to serve, such as students, families or small firms.
  • Value proposition: the bundle of products and services that solves a customer problem or meets a need, and the reason customers choose it, such as a fast, cheap commute.
  • Channels: the ways the business communicates with customers and delivers the offer, such as shops, a website, an app or a sales team.
  • Customer relationships: the type of relationship kept with each segment, such as personal advice, self-service or a loyalty scheme.

Infrastructure: three blocks

The infrastructure blocks describe what the business needs in order to deliver its offer.

  • Key resources: the assets needed to deliver the value proposition, such as premises, staff, technology, patents or capital; a bakery needs its ovens and skilled bakers.
  • Key activities: the most important things the business must do to make the model work, such as production, software development or distribution; a courier firm's key activity is route planning.
  • Key partners: the suppliers and outside organisations whose help the business relies on, such as outsourced providers and alliances; a food brand may rely on a co-packer.

Financial side: two blocks and the test of viability

The financial blocks show how income is earned and what it costs, and their balance decides whether the model is sound.

  • Revenue streams: the sources of income from each customer segment, such as sales, subscriptions, fees or advertising; a gym earns from memberships and personal training.
  • Cost structure: the main costs of running the model, such as wages, rent, materials, marketing and finance costs; a shop's cost structure is dominated by rent and stock.
  • A model is financially sound when its revenue streams exceed its cost structure by enough to sustain the business over time.

Worked example: a completed canvas

This canvas describes Ryanair in general terms to show how the nine blocks link together.

  • Customer segments: price-sensitive leisure travellers and some business travellers on short-haul routes.
  • Value proposition: low fares on short-haul flights, with optional extras such as bags and seat selection charged separately.
  • Channels: mainly the company's own website and app, which keeps distribution costs low.
  • Customer relationships: largely self-service online booking, with limited personal contact.
  • Revenue streams: fares, plus fees for extras and onboard sales.
  • Key resources: a fleet of aircraft, pilots and cabin crew, and the brand.
  • Key activities: flight operations, aircraft maintenance and route planning.
  • Key partners: airports, fuel suppliers and aircraft manufacturers.
  • Cost structure: fuel, airport charges, staff and the cost of aircraft ownership or leasing.

How ethics and sustainability enter the model

Ethical and sustainable choices can be built into the value proposition, the cost structure and the choice of partners.

  • A value proposition can include sustainable materials or fair trade sourcing, which some customers will pay a premium for.
  • Responsible sourcing adds cost to the cost structure, and the model must show that customers' willingness to pay covers it.
  • The conduct of key partners affects the reputation of the whole model, so partners are chosen with care.

Why a sound model underpins success

A business with a good product can still fail if its model does not earn enough from customers to cover costs.

  • A clear model shows where income comes from, so managers can concentrate on the revenue streams that matter most.
  • A model that depends on one partner or one revenue source is fragile if that source falls away.
  • A model can be tested on a small scale before large sums are spent, which reduces the risk of a failed launch.
  • Successful businesses review their models as customers, technology and costs change, rather than treating the model as fixed.

Key terms

business model
The way a business creates, delivers and captures value, including how it earns income from its customers.
business model canvas
A one-page tool with nine linked blocks that shows how an enterprise creates, delivers and captures value.
value creation
Transforming inputs such as materials, skills and knowledge into products or services that customers want.
value capture
The process by which a business earns income from customers that is enough to cover its costs and make a profit.
customer segments
The distinct groups of people or organisations that a business aims to serve.
value proposition
The bundle of products and services that solves a customer problem or meets a need, and the reason customers choose it.
revenue streams
The sources of income a business earns from each customer segment, such as sales, subscriptions or fees.
cost structure
The main costs of running a business model, such as wages, rent, materials, marketing and finance costs.
key partners
The suppliers and outside organisations whose help a business needs to run its model.
