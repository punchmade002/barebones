Digital technology as a driver of change

Digital technology has changed how businesses sell, serve customers and organise their work, creating both threats and opportunities.

  • E-commerce lets a business sell online to customers anywhere, reducing the need for physical premises in every location.
  • Platforms connect buyers and sellers, or users and content, so the platform owner earns income from the connections it makes possible.
  • Data collected from customers allows businesses to target products and to understand buying patterns that were previously hidden.
  • Artificial intelligence and automation cut labour costs and speed up tasks such as customer service and stock control, but they can also displace some jobs.
  • Digital change can have internal drivers, such as a business seeking lower costs, or external drivers, such as customers who now shop online.

Marketplace model

A marketplace model brings buyers and sellers together on a digital platform and earns income by charging a fee on transactions.

  • It earns income through a commission or listing fee on each sale or booking completed through the platform.
  • Key characteristics: the platform owns little or no stock, grows as more buyers and sellers join, and benefits from network effects.
  • Advantages: low inventory risk, rapid scalability and a wide reach that gives small sellers access to customers they could not reach alone.
  • Disadvantages: the platform must attract enough users on both sides, and it faces disputes over quality, delivery and fees.
  • Example: Etsy, an online marketplace where independent sellers list handmade and vintage goods for a commission.

Subscription model in the digital economy

Digital subscription models charge a recurring fee for continuous access to online content, software or services.

  • Revenue comes from monthly or annual payments, often with a free or cheaper tier that brings users in before they pay.
  • Key characteristics: revenue is predictable, the service is updated continuously and keeping customers is central to success.
  • Advantages: predictable income, a close link to customers through usage data and lower marketing costs for existing users.
  • Disadvantages: subscribers can cancel with little effort, so the business must keep adding value, and cancellations are costly.
  • Example: Netflix, which provides on-demand film and television for a regular fee.

Crowdfunding as a model and a source of finance

Crowdfunding raises money from many people, usually through an online platform, and it is both a business model and a way of obtaining finance.

  • The platform takes a fee on the funds raised, while the business gains finance from many backers at once.
  • Reward crowdfunding: backers receive a product or perk in return for their contribution, which suits consumer products.
  • Equity crowdfunding: backers buy shares in the business, so they share in its success and its risks.
  • Lending crowdfunding: backers lend money to the business, which repays them with interest over an agreed period.
  • Advantages: finance without a bank loan, a market test for a product before launch and a ready group of supporters.
  • Disadvantages: campaigns can fail to reach their target, platform fees apply, and reward backers may wait a long time for their items.
  • Example: a small food or design business that pre-sells a first product run to backers on a rewards platform.

Advertising-supported model

An advertising-supported model gives users a service free of charge and earns its income by selling access to their attention to advertisers.

  • Advertisers pay for placements, often according to the number of views, clicks or the targeted audience reached.
  • Key characteristics: a large user base is essential, and the business uses data to target adverts more precisely.
  • Advantages: users pay nothing, which widens reach, and income grows as the audience grows.
  • Disadvantages: revenue depends on the advertising market, some users dislike adverts, and privacy concerns can bring tighter regulation.
  • Example: a social media platform or a free search engine that funds its free service through targeted advertising.

Digital models compared with non-digital models

Digital models share the core logic of traditional models, but they differ in cost structure, reach, growth and the data they collect.

  • Similarity: both must create value for customers and capture enough income to cover their costs.
  • Cost structure: digital models often have high fixed costs for software and data, but a low extra cost for each additional user.
  • Reach: a digital business can serve customers in other countries with little additional physical presence.
  • Growth: network and scale effects let digital models grow faster than models that need premises or extra staff for every new customer.
  • Data: digital models collect customer behaviour continuously, which non-digital models rarely do on the same scale.

How Irish businesses have adapted

Irish firms have moved online or added new models in response to customer demand and competition.

  • A traditional retailer may sell online and offer click-and-collect, so customers order from home and collect from a local store.
  • A food producer may launch a subscription box delivered to homes, creating recurring income from loyal customers.
  • Tesco Ireland offers online grocery ordering with home delivery, extending a store-based business into digital channels.

Key terms

digital economy
The part of economic activity based on digital technology, including online trading, platforms and the use of data.
marketplace model
A business model that brings buyers and sellers together on a platform and earns a fee on each transaction.
crowdfunding
Raising money from many individuals, usually online, in exchange for a reward, shares or repayment.
reward crowdfunding
A form of crowdfunding in which backers receive a product or perk in return for their contribution.
advertising-supported model
A business model that provides a service free to users and earns income from advertisers who pay to reach them.
subscription model
A business model that charges a recurring fee for continued access to a product or service.
network effects
The increase in a platform's value to each user as more people join it, which draws in still more users.
e-commerce
Buying and selling goods or services online, using websites, apps or other digital channels.
churn
The rate at which customers stop using or cancel a subscription service, reducing the base of paying users.
