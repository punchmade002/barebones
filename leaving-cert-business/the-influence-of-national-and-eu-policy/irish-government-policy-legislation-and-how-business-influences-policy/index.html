Policy and legislation

Government policy sets out what the state intends to achieve. Legislation is binding law passed by the Oireachtas.

  • A policy is a statement of intent that sets goals and the means of reaching them, and on its own it creates no legal obligation.
  • Legislation is law: a Bill becomes an Act only after it passes through both Houses of the Oireachtas and is signed by the President.
  • Statutory instruments are secondary legislation that ministers make under an Act to fill in detail, such as rules on food hygiene.
  • A policy often becomes law later: a government goal to cut emissions can be written into an Act that sets legally binding targets.
  • A new government can change policy quickly, while legislation stays in force until the Oireachtas amends or repeals it.

Government policy across sectors

The CSO groups businesses into sectors by main activity, such as agriculture, industry, construction, retail and wholesale, transport, and tourism and hospitality. Each policy below reaches some sectors more than others.

  • Climate Action Plan: a plan updated each year under the Climate Action and Low Carbon Development Act 2015, with sector targets for energy, transport, agriculture and buildings.
  • Carbon tax: a charge on fossil fuels that has risen over time, raising fuel costs for transport firms and giving businesses a reason to invest in efficiency.
  • Housing for All: a plan running to 2030 to increase the supply and affordability of homes, which creates demand for builders, developers and building material suppliers.
  • National Development Plan: a multi-year programme of public capital investment in roads, public transport, water and housing, which supports engineering and construction firms.
  • Food Vision 2030: a strategy for the agri-food sector to grow sustainable production and export value, affecting farmers and food processors such as Kerry Group and Glanbia.
  • National Minimum Wage Act 2000: sets the legal minimum hourly pay, so any change alters labour costs in low-paid sectors such as hospitality and retail.
  • Policy effects are seldom one-sided: a carbon tax increase may raise a haulier's costs while encouraging investment in electric vans.

How business influences policy

Business shapes policy through organised groups and direct engagement, not only through voting.

  • Representative bodies such as IBEC, ISME and Chambers Ireland put the views of employers, small firms and local business communities to government.
  • Sector bodies, such as the Irish Farmers' Association and Food and Drink Industry Ireland, lobby on issues specific to their industry.
  • The Irish Congress of Trade Unions represents workers and takes part in consultation on pay, employment law and social policy.
  • Public consultations and written submissions let firms comment on draft policies before they are finalised.
  • Pre-Budget submissions to the Department of Finance and to Oireachtas committees can influence which tax and spending measures are included.
  • Lobbying means contacting politicians and officials to influence a decision, and it is a normal part of the policy process.
  • Social dialogue is the continuing exchange between government, employers and unions on economic and social issues, and it is less formal than the national partnership agreements of the past.

Regulation of lobbying

The Regulation of Lobbying Act 2015 makes lobbying more transparent by requiring registration and returns of activity.

  • Certain lobbyists must register on the Register of Lobbying and file returns describing the matters they lobbied on.
  • The Register is maintained by the Standards in Public Office Commission and can be searched by the public.
  • The Act covers contact with designated public officials, including ministers and senior officials, so lobbying of them is recorded.
  • Transparency lets citizens, journalists and rival interests see which groups are trying to influence a decision.
  • The Act creates offences for breaches, so a business that lobbies must know whether it is required to register.

Evaluating business influence on policy

Business influence can improve policy but can also distort it, so an evaluation should weigh both sides.

  • Advantage: business brings practical knowledge of markets, costs and jobs, which can produce policy that firms can actually implement.
  • Advantage: consultation gives government evidence it might not otherwise have, which can improve the quality of legislation.
  • Disadvantage: large firms with dedicated lobbying staff can gain more access than small firms, so policy may favour big business.
  • Disadvantage: consumers, workers and the environment can be under-represented when business groups dominate a debate.
  • Interest groups compete with one another, so a proposal may face pressure from employers, unions and environmental groups at the same time.

Key terms

Government policy
A plan of action by the government that sets out goals and the means of achieving them.
Legislation
Laws passed by the Oireachtas that bind individuals, businesses and public bodies.
Statutory instrument
Secondary legislation made by a minister under the authority of an Act to set out detailed rules.
Sector of the economy
A group of businesses that share a main activity, classified by the CSO, such as agriculture or retail.
Interest group
An organised body that represents the shared views of a particular group of people or businesses to government.
Lobbying
Communicating with politicians and public officials in order to influence a decision.
Social dialogue
Continuing discussion between government, employers and trade unions on economic and social matters.
Register of Lobbying
A public record of registered lobbyists and their lobbying activity, maintained by the Standards in Public Office Commission.
