TARGET pe-ethics-fair-play-s2: 2. Codes of ethics
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.0.b] Every sport needs shared rules about how people behave, not just rules about how the game is played. A code of ethics writes those expectations down so that everyone knows them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.0.0] A code of ethics is a written set of principles and standards of behaviour expected of everyone involved in a sport, including players, coaches, parents, spectators and officials.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.0.1] It is usually published by the sport's national governing body (the organisation that runs the sport across the country) or by a club.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.0.2] A code of conduct is the more detailed part: it sets out the specific behaviours expected of each group involved in the sport, so each person knows exactly what is asked of them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.1.b] A code is not paperwork for its own sake. Each reason below answers a real problem that sport faces when people behave badly.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.1.0] They protect participants, especially children, because young people are the most vulnerable to poor treatment in sport.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.1.1] They promote fair play (playing honestly within the rules) and respect between everyone involved.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.1.2] They set clear standards of behaviour for everyone involved, so nobody can claim they did not know what was expected.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.1.3] They give the club or governing body a basis for discipline, because a person who breaks the written code can be dealt with against a standard that was agreed in advance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.1.4] They protect the sport's image, because a sport known for abuse or cheating loses members, volunteers and public respect.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.2.b] In Ireland the protection of children in sport is organised at three levels: a national code, the governing bodies of each sport, and the individual club.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.2.0] Sport Ireland publishes the Code of Ethics and Good Practice for Children's Sport, which is child-centred (it puts the child's needs first) and promotes safe sport.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.2.1] National governing bodies adopt codes of conduct and child safeguarding policies (the rules and procedures that keep children safe from harm) for their own sport.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.2.2] Clubs appoint a children's officer or a designated liaison person for child safeguarding, so that there is a named person in the club who deals with child safeguarding concerns.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.3.b] Participants are the players themselves. Their code is about how they treat the game, the people they play against and the people who run it.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.3.0] Play by the rules and within the spirit of the game, which means respecting what the rules are trying to achieve, not just their wording.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.3.1] Respect opponents and officials (referees, umpires and other match officials), because the game cannot happen without them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.3.2] Never cheat, never dope (use banned performance-enhancing substances) and never abuse anyone.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.3.3] Do your best and be a good team-mate.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.4.b] Parents are not on the pitch, but their behaviour shapes how a child experiences sport. Their code is about supporting the child without adding pressure.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.4.0] Encourage the child rather than pressure them, because pressure from a parent can take the fun out of playing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.4.1] Focus on enjoyment and effort rather than only the result, so the child learns that trying matters as well as winning.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.4.2] Respect the decisions of coaches and officials, even when disagreeing with them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.4.3] Model good behaviour on the sideline, because children copy how the adults around them behave.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.4.4] Support the club.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.5.b] Spectators watch rather than play, but their noise and behaviour affect players and officials directly. Their code is short and about respect.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.5.0] Applaud good play by both sides, not only by the team you support.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.5.1] Never abuse players or officials.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.5.2] Respect the venue and its rules.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.6.b] Coaches have power over players and are role models for them. Their code is the longest because their influence is the greatest.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.6.0] Put the welfare of players first, ahead of winning.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.6.1] Treat all players fairly.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.6.2] Be qualified and trained in child safeguarding (protecting children from harm), because coaches spend time with young players.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.6.3] Model fair play in their own conduct, so players see the behaviour they are asked to copy.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.6.4] Never encourage cheating or doping.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.6.5] Use positive coaching, praising effort and correct behaviour.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.7.b] Club officials (the committee members and others who run the club) are responsible for the whole organisation. Their code covers how the club is run and how problems are handled.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.7.0] Run the club fairly and transparently, meaning decisions are made openly and not in secret.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.7.1] Implement child safeguarding policies, so the written rules are actually put into practice.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.7.2] Ensure coaches are qualified and facilities are safe, because the club is responsible for the people and places its members use.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.7.3] Deal with complaints and discipline consistently, so that everyone is treated by the same standard.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.8.b] An exam may ask you to write statements for a code. Each one has to turn a principle into a clear instruction for a particular group.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.8.0] Each statement should comply with the principles of ethical practice, which are set out in the notes on principles of ethical practice.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.8.1] Each statement should name who it applies to and what that person must do, so it is a clear instruction.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.8.2] Example: Players will accept the referee's decision without dissent (open disagreement).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.8.3] Example: Coaches will give every player a fair opportunity to take part.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.9.b] A code lists what people must do. Sportsmanship is the attitude behind it: how a good sport behaves when the result is at stake.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.9.0] Definition: playing within the rules and the spirit of the game, with respect, honesty and grace in winning and losing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.9.1] Example: helping a fallen opponent up, which shows respect even in competition.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.9.2] Example: kicking the ball out of play when an opponent is injured, so that play stops and the player can be treated.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.9.3] Example: shaking hands with opponents and applauding them at the end of a match, which shows grace whether you win or lose.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.10.b] Gamesmanship sits in a grey area between fair play and cheating. The player stays inside the rules on paper but uses tactics that go against their spirit.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.10.0] Definition: bending the rules, or using dubious, psychological or time-wasting tactics, to gain an advantage without technically cheating.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.10.1] Gamesmanship is not outright cheating: doping, for example, is cheating rather than gamesmanship.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.10.2] Time-wasting: deliberately delaying a restart of play, for instance to run down the clock when ahead.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.10.3] Feigning injury (pretending to be hurt), or diving to win a free (falling over on purpose to get a free kick or free).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.10.4] Psychological tactics: sledging an opponent (insulting or taunting them to put them off), or unsettling them before a serve.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.11.b] Engagement means people taking part and staying involved in a sport, as players, volunteers or officials. The way people behave decides whether they stay.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.11.0] Sportsmanship creates a positive, safe and enjoyable environment that keeps people involved.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.11.1] Sportsmanship attracts new participants and volunteers, because people want to join a club that treats its members well.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.11.2] Gamesmanship can sour the experience and cause conflict between players, teams and officials.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.11.3] Gamesmanship can lead to drop-out, especially of young players and officials, who may decide the sport is not worth the stress.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.11.4] Gamesmanship can damage the sport's image.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s2.11.5] Gamesmanship may win in the short term, but it erodes engagement over time.

CANDIDATES pe-ethics-fair-play-s1: 1. Principles of ethical practice
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.0.b] Every sport has written rules, but not every situation is covered by them. Ethics and fair play describe how people are expected to behave in those gaps and within the rules.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.0.0] Ethics in sport means the moral principles about what is right and wrong that guide behaviour in sport, so they shape what players, coaches and officials choose to do, not just what the rulebook says.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.0.1] Fair play means playing within the rules and the spirit of the game (the idea behind the rules, not only their wording), with respect for others.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.0.2] Ethical practice in sport rests on four principles: integrity, respect, fairness and equity. Each one is taught in the blocks that follow, with its definition, examples, breaches (ways of breaking it) and why it matters.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.1.b] Integrity is the principle about honesty. It is tested most when breaking the rules would go unnoticed, so it is about what a person does when nobody would find out.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.1.0] Definition: honesty and strong moral principles, shown by doing the right thing when no one is watching.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.1.1] In practice: owning up to touching the ball last, even when the referee has not seen it, because the honest call is made even though it costs the player something.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.1.2] In practice: not diving or feigning injury (pretending to be fouled or hurt) to win a free or a penalty.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.1.3] In practice: refusing to dope (use banned performance-enhancing substances), even when no one is watching.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.1.4] Breach: doping, or match-fixing, where the outcome of a contest is arranged in advance for gain.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.1.5] Why it matters: integrity lets players, officials and spectators trust the sport and its results, because they know the outcome was not secretly manipulated.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.1.6] Applied to Gaelic football: a player who tells the referee about a foul the referee missed shows integrity, even though it costs the player's team a free.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.2.b] Respect is about how you treat everyone and everything involved in a game, not only the people on the opposing team. It is what keeps a game safe and pleasant to be part of.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.2.0] Definition: regard for opponents, team-mates, officials, coaches, spectators, the rules and oneself.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.2.1] In practice: accepting officials' decisions, even when a player disagrees with them, because the official's role is to apply the rules and the game cannot work if every call is argued.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.2.2] In practice: shaking hands with opponents and officials at the end of a match, which shows regard for them whatever the result.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.2.3] Breach: abuse of officials, sledging (insulting or taunting an opponent to unsettle them), or violence towards an opponent.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.2.4] Why it matters: a safe, positive environment keeps players and officials involved, so clubs can retain them (stop them leaving the sport).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.2.5] Applied to hurling: a player who stays calm after a hard tackle, rather than retaliating (hitting back), shows respect for the opponent and the rules.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.3.b] Fairness is about a level playing field. If everyone is treated and judged the same way, the result means something.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.3.0] Definition: everyone competes under the same rules and conditions, and no one seeks an unfair advantage.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.3.1] In practice: consistent refereeing, applying the same rules to both teams, so neither side is helped or hindered by the official.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.3.2] In practice: fair team selection, based on ability and commitment rather than favouritism.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.3.3] Breach: cheating, which gives one competitor an advantage the rules do not allow.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.3.4] Breach: win-at-all-costs coaching, where winning is put above the rules and above the players' welfare (their health and wellbeing).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.3.5] Why it matters: the result reflects ability and effort rather than an unfair advantage.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.3.6] Applied to athletics: every runner in a race starts under the same start rules and runs the same distance, so the placings reflect running ability.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.4.b] Equity is easily confused with equality, but the two differ. Equity recognises that people start from different positions, so some need different support to get the same chance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.4.0] Definition: giving everyone access and opportunity according to their needs, so that differences in gender, ability, disability, ethnicity or income do not exclude people.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.4.1] Equity differs from equality: equality treats everyone the same, while equity gives people what they need for a fair chance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.4.2] In practice: equal facilities and coaching for girls' teams as for boys' teams, so gender does not limit the standard of training a team receives.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.4.3] Breach: discrimination (unfair treatment of a person because of who they are), such as excluding a player because of gender, disability, ethnicity or income.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.4.4] Why it matters: removing barriers lets people from every group take part and compete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.4.5] Applied to Gaelic football: a club that gives a player with a disability adapted equipment (equipment changed to suit the player's needs) and coaching gives that player the same chance to take part.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.5.b] Ethical practice does not rest with players alone. Everyone connected with a sport has a part to play, and each group's role is different.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.5.0] Participants: responsible for their own conduct towards opponents, team-mates and officials.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.5.1] Coaches and leaders: model good behaviour (set an example by how they act themselves) and teach fair play.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.5.2] Officials: apply the rules impartially (without favouring either side) to every competitor.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.5.3] Parents and spectators: responsible for their behaviour on the sideline and in the stands.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.5.4] National governing bodies (NGBs) and sports bodies: write codes (written standards of behaviour), provide education, and impose sanctions (punishments) on those who breach the principles.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.6.b] Upholding the four principles is not just about being nice. It affects the people playing, the people watching and the future of the sport itself.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.6.0] Safety and welfare: ethical conduct protects participants from injury and abuse.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.6.1] Enjoyment and participation: a sport with fair play keeps people playing and attracts new players.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.6.2] Credibility of results: results carry weight (are believed and respected) only when every competitor has followed the rules.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.6.3] Sponsorship and image: sponsors (businesses that pay to be linked with a sport) and the public judge a sport by its conduct, so ethical behaviour protects its image and sponsorship.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s1.6.4] Values for life: fair play teaches honesty, respect and fairness that carry over beyond sport.
