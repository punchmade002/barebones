[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.0] Role: a performer's specific position, function and responsibilities within an activity or team, such as goalkeeper, centre-back, point guard, setter in volleyball or lead dancer.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.1] Physical demands: the fitness components a role relies on, such as endurance, power, agility, speed or reaction time.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.2] Personal demands: the psychological and interpersonal qualities a role needs, such as concentration, decision-making, communication, composure, resilience and responsibility.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.3] Technical demands: the skills and tactical knowledge a role needs to perform.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.0] Physical: power to dive and jump, agility to change direction quickly, and reaction time to a shot struck at close range.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.1] Personal: concentration through long periods with little action, then sharp focus when a shot comes.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.2] Personal: composure after conceding a goal, and leadership in directing the defensive line on positioning.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.3] Technical: handling, meaning catching and holding the ball safely, and distribution, meaning throwing or kicking to team-mates to start attacks.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.4] Technical: positioning, which reduces the angle and space the attacker can use to score.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.0] Physical: high cardio-respiratory endurance to cover ground and repeat runs across a whole game.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.1] Physical: acceleration to win loose balls and reach space before an opponent.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.2] Personal: decision-making about when to hold the ball, when to hand-pass and when to run into space.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.3] Personal: resilience to keep working after a missed chance or a turnover.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.4] Technical: hand-passing and kicking accuracy under pressure from an opponent.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.5] Technical: catching a high ball while an opponent is closing in.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.0] Physical: speed and agility to get past defenders and to stay with fast opponents on defence.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.1] Personal: decision-making in a fraction of a second about whether to pass, shoot or drive.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.2] Personal: leadership, because the point guard directs the team's offence and organises team-mates on the court.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.3] Technical: ball-handling, meaning dribbling and protecting the ball under pressure.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.4] Technical: passing to set up team-mates for scoring chances.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.0] Personal: concentration to hold the sequence and its timing for the whole routine.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.1] Personal: confidence to perform in front of an audience.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.2] Personal: responsibility to keep the group in time.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.3] Technical: accurate execution of the choreographed movement, with clean body alignment and line.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.4] Technical: learning the sequence quickly and performing it to the timing of the music.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.0] Affiliation: belonging to a team, club or group and identifying with it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.1] Formal affiliation to a club or governing body also gives access to competitions, insurance and coaching.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.2] Team building develops trust, communication and cohesion through shared goals, team activities and clear roles.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.3] Task cohesion: team members working together towards a shared goal.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.4] Social cohesion: team members getting on well with one another.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.6.0] A responsible captain or leader communicates clearly and sets an example in effort and behaviour.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.6.1] A responsible leader supports team-mates, keeps discipline and puts the team's goals before personal ones.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.6.2] A leader who undermines the coach's plan or shows a poor attitude weakens the group, so responsible leaders support decisions even when they disagree with them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.7.0] Players know their responsibilities, so every area of the pitch or court is covered.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.7.1] Players cover for each other when a team-mate is out of position or tired.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.7.2] Good communication lets players call for the ball, warn of an opponent and adjust during play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.0] Scouting: watching the opponent in previous matches, live or on video.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.1] Video analysis of previous matches shows patterns, such as where a defence leaves space or how a player reacts under pressure.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.2] Notational statistics record how often a player or team uses a skill or position, so repeated patterns can be counted.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.3] Early observation: watching the opening minutes of a game to confirm what scouting suggested.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.b] Each exploitation is a strategy; the strategies themselves are in the notes on Structures, strategies and compositional elements, and here each is linked only to the weakness it exploits.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.0] Technical weakness, such as a weak backhand or a weaker foot: direct serves, passes or shots to that side.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.1] Physical weakness, such as slow defenders: play balls into the space behind them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.2] Physical weakness, such as poor stamina late in a game: run at the tired defender late in the game.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.3] Tactical weakness, such as a high defensive line: play balls into the space behind it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.4] Tactical weakness, such as poor marking at set pieces: attack set pieces with a planned routine that creates a free attacker.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.5] Psychological weakness, such as losing composure under pressure: keep pressure on the player, forcing hurried decisions and errors.

CANDIDATES pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1: 1. Structures, strategies and compositional elements
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.0] Structure: the organised arrangement of players or performers, such as a team formation, positional roles, a set-piece arrangement or a relay order.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.1] Strategy: an overall plan, made before the performance, that uses the team's or performer's strengths and the opponent's weaknesses to achieve a goal.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.2] Tactics: the specific actions used during the performance to carry out the strategy.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.3] One strategy can be carried out through several tactics; when a tactic fails, the tactic changes while the strategy usually stays the same.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.0] Team formation: a fixed arrangement of players by line, which sets how many defend, midfield and attack.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.1] 4-4-2 in soccer: four defenders, four midfielders and two forwards.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.2] 4-3-3 in soccer: four defenders, three midfielders and three forwards, with wide attackers stretching the defence.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.3] Zonal marking: each defender guards an area of the pitch or court and marks any opponent who enters it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.4] Man-to-man marking: each defender is assigned one opponent to mark wherever that opponent moves.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.5] Basketball 2-3 zone: two players stationed higher and three lower, covering areas of the court rather than individual players.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.b] A set play is a planned, rehearsed arrangement used at a stoppage or restart, so each player knows their position before play resumes.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.0] Kick-outs in Gaelic football: the goalkeeper restarts play from the goal area while team-mates position themselves to win or receive the ball.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.1] Line-outs in rugby: forwards form two lines and the ball is thrown in straight between them, with a lifted jumper contesting for it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.2] Short corners in hockey: a planned routine from a set position, in which a short pass is played to a team-mate who shoots or passes into the danger area.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.3] A rehearsed restart can create a scoring chance before the opposing defence has organised itself.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.0] Relay running order: the coach fixes the order of runners in advance, matching each runner's strengths, such as a fast starter or a runner who changes the baton well at speed, to the legs of the race.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.1] Lanes and order: the runner in each lane and leg is set before the race, so every changeover is planned.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.2] Badminton doubles, side-by-side formation in defence: each player covers one half of the court.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.3] Badminton doubles, front-and-back formation in attack: one player stays near the net while the partner stays back to hit smashes.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.0] Width and depth: wide players stretch the defence sideways while runners move into the space in front of or behind it, so defenders cover more ground.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.1] Overlap run: a team-mate runs outside the player with the ball, creating an extra attacker on that flank.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.2] Fast break in basketball: after a defensive rebound or turnover, the team pushes the ball up the court before the defence is set, using speed and quick passes.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.3] Pick and roll in basketball: the ball-handler dribbles towards a team-mate who sets a screen, then the screener rolls to the basket, forcing a defender to choose between two players.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.4] Playing to the space behind a high defensive line: passes and runs are timed into the gap behind defenders who have pushed up, exploiting slow pace or poor recovery.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.0] Pressing: the team applies pressure close to the opponents' goal to win the ball high up the pitch or court, exploiting a team that struggles under pressure.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.1] Pressing risk: the space left behind the pressing players must be covered, or the press is easily bypassed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.2] Deep, compact block: players defend in a tight shape close to their own goal, denying central space and forcing the opponents to play wide.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.3] Double-teaming: two defenders close down one key player, forcing the ball away from the playmaker and exploiting a team that depends on one player.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.6.0] Serve-and-volley: after serving, the server moves to the net and volleys the return before it bounces, shortening the rally and exploiting a returner who struggles against a player at the net.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.6.1] Targeting the backhand: the ball is hit to an opponent's weaker side, so the return is harder to control.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.6.2] Moving the opponent around the court: shots alternate between corners, the baseline and the net, so the opponent covers distance and leaves a gap.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.0] Pacing: the athlete distributes effort over the race so that energy lasts to the finish.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.1] Even pace: a steady speed throughout saves energy for a fast finish and avoids early fatigue.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.2] Fast start: an early burst gains position but risks fatigue later in the race.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.3] Race tactics: sitting in behind a leading group to save energy, then kicking for the line in the final stage of the race.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.b] Compositional elements are the design elements used to build a routine: action, space, dynamics, relationships and choreographic form.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.0] Action: what moves are performed, such as travelling steps, balances, turns, jumps, gestures or gymnastic skills.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.1] Space: levels (high, medium and low), pathways (straight, curved or zigzag), direction (forward, sideways or backward) and formations such as a line, circle or diagonal.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.2] Dynamics: how the movement is performed, through speed, force (strong or light) and flow (smooth or sudden).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.3] Relationships with other performers: mirroring, following, or meeting and parting.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.4] Relationships with props: a prop such as a hoop or ribbon changes the shape of the movement.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.5] Relationships with music: movement is timed on the beat or set against it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.0] Choreographic form is the overall structure of the piece; the main forms are motif and development, canon, unison, binary and rondo.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.1] Motif and development: a short movement idea is repeated and altered by changing its speed, level, direction or dynamics.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.2] Canon: the same movement phrase is performed by each dancer in turn, starting at different times.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.3] Unison: all performers perform the same movement at the same time.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.4] Binary form: two contrasting sections, A then B.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.5] Rondo: a recurring theme alternates with contrasting sections, in the pattern A B A C A.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.6] Contrast: a slow, sustained section followed by a fast, sudden one, so each section stands out.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.7] Climax: energy and complexity build to a peak, usually late in the routine.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.8] Transitions: movements that link sections so the change of pattern looks smooth.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.9] Applying the elements to a group sequence: the action is a travelling phrase, the space uses a diagonal formation, the dynamics change from sustained to sudden, and performers mirror each other on the beat.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.0] Video review: the coach replays the performance to check whether players held their positions and carried out the tactics.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.1] Notational analysis: tallying passes, turnovers and scores from each strategy shows which one worked.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.2] Checklists: each element of the strategy is checked against key principles, such as whether the team kept its shape in defence.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.3] Observation by a coach or peer: a second observer notices what the player cannot see during play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.4] Criteria: whether the structure or strategy achieved its aim, whether each role was carried out, and how the opponents responded.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.5] The analysis methods are described in the notes on Methods of analysis; here they are applied to a specific structure or strategy.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.0] Reasons to modify: the score and time remaining, changes by the opponent, weather or pitch and court conditions, injury or dismissal, fatigue, or a strategy that is not working.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.1] Change formation: an extra defender is added to the back line when protecting a lead, making the defence more compact.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.2] Switch the marking system, for example from zonal to man-to-man marking when one attacker is dominating the game.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.3] Change the point of attack: play switches from a blocked centre to a wing where the defence is thinner.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.4] Pick and roll becomes pick and pop: when the defender stops the roll to the basket, the screener steps out to a shooting position instead, pulling that defender out to guard the shot.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.5] Smaller space: a routine is adapted by using levels and shorter pathways in place of wide travel.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.6] Different music: phrases are shortened or lengthened so that each motif fits the new tempo and beat count.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s4: 4. Applying the fitness components
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.0] Needs analysis: working out which fitness components an activity, position or role demands by examining its duration, intensity, pattern of work and rest, the movements involved and the energy systems used.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.1] The performer's current fitness is then compared with those demands, usually by fitness testing, to find the gaps to train.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.2] Examining the extent: rank each component as essential, important or less important for the activity, and justify each with a specific moment of play or performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.3] Positions and roles differ within one activity: a soccer goalkeeper needs reaction time, power and agility more than cardio-respiratory endurance, while a midfielder needs the reverse.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (essential): players cover long distances over two halves and must recover between efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.1] Speed (essential): to win the race to a breaking ball or get away from a marker.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.2] Agility (essential): to side-step a tackler or turn quickly in defence.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.3] Power (important): to jump for a high catch and to kick long.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.4] Muscular endurance (important): legs keep working for repeated sprints and challenges late in the game.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.0] Flexibility (essential): for splits, walkovers and extended body lines.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.1] Strength (essential): to support body weight in balances and on the hands.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.2] Balance (essential): for held positions and landings.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.3] Power (essential): for jumps, tumbles and vaults.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.4] Co-ordination (essential): to link elements fluently.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.5] Cardio-respiratory endurance (less important): routines are short.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (essential): the event lasts many minutes or hours at a steady pace.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.1] Muscular endurance (essential): the legs repeat thousands of strides.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.2] Body composition (important): a low body-fat percentage reduces the weight carried.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.3] Reaction time (less important): a slow start matters little over a long race.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.b] Fitness can be trained inside the activity itself, while skills are practised under the fatigue and pressure of competition.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.0] Methods: conditioned and small-sided games, game-speed drills and skill circuits.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.1] Example: a small-sided possession game with a two-touch limit develops cardio-respiratory endurance, agility and speed while players pass and move.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.2] Example: a dribbling relay through cones develops speed, agility and co-ordination.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.3] Benefit: specificity, because players use the same movements, energy systems and decisions as in competition.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.4] Benefit: it is motivating and time-efficient, and trains skill under fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.5] Limitation: intensity and overload are harder to control precisely, and some players work less than others.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.6] Limitation: it may not develop maximal strength or flexibility, so it is combined with training outside the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.0] A combined approach develops a health-related and a performance-related component in the same activity or session.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.1] Designing one: name the two components, describe what the performer does with sets, repetitions or time, state how it progresses, explain how each component is trained, and link it to the activity's demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.2] Example, flexibility (HRF) and balance (PRF): a single-leg balance-and-reach routine, standing on one leg and reaching the free foot out slowly to points around the body through a full range, progressing to an unstable surface.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.3] Example, muscular endurance (HRF) and agility (PRF): a circuit alternating ladder footwork drills with body-weight squats and lunges, with timed work and rest at each station.
