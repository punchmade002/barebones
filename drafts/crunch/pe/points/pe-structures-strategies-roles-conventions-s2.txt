TARGET pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2: 2. Roles and relationships
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.b] Every performer in a team or group activity has a job to do. To analyse that job, split what it asks of the performer into three separate headings: physical, personal and technical.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.0] Role: a performer's specific position, function and responsibilities within an activity or team, such as goalkeeper, centre-back, point guard, setter in volleyball or lead dancer.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.1] Physical demands: the fitness components a role relies on, such as endurance, power, agility, speed or reaction time. A midfielder, for example, needs cardio-respiratory endurance (heart and lungs supplying oxygen for long periods).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.2] Personal demands: the psychological and interpersonal qualities (mental and people skills) a role needs, such as concentration, decision-making, communication, leadership, composure (staying calm), resilience (recovering from setbacks) and responsibility.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.0.3] Technical demands: the skills and tactical knowledge a role needs to perform, such as a goalkeeper's handling and distribution, a point guard's ball-handling and passing, or a setter's volleying accuracy.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.b] The goalkeeper's role mixes explosive movement with concentration through long periods with little action.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.0] Physical: power to dive and jump, agility to change direction quickly, and reaction time to a shot struck at close range, because there is almost no time to see the ball and respond.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.1] Personal: concentration through long periods with little action, then sharp focus when a shot comes.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.2] Personal: composure after conceding a goal, so one mistake does not lead to more, and leadership in directing the defensive line on positioning.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.3] Technical: handling, meaning catching and holding the ball safely, and distribution, meaning throwing or kicking to team-mates to start attacks.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.1.4] Technical: positioning, which reduces the angle and space the attacker can use to score.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.b] The midfielder is involved in both attack and defence and needs endurance to cover ground across a whole game, so this role contrasts strongly with the goalkeeper.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.0] Physical: high cardio-respiratory endurance to cover ground and repeat runs across a whole game, because the midfielder is involved in both attack and defence.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.1] Physical: acceleration to win loose balls and reach space before an opponent.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.2] Personal: decision-making about when to hold the ball, when to hand-pass and when to run into space.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.3] Personal: resilience to keep working after a missed chance or a turnover (losing possession).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.4] Technical: hand-passing and kicking accuracy under pressure from an opponent.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.2.5] Technical: catching a high ball while an opponent is closing in.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.b] The point guard is the player who runs the team's attack, so quick thinking and control of the ball are key demands alongside speed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.0] Physical: speed and agility to get past defenders and to stay with fast opponents on defence.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.1] Personal: decision-making in a fraction of a second about whether to pass, shoot or drive (run towards the basket).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.2] Personal: leadership, because the point guard directs the team's offence and organises team-mates on the court.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.3] Technical: ball-handling, meaning dribbling and protecting the ball under pressure.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.3.4] Technical: passing to set up team-mates for scoring chances.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.b] Some questions tell you not to refer to fitness. Then you use only personal and technical demands, and a lead dancer shows how much a role can ask beyond fitness.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.0] Personal: concentration to hold the sequence and its timing for the whole routine.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.1] Personal: confidence to perform in front of an audience, so nerves do not spoil the performance.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.2] Personal: responsibility to keep the group in time, because the other dancers follow the lead.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.3] Technical: accurate execution of the choreographed movement (the planned steps), with clean body alignment and line.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.4.4] Technical: learning the sequence quickly and performing it to the timing of the music.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.b] A group performs better when its members feel they belong and work well together. Affiliation and team building are the ways a team creates that.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.0] Affiliation: belonging to a team, club or group and identifying with it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.1] Formal affiliation to a club or governing body (the organisation that runs a sport) also gives access to competitions, insurance and coaching.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.2] Team building develops trust, communication and cohesion (the bond that holds a group together) through shared goals, team activities and clear roles.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.3] Task cohesion: team members working together towards a shared goal, such as winning a match.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.5.4] Social cohesion: team members getting on well with one another, which makes working together easier.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.6.b] A captain or leader has influence over the whole group, so the way they behave can strengthen or damage the team.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.6.0] A responsible captain or leader communicates clearly and sets an example in effort and behaviour, because team-mates copy what the leader does.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.6.1] A responsible leader supports team-mates, keeps discipline and puts the team's goals before personal ones.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.6.2] A leader who undermines the coach's plan or shows a poor attitude weakens the group, so responsible leaders support decisions even when they disagree with them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.7.b] When everyone knows their job and trusts their team-mates, players cover for each other and communicate well.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.7.0] Players know their responsibilities, so every area of the pitch or court is covered and no two players chase the same task.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.7.1] Players cover for each other when a team-mate is out of position or tired, so gaps do not stay open for the opposition.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.7.2] Good communication lets players call for the ball, warn of an opponent and adjust during play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.b] To exploit an opponent you first have to find where they are weak. Teams gather this information before a match and confirm it during play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.0] Scouting: watching the opponent in previous matches, live or on video.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.1] Video analysis of previous matches shows patterns, such as where a defence leaves space or how a player reacts under pressure.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.2] Notational statistics record how often a player or team uses a skill or position, so repeated patterns can be counted rather than guessed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.8.3] Early observation: watching the opening minutes of a game to confirm what scouting suggested.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.b] Weaknesses fall into four types: technical, physical, tactical and psychological. Each type is matched to a strategy that targets it. There is more on the strategies themselves in Structures, strategies and compositional elements.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.0] Technical weakness (a flaw in a skill), such as a weak backhand or a weaker foot: direct serves, passes or shots to that side.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.1] Physical weakness, such as slow defenders: play balls into the space behind them, where their lack of speed leaves them unable to recover.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.2] Physical weakness, such as poor stamina late in a game: run at the tired defender late in the game.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.3] Tactical weakness (a flaw in team organisation), such as a high defensive line: play balls into the space behind it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.4] Tactical weakness, such as poor marking at set pieces (planned restarts like corners and free kicks): attack set pieces with a planned routine that creates a free attacker.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s2.9.5] Psychological weakness, such as losing composure under pressure: keep pressure on the player, forcing hurried decisions and errors.

CANDIDATES pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1: 1. Structures, strategies and compositional elements
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.b] Teams and performers succeed because they organise themselves and plan ahead, not just because they are fit or skilful. Three linked words describe this: structure, strategy and tactics, and each means something different.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.0] Structure is the organised arrangement of players or performers, such as a team formation, positional roles (who plays where), a set-piece arrangement or a relay order. It gives everyone a clear place and job.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.1] Strategy is an overall plan, made before the performance, to achieve a goal. It uses the team's or performer's strengths and the opponent's weaknesses, so the plan is built around what you do well and what the opponent does badly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.2] Tactics are the specific actions used during the performance to carry out the strategy. In short, strategy is the plan and tactics are the moves that deliver it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.0.3] One strategy can be carried out through several tactics. When a tactic fails, the tactic changes while the strategy usually stays the same, because the goal of the plan has not changed, only the way of reaching it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.b] A formation decides where a team's players stand, and a marking system decides how defenders look after attackers. Together they set the basic shape of a team in attack and in defence.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.0] A team formation is a fixed arrangement of players by line, which sets how many defend, how many play in midfield and how many attack. Formations are written as numbers from defence to attack.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.1] 4-4-2 in soccer means four defenders, four midfielders and two forwards, a balanced shape with solid cover in every area of the pitch.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.2] 4-3-3 in soccer means four defenders, three midfielders and three forwards, with wide attackers stretching the defence so that defenders must cover more ground.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.3] Zonal marking means each defender guards an area of the pitch or court and marks any opponent who enters it. Defenders hold their areas rather than following attackers.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.4] Man-to-man marking means each defender is assigned one opponent to mark wherever that opponent moves, so the defender follows that player around the pitch or court.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.1.5] The basketball 2-3 zone has two players stationed higher and three lower, covering areas of the court rather than individual players.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.b] A set play is a planned, rehearsed arrangement used at a stoppage or restart, so each player knows their position before play resumes. It is a structure because the players are arranged in advance.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.0] Kick-outs in Gaelic football: the goalkeeper restarts play from the goal area while team-mates position themselves to win or receive the ball.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.1] Line-outs in rugby: forwards form two lines and the ball is thrown in straight between them, with a lifted jumper contesting for it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.2] Short corners in hockey: a planned routine from a set position, in which a short pass is played to a team-mate who shoots or passes into the danger area (the area in front of goal where a score is most likely).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.2.3] A rehearsed restart can create a scoring chance before the opposing defence has organised itself, which is why teams practise these routines in training.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.b] Structure is not only for big teams. Relay teams and badminton doubles pairs also arrange their performers in advance so that each person's strengths are used.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.0] Relay running order: the coach fixes the order of runners in advance, matching each runner's strengths, such as a fast starter or a runner who changes the baton well at speed, to the legs (sections) of the race.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.1] Lanes and order: the runner in each lane and leg is set before the race, so every changeover (the handing over of the baton) is planned.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.2] Badminton doubles, side-by-side formation in defence: each player covers one half of the court, so the whole court is guarded.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.3.3] Badminton doubles, front-and-back formation in attack: one player stays near the net while the partner stays back to hit smashes (hard downward shots), so the pair attacks from both areas.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.b] Attacking strategies are plans to create scoring chances. Each one works by using the attackers' strengths or by exploiting a weakness in the defence, usually by creating space.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.0] Width and depth: wide players stretch the defence sideways while runners move into the space in front of or behind it, so defenders cover more ground and gaps open.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.1] Overlap run: a team-mate runs outside the player with the ball, creating an extra attacker on that flank (side of the pitch).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.2] Fast break in basketball: after a defensive rebound or turnover (winning the ball), the team pushes the ball up the court before the defence is set, using speed and quick passes.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.3] Pick and roll in basketball: the ball-handler dribbles towards a team-mate who sets a screen (a legal block of a defender), then the screener rolls to the basket, forcing a defender to choose between two players.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.4.4] Playing to the space behind a high defensive line: passes and runs are timed into the gap behind defenders who have pushed up, exploiting slow pace or poor recovery.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.b] Defensive strategies are plans to win the ball back or stop the opponents scoring. Like attacking ones, each is chosen because it suits your players or targets a weakness in the opponents.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.0] Pressing: the team applies pressure close to the opponents' goal to win the ball high up the pitch or court, exploiting a team that struggles under pressure.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.1] Pressing risk: the space left behind the pressing players must be covered, or the press is easily bypassed with one pass.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.2] Deep, compact block: players defend in a tight shape close to their own goal, denying central space and forcing the opponents to play wide.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.3] Double-teaming: two defenders close down one key player, forcing the ball away from the playmaker and exploiting a team that depends on one player.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.5.4] Zonal and man-to-man marking are also defensive strategies. Zonal marking guards areas and man-to-man marking guards players.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.6.b] In net and wall games such as tennis, badminton and squash, players cannot tackle each other, so strategy is about where and how the ball is hit. The aim is to make the opponent's return weak or leave a gap.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.6.0] Serve-and-volley: after serving, the server moves to the net and volleys the return before it bounces, shortening the rally and exploiting a returner who struggles against a player at the net.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.6.1] Targeting the backhand: the ball is hit to an opponent's weaker side, so the return is harder to control.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.6.2] Moving the opponent around the court: shots alternate between corners, the baseline (the back line of the court) and the net, so the opponent covers distance and leaves a gap.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.b] In running and other individual events, the athlete's strategy is how to spend limited energy over the whole race. Starting too fast or too slow can cost the result.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.0] Pacing: the athlete distributes effort over the race so that energy lasts to the finish.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.1] Even pace: a steady speed throughout saves energy for a fast finish and avoids early fatigue (tiredness).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.2] Fast start: an early burst gains position but risks fatigue later in the race, because energy is used up early.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.7.3] Race tactics: sitting in behind a leading group to save energy, then kicking (sprinting) for the line in the final stage of the race.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.b] Compositional elements are the design elements used to build a routine: action, space, dynamics, relationships and choreographic form. They are for dance and gymnastics, while games use structures and strategies.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.0] Action: what moves are performed, such as travelling steps, balances, turns, jumps, gestures or gymnastic skills.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.1] Space: levels (high, medium and low), pathways (straight, curved or zigzag lines travelled), direction (forward, sideways or backward) and formations such as a line, circle or diagonal.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.2] Dynamics: how the movement is performed, through speed, force (strong or light) and flow (smooth or sudden).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.3] Relationships with other performers: mirroring (copying as if in a mirror), following, or meeting and parting.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.4] Relationships with props: a prop such as a hoop or ribbon changes the shape of the movement.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.8.5] Relationships with music: movement is timed on the beat or set against it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.b] Choreographic form is the overall structure of a piece, and approaches such as contrast, climax and transitions shape how it feels. Choreographers choose these so that a routine holds the audience's interest and flows from start to finish.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.0] Choreographic form is the overall structure of the piece; the main forms are motif and development, canon, unison, binary and rondo.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.1] Motif and development: a motif (a short movement idea) is repeated and altered by changing its speed, level, direction or dynamics.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.2] Canon: the same movement phrase is performed by each dancer in turn, starting at different times.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.3] Unison: all performers perform the same movement at the same time.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.4] Binary form: two contrasting sections, A then B.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.5] Rondo: a recurring theme alternates with contrasting sections, in the pattern A B A C A.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.6] Contrast: a slow, sustained section followed by a fast, sudden one, so each section stands out. Climax: energy and complexity build to a peak, usually late in the routine.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.9.7] Transitions: movements that link sections so the change of pattern looks smooth. Applying the elements to a group sequence: the action is a travelling phrase, the space uses a diagonal formation, the dynamics change from sustained to sudden, and performers mirror each other on the beat.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.b] Planning a structure or strategy is only half the job. You then check whether it worked, using methods of analysis, and judge it against clear criteria.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.0] Video review: the coach replays the performance to check whether players held their positions and carried out the tactics.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.1] Notational analysis: tallying passes, turnovers and scores from each strategy shows which one worked, because the numbers can be compared.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.2] Checklists: each element of the strategy is checked against key principles, such as whether the team kept its shape in defence.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.3] Observation by a coach or peer: a second observer notices what the player cannot see during play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.4] Criteria: whether the structure or strategy achieved its aim, whether each role was carried out, and how the opponents responded.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.10.5] Here these analysis methods are applied to a specific structure or strategy.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.b] A plan that suits the start of a performance may not suit the end. Good performers change their structure or strategy when the situation changes, and they can say what they changed and why it works.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.0] Reasons to modify: the score and time remaining, changes by the opponent, weather or pitch and court conditions, injury or dismissal (a player sent off), fatigue, or a strategy that is not working.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.1] Change formation: an extra defender is added to the back line when protecting a lead, making the defence more compact.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.2] Switch the marking system, for example from zonal to man-to-man marking when one attacker is dominating the game, so that attacker is followed everywhere.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.3] Change the point of attack: play switches from a blocked centre to a wing where the defence is thinner, so attackers meet less resistance.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.4] Pick and roll becomes pick and pop: when the defender stops the roll to the basket, the screener steps out to a shooting position instead, pulling that defender out to guard the shot.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.5] Smaller space: a routine is adapted by using levels and shorter pathways in place of wide travel, so it still fits the area available.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s1.11.6] Different music: phrases are shortened or lengthened so that each motif fits the new tempo and beat count.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s4: 4. Applying the fitness components
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.b] Before you can say which fitness components matter, you need to break an activity down. This is the first step in applying the fitness components to a real sport, position or role.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.0] Needs analysis means working out which fitness components an activity, position or role demands, by examining its duration, intensity, pattern of work and rest, the movements involved and the energy systems used (the ways the body produces energy for the muscles).
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.1] The performer's current fitness is then compared with those demands, usually by fitness testing, to find the gaps to train. This comparison is what turns the analysis into a training plan.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.2] To examine the extent to which a component matters, rank it as essential, important or less important for the activity. Then justify each ranking with a specific moment of play or performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.3] Positions and roles differ within one activity, so analyse the role, not just the sport. A soccer goalkeeper needs reaction time, power and agility more than cardio-respiratory endurance (the ability of the heart and lungs to keep supplying oxygen over long periods), while a midfielder needs the reverse.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.b] An invasion game is one where two teams move into each other's territory to score. Gaelic football mixes long running with sudden bursts, so it draws on many components at once.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (essential): players cover long distances over two halves and must recover between efforts, so a strong heart and lungs keep supplying oxygen to the working muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.1] Speed (essential): the ability to move the body quickly, needed to win the race to a breaking ball or to get away from a marker (the opponent guarding you).
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.2] Agility (essential): the ability to change direction quickly while keeping control, needed to side-step a tackler or turn quickly in defence.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.3] Power (important): the ability to produce force quickly, a mix of strength and speed. It is needed to jump for a high catch and to kick long.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.4] Muscular endurance (important): the ability of a muscle to keep working without tiring. The legs must keep going for repeated sprints and challenges late in the game.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.b] An aesthetic activity is judged on how the movement looks as well as what is achieved. Artistic gymnastics is made of short, controlled routines, so the profile is very different from a running game.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.0] Flexibility (essential): the range of movement at a joint, needed for splits, walkovers and extended body lines, which are marked on how they look.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.1] Strength (essential): the most force a muscle can produce, needed to support body weight in balances and on the hands.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.2] Balance (essential): the ability to keep the body steady, needed for held positions and landings, where a wobble or step costs control.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.3] Power (essential): force produced quickly, needed for the explosive jumps, tumbles and vaults.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.4] Co-ordination (essential): using the senses and body parts together smoothly, needed to link elements of a routine fluently.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.5] Cardio-respiratory endurance (less important): routines are short, so the heart and lungs are not pushed for long periods.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.b] In an endurance activity the performer keeps going at a steady pace for a long time. The profile is narrow: a few components matter very much and most others matter little.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (essential): the event lasts many minutes or hours at a steady pace, so the heart and lungs must keep delivering oxygen to the muscles throughout.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.1] Muscular endurance (essential): the legs repeat thousands of strides, so the leg muscles must resist fatigue (tiring) over the whole race.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.2] Body composition (important): the proportion of the body that is fat compared with muscle and bone. A low body-fat percentage reduces the weight carried, so the runner uses less energy at each stride.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.3] Reaction time (less important): the time taken to respond to a signal. A slow start matters little over a long race, because a fraction of a second is tiny compared with the whole event.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.b] Fitness can be trained inside the activity itself, while skills are practised under the fatigue and pressure of competition. This is what a demanding practice context means.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.0] Methods include conditioned games (games with extra rules that force a type of work), small-sided games (fewer players on a smaller pitch), game-speed drills and skill circuits.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.1] Example: a small-sided possession game with a two-touch limit develops cardio-respiratory endurance, agility and speed while players pass and move.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.2] Example: a dribbling relay through cones develops speed, agility and co-ordination.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.3] Benefit: specificity, because players use the same movements, energy systems and decisions as in competition, so the fitness gained transfers directly to the match.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.4] Benefit: it is motivating and time-efficient, because fitness and skill are trained together, and it trains skill execution under fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.5] Limitation: intensity and overload (working harder than the body is used to) are harder to control precisely, and some players work less than others.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.6] Limitation: it may not develop maximal strength or flexibility, so it is combined with training outside the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.b] Health-related fitness (HRF) components support general health, while performance-related fitness (PRF) components support success in a sport. A combined approach trains one of each together, which links the training to the demands of the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.0] A combined approach develops a health-related and a performance-related component in the same activity or session. It must name one HRF and one PRF component and train both together.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.1] To design one, name the two components, describe what the performer does with sets, repetitions or time, and state how it progresses. Then explain how each component is trained and link it to the activity's demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.2] Example, flexibility (HRF) and balance (PRF): a single-leg balance-and-reach routine, standing on one leg and reaching the free foot out slowly to points around the body through a full range. It progresses to an unstable surface, which makes balance harder.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.3] Example, muscular endurance (HRF) and agility (PRF): a circuit alternating ladder footwork drills with body-weight squats and lunges, with timed work and rest at each station.
