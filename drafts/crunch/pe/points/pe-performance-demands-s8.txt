TARGET pe-performance-demands-s8: 8. Diet, nutrition and hydration
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.b] Food supplies the fuel and the building materials a performer needs. Each nutrient does a different job, so knowing what each does and where it comes from lets you build a diet that supports training and competition.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.0] Carbohydrate is the body's main energy source for moderate and high-intensity exercise, and it is stored as glycogen (the body's stored form of carbohydrate) in the muscles and liver. Sources: bread, pasta, rice, potatoes, cereals and fruit.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.1] Protein is used for growth and repair of muscle and other tissue, which is why it matters after hard training damages muscle fibres. Sources: meat, fish, eggs, dairy and pulses (beans, peas and lentils).
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.2] Fat is a concentrated energy source for low-intensity, long-duration exercise. It also insulates the body and carries fat-soluble vitamins (vitamins that need fat to be absorbed). Sources: oils, nuts, dairy and oily fish.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.3] Iron carries oxygen in red blood cells, so a deficiency (not having enough) causes tiredness and reduces endurance because muscles receive less oxygen.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.4] Calcium is needed for strong bones and for muscle contraction.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.5] B vitamins help release energy from food, so they support the energy supply for training.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.6] Fibre keeps the digestive system healthy, but large amounts just before exercise can cause discomfort because it is slow to digest.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.7] A balanced diet for a performer is mostly carbohydrate-based, with enough protein, limited saturated fat, plenty of fruit and vegetables, and energy intake matched to the training load.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.b] What you eat before an event decides how much fuel you start with and whether your stomach copes with it. The aim is full energy stores without digestive problems.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.0] Eat a meal about 3 to 4 hours before: high in carbohydrate, moderate in protein, and low in fat and fibre, such as pasta with chicken. This tops up glycogen stores and leaves time for digestion to be complete.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.1] If needed, have a small carbohydrate snack about 1 to 2 hours before, such as a banana or toast, to add energy without a heavy stomach.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.2] Eat familiar foods only on competition day, to avoid stomach upsets from something your body is not used to.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.3] Drink plenty of fluid in the hours beforehand so you start properly hydrated.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.4] Carbohydrate loading is for endurance events lasting more than about 90 minutes. Carbohydrate intake is increased over the days before while training tapers (is reduced), to maximise glycogen stores.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.b] During an event the goal is to keep energy and fluid levels up. Afterwards the goal switches to recovery, which means refuelling, repairing and rehydrating.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.0] During short events, water is usually enough because stored glycogen lasts the whole effort.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.1] During events lasting more than about an hour, take carbohydrate from a sports drink, gel or banana, with fluid. This maintains blood glucose and delays fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.2] After exercise, carbohydrate restores muscle glycogen and protein repairs muscle, ideally within about 30 to 60 minutes, such as chocolate milk or a chicken and rice meal.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.3] After exercise, rehydrate with fluid and electrolytes (salts such as sodium) to replace sweat losses.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.4] Match-day example for Gaelic football: a pasta lunch three to four hours before throw-in; a banana an hour before; water and a sports drink at half-time; chocolate milk straight after and a chicken and rice dinner that evening.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.b] Water is involved in almost everything the body does during exercise, and sweating drains it quickly. Even a small fluid shortage harms performance, so performers need to know why water matters, when needs rise and how to check their hydration.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.0] Water regulates body temperature through sweating, transports nutrients and maintains blood volume, so losing it affects cooling and circulation.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.1] Effects of dehydration (not having enough water in the body): raised heart rate and body temperature, fatigue, headache, cramp, reduced concentration and poorer skill.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.2] A fluid loss of about 2% of body mass can impair performance, so waiting until you feel very dehydrated is too late.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.3] Fluid needs are higher in long or high-intensity activities, in hot or humid conditions, in indoor halls, when wearing heavy kit, and at altitude. Swimmers also sweat and need fluid, even though they are in water.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.4] Monitor hydration with urine colour (pale straw is good), by weighing before and after exercise, and by thirst, which is a late sign because it appears after dehydration has begun.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.5] Replace about 1.5 times the fluid lost: 1 kg of body mass lost needs about 1.5 litres.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.6] Drinking large amounts of plain water in very long events can dilute blood sodium (hyponatraemia, dangerously low sodium in the blood), so drinks should include electrolytes.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.b] Sports drinks come in three types, defined by how concentrated they are compared with body fluid. The concentration decides how fast the drink is absorbed and what it is best used for.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.0] Hypotonic drinks have a lower concentration than body fluid, so they are absorbed fastest and suit rapid rehydration.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.1] Isotonic drinks have about 6 to 8% carbohydrate, the same concentration as body fluid. They provide fluid and energy together and are the most common drink during exercise.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.2] Hypertonic drinks have a higher carbohydrate concentration, so they provide energy and are usually used after exercise.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.3] Benefits: they replace fluid, electrolytes (sodium and potassium) and carbohydrate in one convenient drink.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.4] Disadvantages: sugar and calories (dental decay, and weight gain for inactive users), cost, and they are unnecessary for short or low-intensity activity, where water is enough.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.b] Supplements are products taken in addition to food to help performance or recovery. Each has a specific role, but each also brings risks, which is why a balanced diet comes first.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.0] Protein powders help muscle repair and are a convenient way to get protein after training.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.1] Creatine raises phosphocreatine stores (a quick energy store) in muscle, improving repeated short, maximal efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.2] Caffeine is a stimulant that can reduce perceived effort and improve alertness and endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.3] Energy gels give rapid carbohydrate during long events.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.4] Vitamin and mineral supplements correct a diagnosed deficiency, such as iron.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.5] Challenge: products can be contaminated with prohibited substances, and under strict liability the athlete is responsible for anything found in their sample. Batch-tested products reduce but do not remove this risk.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.6] Challenge: side effects, such as stomach upset from caffeine or weight gain from creatine.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.7] Challenges: cost, unregulated claims and possible dependence. Supplements are also usually unnecessary when the diet is balanced, so the advice is food first.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.b] A dietary plan applies everything above to one chosen activity. A good plan shows what the performer eats and drinks, and gives a reason for each choice.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.0] Cover a training day and a competition day, with meals and snacks before, during and after activity and a fluid plan.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.1] Give a rationale (reason) for each choice, linked to the activity's duration, intensity and main energy system, so the food matches the demands of the sport.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.2] Aspect 1, pre-event carbohydrate: maximises glycogen for high-intensity efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.3] Aspect 2, post-event carbohydrate and protein: restores glycogen and repairs muscle for the next session.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.4] Aspect 3, hydration plan: prevents dehydration and keeps concentration and skill levels high.
