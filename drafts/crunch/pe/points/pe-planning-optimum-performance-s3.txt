TARGET pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3: 3. Aesthetic and artistic considerations
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.0] Aesthetic: concerned with the beauty and quality of movement as perceived by the observer, meaning how a movement looks and feels to watch.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.1] Artistic: concerned with creative expression, where the performer deliberately communicates an idea, mood, story or emotion through choreographed movement.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.2] A performance can be aesthetically pleasing without being artistic, because the performer need not be communicating an idea.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.3] Aesthetic and artistic criteria are judged qualitatively, by people, rather than by an objective outcome such as time, distance or score.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.0] Body line, shape and extension: the position the body makes, such as pointed toes, straight legs and full extension of the arms.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.1] Control, poise and balance: positions are held steadily and landings are controlled, such as a balanced finish at the end of a turn on a beam.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.2] Fluency and flow: smooth transitions between elements with no pauses or jerky changes.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.3] Timing, rhythm and musicality: movements matched to the accents of the music.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.0] Expression and communication: a mood or story is conveyed through facial expression and body language, and projected to the audience.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.1] Creativity and originality: choreography uses new or unexpected movements and combinations.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.2] Use of space and variety: levels (high, medium and low), pathways (straight, curved or zigzag) and direction (forwards, sideways or backwards) are varied across the routine.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.3] Synchronisation and relationships: in group or pair work, performers move with matched timing and shape, and their relationships convey the theme.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.4] Presentation: costume, props, entrance and finish, where the activity allows.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.0] Difficulty value: awarded for the elements attempted, separately from how well they are performed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.1] Execution score: starts from a maximum and has deductions for faults such as bent legs, loss of balance or a poor landing.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.2] The final mark combines the difficulty value and the execution score, so a hard routine performed poorly can score less than an easier one performed well.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.3] Diving is scored in the same way, on the difficulty of the dive and its execution.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.4] Dance is scored by judges against criteria such as expression and musicality.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.0] Games, athletics and swimming races are judged on objective outcomes, but they contain aesthetic qualities that are present and not scored.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.1] Example: a fluent passing move in a game is aesthetically pleasing, but the score depends on the result, such as a goal.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.2] Example: an elegant hurdling technique in athletics is aesthetically pleasing, but the race is decided by time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.3] Coaches and spectators notice these qualities informally; they are not recorded on a scoring sheet.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.0] Activities judged mainly on aesthetic or artistic criteria include gymnastics, dance, diving, synchronised swimming and figure skating.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.1] A comparison covers what each activity is judged on, how its scoring works, which criteria the two share and where they differ.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.2] Shared criteria: body line, control and timing appear in both dance and gymnastics.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.3] Difference: dance emphasises expression and musicality, while gymnastics adds a difficulty value to the execution score.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.4] Surfing is a borderline case: it is judged on criteria including speed, power and flow, so its criteria mix performance qualities with aesthetic ones.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.0] Video the performance so it can be replayed and scored more than once against the same criteria.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.1] Score each criterion with a checklist or rating scale.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.2] Gather self, peer and teacher evaluations, and compare the scores with a model performance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.3] Identify strengths, such as clean body lines, and areas to improve, such as transitions that lose their flow.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.4] For each area to improve, state how it will be improved, such as a drill that rehearses the transition.

CANDIDATES pe-learning-skill-technique-s3: 3. Analysing skill: movement and quality perspectives
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.0.b] Physical quantities used to describe movement fall into two types. A scalar tells you only how much; a vector tells you how much and which way. Sorting them correctly is the first step in analysing movement.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.0.0] A scalar quantity has size (magnitude, meaning how much there is) only. Examples are distance, speed, mass and time.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.0.1] A vector quantity has size and direction. Examples are displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum and weight. A vector can be drawn as an arrow whose length shows its size and whose point shows its direction.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.0.2] Distance is the total length of path travelled, so it is a scalar. Displacement is the straight-line change in position from start to finish in a given direction, so it is a vector.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.0.3] A 400 m runner on a standard outdoor track runs a distance of 400 m, but finishes where they started, so their displacement is zero.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.0.4] Direction decides outcomes in sport: a pass's velocity (its speed in a particular direction), not just its speed, decides whether it reaches a team-mate.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.1.b] Speed, velocity, acceleration and momentum are built from distance or displacement, time and mass. Whether each one is a scalar or a vector depends on whether direction is part of it.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.1.0] Speed = distance ÷ time. It is a scalar, because distance has no direction.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.1.1] Velocity = displacement ÷ time. It is a vector, because displacement includes direction.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.1.2] Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken, and it is a vector. Deceleration is negative acceleration (slowing down), such as a defender braking to change direction.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.1.3] Momentum = mass × velocity, and it is a vector. A heavier or faster rugby player has more momentum, so is harder to stop.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.1.4] Classified: speed is a scalar; velocity and momentum are vectors, because velocity includes direction and momentum depends on velocity.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.2.b] A force is a push or pull. A body only starts, stops or changes direction when a force acts on it, so these are the forces a performer deals with in sport.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.2.0] Muscular force is produced by the performer's muscles to move the body or an implement (a piece of equipment such as a bat or javelin).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.2.1] Gravity pulls every body and object towards the ground; its force on a body is its weight.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.2.2] Friction opposes sliding between surfaces, so studs and grip on footwear increase it to stop slipping.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.2.3] Air resistance and water resistance (together called drag) oppose motion through air or water. Streamlining, which means shaping the body or object to let air or water flow past smoothly, reduces them.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.2.4] Ground reaction force is the push back from the ground when a performer pushes against it.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.3.b] Newton's laws link force to motion. Between them they explain why a body starts moving, how quickly it speeds up and why pushing always produces a push back.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.3.0] First law (law of inertia, the tendency of a body to resist a change in its motion): a body stays at rest, or keeps moving at a constant velocity in a straight line, unless an external unbalanced force (one not cancelled out by another force) acts on it.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.3.1] First law examples: a ball on the penalty spot stays still until it is kicked; a sprinter in the blocks stays still until they drive against the blocks; a sliding player slows down only because friction acts on them.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.3.2] Second law (law of acceleration): the acceleration of a body is proportional to the force applied, happens in the direction of the force, and is inversely proportional to the body's mass (so a bigger mass accelerates less).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.3.3] In symbols the second law is F = m × a, with force in newtons, mass in kilograms and acceleration in metres per second squared.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.3.4] Second law examples: a harder kick gives the ball greater acceleration; the same force accelerates a lighter implement more than a heavier one. This is the law used to calculate the acceleration of a kicked ball.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.3.5] Third law (law of action and reaction): for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.3.6] Third law examples: a sprinter pushes back and down on the blocks and the ground reaction force drives them forward; a swimmer's hand pushes water backwards and the water pushes the swimmer forwards; a jumper drives down into the take-off board and is pushed upwards.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.4.b] Exams ask you to apply the laws to one named activity. Swimming works well because each law shows up at a different stage: the start, the stroke and the glide off the wall.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.4.0] First law: the swimmer on the blocks stays at rest until the leg drive applies a force. Off the wall, a streamlined glide keeps speed because less water resistance acts to slow it.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.4.1] Second law: a stronger, better-directed arm pull and leg kick produce greater acceleration for the same body mass, because acceleration is proportional to force.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.4.2] Third law: the hand and forearm push water backwards and the water pushes the swimmer forwards. At the dive, the force into the blocks is returned as forward propulsion.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.5.b] Analysing a skill means finding out why it works or fails. Newton's laws give a coach a way to do that in terms of forces.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.5.0] They explain what starts, stops and changes a movement, so a coach can see the cause of a fault rather than just its effect.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.5.1] They show where and in what direction force must be applied, such as driving through the ball rather than at it.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.5.2] They guide technique corrections, such as directing force in the intended direction of travel at a jump take-off.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.5.3] They inform the choice of equipment, such as a lighter implement for faster acceleration in training, since the second law says the same force accelerates a lighter object more.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.6.b] Quality and effectiveness describe how well a skill is performed. One measure is economy of movement: getting the result without wasting effort, which matters most when a performer is tired.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.6.0] Economy of movement means performing a skill with no unnecessary movement and the least energy needed for the result.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.6.1] It delays fatigue (the tiredness that weakens performance), so technique holds up late in a performance.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.6.2] It improves consistency and accuracy, because there are fewer moving parts to go wrong.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.6.3] It makes a movement look fluent, adding to its aesthetic quality (how good it looks to watch).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.6.4] Front crawl example: a flat, streamlined body position with the hips high reduces drag, so more of each arm pull produces forward motion; sinking legs waste energy.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.6.5] Distance running example: a relaxed arm swing without side-to-side twisting of the trunk saves energy over the race.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.6.6] Economy is improved by practising until the skill is automatic (the autonomous stage, where it needs little thought), correcting technique using video, and building the flexibility and strength to hold efficient positions.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.7.b] The other perspective on quality is creativity. A performer who has mastered a skill can go beyond the standard version and use it to solve the problem in front of them.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.7.0] Creative application means using a well-learned skill in a new, original or unexpected way to solve the problem a situation presents, rather than reproducing the standard version.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.7.1] It depends on the skill being automatic, so the performer's attention is free to read the situation.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.7.2] It deceives and unbalances opponents and opens options when the expected one is closed off.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.7.3] It makes a performer less predictable and harder to defend against.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.7.4] In dance and gymnastics it adds artistic merit and originality.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s3.7.5] Examples: a no-look pass in basketball; a disguised drop shot in badminton; a dummy solo in Gaelic football (a feint that fools a defender); an original linking movement in a gymnastics floor routine.
