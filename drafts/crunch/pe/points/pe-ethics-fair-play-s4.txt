TARGET pe-ethics-fair-play-s4: 4. Anti-doping rules
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.0.b] Anti-doping (the effort to stop athletes using banned drugs and methods) works on two levels. An international agency writes the rules, and a national body applies them in each country.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.0.0] The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) sets the World Anti-Doping Code, the single set of rules for the world, and the Prohibited List, the list of substances and methods that athletes are banned from using.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.0.1] In Ireland, Sport Ireland (the national sports body) runs the Irish Anti-Doping Programme and the Irish Anti-Doping Rules, so the world rules are put into practice at home.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.0.2] The Irish rules apply the World Anti-Doping Code to all athletes in sports governed by national governing bodies (NGBs, such as the association that runs a sport in Ireland) that Sport Ireland recognises.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.1.b] The rules do not ask what the athlete meant to do. They look only at what is found in the athlete's body, and this principle is called strict liability.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.1.0] Athletes are responsible for any prohibited substance found in their body, whether or not they intended to cheat, so claiming it was an accident is not a defence.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.1.1] Athletes must therefore check every medicine and supplement (such as a vitamin or protein product) they take before taking it, because anything they swallow could contain a banned substance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.b] An anti-doping rule violation is any act that breaks the anti-doping rules. The list goes well beyond failing a test, and it also covers people around the athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.0] Presence: a prohibited substance, its metabolites (the substances the body breaks it down into) or markers (signs that it was used) found in a sample taken from the athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.1] Use: use or attempted use of a prohibited substance or method, so an athlete can be in breach even if the attempt did not work.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.2] Refusal: evading, refusing or failing to submit to sample collection after being notified, so an athlete cannot avoid a test without consequences.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.3] Whereabouts failures: three missed tests and/or filing failures (not giving required location details on time) within 12 months. Whereabouts means the athlete's location, given in advance for testing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.4] Tampering: tampering or attempted tampering with any part of doping control (the testing process), such as interfering with a sample.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.5] Possession: possession of a prohibited substance or method.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.6] Trafficking: trafficking or attempted trafficking, including selling a prohibited substance, so selling a performance-enhancing drug (PED) is itself a violation.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.7] Administration: administering or attempting to administer a prohibited substance or method to an athlete, which can involve coaches or doctors rather than the athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.8] Complicity: assisting or covering up a violation. Prohibited association: working with a banned coach or doctor.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.b] Rules only work if they are enforced. Samples, advance location details and long-term monitoring are three ways Ireland checks that athletes follow them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.0] Testing: urine and blood samples are taken in competition and out of competition, often with no advance notice, so athletes cannot time their drug use around a known test.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.1] Each sample is sealed and split into an A and a B sample, which are analysed in WADA-accredited laboratories (labs approved by WADA).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.2] The B sample can be tested to confirm a positive A sample, which gives the athlete a second check on the result.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.3] Whereabouts: athletes in the registered testing pool (the group of athletes chosen for testing) give their location in advance, so testers know where to find them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.4] The whereabouts requirement includes a daily 60-minute slot when the athlete is available for testing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.5] Athlete biological passport: blood and steroid markers are monitored over time to detect the effects of doping, so doping can be spotted from changes even when no banned substance is found.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.b] Once a possible violation is found, a fixed process decides whether the athlete is guilty and what the penalty is. Each step is designed to be fair to the athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.0] Sport Ireland notifies the athlete of the suspected violation.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.1] An independent disciplinary panel (a group not linked to Sport Ireland) hears the case, and appeals are possible, so a decision can be challenged.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.2] Sanctions (penalties) include disqualification of results and loss of medals and prize money.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.3] Sanctions include a period of ineligibility (a time when the athlete is banned from competing), normally four years for an intentional first violation.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.4] The decision is published.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.5.b] Enforcement is not only testing. Ireland also tries to prevent doping through teaching and to catch it by other means.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.5.0] Anti-doping education programmes are run for athletes and support staff (coaches, doctors and others who work with athletes), so that they know the rules and the risks.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.5.1] Investigations use information from sources other than sample tests, so doping can be uncovered even when an athlete has never failed a test.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.b] Some athletes have genuine medical conditions that need drugs which are on the Prohibited List. A therapeutic use exemption lets them take the medicine legally, provided strict conditions are met.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.0] A therapeutic use exemption (TUE) is permission to use a prohibited substance or method to treat a diagnosed medical condition. It is the circumstance in which an athlete can compete with a banned substance in their system.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.1] The athlete applies in advance, except in an emergency, to the relevant anti-doping organisation, with medical evidence.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.2] An independent panel of doctors decides the application.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.3] Condition 1: the athlete's health would be significantly impaired without the substance or method.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.4] Condition 2: it gives no additional performance enhancement beyond a return to normal health, so the athlete is treated but not given an advantage.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.5] Condition 3: there is no reasonable permitted alternative. Condition 4: the need is not a consequence of earlier use without a TUE.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.6] Examples: insulin for type 1 diabetes; certain asthma medications taken above the permitted doses; prescribed stimulant medication for diagnosed ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.7] A doctor's prescription alone does not make a prohibited medicine allowed; a TUE is needed, because a doctor's prescription does not by itself give permission under the anti-doping rules.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.7.b] Doping harms more than the athlete who is caught. The consequences fall on the performer and on the activity (the sport) as a whole.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.7.0] For the performer: a ban, disqualification of results, loss of medals, funding and sponsorship, damage to reputation, and the health risks of the drug itself.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.7.1] For the activity: lost credibility and public trust, withdrawal of sponsors and broadcasters, and young people and parents turning away from the sport, which means fewer people take part.

CANDIDATES pe-ethics-fair-play-s3: 3. Drugs and sport
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.0.b] Sport depends on athletes competing on equal terms. A drug that artificially lifts performance breaks that equal footing, so sport has a body and a list to control them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.0.0] A performance-enhancing drug (PED) is a substance taken to improve performance artificially, which gives the user an unfair advantage over athletes who do not take it.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.0.1] The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) is the international body that fights drug use in sport. It publishes the Prohibited List, which is updated annually.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.0.2] The Prohibited List names substances and methods (ways of improving performance that do not involve a drug). Each is banned at all times, banned in competition only, or banned in particular sports.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.b] Anabolic agents are the drugs that build muscle. They are the PEDs most closely tied to strength and power, and they carry some of the widest health risks.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.0] Examples: anabolic steroids (synthetic versions of male hormones) such as nandrolone, testosterone and stanozolol.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.1] Effect: they increase protein synthesis (the body building new protein, which is what muscle is made of), so muscle mass, strength and power rise. This allows harder training and faster recovery.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.2] Misused in strength and power events, because muscle mass and recovery from hard training matter most there.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.3] Health risks: liver damage, heart disease, acne and aggression.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.4] Hormonal effects: infertility, and masculinisation in women (developing male characteristics).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.b] Hormones are chemical messengers made in the body. Peptide hormones and growth factors copy these messengers to change how the body performs and recovers. The two to know are EPO and HGH.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.0] Erythropoietin (EPO) raises red blood cell production and oxygen-carrying capacity. More oxygen reaches the muscles, which improves endurance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.1] EPO is misused in endurance events such as distance running and cycling, because these events depend on a steady oxygen supply.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.2] EPO health risks: thickened blood, which causes clots, stroke and heart attack.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.3] Human growth hormone (HGH) increases muscle and aids recovery.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.4] HGH health risks: abnormal growth and heart problems.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.b] Stimulants act on the body and brain to speed up and sharpen an athlete in the moment. They also hide the signals that would normally tell an athlete to slow down.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.0] Examples: amphetamines, cocaine and ephedrine.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.1] Effect: they increase alertness and aggression, and mask fatigue and pain (the athlete does not feel tiredness or pain that would normally force them to ease off).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.2] Misused in sports that demand sustained alertness and aggression, such as contact sports and endurance events.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.3] Health risks: high blood pressure, heart problems, overheating, addiction and poor judgement.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.b] These drugs work by changing what leaves the body rather than by building performance directly. A diuretic is a drug that makes the body produce more urine.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.0] Effect: they increase urine output. This allows rapid weight loss to make a weight category (the weight band an athlete must fit in to compete).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.1] Masking agents work by diluting urine or otherwise hiding other drugs, so the athlete is less likely to be caught in a drug test.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.2] Misused in weight-category sports such as boxing, because losing weight fast helps an athlete make the limit.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.3] Health risks: dehydration, cramps and kidney problems.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.b] An analgesic is a painkiller. Narcotic analgesics are strong painkillers that let an athlete keep competing when the body is signalling injury.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.0] Example: morphine.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.1] Effect: it masks pain, so an athlete can compete while injured.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.2] Misused in sports where athletes compete through injury, such as contact sports.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.3] Health risks: worsening injury, because pain is the warning that stops an athlete using an injured body part, and addiction.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.6.b] Beta-blockers calm the body, which is the opposite of a stimulant. That helps in sports where a steady hand matters more than power.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.6.0] Effect: they lower heart rate and steady tremor (shaking of the hands), which helps precision performance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.6.1] They are prohibited in particular precision sports such as archery and shooting, where a steady hand decides the result.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.6.2] Health risks: fatigue and low blood pressure.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.7.b] The Prohibited List is longer than the main categories above. Four more classes are on it.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.7.0] Beta-2 agonists, hormone and metabolic modulators, cannabinoids and glucocorticoids are also on the Prohibited List.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.8.b] Not everything banned is a drug. Some banned methods change the body or the test result without a drug being taken.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.8.0] Blood doping: a transfusion of blood to raise the red blood cell count, which carries more oxygen.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.8.1] Tampering with samples, meaning interfering with the urine or blood sample given for testing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.8.2] Gene doping: altering genes to enhance performance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.b] Athletes know the health and career risks, yet some still use PEDs. The reasons come from pressure and from the benefits a drug seems to offer.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.0] Pressure to win, because success is what the athlete is judged on.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.1] Financial rewards and sponsorship, which go to winners.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.2] National pressure to succeed.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.3] Belief that rivals are doping, so the athlete feels they must do the same to compete on equal terms.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.4] Recovering from injury faster, so they can return to training and competition sooner.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.5] Making a weight category.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.6] Ageing athletes trying to stay competitive as their natural performance declines.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.b] An athlete who is caught, or who simply takes PEDs, faces consequences in their health, their results, their income and their name. Each is a separate implication you can use in an answer.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.0] Health: short- and long-term risks from the categories above, including death in extreme cases.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.1] Sanctions: a ban, normally four years for intentional doping, and disqualification of results.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.2] Losses: medals and records are taken away.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.3] Financial and career: loss of funding, prize money and sponsorship, and the career can end.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.4] Reputation and personal: public disgrace, damage to family, mental-health effects and loss of self-respect.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.b] Doping harms more than the individual. When rules are broken, the whole activity pays, from public trust to the clean athletes who compete against dopers.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.0] Credibility: public trust falls, and every outstanding performance is doubted.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.1] Commercial: sponsors, broadcasters and spectators withdraw, which reduces funding.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.2] Clean athletes (those who do not dope) lose medals, places and earnings, and records may be reallocated years later.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.3] Participation: young people and parents turn away, and others may feel pressure to dope.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.4] Cost and exclusion: testing and education are expensive, and a country or team may be excluded from competition.
