TARGET pe-gender-physical-activity-s3: 3. Gender socialisation and its impact on participation
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.0.b] Gender socialisation shapes which activities young people see as open to them. To understand why boys and girls take part in sport differently, start with how children learn what is expected of them.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.0.0] Socialisation is the lifelong process of learning the norms (the unwritten rules about how to act), values and behaviour expected in a society. It goes on throughout life, not only in childhood.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.0.1] Gender socialisation is the part of that learning that tells you what is seen as appropriate for boys and men, and for girls and women. This is why a child may rule out an activity before ever trying it.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.1.b] An agent of socialisation is a person or group that passes these expectations on. Each agent below teaches children, often without meaning to, which activities suit their sex.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.1.0] Family: the toys children are given, the activities encouraged and the behaviour praised. Children learn what earns approval at home.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.1.1] Peers (friends of the same age): they reward behaviour that fits gender norms and mock behaviour that does not, so children copy what their friends accept.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.1.2] School: the activities offered and the expectations teachers hold. What is on offer, and who is expected to do it, tells pupils what is normal.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.1.3] Media: the images of male and female athletes that young people see. There is more on this in Gender, media and body image.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.1.4] Sports clubs and coaches: the activities offered and the language used with boys and girls.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.1.5] Religion and culture: the roles and behaviour a community treats as proper for each sex.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.2.b] These everyday examples show socialisation at work: small differences in what children are given and told add up to different sporting habits.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.2.0] Boys are given footballs and encouraged to be rough and competitive, so they learn that sport and aggression suit boys.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.2.1] Girls are given dance classes and praised for being neat and graceful, so they learn that looking good while moving is what is expected of girls.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.2.2] Comments such as 'throws like a girl' teach children that some physical skills belong to one sex, and that doing them 'like a girl' is an insult.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.3.b] Society does not only teach roles, it also sets rules about bodies. Social regulation of the body is the idea that these rules decide which physical activity people feel they may take part in, and the past shows this clearly for women.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.3.0] Social regulation of the body is the way society, through its norms, expectations, rules and media images, defines how bodies should look, move and behave.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.3.1] This regulation determines which physical activity people feel is appropriate or available to them, so it limits participation before a person has even chosen.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.3.2] In the past, women were historically excluded or restricted, for example barred from some events and clubs.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.3.3] In the past, women's competition distances were limited and dress rules restricted their participation.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.3.4] In the past, a belief that strenuous exercise harmed women's health discouraged women from taking part.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.3.5] Women's boxing was first included in the Olympic Games in 2012, in London, which shows how recently some barriers were lifted.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.4.b] The old formal bans have largely gone, but the regulation continues in softer forms that still affect who takes part and in what.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.4.0] Dress codes and kit expectations set out what women and men should wear to take part, so people who dislike or feel uneasy in the required kit may stay away.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.4.1] Muscular women, and men who take part in activities seen as feminine, are judged or criticised, which punishes people who step outside the norm.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.4.2] Unequal provision (the facilities, teams and opportunities offered) and unequal pay for women's sport persist.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.4.3] Appearance expectations continue to shape which activities people feel comfortable taking part in. There is more on this in Gender, media and body image.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.5.b] Social regulation does not affect only one sex. It pushes women and men towards different activities, and each group pays a price.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.5.0] For women: girls are steered away from contact, power and strength sports and from visible muscle, because these are not seen as suited to women.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.5.1] For women: self-surveillance, meaning constantly monitoring and judging their own appearance, takes attention away from enjoying the activity.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.5.2] For women: these pressures contribute to drop-out (giving up sport) in adolescence.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.5.3] For men: pressure to be strong, tough and competitive, whether or not they want to be.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.5.4] For men: avoidance of dance, gymnastics and synchronised swimming, which are seen as feminine.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.5.5] For men: pressure to play through pain and injury, rather than stop and look after themselves.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.5.6] For men: reluctance to show weakness or seek help.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.6.b] Hegemonic means dominant and widely accepted. Hegemonic masculinity is the ideal of manhood that a society holds up above all others, and it sets a standard that boys and men are judged against.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.6.0] Hegemonic masculinity is the dominant, widely accepted idea of what it means to be a man in a society, treated as the norm against which men are measured.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.6.1] It is a standard that individual men are measured against; it does not describe all men, so many men do not match it.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.6.2] The concept was developed by the sociologist (a researcher who studies society) R. W. Connell.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.6.3] Characteristics: physical strength, toughness, aggression, competitiveness, dominance, emotional restraint (not showing feelings), risk-taking and heterosexuality (attraction to women).
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.7.b] Because this ideal links manhood with strength and toughness, it rewards some boys, penalises others, and also changes how girls are seen when they play sport.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.7.0] For boys: boys who fit the ideal gain high status in contact team sports, which rewards them for matching the ideal.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.7.1] For boys: boys who are less sporty or prefer aesthetic activities (ones where how movement looks matters, such as dance) are excluded or ridiculed.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.7.2] For boys: boys are encouraged to play through injury and violence, because toughness is part of the ideal.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.7.3] For boys: boys who do not fit the ideal may drop out of sport.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.7.4] For girls: sport itself is defined as masculine, so girls who play are seen as less feminine.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.7.5] For girls: women's sport is treated as second-rate.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.8.b] Alongside the ideal man there is an ideal woman. Hegemonic femininity is the matching standard for girls and women, and it points them towards different activities.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.8.0] Hegemonic femininity is the dominant, widely accepted idea of what it means to be a woman in a society, treated as the norm against which women are measured.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.8.1] It is a standard that individual women are measured against; it does not describe all women.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.8.2] Characteristics: slimness, grace, attractiveness and an appearance focus, gentleness, passivity (taking a quiet, receptive role) and non-aggression, caring and deference (giving way to others).
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.9.b] The ideal of femininity affects girls most directly, but it also shapes what boys feel able to do.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.9.0] For girls: activities that are aggressive, muscular, sweaty or contact-based seem unfeminine, so girls avoid them or drop out.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.9.1] For girls: an emphasis on appearance leads to self-consciousness (worry about being watched and judged) about how they look while taking part.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.9.2] For girls: they are channelled into aesthetic activities, such as dance or gymnastics, which fit the ideal of grace.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.9.3] For boys: activities linked to femininity, such as dance and gymnastics, are avoided for fear of ridicule.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.10.b] Gender norms are learned, so they can be changed. These steps help boys and girls choose activities freely.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.10.0] Visible role models who break stereotypes, such as women playing rugby, boxing and Gaelic games, and men taking part in dance, show young people that these activities are open to everyone.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.10.1] Equal provision and media coverage for women's and men's sport, so women's sport is no longer treated as second-rate.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.10.2] Mixed and choice-based PE (physical education), so boys and girls can choose activities without gender labels.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s3.10.3] Coaches and teachers who challenge stereotyped comments as they happen, so comments like 'throws like a girl' do not go unchallenged.

CANDIDATES pe-gender-physical-activity-s1: 1. Gender, sport and physical activity
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.b] Differences in participation are best understood through gender, the social side of being male or female, rather than through biology alone. So the first step is to separate the two words, which are often used as if they meant the same thing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.0] Sex: the biological characteristics, such as chromosomes (the genetic material that helps determine sex), hormones (chemical messengers in the body) and physical make-up, that define a person as male or female.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.1] Gender: the socially constructed roles, behaviours and expectations (in other words, ideas made by society, not by biology) that a society attaches to being male or female.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.2] Gender expectations differ between societies and change over time, so the activities seen as suitable for each sex are social rather than fixed.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.3] Gendered activities: physical activities that a society labels as suitable for boys or for girls, for example rugby as 'for boys' and dance as 'for girls'.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.b] Boys and girls do not take part in physical activity in the same way. The differences show up in how much they do, which activities they choose and when they stop, and they are described here in words, not percentages.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.0] How much: on average, girls are less active than boys in physical activity and sport.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.1] When: girls drop out of sport in greater numbers than boys during adolescence (the teenage years), especially in the senior cycle years (the last three years of secondary school).
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.2] Which activities: boys more often play competitive team and contact sports (where players physically tackle or collide), such as rugby and Gaelic football.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.3] Which activities: girls more often choose dance, gymnastics, individual fitness and non-contact activities (where players do not tackle or collide).
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.4] Many activities are labelled 'for boys' or 'for girls', which steers young people towards the activities seen as suitable for their gender.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.5] Participation changes across the life course (the stages of life from childhood to older age) and is shaped by many interacting influences rather than by biology alone.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.b] The family is the first place a child meets sport, and parents control much of what a child can try. Their attitudes can push boys and girls towards different activities.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.0] Parents' own activity and encouragement shape whether a child sees physical activity as normal.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.1] Parents decide what they will pay for and drive their children to, which controls access to clubs, coaching and training.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.2] For boys: parents may encourage sons into contact sports and competitive team games.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.3] For girls: parents may steer daughters towards activities they consider suitable, such as dance or gymnastics.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.4] Example: a parent pays a son's club fees and drives him to matches, while a daughter's wish to join the same club is not supported.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.b] Peers (friends and classmates of the same age) matter most in the teenage years, when fitting in feels important. What friends think of sport can decide whether a young person takes part.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.0] Friends' choices can decide whether a young person joins an activity or stays in it.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.1] Fear of being judged by peers stops young people trying new activities.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.2] For teenage girls: being sporty may be seen as uncool, so girls may downplay their interest in sport to fit in with friends.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.3] For boys: sport can be central to status among peers, so skill in team sports can bring respect, while boys who are not sporty may lose standing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.b] School is where most young people do most of their organised sport, so what a school offers and how it runs PE can widen or narrow the gender gap.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.0] The range of activities offered often centres on competitive team games, which draw boys more than girls.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.1] For girls: a narrow range of activities leaves those who prefer non-contact or individual activities with little to choose from.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.2] For boys: competitive team games give boys a visible route into school teams and inter-school competition (matches between schools).
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.3] PE kit and changing facilities can deter girls where changing space is shared, poorly kept or lacks privacy.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.4] Teacher expectations and attention: boys' teams often receive more of the teacher's time, and teachers may hold different expectations of girls' competitiveness.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.5] Timetabling decides which activities run and when, so a fixed timetable can limit the choices open to each group.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.b] Outside school, whether a young person can play depends on what local clubs and facilities offer and on who they see succeeding in sport.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.0] Provision: the teams, coaches, facility time and fixtures (scheduled matches) that let people take part, and how much of each is on offer.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.1] For boys: boys often have more teams, better facility slots and more fixtures, so they can train and play more regularly.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.2] For girls: fewer teams, coaches and facility slots limit how regularly they can train and play.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.3] Role models: fewer visible female athletes, coaches and administrators (people who run clubs and governing bodies) mean girls see fewer people like themselves in sport and its leadership.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.4] For boys: male role models dominate many visible team and contact sports, which reinforces the view that these sports belong to boys.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.b] What young people have to wear for an activity can decide whether they take part. Kit that shows the body can make teenagers, who are already conscious of how they look, want to avoid it.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.0] Tight, short or revealing kit, such as swimwear, leotards, athletics briefs and short skirts, can make teenagers self-conscious about their bodies.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.1] For girls: self-consciousness about kit can lead to avoiding an activity or dropping out, especially in adolescence.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.2] For boys: tight or revealing kit can produce the same self-consciousness, leading some boys to avoid the activity too.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.3] Comfortable kit that students can choose helps young people take part without feeling exposed.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.7.b] Gender shapes boys' participation too. Boys have some advantages, but they also face pressures that can limit what they feel free to do.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.7.0] Provision and pathways: boys often have more established routes from school teams into competitive club sport, so it is easier for them to keep progressing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.7.1] Pressure to be strong and competitive: boys may feel they must excel in contact and team sports to be respected.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.7.2] Ridicule: boys who choose activities labelled feminine, such as dance, may be mocked, which narrows their choices too.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.8.b] Some barriers are practical rather than social. They affect everyone, but they can weigh more heavily on girls.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.8.0] Time, cost and transport limit participation for all young people, especially where clubs and facilities are far from home.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.8.1] For girls: safety concerns about travelling home after evening training or matches can keep them from attending.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.8.2] For boys: the same time, cost and transport limits apply, but safety concerns weigh less heavily.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.9.b] Two further influences work through ideas and images rather than through facilities. Each has its own subsection, so only the main point of each is given here. There is more on these in Gender, media and body image and in Gender socialisation and its impact on participation.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.9.0] Body image and media: idealised thin, toned and muscular bodies in advertising and social media can make young people dissatisfied with their own bodies (their body image) and deter them from activity.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.9.1] Gender socialisation (learning the behaviour expected of your sex) and hegemonic ideals (the dominant, most-valued versions of masculinity and femininity): family, peers, school and media teach boys and girls what is appropriate for their sex, and these ideals steer them towards some activities and away from others.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.b] Once the causes are known, a school can act on each one. Each measure below removes a particular barrier, so match the fix to the problem.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.0] Broaden the activity offer with dance, fitness classes, aquatics (water-based activities such as swimming), outdoor pursuits and non-contact versions of games, and give students a choice, so girls who avoid competitive team games have options.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.1] Offer girls-only or friendship-group options and non-competitive sessions alongside competitive teams, which removes the fear of being judged in mixed or competitive settings.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.2] Provide flexible, comfortable PE kit and private, clean changing facilities, which removes self-consciousness about kit and changing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.3] Give girls' teams equal facility time, fixtures, coaching and recognition, shown in assemblies, on noticeboards and on social media, which removes unequal provision and invisibility.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.4] Bring in female role models and leaders, such as older students acting as leaders and visiting female athletes and coaches, which tackles the shortage of visible women in sport.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.5] Use student voice (asking students what they want): survey students and plan activities from the results, so provision matches what students want.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.6] Track participation by gender to see whether the gap is closing and which measures need changing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.b] Clubs, local bodies and national campaigns can close the gap outside school, by giving girls and women somewhere to play and by making women's sport easier to see.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.0] Clubs offering girls' and women's teams at every age, with female coaches, give girls a team to join at each stage of their development.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.1] Social, beginner and return-to-sport programmes, such as women's recreational leagues and mothers-and-daughters sessions, bring in people who would not join competitive teams.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.2] Allocate pitches, halls and coaching fairly between girls' and boys' teams, so neither group has the better slots.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.3] Provide safe, accessible, well-lit facilities and transport, which tackles safety concerns about travelling after dark.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.4] Local Sports Partnership programmes (local bodies that promote sport and physical activity) targeting teenage girls reach girls through local provision.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.5] The Ladies Gaelic Football Association (LGFA) and the Camogie Association govern ladies' Gaelic football and camogie, giving girls and women their own pathway in those games.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.6] The 20x20 campaign, launched in 2018, aims to increase the visibility of women's sport in Ireland, under the slogan 'If she can't see it, she can't be it'. Sport Ireland has a Women in Sport policy, and media coverage of women's sport adds to its visibility.
