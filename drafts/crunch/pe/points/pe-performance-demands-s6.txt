TARGET pe-performance-demands-s6: 6. Designing a fitness plan
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.b] Different sports tax the body's energy systems in different ways, so there is no single best way to train. These three methods mainly target the aerobic system (which uses oxygen to release energy for long efforts) and the anaerobic system (which works without oxygen for short, intense efforts).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.0] Continuous training is steady, moderate-intensity exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes in the aerobic zone (about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate). It works the aerobic system, so it develops cardio-respiratory endurance (heart and lungs) and muscular endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.1] Continuous training suits activities that are themselves continuous, such as distance running, swimming and cycling, because the training matches what the event demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.2] Interval training alternates periods of work with periods of rest or recovery. Its demand is set by three things: the work-to-rest ratio (how long you work compared with how long you recover), the number of repetitions and the number of sets.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.3] Short, high-intensity intervals with longer recovery develop speed and anaerobic capacity, because the rest lets you repeat hard efforts. Longer intervals at lower intensity develop aerobic fitness instead.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.4] Interval training suits intermittent sports (bursts of effort with pauses) such as hurling, soccer and basketball, because it copies the pattern of work and rest in the game.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.5] Fartlek (speed play) is continuous running with changes of pace and terrain, for example jogging, sprinting between lamp posts and running uphill. It trains the aerobic and anaerobic systems together, so it suits games players.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.b] Muscles can be trained to work for longer, to produce more force, to produce force quickly, or to move through a bigger range. Each of these aims has its own method.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.0] Circuit training is a series of stations worked in turn with set work and rest times. It can be designed for muscular endurance, strength, cardio-respiratory endurance or sport skills, depending on the exercises chosen at the stations.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.1] Resistance (weight) training means working against a load such as free weights, machines or body weight, with exercises such as squats, lunges, presses, rows and curls.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.2] In resistance training, the number of repetitions decides the result: heavy loads with few repetitions build strength, while light loads with many repetitions build muscular endurance (the ability to keep a muscle working).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.3] Plyometric training uses jumping, bounding and hopping. A lengthening contraction on landing is followed immediately by a forceful shortening contraction on take-off, which develops power for jumping and sprinting.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.4] Plyometrics needs a strength base, a forgiving surface and low volume, so it is not for beginners.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.5] Static stretching holds a stretch still at mild tension. It is best in the cool-down.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.6] Dynamic stretching is controlled movement through the full range of a joint, such as leg swings, and it is used in the warm-up to prepare the muscles for the movements to come.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.7] PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) stretching has four steps: stretch the muscle, contract it against resistance from a partner, relax, then stretch further. It gives the greatest gains in range.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.b] The skill in comparing methods is matching each one to what the activity actually demands. The same method can be ideal for one sport and poor for another.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.0] Hurling is stop-start, so interval training and fartlek suit it better than continuous training. Resistance and plyometric work add power for striking and jumping.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.1] Distance swimming depends on the aerobic system, so continuous and long-interval training develop the fitness the event needs.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.2] Gymnastics relies on flexibility and body-weight strength rather than long efforts, so flexibility training and body-weight strength work matter more than long aerobic sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.3] The best method depends on the energy systems and fitness components the activity demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.b] Training can take place in the sport itself or in other forms of exercise that build the same fitness. Each approach has strengths, and good programmes use both.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.0] Training in the activity uses conditioned games and sport-specific drills. It is specific (it copies the real movements), motivating, and trains skill under fatigue, which is when skill usually breaks down in matches.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.1] Training outside the activity includes gym-based resistance training, plyometrics, swimming or cycling as cross-training (training in a different sport), and yoga or Pilates for flexibility and core strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.2] Training outside the activity can overload specific components precisely, build a fitness base, add variety, and reduce overuse of the sport's repeated movements.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.3] The limitation is that training outside the activity is less specific, so it must be linked back to the activity's movements and demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.4] For a hurler, resistance training builds the strength and power for long striking and winning dropping balls, while yoga improves hip and shoulder range for the swing.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.b] A good programme starts from evidence, not guesswork. Fitness test results show what to train, and the rest of the design follows in five steps.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.0] 1. Use the fitness test results to identify the weakest components that matter most for the activity, so effort goes where it will help performance most.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.1] 2. Set goals for those components.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.2] 3. Select training methods suited to each goal.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.3] 4. Apply FITT and the principles of training. Plan sessions per week across several weeks with progression, a warm-up and cool-down in each session, and rest days.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.4] 5. Re-test at the end to evaluate the programme and adjust it, because the new results show whether the goals were met.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.5] Illustrative six-week plan (example values only) for a soccer player whose tests show weak speed and agility: two speed and agility sessions a week (sprints and cone drills with full recovery), one resistance session for leg strength, and one fartlek run.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.6] In the same illustrative plan, repetitions rise every two weeks, week four is lighter, and the player is re-tested in week six.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.b] Training is the stimulus, but the body improves while it recovers afterwards. Recovery strategies help this process along and keep performers ready for the next session.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.0] Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the session. If recovery is adequate, the body rebuilds slightly above its previous level, which is called supercompensation.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.1] Cool-down means light activity and stretching, which help remove waste products and return the body to rest.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.2] Rehydration replaces fluid and electrolytes (salts) lost in sweat. Refuelling soon after exercise matters too: carbohydrate restores muscle glycogen (the muscles' stored fuel) and protein repairs muscle tissue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.3] Sleep supports recovery because most tissue repair and growth takes place during sleep.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.4] Active recovery is a light session, such as an easy swim the day after a match, which keeps blood flowing to the muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.5] Massage and foam rolling reduce muscle tightness and soreness. Cold-water immersion reduces inflammation and soreness after intense exercise, and compression garments may reduce swelling and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.6] Planned rest days and lighter weeks prevent overtraining, which is when training outpaces recovery. There is more on its signs in Safe practice.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.b] Periodisation plans training in stages, so the performer is at their best when it matters.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.0] Periodisation divides a training plan into planned phases or cycles, each with a specific aim, so that volume, intensity and recovery vary systematically to bring the performer to peak performance at target competitions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.1] Cycles run from longest to shortest. The macrocycle is the long-term plan, often a year or a season. The mesocycle is a block of several weeks, often 4 to 6, with one focus, such as building strength. The microcycle is usually one week of sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.2] The preparation phase builds general fitness first, then specific fitness and skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.3] In the competition phase, fitness is maintained and the performer peaks for key events. Tapering before the main event reduces training volume while keeping intensity, so the performer arrives fresh.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.4] The transition phase is active rest and recovery after the season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.5] Coaches use periodisation so the performer peaks at the right time, develops qualities in a logical order, avoids overtraining and injury, and stays motivated through variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.6] Peaking for a single major event, such as an Olympic Games every four years, uses a long-term plan built from these cycles, with each year's macrocycle building towards the target.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s5: 5. Assessing fitness and the principles of training
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.b] A fitness test turns a vague feeling of being fit into a measurement. For the measurement to be worth acting on, the test must be chosen and carried out properly.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.0] Testing has several purposes: to identify strengths and weaknesses against the demands of the activity, to set realistic goals, to design and monitor a training programme, and to motivate the performer.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.1] Results can also be compared with norms (normative data).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.2] A valid test measures what it claims to measure. For example, a sit-and-reach test is valid for hamstring and lower-back flexibility but not for strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.3] A reliable test gives consistent results when repeated under the same conditions. Validity and reliability are different ideas: a test can be consistent without measuring the right thing.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.4] Use a standardised protocol (the same set of instructions every time): the same warm-up, surface, time of day, equipment and tester, so that any change in the results comes from the performer and not from the conditions.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.5] Tests must be safe and suited to the performer. They are repeated before and after a programme to show progress.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.6] Field tests are practical, cheap and can test many people at once; laboratory tests are more accurate but expensive.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.b] Each health-related component of fitness has a standard test. For every one you should be able to name the test, outline how it is carried out and say what is recorded.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (the ability of the heart and lungs to supply oxygen over time): the multistage fitness test (bleep test). The performer runs 20 m shuttles at a pace set by bleeps that get faster each level until they can no longer keep up.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.1] In the multistage fitness test the level reached is recorded, and VO2 max (maximum oxygen uptake, the most oxygen the body can use per minute) is estimated from it.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.2] Cardio-respiratory endurance, alternative: the Cooper 12-minute run, in which the distance the performer covers in 12 minutes is recorded, so a longer distance means better endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.3] Cardio-respiratory endurance for intermittent team sports (sports with bursts of effort and rests): the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test, which uses repeated shuttles with short recovery breaks, so it matches the stop-start pattern of the game.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.4] Muscular endurance (repeated muscle contractions without tiring): press-up and sit-up (curl-up) tests, recording the maximum number in one minute or to exhaustion; or the time a plank is held.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.5] Strength (the most force a muscle can produce): the hand-grip dynamometer, or a one-repetition maximum (1RM), which is the heaviest weight lifted once with correct technique.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.6] Flexibility (range of movement at a joint): the sit-and-reach test, measuring hamstring and lower-back flexibility as the distance reached past the toes.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.7] Body composition (the proportions of fat, muscle and bone): skinfold callipers at set sites, or bioelectrical impedance scales.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.8] BMI (body mass index, based on height and weight) is a third option, but it can wrongly class muscular people as overweight because muscle is heavy and BMI cannot tell muscle from fat.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.b] Performance-related components matter most for success in a particular sport. Their tests are short and practical, and each records a time, a distance or a count.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.0] Speed (how fast you can move): a 30 m sprint from a standing start, timed.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.1] Agility (changing direction quickly and under control): the Illinois agility run or the T-test, timing a set course with changes of direction.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.2] Power (strength applied at speed): the vertical (sergeant) jump, measuring jump height, or the standing broad jump, measuring distance.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.3] Balance (keeping the body steady): the stork stand, timing how long the performer balances on one foot.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.4] Co-ordination (moving body parts smoothly together, for example hand and eye): the alternate-hand wall-toss test, counting catches in 30 seconds.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.5] Reaction time (the delay between a signal and the start of a response): the ruler drop test. A partner drops a ruler between the performer's thumb and finger, and the distance it falls before being caught is recorded. A shorter distance means a faster reaction.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.b] A test battery is a set of tests chosen to measure the components a specific activity demands, based on a needs analysis (a study of what the activity asks of the performer). Building one is a short sequence of decisions.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.0] 1. Analyse the activity's demands and select its key components, so you only test what matters for that sport.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.1] 2. Choose one valid, reliable test for each component that is specific to the activity, so the result tells you something useful about the sport.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.2] 3. Order the tests so fatigue does not distort results. Non-fatiguing tests such as flexibility, power and agility go first, and the maximal endurance test goes last, because a tired performer would score badly on everything after it.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.3] 4. Standardise the conditions, record the results and compare them with norms or previous results.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.4] Example battery for soccer: a 30 m sprint (speed for breaking away from a defender), the Illinois agility run (changes of direction), the vertical jump (power for heading), then the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test (repeated high-intensity running over a match).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.b] The principles of training are rules that make a programme effective and safe. The specification names readiness, specificity, progression, overload and rest/recovery. Exam answers also accept reversibility, individual differences and variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.0] Specificity: training must match the demands of the activity, including its energy systems, muscles, movements and speeds, because the body adapts only to what it is asked to do.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.1] Progression: the training load is increased gradually as the body adapts, so improvement continues without a sudden jump that risks injury.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.2] Overload: the body must work harder than normal to adapt, achieved by raising frequency (how often), intensity (how hard) or time (how long).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.3] Rest and recovery: the body adapts during recovery, so too little rest leads to overtraining (a build-up of fatigue that stops progress or causes injury).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.4] Readiness: the performer must be physically and mentally ready for the training, considering age, maturity, fitness base and skill.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.5] Reversibility: fitness gains are lost if training stops, so training must be kept up.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.6] Individual differences: programmes should suit the individual's fitness, goals and response to training, because two people given the same programme will not adapt in the same way.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.7] Variety (avoiding tedium): varying training keeps motivation high and reduces overuse injuries, which come from repeating the same movements.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.b] Each principle helps performance, but each has a cost if it is applied badly. Evaluating means giving the benefit and the risk together.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.0] Specificity transfers training directly to competition, but too narrow a focus can neglect general fitness and cause overuse injuries.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.1] Overload drives improvement, but progressing too fast raises injury and overtraining risk.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.2] Rest and recovery allow adaptation, but too much rest causes reversibility (the loss of fitness gained).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.3] Readiness protects young athletes, who should not be given adult training loads before they are physically mature, because their bodies are still developing.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.b] FITT is a way to describe a training programme: Frequency (how often), Intensity (how hard), Time (how long) and Type (what kind of exercise). Changing frequency, intensity or time is how overload is applied. The values below are typical guidance, not fixed rules.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance: 3 to 5 times a week, 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate, at least 20 minutes, continuous running, cycling or swimming.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.1] Muscular endurance: 2 to 3 times a week, light loads, 15 or more repetitions in 2 to 3 sets, circuits or body-weight exercises.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.2] Strength: 2 to 3 times a week with rest days between, heavy loads, 3 to 6 repetitions in 3 to 5 sets, free weights or machines.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.3] Flexibility: 3 to 5 times a week, stretch to mild tension, hold each stretch about 30 seconds, static, dynamic or PNF stretching.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.4] Body composition: most days, moderate intensity, longer sessions of 30 minutes or more, aerobic exercise combined with a balanced diet.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.b] The same four letters apply to the performance-related components. Again, the values are typical guidance.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.0] Speed: 2 to 3 times a week, maximal efforts, short sprints with full recovery, sprint intervals.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.1] Agility: 2 to 3 times a week, high intensity, short drills with recovery, ladder and cone drills.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.2] Power: 2 to 3 times a week, explosive maximal efforts, low repetitions with full recovery, plyometrics or jump training.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.3] Balance: 3 or more times a week, progressively harder positions, short holds, single-leg and unstable-surface exercises.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.4] Co-ordination: regular short practices, increasing complexity, skill drills combining body parts.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.5] Reaction time: regular short practices, maximal responses, reaction drills to sounds or visual signals.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.b] Intensity is the hardest FITT letter to judge by eye, so performers use heart rate to set it. Heart rate zones show whether a session is training the aerobic system (using oxygen) or the anaerobic system (working without enough oxygen). These figures are estimates.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.0] Maximum heart rate is estimated as 220 minus age.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.1] Aerobic training zone: about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate. Anaerobic training zone: about 80 to 90%.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.2] Aerobic threshold: the lower edge of the aerobic zone, below which training gives little cardio-respiratory benefit because the heart and lungs are not being challenged enough.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.3] Anaerobic (lactate) threshold: the intensity above which lactic acid builds up faster than it can be removed, which is why exercise above it cannot be kept going for long.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.4] Example: a 17-year-old's estimated maximum heart rate is 220 − 17 = 203 beats per minute, so the aerobic zone is about 122 to 162 beats per minute.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.5] Intensity is monitored by heart rate or by the rating of perceived exertion (RPE), the performer's own score of how hard they are working.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s4: 4. Applying the fitness components
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.b] Before you can say which fitness components matter, you need to break an activity down. This is the first step in applying the fitness components to a real sport, position or role.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.0] Needs analysis means working out which fitness components an activity, position or role demands, by examining its duration, intensity, pattern of work and rest, the movements involved and the energy systems used (the ways the body produces energy for the muscles).
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.1] The performer's current fitness is then compared with those demands, usually by fitness testing, to find the gaps to train. This comparison is what turns the analysis into a training plan.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.2] To examine the extent to which a component matters, rank it as essential, important or less important for the activity. Then justify each ranking with a specific moment of play or performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.3] Positions and roles differ within one activity, so analyse the role, not just the sport. A soccer goalkeeper needs reaction time, power and agility more than cardio-respiratory endurance (the ability of the heart and lungs to keep supplying oxygen over long periods), while a midfielder needs the reverse.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.b] An invasion game is one where two teams move into each other's territory to score. Gaelic football mixes long running with sudden bursts, so it draws on many components at once.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (essential): players cover long distances over two halves and must recover between efforts, so a strong heart and lungs keep supplying oxygen to the working muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.1] Speed (essential): the ability to move the body quickly, needed to win the race to a breaking ball or to get away from a marker (the opponent guarding you).
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.2] Agility (essential): the ability to change direction quickly while keeping control, needed to side-step a tackler or turn quickly in defence.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.3] Power (important): the ability to produce force quickly, a mix of strength and speed. It is needed to jump for a high catch and to kick long.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.4] Muscular endurance (important): the ability of a muscle to keep working without tiring. The legs must keep going for repeated sprints and challenges late in the game.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.b] An aesthetic activity is judged on how the movement looks as well as what is achieved. Artistic gymnastics is made of short, controlled routines, so the profile is very different from a running game.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.0] Flexibility (essential): the range of movement at a joint, needed for splits, walkovers and extended body lines, which are marked on how they look.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.1] Strength (essential): the most force a muscle can produce, needed to support body weight in balances and on the hands.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.2] Balance (essential): the ability to keep the body steady, needed for held positions and landings, where a wobble or step costs control.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.3] Power (essential): force produced quickly, needed for the explosive jumps, tumbles and vaults.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.4] Co-ordination (essential): using the senses and body parts together smoothly, needed to link elements of a routine fluently.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.5] Cardio-respiratory endurance (less important): routines are short, so the heart and lungs are not pushed for long periods.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.b] In an endurance activity the performer keeps going at a steady pace for a long time. The profile is narrow: a few components matter very much and most others matter little.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (essential): the event lasts many minutes or hours at a steady pace, so the heart and lungs must keep delivering oxygen to the muscles throughout.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.1] Muscular endurance (essential): the legs repeat thousands of strides, so the leg muscles must resist fatigue (tiring) over the whole race.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.2] Body composition (important): the proportion of the body that is fat compared with muscle and bone. A low body-fat percentage reduces the weight carried, so the runner uses less energy at each stride.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.3] Reaction time (less important): the time taken to respond to a signal. A slow start matters little over a long race, because a fraction of a second is tiny compared with the whole event.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.b] Fitness can be trained inside the activity itself, while skills are practised under the fatigue and pressure of competition. This is what a demanding practice context means.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.0] Methods include conditioned games (games with extra rules that force a type of work), small-sided games (fewer players on a smaller pitch), game-speed drills and skill circuits.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.1] Example: a small-sided possession game with a two-touch limit develops cardio-respiratory endurance, agility and speed while players pass and move.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.2] Example: a dribbling relay through cones develops speed, agility and co-ordination.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.3] Benefit: specificity, because players use the same movements, energy systems and decisions as in competition, so the fitness gained transfers directly to the match.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.4] Benefit: it is motivating and time-efficient, because fitness and skill are trained together, and it trains skill execution under fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.5] Limitation: intensity and overload (working harder than the body is used to) are harder to control precisely, and some players work less than others.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.6] Limitation: it may not develop maximal strength or flexibility, so it is combined with training outside the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.b] Health-related fitness (HRF) components support general health, while performance-related fitness (PRF) components support success in a sport. A combined approach trains one of each together, which links the training to the demands of the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.0] A combined approach develops a health-related and a performance-related component in the same activity or session. It must name one HRF and one PRF component and train both together.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.1] To design one, name the two components, describe what the performer does with sets, repetitions or time, and state how it progresses. Then explain how each component is trained and link it to the activity's demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.2] Example, flexibility (HRF) and balance (PRF): a single-leg balance-and-reach routine, standing on one leg and reaching the free foot out slowly to points around the body through a full range. It progresses to an unstable surface, which makes balance harder.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.3] Example, muscular endurance (HRF) and agility (PRF): a circuit alternating ladder footwork drills with body-weight squats and lunges, with timed work and rest at each station.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s9: 9. Energy systems
[pe-performance-demands-s9.0.b] Every muscle contraction needs a chemical fuel that the muscle can use immediately. That fuel is ATP, and the body's energy systems exist to keep making more of it.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.0.0] ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the only direct source of energy for muscle contraction, so the energy stored in other fuels, such as carbohydrate, fat or phosphocreatine, has to be used to rebuild ATP before the muscle can use it.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.0.1] Muscles store only enough ATP for about 2 to 3 seconds of work, which means it must be constantly resynthesised (rebuilt) by three energy systems: ATP-PC, lactic acid and aerobic.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.b] The ATP-PC system is the body's fastest way to make ATP. It uses a fuel already stored in the muscle, so it can power short, explosive efforts but runs out quickly.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.0] Fuel: phosphocreatine (PC), a chemical stored in the muscle that is used to rebuild ATP. No oxygen is needed, so the system is anaerobic (without oxygen).
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.1] Intensity and duration: it is the main supplier for maximal intensity work lasting about 8 to 10 seconds, after which the PC stores are used up.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.2] By-products: none that cause fatigue, which is why it is called alactic (no lactic acid). Fatigue comes from PC running out, not from a waste product building up.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.3] Recovery: PC stores are about half restored in 30 seconds and almost fully restored in about 3 minutes of rest, so a full rest lets a performer repeat a maximal effort.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.4] Sporting examples: 100 m sprint, long jump, shot-put, a tackle, a jump to catch a ball. Each is a very short, all-out effort.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.b] When high-intensity work continues beyond about 10 seconds, PC is running low and the body needs another anaerobic source of ATP. The lactic acid system supplies it by breaking down carbohydrate, but at a price.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.0] Fuel: carbohydrate (glycogen, the store of carbohydrate in the muscle, and glucose) broken down without oxygen. This system uses carbohydrate only, not fat.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.1] Intensity and duration: high intensity, from about 10 seconds to about 2 minutes, and it predominates (does most of the work) at roughly 10 to 90 seconds.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.2] By-product: lactic acid (lactate and hydrogen ions), which lowers the pH (makes the muscle more acidic) and so causes fatigue and a burning feeling.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.3] Recovery: removing lactic acid takes much longer than restoring PC, up to about an hour, and is faster with an active cool-down (easy movement after exercise).
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.4] Sporting examples: 400 m race, 100 m swim, a long sustained attack in a team game.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.b] For anything longer than about 2 minutes, the body relies on oxygen. The aerobic system is slower to deliver ATP, but it can keep going for hours and does not cause fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.0] Fuel: carbohydrate and fat (protein only in extreme cases), broken down with oxygen. Using fat as well as carbohydrate is what lets the system last so long.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.1] Intensity and duration: low to moderate intensity, from about 2 minutes to several hours, and it predominates beyond about 2 minutes.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.2] By-products: carbon dioxide and water, which are easily removed (the body breathes out the carbon dioxide), so there are no fatigue-causing by-products.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.3] It produces far more ATP from each molecule of fuel than the anaerobic systems, but more slowly, so it cannot supply the quick bursts of ATP that sprints need.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.4] Sporting examples: marathon, 10 km open-water swim, the background work of a Gaelic football match.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.5] It also powers recovery between high-intensity efforts, such as restoring PC between sprints, because the aerobic system supplies the ATP the body needs to rebuild its PC stores.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.b] The three systems are not separate switches. They work together as a continuum (a smooth scale), and what changes with the activity is which one does most of the work.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.0] All three systems work at all times; the one that predominates depends on the duration and intensity of the activity. Do not say a system 'switches on' only after another runs out.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.1] Short, maximal activities rely mainly on ATP-PC; high-intensity efforts lasting up to about 2 minutes rely mainly on the lactic acid system; longer, moderate efforts rely mainly on the aerobic system.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.2] Main system by event: long jump (3 to 5 seconds, high intensity) ATP-PC; 400 m (45 seconds or more, high intensity) lactic acid; marathon (2 hours or more, low to medium intensity) aerobic.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.3] Intermittent team games (bursts of effort with rests) use all three: ATP-PC for sprints, jumps and tackles; lactic acid for repeated efforts with short recovery; aerobic for the overall duration and for recovery between efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.4] On an energy-system graph of contribution against time, the ATP-PC line peaks first and falls within about 10 seconds, the lactic acid line peaks next, and the aerobic line rises steadily and dominates after about 2 minutes.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.b] Knowing which system an activity relies on lets athletes and coaches plan smarter. The same knowledge guides how to train, how to compete and how to recover.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.0] Preparation: train the predominant system (specificity, meaning training matches the demands of the activity). For ATP-PC, use short maximal sprints with full recovery; for the lactic acid system, repeated high-intensity intervals with short recovery to build tolerance of lactic acid; for the aerobic system, continuous or long-interval training.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.1] Work-to-rest ratios match the system: for ATP-PC, about 1:3 or longer so PC is restored before the next effort; for the lactic acid system, shorter recovery; aerobic work is near-continuous.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.2] Performance: pacing strategy matters, because going too hard early in a 400 m or 1500 m race floods the muscles with lactic acid and causes early fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.3] Performance: substitutions and breaks in play (tactical rests) let team players restore PC between efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.4] Recovery: an active cool-down removes lactic acid faster; carbohydrate refuels glycogen for the lactic acid and aerobic systems; full rest between maximal efforts restores PC.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.5] Diet link: carbohydrate fuels the lactic acid and aerobic systems; creatine supplementation aims to raise PC stores for the ATP-PC system.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.b] Two swimming events at opposite ends of the continuum show how the same knowledge is applied. One is a short, intense race and the other a very long, steady one.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.0] 100 m backstroke: about one minute at very high intensity, so the ATP-PC system powers the start and the lactic acid system supplies most of the race.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.1] Preparation for the 100 m backstroke focuses on maximal starts and turns with full recovery (to restore PC) and on high-intensity intervals with short recovery (to build tolerance of lactic acid).
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.2] 10 km open-water swim: about two hours at moderate intensity, so the aerobic system supplies almost all the energy, using carbohydrate and fat.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.3] Preparation for the 10 km swim focuses on long continuous swims and long intervals, pacing practice and feeding with carbohydrate during the race to keep fuel available.
