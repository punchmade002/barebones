TARGET pe-performance-demands-s6: 6. Designing a fitness plan
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.0] Continuous training: steady, moderate-intensity exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes in the aerobic zone (about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate). Develops cardio-respiratory and muscular endurance through the aerobic system. Suits distance running, swimming and cycling.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.1] Interval training: periods of work alternated with periods of rest or recovery, set by the work-to-rest ratio, the number of repetitions and the number of sets.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.2] Short, high-intensity intervals with longer recovery develop speed and anaerobic capacity; longer intervals at lower intensity develop aerobic fitness. Interval training suits intermittent sports such as hurling, soccer and basketball.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.3] Fartlek (speed play): continuous running with changes of pace and terrain, such as jogging, sprinting between lamp posts and running uphill. Trains the aerobic and anaerobic systems together and suits games players.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.0] Circuit training: a series of stations worked in turn with set work and rest times; can be designed for muscular endurance, strength, cardio-respiratory endurance or sport skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.1] Resistance (weight) training: working against a load such as free weights, machines or body weight, with exercises such as squats, lunges, presses, rows and curls.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.2] Heavy loads with few repetitions build strength; light loads with many repetitions build muscular endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.3] Plyometric training: jumping, bounding and hopping in which a lengthening contraction on landing is immediately followed by a forceful shortening contraction on take-off. Develops power for jumping and sprinting.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.4] Plyometrics needs a strength base, a forgiving surface and low volume, so it is not for beginners.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.5] Static stretching: a stretch held still at mild tension; best in the cool-down.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.6] Dynamic stretching: controlled movement through the full range, such as leg swings; used in the warm-up.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.7] PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) stretching: stretch the muscle, contract it against resistance from a partner, relax, then stretch further; gives the greatest gains in range.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.0] Hurling: interval training and fartlek suit the stop-start game better than continuous training, with resistance and plyometric work for power in striking and jumping.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.1] Distance swimming: continuous and long-interval training develop the aerobic system the event depends on.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.2] Gymnastics: flexibility training and body-weight strength work matter more than long aerobic sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.3] The best method depends on the energy systems and fitness components the activity demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.0] Training in the activity: conditioned games and sport-specific drills that are specific, motivating and train skill under fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.1] Training outside the activity: gym-based resistance training, plyometrics, swimming or cycling as cross-training, and yoga or Pilates for flexibility and core strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.2] Benefits of training outside the activity: overloads specific components precisely, builds a fitness base, adds variety, and reduces overuse of the sport's repeated movements.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.3] Limitation of training outside the activity: it is less specific, so it must be linked back to the activity's movements and demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.4] Example for a hurler: resistance training builds the strength and power for long striking and winning dropping balls; yoga improves hip and shoulder range for the swing.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.0] Step 1: use the fitness test results to identify the weakest components that matter most for the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.1] Step 2: set goals for those components.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.2] Step 3: select training methods suited to each goal.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.3] Step 4: apply FITT and the principles of training, planning sessions per week across several weeks with progression, a warm-up and cool-down in each session, and rest days.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.4] Step 5: re-test at the end to evaluate the programme and adjust it.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.5] Illustrative six-week plan for a soccer player whose tests show weak speed and agility: two speed and agility sessions a week (sprints and cone drills with full recovery), one resistance session for leg strength, one fartlek run; repetitions rise every two weeks; week four is lighter; re-test in week six.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.0] Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the session: if recovery is adequate, the body rebuilds slightly above its previous level (supercompensation).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.1] Cool-down: light activity and stretching help remove waste products and return the body to rest.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.2] Rehydration: replaces fluid and electrolytes lost in sweat.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.3] Refuelling soon after exercise: carbohydrate restores muscle glycogen and protein repairs muscle tissue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.4] Sleep: most tissue repair and growth takes place during sleep.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.5] Active recovery: a light session, such as an easy swim the day after a match, keeps blood flowing to the muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.6] Massage and foam rolling: reduce muscle tightness and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.7] Cold-water immersion: reduces inflammation and soreness after intense exercise.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.8] Compression garments: may reduce swelling and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.9] Planned rest days and lighter weeks prevent overtraining (its signs are in the notes on safe practice).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.0] Periodisation: dividing a training plan into planned phases or cycles, each with a specific aim, so that volume, intensity and recovery vary systematically to bring the performer to peak performance at target competitions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.1] Macrocycle: the long-term plan, often a year or a season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.2] Mesocycle: a block of several weeks, often 4 to 6, with one focus, such as building strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.3] Microcycle: usually one week of sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.4] Preparation phase: general fitness first, then specific fitness and skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.5] Competition phase: fitness is maintained and the performer peaks for key events; tapering before the main event reduces training volume while keeping intensity, so the performer arrives fresh.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.6] Transition phase: active rest and recovery after the season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.7] Why coaches use it: the performer peaks at the right time, develops qualities in a logical order, avoids overtraining and injury, and stays motivated through variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.8] Peaking for a single major event, such as an Olympic Games every four years, uses a long-term plan built from these cycles, with each year's macrocycle building towards the target.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s5: 5. Assessing fitness and the principles of training
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.0] Purposes: identify strengths and weaknesses against the demands of the activity, set realistic goals, design and monitor a training programme, motivate the performer, and compare results with norms (normative data).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.1] Valid: a test measures what it claims to measure.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.2] Reliable: a test gives consistent results when repeated under the same conditions.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.3] Standardised protocol: the same warm-up, surface, time of day, equipment and tester each time, so results can be compared.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.4] Tests must be safe and suited to the performer, and are repeated before and after a programme to show progress.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.5] Field tests are practical, cheap and can test many people at once; laboratory tests are more accurate but expensive.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance: the multistage fitness test (bleep test), running 20 m shuttles at a pace set by bleeps that get faster each level until the performer can no longer keep up; the level reached is recorded and VO2 max (maximum oxygen uptake) is estimated.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.1] Cardio-respiratory endurance: the Cooper 12-minute run, recording the distance covered in 12 minutes.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.2] Cardio-respiratory endurance for intermittent team sports: the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test, repeated shuttles with short recovery breaks.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.3] Muscular endurance: press-up and sit-up (curl-up) tests, the maximum number in one minute or to exhaustion; or the time a plank is held.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.4] Strength: the hand-grip dynamometer, or a one-repetition maximum (1RM), the heaviest weight lifted once with correct technique.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.5] Flexibility: the sit-and-reach test, measuring hamstring and lower-back flexibility as the distance reached past the toes.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.6] Body composition: skinfold callipers at set sites, bioelectrical impedance scales, or BMI (body mass index), which can wrongly class muscular people as overweight.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.0] Speed: a 30 m sprint from a standing start, timed.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.1] Agility: the Illinois agility run or the T-test, timing a set course with changes of direction.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.2] Power: the vertical (sergeant) jump, measuring jump height, or the standing broad jump, measuring distance.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.3] Balance: the stork stand, timing how long the performer balances on one foot.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.4] Co-ordination: the alternate-hand wall-toss test, counting catches in 30 seconds.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.5] Reaction time: the ruler drop test, in which a partner drops a ruler between the performer's thumb and finger and the distance it falls before being caught is recorded.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.0] A test battery is a set of tests chosen to measure the components a specific activity demands, based on a needs analysis.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.1] Step 1: analyse the activity's demands and select its key components.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.2] Step 2: choose one valid, reliable test for each component that is specific to the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.3] Step 3: order the tests so fatigue does not distort results: non-fatiguing tests such as flexibility, power and agility first, and the maximal endurance test last.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.4] Step 4: standardise the conditions, record the results and compare them with norms or previous results.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.5] Example battery for soccer: 30 m sprint (speed for breaking away), Illinois agility run (changes of direction), vertical jump (power for heading), then the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test (repeated high-intensity running).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.b] The specification names readiness, specificity, progression, overload and rest/recovery. Exam answers also accept reversibility, individual differences and variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.0] Specificity: training must match the demands of the activity, including its energy systems, muscles, movements and speeds.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.1] Progression: the training load is increased gradually as the body adapts.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.2] Overload: the body must work harder than normal to adapt, achieved by raising frequency, intensity or time.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.3] Rest and recovery: the body adapts during recovery, so too little rest leads to overtraining.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.4] Readiness: the performer must be physically and mentally ready for the training, considering age, maturity, fitness base and skill.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.5] Reversibility: fitness gains are lost if training stops.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.6] Individual differences: programmes should suit the individual's fitness, goals and response to training.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.7] Variety (avoiding tedium): varying training keeps motivation high and reduces overuse injuries.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.0] Specificity transfers training directly to competition, but too narrow a focus can neglect general fitness and cause overuse injuries.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.1] Overload drives improvement, but progressing too fast raises injury and overtraining risk.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.2] Rest and recovery allow adaptation, but too much rest causes reversibility.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.3] Readiness protects young athletes, who should not be given adult training loads before they are physically mature.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.b] Frequency (how often), Intensity (how hard), Time (how long) and Type (what kind of exercise). The values below are typical guidance, not fixed rules.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance: 3 to 5 times a week, 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate, at least 20 minutes, continuous running, cycling or swimming.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.1] Muscular endurance: 2 to 3 times a week, light loads, 15 or more repetitions in 2 to 3 sets, circuits or body-weight exercises.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.2] Strength: 2 to 3 times a week with rest days between, heavy loads, 3 to 6 repetitions in 3 to 5 sets, free weights or machines.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.3] Flexibility: 3 to 5 times a week, stretch to mild tension, hold each stretch about 30 seconds, static, dynamic or PNF stretching.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.4] Body composition: most days, moderate intensity, longer sessions of 30 minutes or more, aerobic exercise combined with a balanced diet.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.5] Speed: 2 to 3 times a week, maximal efforts, short sprints with full recovery, sprint intervals.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.6] Agility: 2 to 3 times a week, high intensity, short drills with recovery, ladder and cone drills.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.7] Power: 2 to 3 times a week, explosive maximal efforts, low repetitions with full recovery, plyometrics or jump training.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.8] Balance: 3 or more times a week, progressively harder positions, short holds, single-leg and unstable-surface exercises.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.9] Co-ordination: regular short practices, increasing complexity, skill drills combining body parts.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.10] Reaction time: regular short practices, maximal responses, reaction drills to sounds or visual signals.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.0] Maximum heart rate is estimated as 220 minus age.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.1] Aerobic training zone: about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate. Anaerobic training zone: about 80 to 90%.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.2] Aerobic threshold: the lower edge of the aerobic zone, below which training gives little cardio-respiratory benefit.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.3] Anaerobic (lactate) threshold: the intensity above which lactic acid builds up faster than it can be removed.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.4] Example: a 17-year-old's estimated maximum heart rate is 220 − 17 = 203 beats per minute, so the aerobic zone is about 122 to 162 beats per minute.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.5] Intensity is monitored by heart rate or by the rating of perceived exertion (RPE), the performer's own score of how hard they are working.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s4: 4. Applying the fitness components
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.0] Needs analysis: working out which fitness components an activity, position or role demands by examining its duration, intensity, pattern of work and rest, the movements involved and the energy systems used.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.1] The performer's current fitness is then compared with those demands, usually by fitness testing, to find the gaps to train.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.2] Examining the extent: rank each component as essential, important or less important for the activity, and justify each with a specific moment of play or performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.0.3] Positions and roles differ within one activity: a soccer goalkeeper needs reaction time, power and agility more than cardio-respiratory endurance, while a midfielder needs the reverse.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (essential): players cover long distances over two halves and must recover between efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.1] Speed (essential): to win the race to a breaking ball or get away from a marker.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.2] Agility (essential): to side-step a tackler or turn quickly in defence.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.3] Power (important): to jump for a high catch and to kick long.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.1.4] Muscular endurance (important): legs keep working for repeated sprints and challenges late in the game.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.0] Flexibility (essential): for splits, walkovers and extended body lines.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.1] Strength (essential): to support body weight in balances and on the hands.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.2] Balance (essential): for held positions and landings.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.3] Power (essential): for jumps, tumbles and vaults.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.4] Co-ordination (essential): to link elements fluently.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.2.5] Cardio-respiratory endurance (less important): routines are short.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (essential): the event lasts many minutes or hours at a steady pace.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.1] Muscular endurance (essential): the legs repeat thousands of strides.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.2] Body composition (important): a low body-fat percentage reduces the weight carried.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.3.3] Reaction time (less important): a slow start matters little over a long race.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.b] Fitness can be trained inside the activity itself, while skills are practised under the fatigue and pressure of competition.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.0] Methods: conditioned and small-sided games, game-speed drills and skill circuits.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.1] Example: a small-sided possession game with a two-touch limit develops cardio-respiratory endurance, agility and speed while players pass and move.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.2] Example: a dribbling relay through cones develops speed, agility and co-ordination.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.3] Benefit: specificity, because players use the same movements, energy systems and decisions as in competition.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.4] Benefit: it is motivating and time-efficient, and trains skill under fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.5] Limitation: intensity and overload are harder to control precisely, and some players work less than others.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.4.6] Limitation: it may not develop maximal strength or flexibility, so it is combined with training outside the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.0] A combined approach develops a health-related and a performance-related component in the same activity or session.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.1] Designing one: name the two components, describe what the performer does with sets, repetitions or time, state how it progresses, explain how each component is trained, and link it to the activity's demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.2] Example, flexibility (HRF) and balance (PRF): a single-leg balance-and-reach routine, standing on one leg and reaching the free foot out slowly to points around the body through a full range, progressing to an unstable surface.
[pe-performance-demands-s4.5.3] Example, muscular endurance (HRF) and agility (PRF): a circuit alternating ladder footwork drills with body-weight squats and lunges, with timed work and rest at each station.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s9: 9. Energy systems
[pe-performance-demands-s9.0.0] ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the only direct source of energy for muscle contraction.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.0.1] Muscles store only enough ATP for about 2 to 3 seconds of work, so it must be constantly resynthesised by three energy systems.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.0] Fuel: phosphocreatine (PC) stored in the muscle; no oxygen is needed (anaerobic).
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.1] Intensity and duration: maximal intensity, for about 8 to 10 seconds.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.2] By-products: none that cause fatigue; fatigue comes from PC running out.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.3] Recovery: PC stores are about half restored in 30 seconds and almost fully restored in about 3 minutes of rest.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.4] Sporting examples: 100 m sprint, long jump, shot-put, a tackle, a jump to catch a ball.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.0] Fuel: carbohydrate (glycogen and glucose) broken down without oxygen.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.1] Intensity and duration: high intensity, from about 10 seconds to about 2 minutes.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.2] By-product: lactic acid, which lowers the pH in the muscle and causes fatigue and a burning feeling.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.3] Recovery: removing lactic acid takes much longer, up to about an hour, and is faster with an active cool-down.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.4] Sporting examples: 400 m race, 100 m swim, a long sustained attack in a team game.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.0] Fuel: carbohydrate and fat (protein only in extreme cases), broken down with oxygen.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.1] Intensity and duration: low to moderate intensity, from about 2 minutes to several hours.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.2] By-products: carbon dioxide and water, which are easily removed and do not cause fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.3] It produces far more ATP from each molecule of fuel than the anaerobic systems, but more slowly.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.4] Sporting examples: marathon, 10 km open-water swim, the background work of a Gaelic football match.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.5] It also powers recovery between high-intensity efforts, such as restoring PC between sprints.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.0] All three systems work at all times; the one that predominates depends on the duration and intensity of the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.1] Short, maximal activities rely mainly on ATP-PC; high-intensity efforts lasting up to about 2 minutes rely mainly on the lactic acid system; longer, moderate efforts rely mainly on the aerobic system.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.2] Main system by event: long jump (3 to 5 seconds, high intensity) ATP-PC; 400 m (45 seconds or more, high intensity) lactic acid; marathon (2 hours or more, low to medium intensity) aerobic.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.3] Intermittent team games use all three: ATP-PC for sprints, jumps and tackles; lactic acid for repeated efforts with short recovery; aerobic for the overall duration and for recovery between efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.4] On an energy-system graph of contribution against time, the ATP-PC line peaks first and falls within about 10 seconds, the lactic acid line peaks next, and the aerobic line rises steadily and dominates after about 2 minutes.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.0] Preparation: train the predominant system (specificity). For ATP-PC, short maximal sprints with full recovery; for the lactic acid system, repeated high-intensity intervals with short recovery to build tolerance of lactic acid; for the aerobic system, continuous or long-interval training.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.1] Work-to-rest ratios match the system: for ATP-PC, about 1:3 or longer so PC is restored before the next effort; for the lactic acid system, shorter recovery; aerobic work is near-continuous.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.2] Performance: pacing strategy matters; going too hard early in a 400 m or 1500 m race floods the muscles with lactic acid.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.3] Performance: substitutions and breaks in play let team players restore PC between efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.4] Recovery: an active cool-down removes lactic acid faster; carbohydrate refuels glycogen for the lactic acid and aerobic systems; full rest between maximal efforts restores PC.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.5] Diet link: carbohydrate fuels the lactic acid and aerobic systems; creatine supplementation aims to raise PC stores for the ATP-PC system.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.0] 100 m backstroke: about one minute at very high intensity, so the ATP-PC system powers the start and the lactic acid system supplies most of the race. Preparation focuses on maximal starts and turns with full recovery and on high-intensity intervals with short recovery.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.1] 10 km open-water swim: about two hours at moderate intensity, so the aerobic system supplies almost all the energy, using carbohydrate and fat. Preparation focuses on long continuous swims and long intervals, pacing practice and feeding with carbohydrate during the race.
