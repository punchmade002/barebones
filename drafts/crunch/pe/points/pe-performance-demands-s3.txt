TARGET pe-performance-demands-s3: 3. Performance-related fitness
[pe-performance-demands-s3.0.b] Many sports need a player to change direction suddenly without losing control. That skill is agility, one of the six components of performance-related fitness (the fitness qualities that help a person perform well in a sport).
[pe-performance-demands-s3.0.0] Definition: agility is the ability to change the position or direction of the body quickly and with control, while keeping balance.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.0.1] Rugby: a ball carrier side-steps (steps sharply to one side) to evade a tackler. This is agility because the direction changes quickly and the player stays in control.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.0.2] Badminton: a player recovers to the centre of the court after each shot and changes direction to reach the next shuttle (the feathered shot that is hit over the net).
[pe-performance-demands-s3.1.b] Balance is what stops you toppling over, whether you are still or moving. It comes in two types, and exam answers should name both.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.1.0] Definition: balance is the ability to keep the body's centre of mass (the point around which its weight is evenly spread) over its base of support (the part of the body in contact with the ground).
[pe-performance-demands-s3.1.1] Static balance: balance while stationary, such as holding a handstand or an arabesque (a ballet or gymnastics pose standing on one leg with the other leg lifted behind).
[pe-performance-demands-s3.1.2] Dynamic balance: balance while moving, such as a gymnast travelling along the beam or a hurler striking the ball on the run.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.2.b] Most skills need several parts of the body, or the eyes and the body, to work as one. Co-ordination is how well they do that.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.2.0] Definition: co-ordination is the ability to use two or more body parts, or the senses and body parts, together smoothly and efficiently.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.2.1] Hurling: hand-eye co-ordination (the eyes guiding the hands) is needed to strike a moving sliotar (the hurling ball).
[pe-performance-demands-s3.2.2] Front crawl: the swimmer must time the arm pull, the leg kick and the breathing so that they work together.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.3.b] Power combines two other qualities: how strong a movement is and how fast it is done. A strong but slow movement is not powerful.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.3.0] Definition: power is the ability to apply strength (the force a muscle can produce) at speed; power = strength × speed.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.3.1] High jump: the explosive (very fast and forceful) drive of the take-off leg, which pushes the jumper upwards.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.3.2] Shot-put: the rapid extension (straightening) of the legs, trunk and arm at release, when the shot leaves the hand.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.3.3] Badminton: the smash, a hard, fast downward shot.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.4.b] Speed is about how quickly the whole body, or just one part of it, covers a distance. It can be measured, which is why it has a formula.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.4.0] Definition: speed is the ability to move the body, or a part of it, from one point to another in the shortest possible time; speed = distance ÷ time.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.4.1] Whole-body speed: a 100 m sprint, or a basketball fast break (a quick attack up the court before the defence is set).
[pe-performance-demands-s3.4.2] Limb speed (the speed of an arm or leg alone): the speed of the arm in a throw or of the leg in a kick.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.5.b] Reaction time measures how quickly you start to respond, not how quickly you finish. To avoid mixing it up with the movement itself, learn it alongside movement time and response time.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.5.0] Definition: reaction time is the time between the presentation of a stimulus (the signal that prompts a response) and the start of the response.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.5.1] Movement time: the time from starting a movement to completing it.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.5.2] Response time = reaction time + movement time, so response time covers the whole action from signal to finish.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.5.3] Sprinting: reacting to the starting gun.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.5.4] Soccer goalkeeping: reacting to a shot or a deflection (a ball that changes direction after hitting a player or post).
[pe-performance-demands-s3.5.5] Two examples in one activity, tennis: reacting to the opponent's serve, and reacting to a volley (a shot hit before the ball bounces) at the net.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.6.b] Exam tables describe an athlete or event and ask which component matters most. Work out what the athlete actually has to do, then match it to a component.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.6.0] A 100 m breaststroke swimmer needs speed and power off the start and turns, where a fast, forceful push is needed.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.6.1] A tennis server needs power and co-ordination, because the serve combines a forceful strike with well-timed movement of the body and arm.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.6.2] A goalkeeper needs reaction time, agility and power, to respond to a shot, change position and dive or jump.
[pe-performance-demands-s3.6.3] Justify the choice by linking the component to a specific moment in the event.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s1: 1. Physical fitness
[pe-performance-demands-s1.0.b] Before splitting fitness into types, you need to know what health and fitness each mean, because the two words are not interchangeable. Fitness is only one part of health.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.0.0] Health, as defined by the World Health Organization (the United Nations health agency): a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity (weakness or frailty).
[pe-performance-demands-s1.0.1] Health is broader than fitness, because it includes mental and social well-being as well as the body. So a person can be fit for their sport but unwell, or healthy but unfit.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.0.2] Physical fitness: the ability to carry out daily tasks and physical activity with vigour and without undue fatigue (without becoming more tired than the task justifies), with energy left to meet emergencies.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.0.3] The specification names four demands of performance, the things an athlete must get right: health-related fitness, performance-related fitness, psychological preparation (getting the mind ready), and diet and nutrition. Each has its own notes in this chapter.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.1.b] Physical fitness is made up of separate components, and the specification sorts them into two lists: those that serve health and those that serve sporting success. You should be able to list both and place any component in the right one.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.1.0] Health-related fitness (HRF): the components linked to good health and a lower risk of disease that everyone needs for daily life.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.1.1] The HRF components are cardio-respiratory endurance (how long the heart and lungs can keep the body working), muscular endurance (how long a muscle can keep working), strength (the most force a muscle can produce), flexibility (range of movement at a joint) and body composition (the proportions of fat and other tissue in the body). Strength sits here, not under PRF.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.1.2] Performance-related fitness (PRF), also called skill-related fitness: the components needed for successful performance in sport. They are agility (changing direction quickly), balance (staying steady), co-ordination (moving body parts smoothly together), power (force applied quickly), speed (how fast you move) and reaction time (how fast you respond to a signal).
[pe-performance-demands-s1.1.3] Classifying a component: co-ordination, agility and reaction time are PRF; muscular endurance, flexibility and strength are HRF.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.2.b] The two categories differ in why you need them, who needs them, how far training can change them and how you build them. These four contrasts are the answer when you are asked to discuss the difference.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.2.0] Purpose: HRF supports health and the demands of daily living, whereas PRF supports the demands of a particular sport or activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.2.1] Who needs it: everyone needs HRF, because everyone has daily tasks to carry out. PRF matters mainly to performers, and which components matter depends on the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.2.2] Trainability (how far training can improve it): HRF responds well to training for most people. PRF is more strongly influenced by genetics (the abilities a person inherits) and is more specific to the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.2.3] How it is developed: HRF through general exercise such as running, swimming and resistance work (training against a load, such as weights). PRF mainly through sport-specific drills and practice, which repeat the movements of that sport.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.3.b] This is a statement you may be asked to evaluate. The answer is that it is inaccurate, but the two types of fitness are linked, and a good answer says both.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.3.0] The statement is inaccurate, because the two differ in purpose, in who needs them, in how trainable they are and in how they are developed.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.3.1] They are linked, however: a performer needs both, and many sports demand a high level of an HRF component, such as cardio-respiratory endurance for a Gaelic football midfielder.
[pe-performance-demands-s1.3.2] Good HRF also supports PRF: strength underpins power, because power is force applied quickly, and flexibility helps agility.
