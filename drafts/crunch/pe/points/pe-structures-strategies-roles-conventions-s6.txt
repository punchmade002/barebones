[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.0.b] A competition needs more than one person keeping order. Different officials look after different jobs, and the type you meet depends on the activity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.0.0] The referee or umpire is in charge of the contest. They apply the rules and make the final decisions on play, so every other official ultimately supports them.

[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.1.0] Apply and interpret the rules throughout the contest, so that both sides are treated by the same standard.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.1.1] Keep the game flowing by restarting play promptly after each stoppage, so that time is not wasted and players stay warm and focused.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.1.2] Ensure safety by stopping play when a risk appears, for example when a player is injured or equipment is faulty.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.1.3] Record scores and results accurately, because the final result depends on them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.1.4] Manage discipline, using cards or sanctions (penalties) against players who break the rules.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.1.5] Start and finish the contest, enforcing its time limits.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.2.b] You must be able to explain the main rules of the activities you study, covering fouls, sanctions and restarts. These examples show the pattern: what counts as a breach, what the penalty is and how play restarts.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.2.0] Soccer: a foul such as tripping an opponent is penalised with a direct free kick, so the fouled team gets a chance to restart play with an advantage.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.2.1] Soccer: after a goal, play restarts with a kick-off from the centre spot.

[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.4.2] Facilities: lines are clearly marked, lighting is adequate and run-off areas (the space around the pitch where players may slide or fall) are safe.

[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.8.3] Panels score difficulty and execution or artistry, so a harder routine and a cleaner or more expressive performance both earn credit.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.8.4] In gymnastics, the execution score starts from a maximum, and judges deduct for each fault.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.9.b] Officiating is demanding for both body and mind. An official who cannot keep up with play or who loses composure will make poorer decisions.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.9.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (the ability of heart and lungs to keep working) and speed are needed to keep up with play. For example, a rugby or soccer referee covers a large distance with repeated high-intensity efforts.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.9.1] Agility and positioning: getting into the best place to see each decision, which means the official must move quickly and change direction.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.9.2] Concentration for the full contest, without a lapse, because one missed incident can change the result.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.9.3] Confidence and decisiveness, making calls clearly and quickly so that players accept them.

[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.10.5] Importance: attire marks the official out and conveys authority, so players and spectators can recognise who is in charge.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.11.b] Officials must communicate their decisions so that everyone understands and accepts them. The best style is assertive: firm and clear without being hostile.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.11.0] Assertive communication is clear, confident, firm and calm.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.11.1] Decisive signals and a strong, sharp whistle show that the decision has been made, so players do not think the official is unsure.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.11.2] Eye contact when making a call shows confidence and tells the player the decision is being communicated directly to them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.11.3] A brief explanation of a decision when one is needed helps players understand the call without opening a debate.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.11.4] Assertive is not aggressive, such as shouting, and not passive, such as avoiding confrontation. Aggression provokes players, while passivity invites them to challenge decisions.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.12.b] Players, coaches and crowds will sometimes disagree with decisions. The official's job is to keep control without turning the disagreement into an argument.

[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s6.13.4] Apply sanctions consistently and report serious incidents, so that proper action can be taken after the contest.

CANDIDATES pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4: 4. Rules, rituals and conventions
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.0.b] Every physical activity needs agreed limits on what players may do, otherwise nobody could compete on equal terms. Rules are those limits, written down and applied by someone neutral.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.0.0] Rules are the formal, written regulations of an activity, set by its governing body (the organisation in charge of the sport) and enforced by officials such as referees, so every player is judged by the same standard.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.0.1] Rules cover scoring, permitted actions, the playing area and equipment, and time, so everyone knows what counts as a point, what is allowed, where play happens and how long it lasts.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.0.2] Rules also set sanctions. A sanction is a penalty for breaking a rule, such as a free kick, a card or a suspension, and it stops players gaining from cheating.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.0.3] Offside in soccer: an attacker in an offside position when a team-mate plays the ball forward may be penalised, and the defending team is awarded an indirect free kick.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.0.4] Four-step rule in Gaelic football: a player may take four steps while holding the ball, then must bounce, hand-pass or kick it, so nobody can simply run with the ball.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.0.5] Foot fault in tennis: a server's foot touches the line, or the court beyond it, before the ball is struck, so the serve is not allowed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.1.b] Not everything around a game is about winning points. Rituals are the repeated, symbolic actions that give a game its sense of occasion.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.1.0] Rituals are customary, often symbolic actions repeated before, during or after an activity. They are repeated traditions that give the activity a sense of occasion.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.1.1] The national anthem is played before an international match, which reminds players and supporters that they are representing their country.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.1.2] The haka is performed by the New Zealand men's rugby team before international matches, expressing the team's identity and tradition.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.1.3] A team huddle before the start focuses the group on a shared goal, so players begin the contest united.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.1.4] A minute's silence honours a person or event, giving everyone present a moment to show respect together.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.2.b] Some good behaviour is expected in sport even though no rule demands it. These unwritten codes are conventions, and they are what keep competition respectful.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.2.0] Conventions are unwritten codes of behaviour that participants are expected to follow, and that the rules do not enforce. Players follow them out of respect, not fear of a penalty.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.2.1] Shaking hands with opponents and officials after a match shows that the contest is over and respect remains.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.2.2] Kicking the ball out of play when an opponent is injured, then returning possession to the opposing team at the restart, puts care for a player ahead of gaining an advantage.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.2.3] A tennis player raises a hand to apologise for a net cord winner (a point won by luck when the ball clips the net), acknowledging the opponent was unlucky.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.2.4] Applauding opponents off the field after a hard contest shows respect for their effort.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.2.5] A convention becomes a rule when the rule-book requires it, such as a rule about kit colours. Once written into the rules it is enforced, so it is no longer a convention.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.3.b] Each of the three does a different job. Together they make an activity fair, meaningful and respectful.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.3.0] Rules make competition fair and safe, because everyone plays under the same limits and dangerous actions can be punished.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.3.1] Rituals build identity, tradition and focus, because repeating them binds a team and its supporters together and prepares players mentally.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.3.2] Conventions express respect and fair play and keep the spirit of the game, because they cover the good behaviour that rules cannot list or enforce.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.b] An event only respects the rules, rituals and conventions of an activity if the organiser plans for them. Good organisation has three stages: planning, building in traditions, and evaluating afterwards.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.0] Planning: the fixture and format (who plays whom, and how the competition is structured), the venue and the equipment are set in advance so nothing is left to chance on the day.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.1] Officials are appointed and briefed, and the rules are clarified to teams in advance, so everyone applies and expects the same rules.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.2] A scoring and recording system is in place before the first contest, so results are accurate and disputes can be settled.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.3] Roles are assigned to the organiser, officials, timekeeper, first aid and stewards (people who guide spectators and keep order), so everyone knows their job.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.4] Rituals are built in, such as team introductions before the start, which give the event a sense of occasion.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.5] Conventions are built in: handshakes at the end, presentation of awards, and acknowledgement of officials and opponents, which promote respect.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.6] Evaluation checks whether the event ran on time and whether rules were applied consistently.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.4.7] Evaluation also checks whether participants behaved respectfully and what would change next time, so the next event improves.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.5.b] Communication is part of respecting an activity. How you speak to players and officials affects performance, behaviour and the atmosphere of the whole event.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.5.0] With players: give clear, calm instructions and listen before reacting, so players understand what is wanted and feel heard.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.5.1] With players: use encouragement rather than criticism, and appropriate language, because positive comments keep confidence and effort high.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.5.2] With officials: accept decisions without dissent, meaning no arguing, gesturing or shouting, because dissent damages the spirit of the game.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.5.3] With officials: ask questions through the captain at an appropriate time, not by confronting the official during play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.5.4] Thank officials after the contest, which shows respect for the job they do.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.6.b] Much of what is communicated in sport is not spoken. In a noisy venue, a gesture or a look can reach a player when words cannot.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.6.0] Hand signals, such as a referee's signals, communicate decisions quickly to players and spectators, even from a distance or over crowd noise.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.6.1] Body language and gestures show approval, instruction or warning without words, so a nod or open posture can reassure a player.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.6.2] Eye contact holds a player's attention and shows that the message is meant for them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.6.3] Tone of voice: a calm, steady tone reduces tension, while a sharp tone signals urgency, so the same words can feel very different.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.7.b] Good communicators in sport share a set of habits that make a message clear and trusted.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.7.0] A clear voice, projected so every team-mate can hear, means instructions are not missed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.7.1] Short, precise instructions given at the right moment are easy to act on, while long ones are forgotten or arrive too late.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.7.2] Active listening means checking understanding by listening carefully and asking questions, so messages are received correctly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.7.3] Consistent signals: the same gesture or call always means the same thing, so there is no confusion in the middle of play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s4.7.4] Whistle use: a clear, distinct blast signals a stoppage or a restart, so everyone knows play has changed.

CANDIDATES pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3: 3. Safe practice
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.b] Sport carries a risk of injury, but much of that risk can be reduced by how people prepare and behave. Safe practice is the name for those habits.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.0] Safe practice means the procedures and behaviours that reduce the risk of injury or harm before, during and after physical activity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.1] In training and performance this includes a warm-up and cool-down, correct technique, and suitable progression and loads, so the body is never asked to do too much too soon.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.2] It also includes supervision by a qualified coach, with rules followed and participants matched by size, age or ability where relevant, so that a smaller or less skilled player is not put at risk by a much stronger opponent.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.3] Intrinsic risk factors come from the performer: fitness level, previous injury, technique and fatigue. For example, a tired player with poor technique is more likely to be hurt.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.4] Extrinsic risk factors come from outside the performer: equipment, playing surface, weather, opponents and coaching. For example, a wet pitch makes slips more likely.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.b] When you run a competition or event, the safety of participants, officials and spectators is the organiser's responsibility. Safety is planned in advance, not left to chance.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.0] Carry out a risk assessment, a systematic check of the venue and activity to identify hazards and the people who could be harmed, so problems are dealt with before the event.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.1] Arrange first aid cover and an emergency action plan, so casualties are treated and moved quickly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.2] Use trained stewards and officials, and check facilities and equipment before the event starts.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.3] Set clear rules and give a briefing to participants and officials, so everyone knows what is expected and what to do in an emergency.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.4] Manage crowds and traffic, for spectators and vehicles, so people can enter, leave and be reached by emergency vehicles safely.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.5] Prepare a weather contingency, with hydration points and shelter for participants, so heat, cold or rain does not cause harm.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.6] Set up communication systems between organisers, first aid and emergency services, so help can be called without delay.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.b] A warm-up is the preparation done just before training or competition. It is built in three phases that move from general to specific, so the body is ready for the exact demands of the activity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.0] Phase 1, pulse raiser: light activity, such as jogging, that gradually increases in intensity to raise the heart rate.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.1] Phase 2, mobility: dynamic stretching (stretching while moving) through the ranges of movement the activity uses, so the joints and muscles are ready for those actions.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.2] Phase 3, activity-specific drills: skill rehearsal that builds up to game intensity, so the performer practises the real movements before they matter.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.3] Mental preparation, such as rehearsing the first passages of play, can be included to focus the performer.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.4] Illustrative soccer warm-up of about 15 minutes: five minutes of jogging, five minutes of dynamic stretches, then five minutes of passing and shooting drills at increasing speed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.b] Each part of a warm-up has a physical or mental effect. Together they make injury less likely and performance better.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.0] It raises heart rate and the temperature of the body and muscles, because warm muscles contract and relax more efficiently.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.1] It increases blood flow and oxygen delivery to the muscles, so they have the fuel they need once work begins.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.2] It improves muscle elasticity and joint mobility, which reduces injury risk because warm, stretchy muscle is less likely to tear.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.3] It rehearses skills and prepares the performer mentally, so they start focused rather than cold.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.b] A cool-down is done after activity, and it eases the body back to rest rather than stopping suddenly. Its content mirrors a warm-up in reverse.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.0] Content: light activity, such as easy jogging or walking, that gradually reduces in intensity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.1] Content: static stretching (holding a stretch still) of the muscles used in the activity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.2] It gradually returns heart rate and breathing to rest, so the body is not left working hard with nothing to do.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.3] It prevents blood pooling (blood collecting in the legs) and the dizziness that can follow stopping suddenly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.4] It speeds the removal of lactic acid (a waste product that builds up in working muscles) from the muscles.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.5] It may reduce delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS), the stiffness and ache that can follow hard exercise.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.6] It helps flexibility, because muscles are warm and stretch easily, and it gives time to reflect on the performance.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.b] What a performer wears and uses affects both comfort and risk of injury. The right choice protects the performer and the people around them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.0] Footwear should suit the surface and activity: studs for grass and court shoes for indoor courts, so the player has grip without twisting an ankle.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.1] Clothing should suit the conditions, with layers for cold or wet weather, so the performer stays warm and dry.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.2] Jewellery is removed before activity, because it can injure the wearer or others.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.3] Helmets with faceguards are compulsory in hurling under GAA rules, and helmets are worn in camogie, to protect the head and face from the ball and hurleys.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.4] Gumshields protect the teeth and mouth in rugby.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.5] Shin guards protect the lower leg in soccer and hockey.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.6] Buoyancy aids are worn in water activities, so a performer stays afloat if they get into difficulty.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.7] Selection: equipment must be the correct size and fit, and meet approved standards. It should also suit the performer's level, so a racket's grip size and head size are chosen to suit their ability.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.b] Even well-chosen equipment becomes dangerous if it is damaged or poorly kept. Regular maintenance keeps facilities safe to use.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.0] Nets, posts and goals are inspected and repaired regularly, and goals must be secured in place.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.1] Surfaces are checked for holes, debris and other hazards before use, so players do not trip or twist an ankle.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.2] Pool water quality is tested and maintained, so swimmers are not exposed to harmful bacteria or chemicals.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.3] Proper storage keeps equipment clean, dry and undamaged.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.4] Maintenance prevents injury, keeps equipment effective and extends its life.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.5] Example: an unsecured goal can topple onto a player, so securing it before each session prevents that injury.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.b] Injuries are grouped by how they start and by the tissue damaged. Knowing the groups helps you name an injury correctly and treat it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.0] Acute injury: sudden onset, such as a sprain, strain, fracture, dislocation, concussion or cut.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.1] Chronic (overuse) injury: develops gradually from repeated stress, such as tendinitis, shin splints, stress fracture or tennis elbow.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.2] Sprain: damage to a ligament, the tissue that joins bone to bone, for example an ankle sprain from landing awkwardly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.3] Strain: damage to a muscle or tendon, the tissue that joins muscle to bone, for example a hamstring strain from sprinting without a warm-up.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.b] Each activity has injuries that happen again and again, because of the movements and contact it involves. Knowing the cause shows how to prevent the injury.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.0] Soccer: ankle sprain, caused by landing awkwardly, which twists the ankle and overstretches a ligament.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.1] Soccer: hamstring strain, caused by sprinting without a warm-up, when cold muscle is stretched too fast.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.2] Rugby: concussion, caused by a collision or tackle that shakes the brain inside the skull.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.3] Rugby: shoulder dislocation, caused by a heavy tackle or a fall onto the shoulder, which forces the joint out of its socket.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.b] Soft-tissue injuries, such as sprains and strains, swell and bleed under the skin. PRICE is a five-step routine that limits this damage in the first 48 to 72 hours.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.0] PRICE stands for Protect, Rest, Ice, Compression and Elevation, and is used in the first 48 to 72 hours after a soft-tissue injury.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.1] Protect: remove the player from the activity and guard the injured area from further damage.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.2] Rest: stop using the injured part, since movement can make the injury worse.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.3] Ice: a cold pack wrapped in cloth and applied in short periods, never directly on the skin, to reduce pain and swelling.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.4] Compression: an elastic bandage applied firmly, but not so tight that it cuts off circulation.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.5] Elevation: raise the injured limb above heart level to reduce swelling.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.6] Avoid heat on a fresh soft-tissue injury, because it increases swelling.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.7] Refer for medical assessment if the injury is severe.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.b] Before deciding whether an injured player can carry on, a coach or first aider needs a quick, orderly check. SALTAPS gives the steps in order.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.0] SALTAPS stands for See, Ask, Look, Touch, Active movement, Passive movement and Strength.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.1] See: watch how the player fell and how they move.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.2] Ask: ask what happened and where it hurts.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.3] Look: check for swelling, bruising or deformity (a joint or limb that looks the wrong shape).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.4] Touch: feel the injured area for tenderness.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.5] Active movement: the player moves the joint themselves.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.6] Passive movement: the examiner moves the joint while the player relaxes.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.7] Strength: the player pushes or pulls against resistance. Pain, weakness or instability at any step means the player should not return to play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.b] Two injuries need special rules because they can be serious or can spread infection: a blow to the head and an open wound.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.0] Concussion signs: confusion, headache, dizziness, loss of consciousness and balance problems.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.1] Concussion can occur without loss of consciousness, so a player who stays awake may still be concussed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.2] Remove the player from play immediately and do not return them that day. The guidance is: if in doubt, sit them out.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.3] Medical clearance is needed before a graduated return to play, meaning a step-by-step return rather than straight back to full play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.4] Cuts and bleeding: apply pressure with a clean dressing, wearing gloves, to stop the blood and protect the helper from infection.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.5] Blood rule: the player leaves the field until the bleeding stops.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.b] Training makes you fitter only when the body has time to recover. Without that recovery, more training makes performance worse, which is overtraining.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.0] Overtraining: training too hard or too often without enough recovery, so performance declines.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.1] Symptoms: persistent fatigue, a raised resting heart rate and poor sleep.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.2] Symptoms: frequent illness and injury, because a tired body is less able to resist infection or cope with stress.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.3] Symptoms: loss of motivation and irritability.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.4] Symptoms: falling performance and muscle soreness that does not go away.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.b] A good schedule balances work and recovery. These principles keep the load rising without pushing the athlete into overtraining or fatigue.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.0] Progress gradually: a common rule of thumb is to raise weekly load by about 10%, which is guidance rather than a law.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.1] Build in rest days and easy weeks so the body can recover.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.2] Periodisation, alternating harder and easier blocks of training, is one way to plan recovery into a schedule.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.3] Vary intensity so that not every session is maximal.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.4] Monitor load with session RPE (how hard the athlete rates the session) multiplied by session duration, heart rate or a wellness questionnaire.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.5] Adequate sleep and nutrition support recovery between sessions.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.6] Listen to the athlete: reduce load when they report fatigue or pain.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.14.b] Evaluating a schedule means judging it against those principles and then suggesting improvements. You look for where load rises too fast or recovery is missing.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.14.0] Check where weekly load rises sharply, since a sudden jump increases injury and overtraining risk.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.14.1] Check whether the schedule includes rest days and easy weeks, and whether intensity varies, because a plan with every session hard gives no time to recover.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.14.2] Propose changes that smooth the jump, add rest or vary the intensity.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s7: 7. Psychological preparation
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.b] Skill alone does not decide a performance; what the performer believes about their skill matters too. Confidence shapes how hard they try, what they attempt and how they cope under pressure.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.0] Confidence is a performer's belief in their ability to perform successfully. Self-efficacy is confidence in one specific situation, such as taking a penalty in a final, so a player can be confident in training yet doubtful in that moment.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.1] Albert Bandura's theory gives four sources of self-efficacy. The first is performance accomplishments (past success).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.2] The second source is vicarious experiences (watching similar others succeed), which tells the performer 'someone like me can do this'. The third is verbal persuasion (encouragement from others).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.3] The fourth source is emotional arousal: how the performer interprets their body's state, for example reading a racing heart as readiness rather than as fear.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.4] High confidence leads to more effort and persistence, and to willingness to attempt difficult skills and to take responsibility at key moments, because the performer expects to succeed.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.5] Low confidence leads to avoiding the ball or difficult moves, hesitation, and more anxiety, because the performer expects to fail. Over-confidence can also harm, causing complacency (relaxing too much) and poor preparation.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.6] Strategies to build confidence: set achievable goals so success builds up; recall or watch video of past successes; use positive self-talk; use imagery (picturing yourself performing well); watch demonstrations by similar performers; and receive specific praise from the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.b] Before anyone can understand anxiety they need to know about arousal, the body and mind's level of activation. Performers need some arousal to perform well, but the right amount depends on the task and the person.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.0] Arousal is the level of physical and mental activation or readiness, on a scale from deep sleep to extreme excitement. It is neutral: it is simply how switched on the performer is, not a good or bad feeling.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.1] The inverted-U hypothesis (based on the Yerkes-Dodson law) says performance improves as arousal rises to an optimal level, then declines if arousal keeps rising. Plotted on a graph this makes an upside-down U.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.2] Under-aroused, the performer is bored, slow to react and makes careless errors, because they are not alert enough for the task.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.3] Over-aroused, muscles tense, attention narrows, and decisions and co-ordination suffer, because the performer is overloaded.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.4] The optimal level is lower for complex and fine skills and for beginners, such as a golf putt or a beginner's handspring, because these need precise control and a lot of thinking.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.5] The optimal level is higher for simple and gross skills and for experts, such as a rugby tackle or a weightlift, because these need force and speed and the expert has them well learned.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.b] Anxiety is what arousal can turn into when a performer feels threatened. It has a mental side and a physical side, and each affects performance in its own way.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.0] Anxiety is a negative emotional state of worry and apprehension (a fearful sense that something will go wrong), linked to high arousal.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.1] Cognitive anxiety is the mental side: worry, negative thoughts and self-doubt, for example dwelling on missing a free.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.2] Somatic anxiety is the physical side: a raised heart rate, sweating, muscle tension and butterflies in the stomach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.3] State anxiety is anxiety felt in a particular situation, such as before a penalty. Trait anxiety is a person's general tendency to be anxious across situations, so a high-trait performer feels it more often.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.4] Effects on performance: attention is narrowed or distracted, muscles tense, and co-ordination and decision-making break down. A performer may choke under pressure (fail at a skill they can normally do).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.5] Some anxiety can help if the performer interprets the feelings as readiness rather than threat, which is how nerves can become useful energy.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.6] Strategies: breathing control; progressive muscular relaxation (tensing and relaxing muscle groups in turn); positive self-talk and reframing nerves as excitement; imagery; a pre-performance routine; and simulating competition pressure in practice so it feels familiar.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.7] Match the strategy to the problem: relaxation and breathing lower somatic anxiety, while self-talk and reframing tackle the worry of cognitive anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.b] Motivation is what gets a performer to the training session and keeps them going when it is hard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.0] Motivation is the drive to begin, direct and keep going with an activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.1] Intrinsic motivation comes from within, such as enjoyment, mastery and satisfaction. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside, such as trophies, praise, selection and rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.2] Effects on practice: a motivated performer attends, works hard, keeps the quality of repetitions high when no one is watching and persists when progress is slow.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.3] Effects on performance: persistence when losing, effort to the final whistle, and willingness to train through difficult phases.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.4] Strategies: SMART goal setting; variety in training so it stays interesting; recording progress so improvement is visible; and specific praise and positive feedback.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.5] More strategies: give the performer choice and ownership, so they feel the training is theirs; set team targets; and use appropriate rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.6] Relying too much on extrinsic rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, because the performer starts to train for the prize rather than the enjoyment. Rewards should therefore recognise effort and improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.b] Concentration means putting your attention on the right thing at the right time. Different moments in a game need different kinds of focus, which Nideffer's model describes.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.0] Concentration is the ability to focus attention on the relevant cues (the information that matters), ignore distractions, and keep that focus over time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.1] Robert Nideffer described attentional focus as broad or narrow and internal or external. Broad takes in lots of information; narrow takes in one thing; internal is inside the performer; external is the outside world.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.2] Broad-external is reading the whole game, such as a quarterback or midfielder scanning the field. Narrow-external is focusing on one cue, such as watching the ball onto the racket.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.3] Broad-internal is planning tactics or analysing performance in the mind. Narrow-internal is focusing on one's own body or thoughts, such as a pre-shot routine or a key cue word.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.4] Effects of lapses: errors, missed cues and poor decisions, especially late in a contest or during long waits between efforts, such as between attempts in a field event.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.5] Strategies: pre-performance routines; cue words (short trigger words); focusing on process goals (what to do, not the result); and imagery.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.6] Further strategies: thought stopping (recognising a negative thought and replacing it with a positive one); simulation training with crowd noise and distractions, so distractions lose their effect; and breaking a long event into short segments.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.b] Feedback is how a performer finds out what happened, and it drives improvement. Feedback is classified in several ways, and exam questions often ask you to identify the type.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.0] Feedback is information a performer receives about their performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.1] Intrinsic feedback comes from within, through the senses and the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic feedback), such as feeling a clean strike. Extrinsic feedback comes from outside, such as a coach, video or the scoreboard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.2] Knowledge of results (KR) is information about the outcome, such as whether the shot went in or the time achieved.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.3] Knowledge of performance (KP) is information about the quality of the movement or technique, such as the elbow position at release. Do not swap KR and KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.4] Positive feedback tells the performer what went well and motivates. Negative feedback tells them what went wrong and helps correct it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.5] Concurrent feedback is given during the performance; terminal feedback is given after it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.6] Beginners need frequent, simple, positive, extrinsic feedback and KR, because they cannot yet judge their own movement. Experienced performers rely more on intrinsic feedback and detailed KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.7] Good feedback is specific, timely and constructive. Too much feedback overloads the learner and creates dependence on the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.b] Mental preparation is not only a pre-match job. Strategies are used at three stages, and each strategy has strengths and limits worth knowing.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.0] Before: goal setting for the performance; imagery of key moments; a pre-performance routine; relaxation and breathing; positive self-talk; and preparing equipment and a game plan, so nothing is left to worry about.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.1] During: cue words and self-talk; a refocusing routine after errors, such as a deep breath and a reset word; breathing control in breaks in play; and focusing on the process, one point at a time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.2] After: reflection and performance review; constructive feedback; relaxation and recovery; and resetting goals. A poor result is reframed (viewed differently) as information for improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.3] Evaluating imagery: it builds confidence and rehearses skills without physical fatigue, but it needs practice and a clear, controllable image to work.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.4] Evaluating goal setting: it directs effort and raises motivation, but unrealistic or outcome-only goals can increase anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.5] Evaluating relaxation techniques: they lower somatic anxiety quickly, but too much relaxation before a power event can leave the performer under-aroused.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.b] A personal action plan turns the psychological factors into a plan you could actually follow. It starts from the performer's own weaknesses and ends with a check on whether it worked.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.0] Step 1: identify the psychological factors to target from self-assessment, such as an anxiety questionnaire or a reflective diary.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.1] Step 2: set goals for those factors.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.2] Step 3: choose one or two strategies for before, during and after performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.3] Step 4: give a rationale for each strategy, explaining how it affects the targeted factor. A rationale is the reason the strategy was chosen.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.4] Step 5: say how progress will be monitored and reviewed, such as rating confidence after each match.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.5] Illustrative plan for a basketball free-throw shooter with high anxiety: before, breathing control and imagery of successful shots to lower somatic anxiety and build confidence.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.6] During, the same pre-shot routine and a cue word at every free throw to narrow focus. After, a review of video and a diary rating of anxiety to track progress.
