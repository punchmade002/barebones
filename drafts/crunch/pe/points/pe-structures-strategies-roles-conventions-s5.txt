CANDIDATES pe-learning-skill-technique-s4: 4. Skill acquisition
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.b] A skill (a learned movement or action, such as a shot or a throw) is not mastered all at once. Fitts and Posner (1967) described three stages in order: cognitive, associative, then autonomous. What the performer does, how the movement looks and what the coach should do all change at each stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.0] Cognitive (beginner) stage: the performer forms a mental picture of the skill, usually from a demonstration, and works out what to do.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.1] Cognitive stage: movements are slow, jerky and inconsistent. Errors are frequent and large and the performer often cannot detect them, because full conscious attention (deliberate thinking) is on the movement.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.2] Cognitive stage coaching: simple, unopposed practice (no defender or opponent), clear demonstrations and frequent, specific feedback from the coach, because the performer cannot yet spot errors alone. Progress is rapid but fragile, meaning it is easily lost.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.3] Associative (intermediate) stage: the basic movement pattern is in place, so movements are smoother and more consistent. Errors are fewer and smaller, and the performer begins to detect some through the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic sense).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.4] Associative stage coaching: add pressure, speed and simple opposition, and make feedback more detailed. Most learners spend the longest time at this stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.5] Autonomous (expert) stage: the skill is performed automatically, consistently and fluently with little conscious attention, so attention is free for tactics, opponents and decisions.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.6] Autonomous stage coaching: the performer detects and corrects their own errors. Practice keeps the skill grooved (so well learned it repeats the same way) under competitive conditions, and feedback is fine-tuning.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.b] Exams ask you to describe one named skill at each stage. The basketball jump shot (shooting while jumping) shows how the same skill looks different as the learner improves.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.0] Cognitive: the feet are not set, the elbow flares out, the ball is pushed from the chest and the release point varies. The player can shoot only when stationary and unopposed.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.1] Associative: the set-up and release are mostly correct and the success rate rises, but the shot breaks down when a defender closes or the player is tired.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.2] Autonomous: the shot is released quickly and consistently off the dribble (while bouncing the ball) or the catch, under pressure, while the player reads the defence.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.3] Identifying a stage from evidence: an elite athlete winning national titles with a consistent technique is at the autonomous stage. Someone new to a sport who is inconsistent and needs instructions is at the cognitive stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.b] Learners improve faster when the coach gives them the right kind of help. Guidance can be visual, verbal, manual or mechanical, and it works best alongside feedback, mental rehearsal and motivation.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.0] Visual guidance and demonstration: accurate demonstrations, video and model performers (someone who performs the skill well) give the learner a clear picture, which matters most at the cognitive stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.1] Verbal guidance: short, clear key teaching points, because too much information overloads a beginner.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.2] Manual guidance: physically moving the learner through the movement, such as a coach guiding the arm through a swing.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.3] Mechanical guidance: equipment that supports the learner, such as a float in swimming or a harness on a trampoline.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.4] Manual and mechanical guidance build safety and confidence, but over-use creates dependence and the learner may not develop the feel of the movement.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.5] Feedback after attempts tells the learner what to change. There is more on this in Psychological preparation, which covers the types of feedback.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.6] Mental rehearsal (imagery): imagining performing the skill between physical attempts strengthens the mental picture.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.7] Motivation (the drive to keep trying), clear goals and practice matched to the learner's stage keep learning effective.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.b] A coach must decide whether to teach a skill as one piece or in sections. Whole practice, part practice, whole-part-whole and progressive part are the four ways of breaking a skill down.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.0] Whole practice: the skill is practised in its entirety. It suits skills that are fast and fluid or cannot easily be broken into parts, such as a golf swing or a javelin throw.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.1] Part practice: one sub-routine (a section of the skill) is practised in isolation, such as the ball toss in a tennis serve. It suits complex serial skills (skills made of several linked parts) and dangerous elements.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.2] Whole-part-whole: the learner tries the whole skill, a weak part is isolated and practised, then the whole skill is performed again.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.3] Progressive part (chaining): the first part is learned, then the second is added, and so on, such as the hop, step and jump of a triple jump.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.b] Practice can also be organised by how much rest it includes and by whether conditions stay the same. Massed or distributed is about rest, and fixed or variable is about conditions.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.0] Massed practice: continuous practice with little or no rest. It suits simple, closed (predictable, in a stable setting), discrete (a short skill with a clear start and end) skills and motivated, fit, experienced performers, such as repeated basketball free throws.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.1] Distributed practice: practice broken up by rest or other activities. It suits beginners and complex or tiring skills, and lowers fatigue and injury risk.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.2] Fixed practice: the skill repeated in unchanging conditions to groove the movement. It suits closed skills such as a free throw or a penalty kick.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.3] Variable practice: the skill practised in changing situations. It suits open skills (skills where the situation keeps changing) because it builds adaptability, such as passing under different levels of defensive pressure.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.4] The pairs are separate choices: whole or part (how the skill is broken down), massed or distributed (rest), fixed or variable (conditions).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.b] Good practice follows a set of principles, which are the rules a coach uses to plan and judge a session. Exam answers use these seven.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.0] Specificity: practice reproduces the demands of the competitive situation, including the same skills, speeds, conditions and fatigue, so learning transfers to performance.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.1] Progression: practice moves from simple to complex, unopposed to opposed, slow to game speed and closed to open, raising the demand only when the previous level is secure.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.2] Appropriate distribution: the balance of practice and rest (massed or distributed) suits the learner and the skill.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.3] Variety: different drills and conditions keep motivation high and build adaptability.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.4] Quality of repetitions: correct technique is repeated, not just volume, and practice stops before fatigue breaks down technique.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.5] Clear goals and regular feedback direct each session.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.6] Practice is matched to the individual's stage of learning and ability, is safe and begins with a warm-up.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.b] A practice schedule applies the methods and principles to one skill. It plans a single session and then the weeks of work, and every choice is justified.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.0] Session structure: warm-up; introduction and demonstration with key teaching points; progressive drills from simple to complex; a conditioned game (a game with rules changed to focus on the skill) or competitive scenario; cool-down; review and reflection.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.1] Over several weeks: set the number of sessions per week and their length, raise the difficulty week by week, choose practice methods and record progress, such as a success rate out of ten attempts.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.2] Justify each choice by the principles and the type of skill: fixed, massed whole practice suits a closed free throw; variable practice suits an open pass.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.3] Illustrative six-week plan for a basketball lay-up (a shot taken close to the basket after running): weeks 1 and 2 standing footwork then one-dribble lay-ups unopposed; weeks 3 and 4 lay-ups from the wing at game speed off either hand.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.4] Weeks 5 and 6 of the same illustrative lay-up plan: lay-ups against a passive then an active defender, with success recorded each session.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s6: 6. Designing a fitness plan
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.0] Continuous training: steady, moderate-intensity exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes in the aerobic zone (about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate). Develops cardio-respiratory and muscular endurance through the aerobic system. Suits distance running, swimming and cycling.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.1] Interval training: periods of work alternated with periods of rest or recovery, set by the work-to-rest ratio, the number of repetitions and the number of sets.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.2] Short, high-intensity intervals with longer recovery develop speed and anaerobic capacity; longer intervals at lower intensity develop aerobic fitness. Interval training suits intermittent sports such as hurling, soccer and basketball.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.3] Fartlek (speed play): continuous running with changes of pace and terrain, such as jogging, sprinting between lamp posts and running uphill. Trains the aerobic and anaerobic systems together and suits games players.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.0] Circuit training: a series of stations worked in turn with set work and rest times; can be designed for muscular endurance, strength, cardio-respiratory endurance or sport skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.1] Resistance (weight) training: working against a load such as free weights, machines or body weight, with exercises such as squats, lunges, presses, rows and curls.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.2] Heavy loads with few repetitions build strength; light loads with many repetitions build muscular endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.3] Plyometric training: jumping, bounding and hopping in which a lengthening contraction on landing is immediately followed by a forceful shortening contraction on take-off. Develops power for jumping and sprinting.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.4] Plyometrics needs a strength base, a forgiving surface and low volume, so it is not for beginners.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.5] Static stretching: a stretch held still at mild tension; best in the cool-down.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.6] Dynamic stretching: controlled movement through the full range, such as leg swings; used in the warm-up.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.7] PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) stretching: stretch the muscle, contract it against resistance from a partner, relax, then stretch further; gives the greatest gains in range.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.0] Hurling: interval training and fartlek suit the stop-start game better than continuous training, with resistance and plyometric work for power in striking and jumping.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.1] Distance swimming: continuous and long-interval training develop the aerobic system the event depends on.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.2] Gymnastics: flexibility training and body-weight strength work matter more than long aerobic sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.3] The best method depends on the energy systems and fitness components the activity demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.0] Training in the activity: conditioned games and sport-specific drills that are specific, motivating and train skill under fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.1] Training outside the activity: gym-based resistance training, plyometrics, swimming or cycling as cross-training, and yoga or Pilates for flexibility and core strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.2] Benefits of training outside the activity: overloads specific components precisely, builds a fitness base, adds variety, and reduces overuse of the sport's repeated movements.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.3] Limitation of training outside the activity: it is less specific, so it must be linked back to the activity's movements and demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.4] Example for a hurler: resistance training builds the strength and power for long striking and winning dropping balls; yoga improves hip and shoulder range for the swing.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.0] Step 1: use the fitness test results to identify the weakest components that matter most for the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.1] Step 2: set goals for those components.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.2] Step 3: select training methods suited to each goal.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.3] Step 4: apply FITT and the principles of training, planning sessions per week across several weeks with progression, a warm-up and cool-down in each session, and rest days.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.4] Step 5: re-test at the end to evaluate the programme and adjust it.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.5] Illustrative six-week plan for a soccer player whose tests show weak speed and agility: two speed and agility sessions a week (sprints and cone drills with full recovery), one resistance session for leg strength, one fartlek run; repetitions rise every two weeks; week four is lighter; re-test in week six.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.0] Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the session: if recovery is adequate, the body rebuilds slightly above its previous level (supercompensation).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.1] Cool-down: light activity and stretching help remove waste products and return the body to rest.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.2] Rehydration: replaces fluid and electrolytes lost in sweat.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.3] Refuelling soon after exercise: carbohydrate restores muscle glycogen and protein repairs muscle tissue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.4] Sleep: most tissue repair and growth takes place during sleep.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.5] Active recovery: a light session, such as an easy swim the day after a match, keeps blood flowing to the muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.6] Massage and foam rolling: reduce muscle tightness and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.7] Cold-water immersion: reduces inflammation and soreness after intense exercise.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.8] Compression garments: may reduce swelling and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.9] Planned rest days and lighter weeks prevent overtraining (its signs are in the notes on safe practice).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.0] Periodisation: dividing a training plan into planned phases or cycles, each with a specific aim, so that volume, intensity and recovery vary systematically to bring the performer to peak performance at target competitions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.1] Macrocycle: the long-term plan, often a year or a season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.2] Mesocycle: a block of several weeks, often 4 to 6, with one focus, such as building strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.3] Microcycle: usually one week of sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.4] Preparation phase: general fitness first, then specific fitness and skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.5] Competition phase: fitness is maintained and the performer peaks for key events; tapering before the main event reduces training volume while keeping intensity, so the performer arrives fresh.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.6] Transition phase: active rest and recovery after the season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.7] Why coaches use it: the performer peaks at the right time, develops qualities in a logical order, avoids overtraining and injury, and stays motivated through variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.8] Peaking for a single major event, such as an Olympic Games every four years, uses a long-term plan built from these cycles, with each year's macrocycle building towards the target.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s7: 7. Psychological preparation
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.0] Confidence: a performer's belief in their ability to perform successfully. Self-efficacy is confidence in a specific situation, such as taking a penalty in a final.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.1] Albert Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy: performance accomplishments (past success), vicarious experiences (seeing similar others succeed), verbal persuasion (encouragement from others) and emotional arousal (how the performer interprets their body's state).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.2] Effects of high confidence: more effort and persistence, and willingness to attempt difficult skills and to take responsibility at key moments.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.3] Effects of low confidence: avoiding the ball or difficult moves, hesitation, and more anxiety; over-confidence can cause complacency and poor preparation.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.4] Strategies: set achievable goals so success builds up; recall or watch video of past successes; positive self-talk; imagery of performing well; watch demonstrations by similar performers; specific praise from the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.0] Arousal: the level of physical and mental activation or readiness, on a scale from deep sleep to extreme excitement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.1] Inverted-U hypothesis (based on the Yerkes-Dodson law): performance improves as arousal rises to an optimal level, then declines if arousal keeps rising.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.2] Under-aroused: the performer is bored, slow to react and makes careless errors. Over-aroused: muscles tense, attention narrows, decisions and co-ordination suffer.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.3] The optimal level is lower for complex and fine skills and for beginners (such as a golf putt or a beginner's handspring), and higher for simple and gross skills and for experts (such as a rugby tackle or a weightlift).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.0] Anxiety: a negative emotional state of worry and apprehension, linked to high arousal.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.1] Cognitive anxiety: the mental side, such as worry, negative thoughts and self-doubt, for example dwelling on missing a free.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.2] Somatic anxiety: the physical side, such as a raised heart rate, sweating, muscle tension and butterflies in the stomach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.3] State anxiety: anxiety felt in a particular situation, such as before a penalty. Trait anxiety: a person's general tendency to be anxious across situations.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.4] Effects on performance: attention is narrowed or distracted, muscles tense, co-ordination and decision-making break down, and a performer may choke under pressure.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.5] Some anxiety can help if the performer interprets the feelings as readiness rather than threat.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.6] Strategies: breathing control; progressive muscular relaxation (tensing and relaxing muscle groups in turn); positive self-talk and reframing nerves as excitement; imagery; a pre-performance routine; simulating competition pressure in practice.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.0] Motivation: the drive to begin, direct and keep going with an activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.1] Intrinsic motivation comes from within, such as enjoyment, mastery and satisfaction; extrinsic motivation comes from outside, such as trophies, praise, selection and rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.2] Effects on practice: a motivated performer attends, works hard, keeps the quality of repetitions high when no one is watching and persists when progress is slow.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.3] Effects on performance: persistence when losing, effort to the final whistle, and willingness to train through difficult phases.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.4] Strategies: SMART goal setting; variety in training; recording progress so improvement is visible; specific praise and positive feedback; giving the performer choice and ownership; team targets; appropriate rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.5] Relying too much on extrinsic rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, so rewards should recognise effort and improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.0] Concentration: the ability to focus attention on the relevant cues, ignore distractions, and keep that focus over time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.1] Robert Nideffer described attentional focus as broad or narrow and internal or external.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.2] Broad-external: reading the whole game, such as a quarterback or midfielder scanning the field.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.3] Narrow-external: focusing on one cue, such as watching the ball onto the racket.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.4] Broad-internal: planning tactics or analysing performance in the mind.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.5] Narrow-internal: focusing on one's own body or thoughts, such as a pre-shot routine or a key cue word.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.6] Effects of lapses: errors, missed cues and poor decisions, especially late in a contest or during long waits between efforts, such as between attempts in a field event.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.7] Strategies: pre-performance routines; cue words; focusing on process goals; thought stopping (recognising a negative thought and replacing it with a positive one); simulation training with crowd noise and distractions; imagery; breaking a long event into short segments.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.0] Feedback: information a performer receives about their performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.1] Intrinsic feedback: comes from within, through the senses and the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic feedback), such as feeling a clean strike.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.2] Extrinsic feedback: comes from outside, such as a coach, video or the scoreboard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.3] Knowledge of results (KR): information about the outcome, such as whether the shot went in or the time achieved.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.4] Knowledge of performance (KP): information about the quality of the movement or technique, such as the elbow position at release.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.5] Positive feedback tells the performer what went well and motivates; negative feedback tells them what went wrong and helps correct it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.6] Concurrent feedback is given during the performance; terminal feedback is given after it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.7] Beginners need frequent, simple, positive, extrinsic feedback and KR; experienced performers rely more on intrinsic feedback and detailed KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.8] Good feedback is specific, timely and constructive; too much feedback overloads the learner and creates dependence on the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.0] Before: goal setting for the performance; imagery of key moments; a pre-performance routine; relaxation and breathing; positive self-talk; preparing equipment and a game plan.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.1] During: cue words and self-talk; a refocusing routine after errors, such as a deep breath and a reset word; breathing control in breaks in play; focusing on the process, one point at a time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.2] After: reflection and performance review; constructive feedback; relaxation and recovery; resetting goals; reframing a poor result as information for improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.3] Evaluating imagery: it builds confidence and rehearses skills without physical fatigue, but it needs practice and a clear, controllable image to work.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.4] Evaluating goal setting: it directs effort and raises motivation, but unrealistic or outcome-only goals can increase anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.5] Evaluating relaxation techniques: they lower somatic anxiety quickly, but too much relaxation before a power event can leave the performer under-aroused.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.0] Step 1: identify the psychological factors to target from self-assessment, such as an anxiety questionnaire or a reflective diary.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.1] Step 2: set goals for those factors.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.2] Step 3: choose one or two strategies for before, during and after performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.3] Step 4: give a rationale for each strategy, explaining how it affects the targeted factor.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.4] Step 5: say how progress will be monitored and reviewed, such as rating confidence after each match.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.5] Illustrative plan for a basketball free-throw shooter with high anxiety: before, breathing control and imagery of successful shots to lower somatic anxiety and build confidence; during, the same pre-shot routine and a cue word at every free throw to narrow focus; after, a review of video and a diary rating of anxiety to track progress.
