TARGET pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5: 5. The role of coach/choreographer
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.b] A coach (or a choreographer, who creates and teaches a dance or movement routine) is a non-playing role, but it is demanding. The performers depend on this person for knowledge, organisation and safety, so the job asks for much more than turning up to sessions.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.0] Technical knowledge of the activity: its skills, tactics and rules or, for a choreographer, the movement and composition of the routine. Without it the coach cannot teach correctly or spot errors.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.1] Knowledge of fitness, psychology and safety, so the coach can plan suitable training, support the performers' confidence and focus, and manage the risk of injury.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.2] Planning and organisation of sessions, equipment and the programme over a season, so that training builds towards the competitions that matter.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.3] Time commitment for sessions, travel, preparation and reviewing performance. Much of the work happens outside the training hour.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.4] Managing a group, including keeping everyone involved, resolving conflict and treating each performer fairly, so the team stays united.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.5] Making selection decisions (who plays or performs), which must be fair and explained, otherwise performers feel resentful and trust in the coach falls.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.6] Duty of care: responsibility for the safety and welfare of performers while they are under the coach's supervision.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.0.7] Safeguarding in Ireland: people working with children in sport must be Garda vetted (checked by the police) and complete child-safeguarding training under their governing body's procedures, which protects young performers from harm.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.1.b] Interpersonal skills are the skills used when dealing with other people. A coach can know the activity perfectly and still fail if performers cannot understand, trust or approach them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.1.0] Clear communication and active listening (giving full attention to what the performer says, not just waiting to speak), so instructions are understood and the performer's concerns are heard.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.1.1] Empathy: understanding how the performer feels about their performance, so the coach responds to a nervous or disappointed performer in a helpful way.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.1.2] Patience and approachability, so performers feel able to ask questions rather than staying silent when they are confused.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.1.3] Enthusiasm that motivates the group, because performers tend to copy the energy of the person leading them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.1.4] Fairness and consistency in selection, discipline and feedback, so performers know the same standards apply to everyone.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.1.5] Tact when delivering criticism, so that it is accepted rather than resented.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.1.6] Leadership and emotional control under pressure, so the coach stays calm and gives clear direction during a difficult match or rehearsal.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.2.b] Beyond interpersonal skills, successful coaches share certain personal qualities (what kind of person they are) and technical qualities (how well they coach the activity). Examiners ask you to discuss both.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.2.0] Personal quality, integrity: being honest with performers and selectors about performance and decisions, which builds trust.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.2.1] Personal quality, patience: teaching new skills at the performer's pace rather than the coach's, because rushing leads to poor technique.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.2.2] Personal quality, resilience: recovering from losses and keeping the group focused, so a bad result does not derail the season.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.2.3] Personal quality, commitment to the performers' development rather than personal recognition, so decisions are made for the performer's benefit.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.2.4] Technical quality, demonstration: showing skills accurately so that performers can copy them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.2.5] Technical quality, analysis: identifying the cause of an error, such as a poor body position behind a missed shot, so the coach corrects the cause rather than the symptom.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.2.6] Technical quality, practice design: designing practices that match the demands of the activity so that training transfers to competition.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.3.b] A goal gives training a purpose. Coaches break a big ambition into smaller steps, and they guide the performer to set goals rather than simply handing them over.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.3.0] Short-term goals cover days to weeks and act as steps towards the long-term goal, so the performer sees regular progress.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.3.1] Long-term goals cover a season or more and describe the bigger target the performer is working towards.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.3.2] Agree goals together, using the performer's performance analysis as the starting point, so the goals fit the real strengths and weaknesses and the performer is committed to them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.3.3] Write the goals down so that both coach and performer can refer to them and are not relying on memory.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.3.4] Review goals regularly and adjust them when circumstances change, for example after an injury or a big improvement.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.4.b] A vague goal such as "get better" is hard to act on or check. SMART is a checklist that turns a goal into one a performer can work towards and measure.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.4.0] SMART stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic and Time-bound.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.4.1] Specific: the goal states exactly what is to be achieved, so there is no doubt about the target.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.4.2] Measurable: the goal has a number or observable outcome that shows progress, so the performer knows when it has been met.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.4.3] Achievable: the goal is reachable with effort at the performer's current level, so it challenges without being impossible.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.4.4] Realistic: the goal fits the time, resources and ability available, such as the number of training sessions per week.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.4.5] Time-bound: the goal has a deadline or review date, which keeps the performer working towards it.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.4.6] SMARTER adds Evaluate and Review, so the goal is checked and revised rather than set and forgotten.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.5.b] Goals can focus on three different things. Coaches use all three, but they favour the ones the performer can actually control.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.5.0] Outcome goal: the result, such as winning a match.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.5.1] Performance goal: a personal standard, such as getting 80% of first serves in.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.5.2] Process goal: a technique focus, such as a high ball toss on the serve.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.5.3] Performance and process goals are more within the performer's control than outcome goals, which also depend on the opponent. A performer can hit every target and still lose to a stronger opponent.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.6.b] A programme for a specific performer is built in a logical order: start with where the performer is, then plan how to get to the goal. The coach applies methods and principles taught elsewhere, such as FITT (Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type), to one real person.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.6.0] 1. Analyse the performer through fitness tests and skill analysis, so the programme starts from their real strengths and weaknesses.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.6.1] 2. Set goals from the analysis, then choose fitness methods and skill practices that match them.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.6.2] 3. Apply FITT and the principles of training and effective practice. There is more on this in Designing a fitness plan and Skill acquisition.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.6.3] 4. Plan weekly sessions with progression (gradually increasing the demand), starting from where the performer is.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.6.4] 5. Monitor the performer and adjust the programme when results or fatigue show a need, because a plan that never changes will not fit a performer who is improving or tiring.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.7.b] How the coach communicates decides whether performers understand and act on the coaching. Good communication is clear, checked and positive.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.7.0] Short, clear instructions with a few key teaching points, not long explanations, because performers can only take in and remember a small amount at once.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.7.1] Demonstrations show the performer what the skill or sequence should look like, which is easier to copy than a verbal description.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.7.2] Questioning checks understanding, for example by asking the performer to explain the key point back.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.7.3] Positive body language, with eye contact and an open stance, shows the coach is interested and approachable.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.7.4] Feedback that is specific, timely and constructive, such as praise, then correction, then praise, so the performer hears what to fix without losing confidence.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.7.5] Language adjusted to the performer's age and level, so a beginner or a child is not confused by technical terms.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.8.b] A plan rarely survives contact with real performers unchanged. A good coach adapts what is happening as the need arises, rather than carrying on regardless.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.8.0] Simplify or make the task harder to suit the performer's current level, so it stays challenging but achievable.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.8.1] Change the drill, or add or remove opposition, to make a practice easier or more game-like.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.8.2] Adapt for injury, fatigue or weather, so that performers stay safe and the session is still useful.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.8.3] Change tactics or choreography at half-time, based on what the review showed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.8.4] A choreographer changes the sequence, formation or timing of the music when a routine does not fit the performers or the space.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.9.b] Performers train harder and for longer when they want to. Coaches use strategies to keep that motivation going through the weeks when progress is slow.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.9.0] Goal setting gives the performer direction and a reason to train.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.9.1] Variety in sessions keeps training interesting, so performers do not become bored.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.9.2] Recording progress lets the performer see improvement, which encourages them to continue.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.9.3] Praise and recognition for effort as well as results, so performers keep trying even when they lose.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.9.4] Ownership and choice: the performer chooses some drills or goals, which makes them more committed to the plan.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.9.5] Punishment, such as running laps as a penalty, is avoided as a training tool because it links training with negative feeling. A positive training environment works better.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.10.b] Reflection means looking back on a session or performance to learn from it. A coach does not just tell the performer what to think but guides them to reflect for themselves.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.10.0] Training diaries: the performer records what they did, how it felt and what to improve, which builds a record they can look back on.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.10.1] Video review together, pausing at key moments to discuss them, so the performer sees what actually happened.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.10.2] Questioning: what went well, and what would you change? This makes the performer do the thinking.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.10.3] Reflection is linked to the goals of the next session, so what is learned leads to action.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.11.b] Practice time is limited, so time wasted standing around or setting up is time not spent improving. Managing it well means performers get the most useful activity from every session.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.11.0] Session plan with a timing for each activity, so the coach knows what must happen and when.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.11.1] Equipment set up in advance, so no time is lost during the session.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.11.2] Minimal waiting: small groups or rotating stations keep every performer active.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.11.3] Clear transitions between activities, signalled in advance, so the group moves on quickly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.11.4] Keep to the plan while allowing flexibility when the session needs changing.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.11.5] If time runs short, protect the session's main objective first, so the most important work is not the part that gets dropped.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.12.b] The syllabus asks you to analyse your own coaching, using a coach or choreographer you admire as a point of reference. The coach you choose is your own choice.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.12.0] Choose criteria such as communication, planning, motivation, technical knowledge and results, so the comparison is fair and specific.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.12.1] Compare your coaching with the admired coach's on each criterion, honestly, including where you fall short.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.12.2] Identify one strength to keep and one area to develop.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s5.12.3] Set a specific action to improve the chosen area, such as reviewing video of your own sessions.

CANDIDATES pe-learning-skill-technique-s4: 4. Skill acquisition
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.b] A skill (a learned movement or action, such as a shot or a throw) is not mastered all at once. Fitts and Posner (1967) described three stages in order: cognitive, associative, then autonomous. What the performer does, how the movement looks and what the coach should do all change at each stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.0] Cognitive (beginner) stage: the performer forms a mental picture of the skill, usually from a demonstration, and works out what to do.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.1] Cognitive stage: movements are slow, jerky and inconsistent. Errors are frequent and large and the performer often cannot detect them, because full conscious attention (deliberate thinking) is on the movement.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.2] Cognitive stage coaching: simple, unopposed practice (no defender or opponent), clear demonstrations and frequent, specific feedback from the coach, because the performer cannot yet spot errors alone. Progress is rapid but fragile, meaning it is easily lost.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.3] Associative (intermediate) stage: the basic movement pattern is in place, so movements are smoother and more consistent. Errors are fewer and smaller, and the performer begins to detect some through the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic sense).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.4] Associative stage coaching: add pressure, speed and simple opposition, and make feedback more detailed. Most learners spend the longest time at this stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.5] Autonomous (expert) stage: the skill is performed automatically, consistently and fluently with little conscious attention, so attention is free for tactics, opponents and decisions.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.6] Autonomous stage coaching: the performer detects and corrects their own errors. Practice keeps the skill grooved (so well learned it repeats the same way) under competitive conditions, and feedback is fine-tuning.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.b] Exams ask you to describe one named skill at each stage. The basketball jump shot (shooting while jumping) shows how the same skill looks different as the learner improves.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.0] Cognitive: the feet are not set, the elbow flares out, the ball is pushed from the chest and the release point varies. The player can shoot only when stationary and unopposed.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.1] Associative: the set-up and release are mostly correct and the success rate rises, but the shot breaks down when a defender closes or the player is tired.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.2] Autonomous: the shot is released quickly and consistently off the dribble (while bouncing the ball) or the catch, under pressure, while the player reads the defence.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.3] Identifying a stage from evidence: an elite athlete winning national titles with a consistent technique is at the autonomous stage. Someone new to a sport who is inconsistent and needs instructions is at the cognitive stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.b] Learners improve faster when the coach gives them the right kind of help. Guidance can be visual, verbal, manual or mechanical, and it works best alongside feedback, mental rehearsal and motivation.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.0] Visual guidance and demonstration: accurate demonstrations, video and model performers (someone who performs the skill well) give the learner a clear picture, which matters most at the cognitive stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.1] Verbal guidance: short, clear key teaching points, because too much information overloads a beginner.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.2] Manual guidance: physically moving the learner through the movement, such as a coach guiding the arm through a swing.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.3] Mechanical guidance: equipment that supports the learner, such as a float in swimming or a harness on a trampoline.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.4] Manual and mechanical guidance build safety and confidence, but over-use creates dependence and the learner may not develop the feel of the movement.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.5] Feedback after attempts tells the learner what to change. There is more on this in Psychological preparation, which covers the types of feedback.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.6] Mental rehearsal (imagery): imagining performing the skill between physical attempts strengthens the mental picture.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.7] Motivation (the drive to keep trying), clear goals and practice matched to the learner's stage keep learning effective.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.b] A coach must decide whether to teach a skill as one piece or in sections. Whole practice, part practice, whole-part-whole and progressive part are the four ways of breaking a skill down.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.0] Whole practice: the skill is practised in its entirety. It suits skills that are fast and fluid or cannot easily be broken into parts, such as a golf swing or a javelin throw.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.1] Part practice: one sub-routine (a section of the skill) is practised in isolation, such as the ball toss in a tennis serve. It suits complex serial skills (skills made of several linked parts) and dangerous elements.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.2] Whole-part-whole: the learner tries the whole skill, a weak part is isolated and practised, then the whole skill is performed again.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.3] Progressive part (chaining): the first part is learned, then the second is added, and so on, such as the hop, step and jump of a triple jump.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.b] Practice can also be organised by how much rest it includes and by whether conditions stay the same. Massed or distributed is about rest, and fixed or variable is about conditions.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.0] Massed practice: continuous practice with little or no rest. It suits simple, closed (predictable, in a stable setting), discrete (a short skill with a clear start and end) skills and motivated, fit, experienced performers, such as repeated basketball free throws.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.1] Distributed practice: practice broken up by rest or other activities. It suits beginners and complex or tiring skills, and lowers fatigue and injury risk.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.2] Fixed practice: the skill repeated in unchanging conditions to groove the movement. It suits closed skills such as a free throw or a penalty kick.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.3] Variable practice: the skill practised in changing situations. It suits open skills (skills where the situation keeps changing) because it builds adaptability, such as passing under different levels of defensive pressure.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.4] The pairs are separate choices: whole or part (how the skill is broken down), massed or distributed (rest), fixed or variable (conditions).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.b] Good practice follows a set of principles, which are the rules a coach uses to plan and judge a session. Exam answers use these seven.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.0] Specificity: practice reproduces the demands of the competitive situation, including the same skills, speeds, conditions and fatigue, so learning transfers to performance.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.1] Progression: practice moves from simple to complex, unopposed to opposed, slow to game speed and closed to open, raising the demand only when the previous level is secure.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.2] Appropriate distribution: the balance of practice and rest (massed or distributed) suits the learner and the skill.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.3] Variety: different drills and conditions keep motivation high and build adaptability.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.4] Quality of repetitions: correct technique is repeated, not just volume, and practice stops before fatigue breaks down technique.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.5] Clear goals and regular feedback direct each session.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.6] Practice is matched to the individual's stage of learning and ability, is safe and begins with a warm-up.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.b] A practice schedule applies the methods and principles to one skill. It plans a single session and then the weeks of work, and every choice is justified.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.0] Session structure: warm-up; introduction and demonstration with key teaching points; progressive drills from simple to complex; a conditioned game (a game with rules changed to focus on the skill) or competitive scenario; cool-down; review and reflection.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.1] Over several weeks: set the number of sessions per week and their length, raise the difficulty week by week, choose practice methods and record progress, such as a success rate out of ten attempts.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.2] Justify each choice by the principles and the type of skill: fixed, massed whole practice suits a closed free throw; variable practice suits an open pass.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.3] Illustrative six-week plan for a basketball lay-up (a shot taken close to the basket after running): weeks 1 and 2 standing footwork then one-dribble lay-ups unopposed; weeks 3 and 4 lay-ups from the wing at game speed off either hand.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.4] Weeks 5 and 6 of the same illustrative lay-up plan: lay-ups against a passive then an active defender, with success recorded each session.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s6: 6. Designing a fitness plan
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.b] Different sports tax the body's energy systems in different ways, so there is no single best way to train. These three methods mainly target the aerobic system (which uses oxygen to release energy for long efforts) and the anaerobic system (which works without oxygen for short, intense efforts).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.0] Continuous training is steady, moderate-intensity exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes in the aerobic zone (about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate). It works the aerobic system, so it develops cardio-respiratory endurance (heart and lungs) and muscular endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.1] Continuous training suits activities that are themselves continuous, such as distance running, swimming and cycling, because the training matches what the event demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.2] Interval training alternates periods of work with periods of rest or recovery. Its demand is set by three things: the work-to-rest ratio (how long you work compared with how long you recover), the number of repetitions and the number of sets.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.3] Short, high-intensity intervals with longer recovery develop speed and anaerobic capacity, because the rest lets you repeat hard efforts. Longer intervals at lower intensity develop aerobic fitness instead.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.4] Interval training suits intermittent sports (bursts of effort with pauses) such as hurling, soccer and basketball, because it copies the pattern of work and rest in the game.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.5] Fartlek (speed play) is continuous running with changes of pace and terrain, for example jogging, sprinting between lamp posts and running uphill. It trains the aerobic and anaerobic systems together, so it suits games players.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.b] Muscles can be trained to work for longer, to produce more force, to produce force quickly, or to move through a bigger range. Each of these aims has its own method.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.0] Circuit training is a series of stations worked in turn with set work and rest times. It can be designed for muscular endurance, strength, cardio-respiratory endurance or sport skills, depending on the exercises chosen at the stations.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.1] Resistance (weight) training means working against a load such as free weights, machines or body weight, with exercises such as squats, lunges, presses, rows and curls.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.2] In resistance training, the number of repetitions decides the result: heavy loads with few repetitions build strength, while light loads with many repetitions build muscular endurance (the ability to keep a muscle working).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.3] Plyometric training uses jumping, bounding and hopping. A lengthening contraction on landing is followed immediately by a forceful shortening contraction on take-off, which develops power for jumping and sprinting.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.4] Plyometrics needs a strength base, a forgiving surface and low volume, so it is not for beginners.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.5] Static stretching holds a stretch still at mild tension. It is best in the cool-down.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.6] Dynamic stretching is controlled movement through the full range of a joint, such as leg swings, and it is used in the warm-up to prepare the muscles for the movements to come.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.7] PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) stretching has four steps: stretch the muscle, contract it against resistance from a partner, relax, then stretch further. It gives the greatest gains in range.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.b] The skill in comparing methods is matching each one to what the activity actually demands. The same method can be ideal for one sport and poor for another.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.0] Hurling is stop-start, so interval training and fartlek suit it better than continuous training. Resistance and plyometric work add power for striking and jumping.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.1] Distance swimming depends on the aerobic system, so continuous and long-interval training develop the fitness the event needs.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.2] Gymnastics relies on flexibility and body-weight strength rather than long efforts, so flexibility training and body-weight strength work matter more than long aerobic sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.3] The best method depends on the energy systems and fitness components the activity demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.b] Training can take place in the sport itself or in other forms of exercise that build the same fitness. Each approach has strengths, and good programmes use both.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.0] Training in the activity uses conditioned games and sport-specific drills. It is specific (it copies the real movements), motivating, and trains skill under fatigue, which is when skill usually breaks down in matches.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.1] Training outside the activity includes gym-based resistance training, plyometrics, swimming or cycling as cross-training (training in a different sport), and yoga or Pilates for flexibility and core strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.2] Training outside the activity can overload specific components precisely, build a fitness base, add variety, and reduce overuse of the sport's repeated movements.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.3] The limitation is that training outside the activity is less specific, so it must be linked back to the activity's movements and demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.4] For a hurler, resistance training builds the strength and power for long striking and winning dropping balls, while yoga improves hip and shoulder range for the swing.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.b] A good programme starts from evidence, not guesswork. Fitness test results show what to train, and the rest of the design follows in five steps.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.0] 1. Use the fitness test results to identify the weakest components that matter most for the activity, so effort goes where it will help performance most.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.1] 2. Set goals for those components.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.2] 3. Select training methods suited to each goal.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.3] 4. Apply FITT and the principles of training. Plan sessions per week across several weeks with progression, a warm-up and cool-down in each session, and rest days.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.4] 5. Re-test at the end to evaluate the programme and adjust it, because the new results show whether the goals were met.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.5] Illustrative six-week plan (example values only) for a soccer player whose tests show weak speed and agility: two speed and agility sessions a week (sprints and cone drills with full recovery), one resistance session for leg strength, and one fartlek run.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.6] In the same illustrative plan, repetitions rise every two weeks, week four is lighter, and the player is re-tested in week six.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.b] Training is the stimulus, but the body improves while it recovers afterwards. Recovery strategies help this process along and keep performers ready for the next session.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.0] Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the session. If recovery is adequate, the body rebuilds slightly above its previous level, which is called supercompensation.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.1] Cool-down means light activity and stretching, which help remove waste products and return the body to rest.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.2] Rehydration replaces fluid and electrolytes (salts) lost in sweat. Refuelling soon after exercise matters too: carbohydrate restores muscle glycogen (the muscles' stored fuel) and protein repairs muscle tissue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.3] Sleep supports recovery because most tissue repair and growth takes place during sleep.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.4] Active recovery is a light session, such as an easy swim the day after a match, which keeps blood flowing to the muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.5] Massage and foam rolling reduce muscle tightness and soreness. Cold-water immersion reduces inflammation and soreness after intense exercise, and compression garments may reduce swelling and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.6] Planned rest days and lighter weeks prevent overtraining, which is when training outpaces recovery. There is more on its signs in Safe practice.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.b] Periodisation plans training in stages, so the performer is at their best when it matters.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.0] Periodisation divides a training plan into planned phases or cycles, each with a specific aim, so that volume, intensity and recovery vary systematically to bring the performer to peak performance at target competitions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.1] Cycles run from longest to shortest. The macrocycle is the long-term plan, often a year or a season. The mesocycle is a block of several weeks, often 4 to 6, with one focus, such as building strength. The microcycle is usually one week of sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.2] The preparation phase builds general fitness first, then specific fitness and skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.3] In the competition phase, fitness is maintained and the performer peaks for key events. Tapering before the main event reduces training volume while keeping intensity, so the performer arrives fresh.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.4] The transition phase is active rest and recovery after the season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.5] Coaches use periodisation so the performer peaks at the right time, develops qualities in a logical order, avoids overtraining and injury, and stays motivated through variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.6] Peaking for a single major event, such as an Olympic Games every four years, uses a long-term plan built from these cycles, with each year's macrocycle building towards the target.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s7: 7. Psychological preparation
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.b] Skill alone does not decide a performance; what the performer believes about their skill matters too. Confidence shapes how hard they try, what they attempt and how they cope under pressure.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.0] Confidence is a performer's belief in their ability to perform successfully. Self-efficacy is confidence in one specific situation, such as taking a penalty in a final, so a player can be confident in training yet doubtful in that moment.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.1] Albert Bandura's theory gives four sources of self-efficacy. The first is performance accomplishments (past success).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.2] The second source is vicarious experiences (watching similar others succeed), which tells the performer 'someone like me can do this'. The third is verbal persuasion (encouragement from others).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.3] The fourth source is emotional arousal: how the performer interprets their body's state, for example reading a racing heart as readiness rather than as fear.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.4] High confidence leads to more effort and persistence, and to willingness to attempt difficult skills and to take responsibility at key moments, because the performer expects to succeed.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.5] Low confidence leads to avoiding the ball or difficult moves, hesitation, and more anxiety, because the performer expects to fail. Over-confidence can also harm, causing complacency (relaxing too much) and poor preparation.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.6] Strategies to build confidence: set achievable goals so success builds up; recall or watch video of past successes; use positive self-talk; use imagery (picturing yourself performing well); watch demonstrations by similar performers; and receive specific praise from the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.b] Before anyone can understand anxiety they need to know about arousal, the body and mind's level of activation. Performers need some arousal to perform well, but the right amount depends on the task and the person.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.0] Arousal is the level of physical and mental activation or readiness, on a scale from deep sleep to extreme excitement. It is neutral: it is simply how switched on the performer is, not a good or bad feeling.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.1] The inverted-U hypothesis (based on the Yerkes-Dodson law) says performance improves as arousal rises to an optimal level, then declines if arousal keeps rising. Plotted on a graph this makes an upside-down U.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.2] Under-aroused, the performer is bored, slow to react and makes careless errors, because they are not alert enough for the task.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.3] Over-aroused, muscles tense, attention narrows, and decisions and co-ordination suffer, because the performer is overloaded.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.4] The optimal level is lower for complex and fine skills and for beginners, such as a golf putt or a beginner's handspring, because these need precise control and a lot of thinking.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.5] The optimal level is higher for simple and gross skills and for experts, such as a rugby tackle or a weightlift, because these need force and speed and the expert has them well learned.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.b] Anxiety is what arousal can turn into when a performer feels threatened. It has a mental side and a physical side, and each affects performance in its own way.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.0] Anxiety is a negative emotional state of worry and apprehension (a fearful sense that something will go wrong), linked to high arousal.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.1] Cognitive anxiety is the mental side: worry, negative thoughts and self-doubt, for example dwelling on missing a free.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.2] Somatic anxiety is the physical side: a raised heart rate, sweating, muscle tension and butterflies in the stomach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.3] State anxiety is anxiety felt in a particular situation, such as before a penalty. Trait anxiety is a person's general tendency to be anxious across situations, so a high-trait performer feels it more often.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.4] Effects on performance: attention is narrowed or distracted, muscles tense, and co-ordination and decision-making break down. A performer may choke under pressure (fail at a skill they can normally do).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.5] Some anxiety can help if the performer interprets the feelings as readiness rather than threat, which is how nerves can become useful energy.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.6] Strategies: breathing control; progressive muscular relaxation (tensing and relaxing muscle groups in turn); positive self-talk and reframing nerves as excitement; imagery; a pre-performance routine; and simulating competition pressure in practice so it feels familiar.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.7] Match the strategy to the problem: relaxation and breathing lower somatic anxiety, while self-talk and reframing tackle the worry of cognitive anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.b] Motivation is what gets a performer to the training session and keeps them going when it is hard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.0] Motivation is the drive to begin, direct and keep going with an activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.1] Intrinsic motivation comes from within, such as enjoyment, mastery and satisfaction. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside, such as trophies, praise, selection and rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.2] Effects on practice: a motivated performer attends, works hard, keeps the quality of repetitions high when no one is watching and persists when progress is slow.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.3] Effects on performance: persistence when losing, effort to the final whistle, and willingness to train through difficult phases.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.4] Strategies: SMART goal setting; variety in training so it stays interesting; recording progress so improvement is visible; and specific praise and positive feedback.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.5] More strategies: give the performer choice and ownership, so they feel the training is theirs; set team targets; and use appropriate rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.6] Relying too much on extrinsic rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, because the performer starts to train for the prize rather than the enjoyment. Rewards should therefore recognise effort and improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.b] Concentration means putting your attention on the right thing at the right time. Different moments in a game need different kinds of focus, which Nideffer's model describes.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.0] Concentration is the ability to focus attention on the relevant cues (the information that matters), ignore distractions, and keep that focus over time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.1] Robert Nideffer described attentional focus as broad or narrow and internal or external. Broad takes in lots of information; narrow takes in one thing; internal is inside the performer; external is the outside world.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.2] Broad-external is reading the whole game, such as a quarterback or midfielder scanning the field. Narrow-external is focusing on one cue, such as watching the ball onto the racket.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.3] Broad-internal is planning tactics or analysing performance in the mind. Narrow-internal is focusing on one's own body or thoughts, such as a pre-shot routine or a key cue word.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.4] Effects of lapses: errors, missed cues and poor decisions, especially late in a contest or during long waits between efforts, such as between attempts in a field event.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.5] Strategies: pre-performance routines; cue words (short trigger words); focusing on process goals (what to do, not the result); and imagery.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.6] Further strategies: thought stopping (recognising a negative thought and replacing it with a positive one); simulation training with crowd noise and distractions, so distractions lose their effect; and breaking a long event into short segments.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.b] Feedback is how a performer finds out what happened, and it drives improvement. Feedback is classified in several ways, and exam questions often ask you to identify the type.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.0] Feedback is information a performer receives about their performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.1] Intrinsic feedback comes from within, through the senses and the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic feedback), such as feeling a clean strike. Extrinsic feedback comes from outside, such as a coach, video or the scoreboard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.2] Knowledge of results (KR) is information about the outcome, such as whether the shot went in or the time achieved.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.3] Knowledge of performance (KP) is information about the quality of the movement or technique, such as the elbow position at release. Do not swap KR and KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.4] Positive feedback tells the performer what went well and motivates. Negative feedback tells them what went wrong and helps correct it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.5] Concurrent feedback is given during the performance; terminal feedback is given after it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.6] Beginners need frequent, simple, positive, extrinsic feedback and KR, because they cannot yet judge their own movement. Experienced performers rely more on intrinsic feedback and detailed KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.7] Good feedback is specific, timely and constructive. Too much feedback overloads the learner and creates dependence on the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.b] Mental preparation is not only a pre-match job. Strategies are used at three stages, and each strategy has strengths and limits worth knowing.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.0] Before: goal setting for the performance; imagery of key moments; a pre-performance routine; relaxation and breathing; positive self-talk; and preparing equipment and a game plan, so nothing is left to worry about.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.1] During: cue words and self-talk; a refocusing routine after errors, such as a deep breath and a reset word; breathing control in breaks in play; and focusing on the process, one point at a time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.2] After: reflection and performance review; constructive feedback; relaxation and recovery; and resetting goals. A poor result is reframed (viewed differently) as information for improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.3] Evaluating imagery: it builds confidence and rehearses skills without physical fatigue, but it needs practice and a clear, controllable image to work.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.4] Evaluating goal setting: it directs effort and raises motivation, but unrealistic or outcome-only goals can increase anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.5] Evaluating relaxation techniques: they lower somatic anxiety quickly, but too much relaxation before a power event can leave the performer under-aroused.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.b] A personal action plan turns the psychological factors into a plan you could actually follow. It starts from the performer's own weaknesses and ends with a check on whether it worked.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.0] Step 1: identify the psychological factors to target from self-assessment, such as an anxiety questionnaire or a reflective diary.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.1] Step 2: set goals for those factors.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.2] Step 3: choose one or two strategies for before, during and after performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.3] Step 4: give a rationale for each strategy, explaining how it affects the targeted factor. A rationale is the reason the strategy was chosen.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.4] Step 5: say how progress will be monitored and reviewed, such as rating confidence after each match.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.5] Illustrative plan for a basketball free-throw shooter with high anxiety: before, breathing control and imagery of successful shots to lower somatic anxiety and build confidence.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.6] During, the same pre-shot routine and a cue word at every free throw to narrow focus. After, a review of video and a diary rating of anxiety to track progress.
