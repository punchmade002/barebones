CANDIDATES pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2: 2. Methods of analysis
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.0] Observation with a checklist: an observation schedule built from the key coaching points is ticked off during live observation, recording whether each point is performed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.1] What it shows: which key points are present or absent, and how often they are carried out correctly.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.2] Strengths of observation: cheap and immediate, needs little equipment, and can be used during training sessions.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.3] Limitations of observation: fast actions are easy to miss, and different observers may judge differently, so observers must use the same points.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.4] Video analysis: a performance is recorded and reviewed using slow motion, frame-by-frame playback and freeze-frame to examine a position closely.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.5] Side-by-side playback shows two performances together; overlay comparison superimposes one recording on another to compare position and timing.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.6] Drawing tools in analysis software or apps mark angles, lines and paths on a paused frame.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.7] Strengths of video: the performer sees their own technique, and the recording can be replayed and compared over time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.8] Limitations of video: a fixed camera position and setup time are needed, and the footage must be reviewed afterwards, so feedback is delayed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.0] Notational analysis: events during a performance are tallied, such as passes completed, turnovers, shots and scores, or unforced errors in racket sports.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.1] The tallies produce statistics, such as the percentage of passes completed, that show patterns across a whole performance or season.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.2] Notational analysis can be done by hand with a tally sheet or with software that logs events on a timeline.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.3] Strengths of notational analysis: objective and quick to compare across performances, and it shows patterns that memory misses.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.4] Limitations of notational analysis: it records what happened but not why, and a trained analyst must define each event consistently.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.5] Heart-rate monitors record heart rate during a performance, showing the intensity of effort and whether a session stayed at the intended level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.6] GPS units record the distance covered and speed, showing how much running a player did in a match and at what speeds.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.7] Limitations of fitness data: devices can be inaccurate or costly, and the figures must be interpreted against the demands of the activity.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.8] Fitness test batteries: a set of standard tests whose results are compared with earlier results to show change.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.0] Judges' criteria: the list of elements a judged activity scores, with a scoring sheet recording the mark given for each criterion.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.1] Strengths: a transparent, standard way to rate quality, and the same sheet can be used across performances.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.2] Limitations: judgement varies between judges, so judges must be trained on the same criteria before scoring.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.0] Questionnaires and self-report inventories: the performer answers standard statements about their own thoughts, feelings or behaviour, and the answers are scored.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.1] Competitive anxiety questionnaire: the SCAT (Sport Competition Anxiety Test), developed by Rainer Martens, measures competitive trait anxiety.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.2] A mood profile is a self-report inventory that rates the performer's mood states over a period.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.3] Strengths of self-report: captures thoughts and feelings that cannot be observed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.4] Limitations of self-report: answers depend on honesty and can be shaped by mood on the day.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.5] Interviews: a conversation with the performer, coach or team-mates that explores the reasons behind a performance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.6] Reflective diaries: after each session the performer records what went well, what did not and why.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.7] Limitations of diaries: they depend on honest recall and take time to keep up.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.4.0] Video analysis with a technique checklist: each phase of the skill is compared with the key coaching points on slow-motion replay.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.4.1] Success rate: the number of successful attempts out of a set number, such as shots taken in training, measures accuracy and consistency.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.5.0] Notational analysis of team actions: records where possession is lost, how many passes occur in each phase and the outcome of each set play.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.5.1] Match video with positional tagging: each clip is labelled with the position of the player involved and the area of play where the action happened.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.6.0] Video reviewed against a checklist of compositional requirements: use of space, levels, variety, musicality and transitions, each checked in turn.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.6.1] Scoring by judges or peers against agreed criteria, so the choreography is rated on the same points each time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.0] Performance-related test battery: a 30 m sprint, an Illinois agility test, a vertical jump and a ruler drop.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.1] Health-related test battery: a multistage fitness test, a sit-and-reach test and a press-up test.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.2] Each battery is repeated under the same conditions so that scores can be compared across sessions.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.0] A validated questionnaire completed before competition, such as the SCAT, shows the level of competitive anxiety the performer brings to the event.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.1] Interviews with the performer about their confidence, focus and expectations before competition.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.2] A reflective diary in which the performer rates confidence and concentration after each session, showing how they change over a programme.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.b] A model performer is a more skilled performer at the same skill, used as a visual target for comparison.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.0] Choose a more skilled performer at the same skill and record both performances from the same angle, so the images line up.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.1] Compare the two phase by phase against the key coaching points, noting each difference in body position, timing or sequence.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.2] Benefit one: the model gives a clear visual target showing what the skill should look like.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.3] Benefit two: the phase-by-phase comparison identifies specific technical faults and the phase in which each one occurs.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.4] The comparison motivates the performer by showing that the standard is achievable, and the gap between the two performances helps set realistic goals.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.5] Limitation: the model's body type, strength and experience differ from the performer's, so copying every feature of the model's style is not always appropriate.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.0] Four specific areas are identified from the analysis, for example one technical, one tactical, one fitness and one psychological area.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.1] Each area is backed by evidence from the analysis, such as a checklist result, a success rate or a questionnaire score.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.2] These areas become the performance goals in the next stage of the cycle, covered in the notes on planning for optimum performance.

CANDIDATES pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1: 1. Personal performance analysis
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.b] Performance analysis is the systematic observation, measurement and evaluation of a performance, or part of it, to identify strengths and weaknesses and inform improvement.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.0] Systematic: the same observation or measurement method is used each time, so results can be compared, rather than a general impression of how the performance went.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.1] It can cover a whole performance, such as a full match or routine, or one part of it, such as one phase of a jump or one set play.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.2] Strengths are identified so they can be kept, and weaknesses are identified so they can be targeted in training.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.3] Analysis is used at the start of a plan to find priorities and again at the end to measure progress.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.b] Analysis replaces guesswork with evidence before the performer decides what to train.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.0] Objective evidence: recorded data, such as counts of successful actions or measured times, replaces opinion about how well the performance went.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.1] Identifies priorities: it shows which weaknesses cost the most performance, so training time goes to them first.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.2] Sets realistic goals: current levels are measured, so goals can be set just beyond them rather than at an unknown level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.3] Tracks progress: repeated analysis shows whether training is working and when a target has been reached.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.4] Motivates: measurable improvement shows the performer that effort is producing results.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.5] Improves decision-making: evidence of which choices succeeded in a performance helps the performer choose better in future.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.6] Improves technique: analysis pinpoints which part of a movement needs changing.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.b] Performance analysis is the first of four linked stages. Each stage feeds the next, and the results of the final stage start the next cycle.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.0] Stage 1, analyse: observe and measure the performance and identify strengths and weaknesses.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.1] Stage 2, set goals: turn the weaknesses into realistic performance goals based on sound theory.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.2] Stage 3, train or practise: carry out a designed programme, monitor it with ongoing evidence, reflect on it and adapt it where needed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.3] Stage 4, concluding analysis: evaluate a performance using the same methods as the first analysis, so the two can be compared fairly.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.4] The results of the concluding analysis inform the next set of goals.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.b] Physical factors set what a performer's body can do; technical factors set how efficiently the skill is carried out.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.0] Fitness levels: the health-related and performance-related components of fitness set how long and how hard a performer can work; a sprinter depends on speed and power, while a long-distance swimmer depends on endurance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.1] Health: illness reduces training time and the energy available for practice, lowering performance until the performer recovers.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.2] Injury: an injury stops or limits training and can change movement patterns, so technique and fitness both suffer until rehabilitation is complete.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.3] Body type: height, limb length and body mass affect suitability for an activity; a tall basketball player has an advantage in rebounding.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.4] Age and maturation: physical maturity affects strength, speed and size, so the same training load can suit one young performer and overload another.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.5] Fatigue: tired muscles lose strength and co-ordination, so late in a match a player's shooting accuracy and decision-making decline.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.6] Level of skill: a performer who has learned the correct technique for a skill, such as a golf putt, repeats it more often under pressure.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.7] Technique: efficient technique uses less energy for the same output, so a sprinter with an efficient running action produces more speed from the same effort.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.8] Consistency: a performer who repeats the same technique under different conditions produces more reliable results.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.0] Game understanding: recognising patterns of play, such as an attacking team leaving space behind its defence, lets a performer anticipate and move before the play develops.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.1] Decision-making: choosing between passing, shooting and dribbling in the time available; poor choices waste possession even when skill is high.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.2] Knowledge of structures and strategies: knowing a team's set plays and the opponent's defensive system lets a player fit into the plan.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.3] Confidence: a confident performer attempts difficult actions, such as a long-range shot in football, that a doubtful performer avoids.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.4] Anxiety and arousal: too much arousal causes tension and poor co-ordination, while too little leaves the performer sluggish; the best level differs between performers and tasks.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.5] Motivation: a performer who wants to improve practises more and persists with difficult drills, so training gains are larger.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.6] Concentration: a performer who loses focus during a game misses cues, such as a team-mate's run or a change in the opponent's position.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.0] Diet and hydration: a balanced diet supplies energy for training and recovery, and dehydration reduces endurance and concentration.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.1] Sleep: sleep allows the body to recover from training, so a performer short of sleep recovers more slowly and performs below their level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.2] Alcohol and smoking: smoking reduces lung capacity and oxygen delivery, and alcohol impairs recovery and co-ordination.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.3] Balancing study, work and training: time pressure can cut training volume or sleep, so the performer must plan rest and practice around other commitments.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.4] Family support: encouragement, transport to training and financial help allow a young performer to attend sessions; without them participation may be limited.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.5] Peers and team-mates: a supportive group raises effort and enjoyment, while a poor team environment lowers motivation and confidence.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.6] Coach relationship: a coach who gives clear feedback and earns trust helps a performer improve, while a poor relationship makes instructions harder to act on.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.7] Role models: a performer may copy the technique or dedication of an admired athlete, which can raise standards or set unrealistic expectations.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.0] Weather: wind alters the flight of a ball or an arrow, and rain makes surfaces slippery.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.1] Playing surface: a wet or uneven pitch changes the bounce of a ball and the footing of a player.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.2] Quality of facilities and equipment: well-fitted equipment supports correct technique, while worn or badly fitted equipment causes errors or injury.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.3] Access to coaching: a performer far from a qualified coach has fewer structured sessions and less feedback.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.4] Home or away venue: a home venue brings familiar surfaces and crowd support, while an away venue brings travel fatigue and unfamiliar conditions.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.5] Altitude: thinner air at height gives less oxygen per breath, reducing endurance performance until the body acclimatises.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.6] Heat: high temperatures increase fatigue and sweat loss and raise the risk of heat illness, so performance drops in long events.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.0] Cost of equipment: specialist equipment, such as a bicycle or skis, may be unaffordable, limiting participation and access to the best kit.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.1] Membership fees: club and gym membership fees limit how often a performer can train and which facilities they can use.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.2] Travel costs: travel to training, competitions and coaching can make participation expensive, especially for performers far from clubs.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.3] Coaching fees: private coaching can improve technique quickly but may be out of reach for many performers.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.b] Method: pick the two factors that most affect performance in the named activity, preferably from different groups, and link each to specific moments of performance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.0] Start by listing what the activity demands; the factors that match those demands are the most important.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.1] Basketball free-throw shooter: technique and concentration are the two most important factors, because the shot is a repeated movement taken alone under pressure.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.2] Technique: a small fault in the release shows in every attempt, so technique largely determines accuracy.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.3] Concentration: the shooter must block out the crowd and the score and complete the same routine before each shot, so concentration decides whether the routine is completed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.4] Marathon runner: cardio-respiratory endurance and motivation are the two most important factors.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.5] Endurance decides whether pace holds after the halfway point; motivation decides whether the runner keeps pushing through the final stages when fatigue rises.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s6: 6. Designing a fitness plan
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.0] Continuous training: steady, moderate-intensity exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes in the aerobic zone (about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate). Develops cardio-respiratory and muscular endurance through the aerobic system. Suits distance running, swimming and cycling.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.1] Interval training: periods of work alternated with periods of rest or recovery, set by the work-to-rest ratio, the number of repetitions and the number of sets.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.2] Short, high-intensity intervals with longer recovery develop speed and anaerobic capacity; longer intervals at lower intensity develop aerobic fitness. Interval training suits intermittent sports such as hurling, soccer and basketball.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.3] Fartlek (speed play): continuous running with changes of pace and terrain, such as jogging, sprinting between lamp posts and running uphill. Trains the aerobic and anaerobic systems together and suits games players.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.0] Circuit training: a series of stations worked in turn with set work and rest times; can be designed for muscular endurance, strength, cardio-respiratory endurance or sport skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.1] Resistance (weight) training: working against a load such as free weights, machines or body weight, with exercises such as squats, lunges, presses, rows and curls.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.2] Heavy loads with few repetitions build strength; light loads with many repetitions build muscular endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.3] Plyometric training: jumping, bounding and hopping in which a lengthening contraction on landing is immediately followed by a forceful shortening contraction on take-off. Develops power for jumping and sprinting.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.4] Plyometrics needs a strength base, a forgiving surface and low volume, so it is not for beginners.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.5] Static stretching: a stretch held still at mild tension; best in the cool-down.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.6] Dynamic stretching: controlled movement through the full range, such as leg swings; used in the warm-up.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.7] PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) stretching: stretch the muscle, contract it against resistance from a partner, relax, then stretch further; gives the greatest gains in range.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.0] Hurling: interval training and fartlek suit the stop-start game better than continuous training, with resistance and plyometric work for power in striking and jumping.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.1] Distance swimming: continuous and long-interval training develop the aerobic system the event depends on.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.2] Gymnastics: flexibility training and body-weight strength work matter more than long aerobic sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.3] The best method depends on the energy systems and fitness components the activity demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.0] Training in the activity: conditioned games and sport-specific drills that are specific, motivating and train skill under fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.1] Training outside the activity: gym-based resistance training, plyometrics, swimming or cycling as cross-training, and yoga or Pilates for flexibility and core strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.2] Benefits of training outside the activity: overloads specific components precisely, builds a fitness base, adds variety, and reduces overuse of the sport's repeated movements.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.3] Limitation of training outside the activity: it is less specific, so it must be linked back to the activity's movements and demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.4] Example for a hurler: resistance training builds the strength and power for long striking and winning dropping balls; yoga improves hip and shoulder range for the swing.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.0] Step 1: use the fitness test results to identify the weakest components that matter most for the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.1] Step 2: set goals for those components.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.2] Step 3: select training methods suited to each goal.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.3] Step 4: apply FITT and the principles of training, planning sessions per week across several weeks with progression, a warm-up and cool-down in each session, and rest days.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.4] Step 5: re-test at the end to evaluate the programme and adjust it.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.5] Illustrative six-week plan for a soccer player whose tests show weak speed and agility: two speed and agility sessions a week (sprints and cone drills with full recovery), one resistance session for leg strength, one fartlek run; repetitions rise every two weeks; week four is lighter; re-test in week six.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.0] Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the session: if recovery is adequate, the body rebuilds slightly above its previous level (supercompensation).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.1] Cool-down: light activity and stretching help remove waste products and return the body to rest.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.2] Rehydration: replaces fluid and electrolytes lost in sweat.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.3] Refuelling soon after exercise: carbohydrate restores muscle glycogen and protein repairs muscle tissue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.4] Sleep: most tissue repair and growth takes place during sleep.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.5] Active recovery: a light session, such as an easy swim the day after a match, keeps blood flowing to the muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.6] Massage and foam rolling: reduce muscle tightness and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.7] Cold-water immersion: reduces inflammation and soreness after intense exercise.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.8] Compression garments: may reduce swelling and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.9] Planned rest days and lighter weeks prevent overtraining (its signs are in the notes on safe practice).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.0] Periodisation: dividing a training plan into planned phases or cycles, each with a specific aim, so that volume, intensity and recovery vary systematically to bring the performer to peak performance at target competitions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.1] Macrocycle: the long-term plan, often a year or a season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.2] Mesocycle: a block of several weeks, often 4 to 6, with one focus, such as building strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.3] Microcycle: usually one week of sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.4] Preparation phase: general fitness first, then specific fitness and skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.5] Competition phase: fitness is maintained and the performer peaks for key events; tapering before the main event reduces training volume while keeping intensity, so the performer arrives fresh.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.6] Transition phase: active rest and recovery after the season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.7] Why coaches use it: the performer peaks at the right time, develops qualities in a logical order, avoids overtraining and injury, and stays motivated through variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.8] Peaking for a single major event, such as an Olympic Games every four years, uses a long-term plan built from these cycles, with each year's macrocycle building towards the target.
