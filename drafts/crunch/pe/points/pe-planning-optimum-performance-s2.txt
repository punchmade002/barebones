TARGET pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2: 2. Methods of analysis
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.0] Observation with a checklist: an observation schedule built from the key coaching points is ticked off during live observation, recording whether each point is performed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.1] What it shows: which key points are present or absent, and how often they are carried out correctly.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.2] Strengths of observation: cheap and immediate, needs little equipment, and can be used during training sessions.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.3] Limitations of observation: fast actions are easy to miss, and different observers may judge differently, so observers must use the same points.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.4] Video analysis: a performance is recorded and reviewed using slow motion, frame-by-frame playback and freeze-frame to examine a position closely.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.5] Side-by-side playback shows two performances together; overlay comparison superimposes one recording on another to compare position and timing.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.6] Drawing tools in analysis software or apps mark angles, lines and paths on a paused frame.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.7] Strengths of video: the performer sees their own technique, and the recording can be replayed and compared over time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.8] Limitations of video: a fixed camera position and setup time are needed, and the footage must be reviewed afterwards, so feedback is delayed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.0] Notational analysis: events during a performance are tallied, such as passes completed, turnovers, shots and scores, or unforced errors in racket sports.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.1] The tallies produce statistics, such as the percentage of passes completed, that show patterns across a whole performance or season.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.2] Notational analysis can be done by hand with a tally sheet or with software that logs events on a timeline.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.3] Strengths of notational analysis: objective and quick to compare across performances, and it shows patterns that memory misses.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.4] Limitations of notational analysis: it records what happened but not why, and a trained analyst must define each event consistently.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.5] Heart-rate monitors record heart rate during a performance, showing the intensity of effort and whether a session stayed at the intended level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.6] GPS units record the distance covered and speed, showing how much running a player did in a match and at what speeds.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.7] Limitations of fitness data: devices can be inaccurate or costly, and the figures must be interpreted against the demands of the activity.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.8] Fitness test batteries: a set of standard tests whose results are compared with earlier results to show change.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.0] Judges' criteria: the list of elements a judged activity scores, with a scoring sheet recording the mark given for each criterion.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.1] Strengths: a transparent, standard way to rate quality, and the same sheet can be used across performances.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.2] Limitations: judgement varies between judges, so judges must be trained on the same criteria before scoring.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.0] Questionnaires and self-report inventories: the performer answers standard statements about their own thoughts, feelings or behaviour, and the answers are scored.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.1] Competitive anxiety questionnaire: the SCAT (Sport Competition Anxiety Test), developed by Rainer Martens, measures competitive trait anxiety.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.2] A mood profile is a self-report inventory that rates the performer's mood states over a period.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.3] Strengths of self-report: captures thoughts and feelings that cannot be observed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.4] Limitations of self-report: answers depend on honesty and can be shaped by mood on the day.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.5] Interviews: a conversation with the performer, coach or team-mates that explores the reasons behind a performance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.6] Reflective diaries: after each session the performer records what went well, what did not and why.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.7] Limitations of diaries: they depend on honest recall and take time to keep up.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.4.0] Video analysis with a technique checklist: each phase of the skill is compared with the key coaching points on slow-motion replay.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.4.1] Success rate: the number of successful attempts out of a set number, such as shots taken in training, measures accuracy and consistency.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.5.0] Notational analysis of team actions: records where possession is lost, how many passes occur in each phase and the outcome of each set play.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.5.1] Match video with positional tagging: each clip is labelled with the position of the player involved and the area of play where the action happened.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.6.0] Video reviewed against a checklist of compositional requirements: use of space, levels, variety, musicality and transitions, each checked in turn.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.6.1] Scoring by judges or peers against agreed criteria, so the choreography is rated on the same points each time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.0] Performance-related test battery: a 30 m sprint, an Illinois agility test, a vertical jump and a ruler drop.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.1] Health-related test battery: a multistage fitness test, a sit-and-reach test and a press-up test.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.2] Each battery is repeated under the same conditions so that scores can be compared across sessions.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.0] A validated questionnaire completed before competition, such as the SCAT, shows the level of competitive anxiety the performer brings to the event.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.1] Interviews with the performer about their confidence, focus and expectations before competition.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.2] A reflective diary in which the performer rates confidence and concentration after each session, showing how they change over a programme.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.b] A model performer is a more skilled performer at the same skill, used as a visual target for comparison.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.0] Choose a more skilled performer at the same skill and record both performances from the same angle, so the images line up.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.1] Compare the two phase by phase against the key coaching points, noting each difference in body position, timing or sequence.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.2] Benefit one: the model gives a clear visual target showing what the skill should look like.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.3] Benefit two: the phase-by-phase comparison identifies specific technical faults and the phase in which each one occurs.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.4] The comparison motivates the performer by showing that the standard is achievable, and the gap between the two performances helps set realistic goals.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.5] Limitation: the model's body type, strength and experience differ from the performer's, so copying every feature of the model's style is not always appropriate.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.0] Four specific areas are identified from the analysis, for example one technical, one tactical, one fitness and one psychological area.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.1] Each area is backed by evidence from the analysis, such as a checklist result, a success rate or a questionnaire score.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.2] These areas become the performance goals in the next stage of the cycle, covered in the notes on planning for optimum performance.

CANDIDATES pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1: 1. Personal performance analysis
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.b] Performance analysis is the systematic observation, measurement and evaluation of a performance, or part of it, to identify strengths and weaknesses and inform improvement.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.0] Systematic: the same observation or measurement method is used each time, so results can be compared, rather than a general impression of how the performance went.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.1] It can cover a whole performance, such as a full match or routine, or one part of it, such as one phase of a jump or one set play.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.2] Strengths are identified so they can be kept, and weaknesses are identified so they can be targeted in training.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.3] Analysis is used at the start of a plan to find priorities and again at the end to measure progress.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.b] Analysis replaces guesswork with evidence before the performer decides what to train.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.0] Objective evidence: recorded data, such as counts of successful actions or measured times, replaces opinion about how well the performance went.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.1] Identifies priorities: it shows which weaknesses cost the most performance, so training time goes to them first.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.2] Sets realistic goals: current levels are measured, so goals can be set just beyond them rather than at an unknown level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.3] Tracks progress: repeated analysis shows whether training is working and when a target has been reached.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.4] Motivates: measurable improvement shows the performer that effort is producing results.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.5] Improves decision-making: evidence of which choices succeeded in a performance helps the performer choose better in future.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.6] Improves technique: analysis pinpoints which part of a movement needs changing.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.b] Performance analysis is the first of four linked stages. Each stage feeds the next, and the results of the final stage start the next cycle.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.0] Stage 1, analyse: observe and measure the performance and identify strengths and weaknesses.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.1] Stage 2, set goals: turn the weaknesses into realistic performance goals based on sound theory.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.2] Stage 3, train or practise: carry out a designed programme, monitor it with ongoing evidence, reflect on it and adapt it where needed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.3] Stage 4, concluding analysis: evaluate a performance using the same methods as the first analysis, so the two can be compared fairly.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.4] The results of the concluding analysis inform the next set of goals.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.b] Physical factors set what a performer's body can do; technical factors set how efficiently the skill is carried out.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.0] Fitness levels: the health-related and performance-related components of fitness set how long and how hard a performer can work; a sprinter depends on speed and power, while a long-distance swimmer depends on endurance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.1] Health: illness reduces training time and the energy available for practice, lowering performance until the performer recovers.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.2] Injury: an injury stops or limits training and can change movement patterns, so technique and fitness both suffer until rehabilitation is complete.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.3] Body type: height, limb length and body mass affect suitability for an activity; a tall basketball player has an advantage in rebounding.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.4] Age and maturation: physical maturity affects strength, speed and size, so the same training load can suit one young performer and overload another.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.5] Fatigue: tired muscles lose strength and co-ordination, so late in a match a player's shooting accuracy and decision-making decline.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.6] Level of skill: a performer who has learned the correct technique for a skill, such as a golf putt, repeats it more often under pressure.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.7] Technique: efficient technique uses less energy for the same output, so a sprinter with an efficient running action produces more speed from the same effort.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.8] Consistency: a performer who repeats the same technique under different conditions produces more reliable results.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.0] Game understanding: recognising patterns of play, such as an attacking team leaving space behind its defence, lets a performer anticipate and move before the play develops.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.1] Decision-making: choosing between passing, shooting and dribbling in the time available; poor choices waste possession even when skill is high.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.2] Knowledge of structures and strategies: knowing a team's set plays and the opponent's defensive system lets a player fit into the plan.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.3] Confidence: a confident performer attempts difficult actions, such as a long-range shot in football, that a doubtful performer avoids.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.4] Anxiety and arousal: too much arousal causes tension and poor co-ordination, while too little leaves the performer sluggish; the best level differs between performers and tasks.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.5] Motivation: a performer who wants to improve practises more and persists with difficult drills, so training gains are larger.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.6] Concentration: a performer who loses focus during a game misses cues, such as a team-mate's run or a change in the opponent's position.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.0] Diet and hydration: a balanced diet supplies energy for training and recovery, and dehydration reduces endurance and concentration.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.1] Sleep: sleep allows the body to recover from training, so a performer short of sleep recovers more slowly and performs below their level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.2] Alcohol and smoking: smoking reduces lung capacity and oxygen delivery, and alcohol impairs recovery and co-ordination.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.3] Balancing study, work and training: time pressure can cut training volume or sleep, so the performer must plan rest and practice around other commitments.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.4] Family support: encouragement, transport to training and financial help allow a young performer to attend sessions; without them participation may be limited.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.5] Peers and team-mates: a supportive group raises effort and enjoyment, while a poor team environment lowers motivation and confidence.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.6] Coach relationship: a coach who gives clear feedback and earns trust helps a performer improve, while a poor relationship makes instructions harder to act on.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.7] Role models: a performer may copy the technique or dedication of an admired athlete, which can raise standards or set unrealistic expectations.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.0] Weather: wind alters the flight of a ball or an arrow, and rain makes surfaces slippery.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.1] Playing surface: a wet or uneven pitch changes the bounce of a ball and the footing of a player.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.2] Quality of facilities and equipment: well-fitted equipment supports correct technique, while worn or badly fitted equipment causes errors or injury.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.3] Access to coaching: a performer far from a qualified coach has fewer structured sessions and less feedback.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.4] Home or away venue: a home venue brings familiar surfaces and crowd support, while an away venue brings travel fatigue and unfamiliar conditions.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.5] Altitude: thinner air at height gives less oxygen per breath, reducing endurance performance until the body acclimatises.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.6] Heat: high temperatures increase fatigue and sweat loss and raise the risk of heat illness, so performance drops in long events.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.0] Cost of equipment: specialist equipment, such as a bicycle or skis, may be unaffordable, limiting participation and access to the best kit.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.1] Membership fees: club and gym membership fees limit how often a performer can train and which facilities they can use.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.2] Travel costs: travel to training, competitions and coaching can make participation expensive, especially for performers far from clubs.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.3] Coaching fees: private coaching can improve technique quickly but may be out of reach for many performers.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.b] Method: pick the two factors that most affect performance in the named activity, preferably from different groups, and link each to specific moments of performance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.0] Start by listing what the activity demands; the factors that match those demands are the most important.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.1] Basketball free-throw shooter: technique and concentration are the two most important factors, because the shot is a repeated movement taken alone under pressure.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.2] Technique: a small fault in the release shows in every attempt, so technique largely determines accuracy.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.3] Concentration: the shooter must block out the crowd and the score and complete the same routine before each shot, so concentration decides whether the routine is completed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.4] Marathon runner: cardio-respiratory endurance and motivation are the two most important factors.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.5] Endurance decides whether pace holds after the halfway point; motivation decides whether the runner keeps pushing through the final stages when fatigue rises.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s5: 5. Assessing fitness and the principles of training
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.0] Purposes: identify strengths and weaknesses against the demands of the activity, set realistic goals, design and monitor a training programme, motivate the performer, and compare results with norms (normative data).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.1] Valid: a test measures what it claims to measure.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.2] Reliable: a test gives consistent results when repeated under the same conditions.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.3] Standardised protocol: the same warm-up, surface, time of day, equipment and tester each time, so results can be compared.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.4] Tests must be safe and suited to the performer, and are repeated before and after a programme to show progress.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.5] Field tests are practical, cheap and can test many people at once; laboratory tests are more accurate but expensive.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance: the multistage fitness test (bleep test), running 20 m shuttles at a pace set by bleeps that get faster each level until the performer can no longer keep up; the level reached is recorded and VO2 max (maximum oxygen uptake) is estimated.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.1] Cardio-respiratory endurance: the Cooper 12-minute run, recording the distance covered in 12 minutes.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.2] Cardio-respiratory endurance for intermittent team sports: the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test, repeated shuttles with short recovery breaks.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.3] Muscular endurance: press-up and sit-up (curl-up) tests, the maximum number in one minute or to exhaustion; or the time a plank is held.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.4] Strength: the hand-grip dynamometer, or a one-repetition maximum (1RM), the heaviest weight lifted once with correct technique.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.5] Flexibility: the sit-and-reach test, measuring hamstring and lower-back flexibility as the distance reached past the toes.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.6] Body composition: skinfold callipers at set sites, bioelectrical impedance scales, or BMI (body mass index), which can wrongly class muscular people as overweight.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.0] Speed: a 30 m sprint from a standing start, timed.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.1] Agility: the Illinois agility run or the T-test, timing a set course with changes of direction.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.2] Power: the vertical (sergeant) jump, measuring jump height, or the standing broad jump, measuring distance.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.3] Balance: the stork stand, timing how long the performer balances on one foot.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.4] Co-ordination: the alternate-hand wall-toss test, counting catches in 30 seconds.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.5] Reaction time: the ruler drop test, in which a partner drops a ruler between the performer's thumb and finger and the distance it falls before being caught is recorded.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.0] A test battery is a set of tests chosen to measure the components a specific activity demands, based on a needs analysis.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.1] Step 1: analyse the activity's demands and select its key components.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.2] Step 2: choose one valid, reliable test for each component that is specific to the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.3] Step 3: order the tests so fatigue does not distort results: non-fatiguing tests such as flexibility, power and agility first, and the maximal endurance test last.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.4] Step 4: standardise the conditions, record the results and compare them with norms or previous results.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.5] Example battery for soccer: 30 m sprint (speed for breaking away), Illinois agility run (changes of direction), vertical jump (power for heading), then the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test (repeated high-intensity running).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.b] The specification names readiness, specificity, progression, overload and rest/recovery. Exam answers also accept reversibility, individual differences and variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.0] Specificity: training must match the demands of the activity, including its energy systems, muscles, movements and speeds.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.1] Progression: the training load is increased gradually as the body adapts.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.2] Overload: the body must work harder than normal to adapt, achieved by raising frequency, intensity or time.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.3] Rest and recovery: the body adapts during recovery, so too little rest leads to overtraining.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.4] Readiness: the performer must be physically and mentally ready for the training, considering age, maturity, fitness base and skill.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.5] Reversibility: fitness gains are lost if training stops.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.6] Individual differences: programmes should suit the individual's fitness, goals and response to training.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.7] Variety (avoiding tedium): varying training keeps motivation high and reduces overuse injuries.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.0] Specificity transfers training directly to competition, but too narrow a focus can neglect general fitness and cause overuse injuries.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.1] Overload drives improvement, but progressing too fast raises injury and overtraining risk.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.2] Rest and recovery allow adaptation, but too much rest causes reversibility.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.3] Readiness protects young athletes, who should not be given adult training loads before they are physically mature.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.b] Frequency (how often), Intensity (how hard), Time (how long) and Type (what kind of exercise). The values below are typical guidance, not fixed rules.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance: 3 to 5 times a week, 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate, at least 20 minutes, continuous running, cycling or swimming.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.1] Muscular endurance: 2 to 3 times a week, light loads, 15 or more repetitions in 2 to 3 sets, circuits or body-weight exercises.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.2] Strength: 2 to 3 times a week with rest days between, heavy loads, 3 to 6 repetitions in 3 to 5 sets, free weights or machines.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.3] Flexibility: 3 to 5 times a week, stretch to mild tension, hold each stretch about 30 seconds, static, dynamic or PNF stretching.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.4] Body composition: most days, moderate intensity, longer sessions of 30 minutes or more, aerobic exercise combined with a balanced diet.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.5] Speed: 2 to 3 times a week, maximal efforts, short sprints with full recovery, sprint intervals.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.6] Agility: 2 to 3 times a week, high intensity, short drills with recovery, ladder and cone drills.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.7] Power: 2 to 3 times a week, explosive maximal efforts, low repetitions with full recovery, plyometrics or jump training.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.8] Balance: 3 or more times a week, progressively harder positions, short holds, single-leg and unstable-surface exercises.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.9] Co-ordination: regular short practices, increasing complexity, skill drills combining body parts.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.10] Reaction time: regular short practices, maximal responses, reaction drills to sounds or visual signals.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.0] Maximum heart rate is estimated as 220 minus age.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.1] Aerobic training zone: about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate. Anaerobic training zone: about 80 to 90%.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.2] Aerobic threshold: the lower edge of the aerobic zone, below which training gives little cardio-respiratory benefit.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.3] Anaerobic (lactate) threshold: the intensity above which lactic acid builds up faster than it can be removed.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.4] Example: a 17-year-old's estimated maximum heart rate is 220 − 17 = 203 beats per minute, so the aerobic zone is about 122 to 162 beats per minute.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.5] Intensity is monitored by heart rate or by the rating of perceived exertion (RPE), the performer's own score of how hard they are working.

CANDIDATES pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3: 3. Aesthetic and artistic considerations
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.0] Aesthetic: concerned with the beauty and quality of movement as perceived by the observer, meaning how a movement looks and feels to watch.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.1] Artistic: concerned with creative expression, where the performer deliberately communicates an idea, mood, story or emotion through choreographed movement.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.2] A performance can be aesthetically pleasing without being artistic, because the performer need not be communicating an idea.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.3] Aesthetic and artistic criteria are judged qualitatively, by people, rather than by an objective outcome such as time, distance or score.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.0] Body line, shape and extension: the position the body makes, such as pointed toes, straight legs and full extension of the arms.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.1] Control, poise and balance: positions are held steadily and landings are controlled, such as a balanced finish at the end of a turn on a beam.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.2] Fluency and flow: smooth transitions between elements with no pauses or jerky changes.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.3] Timing, rhythm and musicality: movements matched to the accents of the music.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.0] Expression and communication: a mood or story is conveyed through facial expression and body language, and projected to the audience.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.1] Creativity and originality: choreography uses new or unexpected movements and combinations.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.2] Use of space and variety: levels (high, medium and low), pathways (straight, curved or zigzag) and direction (forwards, sideways or backwards) are varied across the routine.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.3] Synchronisation and relationships: in group or pair work, performers move with matched timing and shape, and their relationships convey the theme.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.4] Presentation: costume, props, entrance and finish, where the activity allows.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.0] Difficulty value: awarded for the elements attempted, separately from how well they are performed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.1] Execution score: starts from a maximum and has deductions for faults such as bent legs, loss of balance or a poor landing.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.2] The final mark combines the difficulty value and the execution score, so a hard routine performed poorly can score less than an easier one performed well.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.3] Diving is scored in the same way, on the difficulty of the dive and its execution.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.4] Dance is scored by judges against criteria such as expression and musicality.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.0] Games, athletics and swimming races are judged on objective outcomes, but they contain aesthetic qualities that are present and not scored.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.1] Example: a fluent passing move in a game is aesthetically pleasing, but the score depends on the result, such as a goal.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.2] Example: an elegant hurdling technique in athletics is aesthetically pleasing, but the race is decided by time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.3] Coaches and spectators notice these qualities informally; they are not recorded on a scoring sheet.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.0] Activities judged mainly on aesthetic or artistic criteria include gymnastics, dance, diving, synchronised swimming and figure skating.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.1] A comparison covers what each activity is judged on, how its scoring works, which criteria the two share and where they differ.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.2] Shared criteria: body line, control and timing appear in both dance and gymnastics.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.3] Difference: dance emphasises expression and musicality, while gymnastics adds a difficulty value to the execution score.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.4] Surfing is a borderline case: it is judged on criteria including speed, power and flow, so its criteria mix performance qualities with aesthetic ones.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.0] Video the performance so it can be replayed and scored more than once against the same criteria.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.1] Score each criterion with a checklist or rating scale.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.2] Gather self, peer and teacher evaluations, and compare the scores with a model performance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.3] Identify strengths, such as clean body lines, and areas to improve, such as transitions that lose their flow.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.4] For each area to improve, state how it will be improved, such as a drill that rehearses the transition.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s7: 7. Psychological preparation
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.0] Confidence: a performer's belief in their ability to perform successfully. Self-efficacy is confidence in a specific situation, such as taking a penalty in a final.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.1] Albert Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy: performance accomplishments (past success), vicarious experiences (seeing similar others succeed), verbal persuasion (encouragement from others) and emotional arousal (how the performer interprets their body's state).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.2] Effects of high confidence: more effort and persistence, and willingness to attempt difficult skills and to take responsibility at key moments.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.3] Effects of low confidence: avoiding the ball or difficult moves, hesitation, and more anxiety; over-confidence can cause complacency and poor preparation.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.4] Strategies: set achievable goals so success builds up; recall or watch video of past successes; positive self-talk; imagery of performing well; watch demonstrations by similar performers; specific praise from the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.0] Arousal: the level of physical and mental activation or readiness, on a scale from deep sleep to extreme excitement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.1] Inverted-U hypothesis (based on the Yerkes-Dodson law): performance improves as arousal rises to an optimal level, then declines if arousal keeps rising.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.2] Under-aroused: the performer is bored, slow to react and makes careless errors. Over-aroused: muscles tense, attention narrows, decisions and co-ordination suffer.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.3] The optimal level is lower for complex and fine skills and for beginners (such as a golf putt or a beginner's handspring), and higher for simple and gross skills and for experts (such as a rugby tackle or a weightlift).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.0] Anxiety: a negative emotional state of worry and apprehension, linked to high arousal.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.1] Cognitive anxiety: the mental side, such as worry, negative thoughts and self-doubt, for example dwelling on missing a free.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.2] Somatic anxiety: the physical side, such as a raised heart rate, sweating, muscle tension and butterflies in the stomach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.3] State anxiety: anxiety felt in a particular situation, such as before a penalty. Trait anxiety: a person's general tendency to be anxious across situations.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.4] Effects on performance: attention is narrowed or distracted, muscles tense, co-ordination and decision-making break down, and a performer may choke under pressure.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.5] Some anxiety can help if the performer interprets the feelings as readiness rather than threat.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.6] Strategies: breathing control; progressive muscular relaxation (tensing and relaxing muscle groups in turn); positive self-talk and reframing nerves as excitement; imagery; a pre-performance routine; simulating competition pressure in practice.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.0] Motivation: the drive to begin, direct and keep going with an activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.1] Intrinsic motivation comes from within, such as enjoyment, mastery and satisfaction; extrinsic motivation comes from outside, such as trophies, praise, selection and rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.2] Effects on practice: a motivated performer attends, works hard, keeps the quality of repetitions high when no one is watching and persists when progress is slow.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.3] Effects on performance: persistence when losing, effort to the final whistle, and willingness to train through difficult phases.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.4] Strategies: SMART goal setting; variety in training; recording progress so improvement is visible; specific praise and positive feedback; giving the performer choice and ownership; team targets; appropriate rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.5] Relying too much on extrinsic rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, so rewards should recognise effort and improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.0] Concentration: the ability to focus attention on the relevant cues, ignore distractions, and keep that focus over time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.1] Robert Nideffer described attentional focus as broad or narrow and internal or external.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.2] Broad-external: reading the whole game, such as a quarterback or midfielder scanning the field.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.3] Narrow-external: focusing on one cue, such as watching the ball onto the racket.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.4] Broad-internal: planning tactics or analysing performance in the mind.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.5] Narrow-internal: focusing on one's own body or thoughts, such as a pre-shot routine or a key cue word.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.6] Effects of lapses: errors, missed cues and poor decisions, especially late in a contest or during long waits between efforts, such as between attempts in a field event.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.7] Strategies: pre-performance routines; cue words; focusing on process goals; thought stopping (recognising a negative thought and replacing it with a positive one); simulation training with crowd noise and distractions; imagery; breaking a long event into short segments.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.0] Feedback: information a performer receives about their performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.1] Intrinsic feedback: comes from within, through the senses and the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic feedback), such as feeling a clean strike.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.2] Extrinsic feedback: comes from outside, such as a coach, video or the scoreboard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.3] Knowledge of results (KR): information about the outcome, such as whether the shot went in or the time achieved.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.4] Knowledge of performance (KP): information about the quality of the movement or technique, such as the elbow position at release.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.5] Positive feedback tells the performer what went well and motivates; negative feedback tells them what went wrong and helps correct it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.6] Concurrent feedback is given during the performance; terminal feedback is given after it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.7] Beginners need frequent, simple, positive, extrinsic feedback and KR; experienced performers rely more on intrinsic feedback and detailed KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.8] Good feedback is specific, timely and constructive; too much feedback overloads the learner and creates dependence on the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.0] Before: goal setting for the performance; imagery of key moments; a pre-performance routine; relaxation and breathing; positive self-talk; preparing equipment and a game plan.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.1] During: cue words and self-talk; a refocusing routine after errors, such as a deep breath and a reset word; breathing control in breaks in play; focusing on the process, one point at a time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.2] After: reflection and performance review; constructive feedback; relaxation and recovery; resetting goals; reframing a poor result as information for improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.3] Evaluating imagery: it builds confidence and rehearses skills without physical fatigue, but it needs practice and a clear, controllable image to work.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.4] Evaluating goal setting: it directs effort and raises motivation, but unrealistic or outcome-only goals can increase anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.5] Evaluating relaxation techniques: they lower somatic anxiety quickly, but too much relaxation before a power event can leave the performer under-aroused.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.0] Step 1: identify the psychological factors to target from self-assessment, such as an anxiety questionnaire or a reflective diary.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.1] Step 2: set goals for those factors.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.2] Step 3: choose one or two strategies for before, during and after performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.3] Step 4: give a rationale for each strategy, explaining how it affects the targeted factor.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.4] Step 5: say how progress will be monitored and reviewed, such as rating confidence after each match.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.5] Illustrative plan for a basketball free-throw shooter with high anxiety: before, breathing control and imagery of successful shots to lower somatic anxiety and build confidence; during, the same pre-shot routine and a cue word at every free throw to narrow focus; after, a review of video and a diary rating of anxiety to track progress.
