TARGET pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2: 2. Methods of analysis
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.b] Analysis starts with looking carefully at a performance. Observation does this live, while video analysis records the performance so it can be studied again and again.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.0] Observation with a checklist: an observation schedule (a list of the key coaching points for the skill) is ticked off during live observation, recording whether each point is performed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.1] What a checklist shows: which key points are present or absent, and how often they are carried out correctly.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.2] Strengths of observation: it is cheap and immediate, needs little equipment, and can be used during training sessions, so feedback can be given straight away.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.3] Limitations of observation: fast actions are easy to miss, and different observers may judge differently, so observers must use the same points.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.4] Video analysis: a performance is recorded and reviewed using slow motion, frame-by-frame playback and freeze-frame (pausing on one image) to examine a position closely.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.5] Side-by-side playback shows two performances together. Overlay comparison superimposes (places) one recording on top of another so that differences in position and timing show up clearly.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.6] Drawing tools in analysis software or apps mark angles, lines and paths on a paused frame, which turns 'it looks wrong' into a measured difference.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.7] Strengths of video: the performer sees their own technique, and the recording can be replayed and compared over time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.0.8] Limitations of video: a fixed camera position and setup time are needed, and the footage must be reviewed afterwards, so feedback is delayed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.b] Some methods turn a performance into numbers. Notational analysis counts what happens, and fitness data measures how hard the body is working.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.0] Notational analysis: events during a performance are tallied (counted as they happen), such as passes completed, turnovers, shots and scores, or unforced errors in racket sports.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.1] The tallies produce statistics, such as the percentage of passes completed, that show patterns across a whole performance or season.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.2] Notational analysis can be done by hand with a tally sheet or with software that logs events on a timeline.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.3] Strengths of notational analysis: it is objective and quick to compare across performances, and it shows patterns that memory misses.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.4] Limitations of notational analysis: it records what happened but not why, and a trained analyst must define each event consistently, otherwise two analysts would count differently.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.5] Heart-rate monitors record heart rate during a performance, showing the intensity of effort and whether a session stayed at the intended level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.6] GPS units record the distance covered and speed, showing how much running a player did in a match and at what speeds.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.7] Limitations of fitness data: devices can be inaccurate or costly, and the figures must be interpreted against the demands of the activity, because the same heart rate means different things in different sports.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.1.8] Fitness test batteries: a set of standard tests whose results are compared with earlier results to show change.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.b] In activities that are judged rather than timed or counted, quality is rated against agreed standards. Judges' criteria and scoring sheets make that rating consistent.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.0] Judges' criteria: the list of elements a judged activity scores, with a scoring sheet recording the mark given for each criterion.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.1] Strengths: a transparent, standard way to rate quality, and the same sheet can be used across performances, so scores can be compared.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.2.2] Limitations: judgement varies between judges, so judges must be trained on the same criteria before scoring.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.b] Thoughts and feelings cannot be seen or timed, so the performer has to report them. Self-report tools ask the performer, or the people around them, directly.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.0] Questionnaires and self-report inventories: the performer answers standard statements about their own thoughts, feelings or behaviour, and the answers are scored.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.1] Competitive anxiety questionnaire: the SCAT (Sport Competition Anxiety Test), developed by Rainer Martens, measures competitive trait anxiety (how anxious a performer usually is in competition).
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.2] A mood profile is a self-report inventory that rates the performer's mood states over a period.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.3] Strengths of self-report: it captures thoughts and feelings that cannot be observed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.4] Limitations of self-report: answers depend on honesty and can be shaped by mood on the day, so the results may not be a true picture.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.5] Interviews: a conversation with the performer, coach or team-mates that explores the reasons behind a performance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.6] Reflective diaries: after each session the performer records what went well, what did not and why.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.3.7] Limitations of diaries: they depend on honest recall and take time to keep up.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.4.b] Skill and technique are about how well a movement is performed. The right methods compare the movement with the ideal and measure how often it works.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.4.0] Video analysis with a technique checklist: each phase of the skill is compared with the key coaching points on slow-motion replay, so faults can be found phase by phase.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.4.1] Success rate: the number of successful attempts out of a set number, such as shots taken in training, measures accuracy and consistency.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.4.2] Comparison with a model performer (a more skilled performer at the same skill) is a third method for skill and technique, because it shows exactly where your technique differs from the ideal.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.5.b] Structures and strategies are team plans, such as how players are positioned and how set plays are used. They are judged by looking at team actions across the whole game.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.5.0] Notational analysis of team actions: records where possession is lost, how many passes occur in each phase and the outcome of each set play, which shows which parts of the plan work.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.5.1] Match video with positional tagging: each clip is labelled with the position of the player involved and the area of play where the action happened, so patterns by position or area can be found.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.6.b] Choreography is the planned design of a dance or routine. Its quality depends on set requirements, so the methods check the work against those requirements.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.6.0] Video reviewed against a checklist of compositional requirements (the things a good composition must include): use of space, levels, variety, musicality and transitions, each checked in turn.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.6.1] Scoring by judges or peers against agreed criteria, so the choreography is rated on the same points each time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.b] Fitness is analysed by testing. A battery is a set of tests, and each battery is chosen to match the type of fitness being analysed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.0] Performance-related test battery: a 30 m sprint, an Illinois agility test, a vertical jump and a ruler drop.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.1] Health-related test battery: a multistage fitness test, a sit-and-reach test and a press-up test.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.7.2] Each battery is repeated under the same conditions so that scores can be compared across sessions, because a change in conditions would make the comparison unfair.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.b] Psychological preparedness means how ready the performer's mind is for competition. Because it cannot be seen, it is measured through what the performer reports.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.0] A validated questionnaire (one shown to measure what it claims to) completed before competition, such as the SCAT, shows the level of competitive anxiety the performer brings to the event.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.1] Interviews with the performer about their confidence, focus and expectations before competition.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.8.2] A reflective diary in which the performer rates confidence and concentration after each session, showing how they change over a programme.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.b] A model performer is a more skilled performer at the same skill, used as a visual target for comparison.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.0] Choose a more skilled performer at the same skill and record both performances from the same angle, so the images line up.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.1] Compare the two phase by phase against the key coaching points, noting each difference in body position, timing or sequence.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.2] Benefit one: the model gives a clear visual target showing what the skill should look like.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.3] Benefit two: the phase-by-phase comparison identifies specific technical faults and the phase in which each one occurs.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.4] The comparison motivates the performer by showing that the standard is achievable, and the gap between the two performances helps set realistic goals.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.9.5] Limitation: the model's body type, strength and experience differ from the performer's, so copying every feature of the model's style is not always appropriate.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.b] Analysis is only useful if it leads to action. The last step is to pick the areas of the performance that most need work.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.0] Four specific areas are identified from the analysis, for example one technical, one tactical, one fitness and one psychological area.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.1] Each area is backed by evidence from the analysis, such as a checklist result, a success rate or a questionnaire score, so the choice is not just opinion.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s2.10.2] These areas become the four performance goals in the next stage of the cycle. There is more on this in Planning for optimum performance.

CANDIDATES pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1: 1. Personal performance analysis
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.b] Before a performer can improve, they need to know exactly where they stand. Performance analysis is the systematic observation, measurement and evaluation of a performance, or part of it, to identify strengths and weaknesses and inform improvement.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.0] Systematic means the same observation or measurement method is used each time, so results can be compared fairly, rather than relying on a general impression of how the performance went.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.1] It can cover a whole performance, such as a full match or routine, or one part of it, such as one phase of a jump or one set play (a rehearsed team move).
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.2] Strengths are identified so they can be kept, and weaknesses are identified so they can be targeted in training, which stops time being wasted on things already done well.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.0.3] Analysis is used at the start of a plan to find priorities and again at the end to measure progress, so the same method gives a fair before-and-after comparison.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.b] Analysis replaces guesswork with evidence before the performer decides what to train. This is why athletes carry out a performance analysis, and each benefit below is a reason you can use.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.0] Objective evidence: recorded data, such as counts of successful actions or measured times, replaces opinion about how well the performance went, so the performer is not misled by feelings.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.1] Identifies priorities: it shows which weaknesses cost the most performance, so training time goes to them first.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.2] Sets realistic goals: current levels are measured, so goals can be set just beyond them rather than at an unknown level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.3] Tracks progress: repeated analysis shows whether training is working and when a target has been reached.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.4] Motivates: measurable improvement shows the performer that effort is producing results, which encourages them to keep training.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.5] Improves decision-making: evidence of which choices succeeded in a performance helps the performer choose better in future.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.1.6] Improves technique: analysis pinpoints which part of a movement needs changing, so practice is aimed at the actual fault.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.b] Performance analysis is the first of four linked stages. Each stage feeds the next, and the results of the final stage start the next cycle. The next three subsections cover stages 2 to 4 in detail.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.0] Stage 1, analyse: observe and measure the performance and identify strengths and weaknesses.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.1] Stage 2, set goals: turn the weaknesses into realistic performance goals based on sound theory (principles known to work in training).
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.2] Stage 3, train or practise: carry out a designed programme, monitor it with ongoing evidence, reflect on it and adapt it where needed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.3] Stage 4, concluding analysis: evaluate a performance using the same methods as the first analysis, so the two can be compared fairly.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.2.4] The results of the concluding analysis inform the next set of goals, which is why it is a cycle and not a straight line.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.b] Physical factors set what a performer's body can do. They decide how hard and how long someone can train and perform.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.0] Fitness levels: the health-related and performance-related components of fitness set how long and how hard a performer can work; a sprinter depends on speed and power, while a long-distance swimmer depends on endurance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.1] Health: illness reduces training time and the energy available for practice, lowering performance until the performer recovers.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.2] Injury: an injury stops or limits training and can change movement patterns, so technique and fitness both suffer until rehabilitation (the recovery programme) is complete.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.3] Body type: height, limb length and body mass affect suitability for an activity; a tall basketball player has an advantage in rebounding.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.4] Age and maturation (physical development): physical maturity affects strength, speed and size, so the same training load can suit one young performer and overload another.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.3.5] Fatigue: tired muscles lose strength and co-ordination, so late in a match a player's shooting accuracy and decision-making decline.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.b] Technical factors are about how well a skill is carried out. Two performers with the same fitness can perform very differently if one has better technique.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.0] Level of skill: a performer who has learned the correct technique for a skill, such as a golf putt, repeats it more often under pressure.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.1] Technique: efficient technique uses less energy for the same output, so a sprinter with an efficient running action produces more speed from the same effort.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.4.2] Consistency: a performer who repeats the same technique under different conditions produces more reliable results, because the movement does not break down when circumstances change.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.b] Tactical factors are about thinking and reading the game; psychological factors are about the performer's mind. Both decide whether physical ability and skill are used well on the day.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.0] Game understanding: recognising patterns of play, such as an attacking team leaving space behind its defence, lets a performer anticipate and move before the play develops.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.1] Decision-making: choosing between passing, shooting and dribbling in the time available; poor choices waste possession even when skill is high.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.2] Knowledge of structures and strategies: knowing a team's set plays and the opponent's defensive system lets a player fit into the plan.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.3] Confidence: a confident performer attempts difficult actions, such as a long-range shot in football, that a doubtful performer avoids.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.4] Anxiety and arousal (how alert and excited the body and mind are): too much arousal causes tension and poor co-ordination, while too little leaves the performer sluggish; the best level differs between performers and tasks.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.5] Motivation: a performer who wants to improve practises more and persists with difficult drills, so training gains are larger.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.5.6] Concentration: a performer who loses focus during a game misses cues, such as a team-mate's run or a change in the opponent's position.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.b] What a performer does away from training, and the people around them, can help or hold back performance as much as the training itself.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.0] Diet and hydration: a balanced diet supplies energy for training and recovery, and dehydration reduces endurance and concentration.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.1] Sleep: sleep allows the body to recover from training, so a performer short of sleep recovers more slowly and performs below their level.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.2] Alcohol and smoking: smoking reduces lung capacity and oxygen delivery, and alcohol impairs recovery and co-ordination.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.3] Balancing study, work and training: time pressure can cut training volume or sleep, so the performer must plan rest and practice around other commitments.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.4] Family support: encouragement, transport to training and financial help allow a young performer to attend sessions; without them participation may be limited.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.5] Peers and team-mates: a supportive group raises effort and enjoyment, while a poor team environment lowers motivation and confidence.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.6] Coach relationship: a coach who gives clear feedback and earns trust helps a performer improve, while a poor relationship makes instructions harder to act on.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.6.7] Role models: a performer may copy the technique or dedication of an admired athlete, which can raise standards or set unrealistic expectations.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.b] The conditions and facilities around a performance can change what the performer is able to do, even when their fitness and skill stay the same.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.0] Weather: wind alters the flight of a ball or an arrow, and rain makes surfaces slippery.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.1] Playing surface: a wet or uneven pitch changes the bounce of a ball and the footing of a player.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.2] Quality of facilities and equipment: well-fitted equipment supports correct technique, while worn or badly fitted equipment causes errors or injury.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.3] Access to coaching: a performer far from a qualified coach has fewer structured sessions and less feedback.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.4] Home or away venue: a home venue brings familiar surfaces and crowd support, while an away venue brings travel fatigue and unfamiliar conditions.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.5] Altitude: thinner air at height gives less oxygen per breath, reducing endurance performance until the body acclimatises (adjusts to the conditions).
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.7.6] Heat: high temperatures increase fatigue and sweat loss and raise the risk of heat illness, so performance drops in long events.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.b] Sport costs money, and money decides which equipment, clubs and coaching a performer can use. This is why two equally talented performers may not get equal opportunities.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.0] Cost of equipment: specialist equipment, such as a bicycle or skis, may be unaffordable, limiting participation and access to the best kit.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.1] Membership fees: club and gym membership fees limit how often a performer can train and which facilities they can use.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.2] Travel costs: travel to training, competitions and coaching can make participation expensive, especially for performers far from clubs.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.8.3] Coaching fees: private coaching can improve technique quickly but may be out of reach for many performers.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.9.b] Exam questions often name an activity and ask for the two factors that matter most. The method is to pick the two that most affect performance in that activity, preferably from different groups, and link each to specific moments of performance.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.9.0] Start by listing what the activity demands; the factors that match those demands are the most important.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.9.1] Basketball free-throw shooter: technique and concentration are the two most important factors, because the shot is a repeated movement taken alone under pressure.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.9.2] Technique: a small fault in the release shows in every attempt, so technique largely determines accuracy.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.9.3] Concentration: the shooter must block out the crowd and the score and complete the same routine before each shot, so concentration decides whether the routine is completed.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.9.4] Marathon runner: cardio-respiratory endurance (the ability of the heart and lungs to supply oxygen over a long time) and pacing/motivation are the two most important factors.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s1.9.5] Endurance decides whether pace holds after the halfway point; pacing and motivation decide whether the runner keeps pushing through the final stages when fatigue rises.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s5: 5. Assessing fitness and the principles of training
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.b] A fitness test turns a vague feeling of being fit into a measurement. For the measurement to be worth acting on, the test must be chosen and carried out properly.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.0] Testing has several purposes: to identify strengths and weaknesses against the demands of the activity, to set realistic goals, to design and monitor a training programme, and to motivate the performer.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.1] Results can also be compared with norms (normative data).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.2] A valid test measures what it claims to measure. For example, a sit-and-reach test is valid for hamstring and lower-back flexibility but not for strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.3] A reliable test gives consistent results when repeated under the same conditions. Validity and reliability are different ideas: a test can be consistent without measuring the right thing.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.4] Use a standardised protocol (the same set of instructions every time): the same warm-up, surface, time of day, equipment and tester, so that any change in the results comes from the performer and not from the conditions.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.5] Tests must be safe and suited to the performer. They are repeated before and after a programme to show progress.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.0.6] Field tests are practical, cheap and can test many people at once; laboratory tests are more accurate but expensive.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.b] Each health-related component of fitness has a standard test. For every one you should be able to name the test, outline how it is carried out and say what is recorded.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance (the ability of the heart and lungs to supply oxygen over time): the multistage fitness test (bleep test). The performer runs 20 m shuttles at a pace set by bleeps that get faster each level until they can no longer keep up.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.1] In the multistage fitness test the level reached is recorded, and VO2 max (maximum oxygen uptake, the most oxygen the body can use per minute) is estimated from it.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.2] Cardio-respiratory endurance, alternative: the Cooper 12-minute run, in which the distance the performer covers in 12 minutes is recorded, so a longer distance means better endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.3] Cardio-respiratory endurance for intermittent team sports (sports with bursts of effort and rests): the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test, which uses repeated shuttles with short recovery breaks, so it matches the stop-start pattern of the game.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.4] Muscular endurance (repeated muscle contractions without tiring): press-up and sit-up (curl-up) tests, recording the maximum number in one minute or to exhaustion; or the time a plank is held.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.5] Strength (the most force a muscle can produce): the hand-grip dynamometer, or a one-repetition maximum (1RM), which is the heaviest weight lifted once with correct technique.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.6] Flexibility (range of movement at a joint): the sit-and-reach test, measuring hamstring and lower-back flexibility as the distance reached past the toes.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.7] Body composition (the proportions of fat, muscle and bone): skinfold callipers at set sites, or bioelectrical impedance scales.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.1.8] BMI (body mass index, based on height and weight) is a third option, but it can wrongly class muscular people as overweight because muscle is heavy and BMI cannot tell muscle from fat.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.b] Performance-related components matter most for success in a particular sport. Their tests are short and practical, and each records a time, a distance or a count.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.0] Speed (how fast you can move): a 30 m sprint from a standing start, timed.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.1] Agility (changing direction quickly and under control): the Illinois agility run or the T-test, timing a set course with changes of direction.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.2] Power (strength applied at speed): the vertical (sergeant) jump, measuring jump height, or the standing broad jump, measuring distance.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.3] Balance (keeping the body steady): the stork stand, timing how long the performer balances on one foot.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.4] Co-ordination (moving body parts smoothly together, for example hand and eye): the alternate-hand wall-toss test, counting catches in 30 seconds.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.2.5] Reaction time (the delay between a signal and the start of a response): the ruler drop test. A partner drops a ruler between the performer's thumb and finger, and the distance it falls before being caught is recorded. A shorter distance means a faster reaction.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.b] A test battery is a set of tests chosen to measure the components a specific activity demands, based on a needs analysis (a study of what the activity asks of the performer). Building one is a short sequence of decisions.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.0] 1. Analyse the activity's demands and select its key components, so you only test what matters for that sport.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.1] 2. Choose one valid, reliable test for each component that is specific to the activity, so the result tells you something useful about the sport.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.2] 3. Order the tests so fatigue does not distort results. Non-fatiguing tests such as flexibility, power and agility go first, and the maximal endurance test goes last, because a tired performer would score badly on everything after it.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.3] 4. Standardise the conditions, record the results and compare them with norms or previous results.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.3.4] Example battery for soccer: a 30 m sprint (speed for breaking away from a defender), the Illinois agility run (changes of direction), the vertical jump (power for heading), then the Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test (repeated high-intensity running over a match).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.b] The principles of training are rules that make a programme effective and safe. The specification names readiness, specificity, progression, overload and rest/recovery. Exam answers also accept reversibility, individual differences and variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.0] Specificity: training must match the demands of the activity, including its energy systems, muscles, movements and speeds, because the body adapts only to what it is asked to do.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.1] Progression: the training load is increased gradually as the body adapts, so improvement continues without a sudden jump that risks injury.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.2] Overload: the body must work harder than normal to adapt, achieved by raising frequency (how often), intensity (how hard) or time (how long).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.3] Rest and recovery: the body adapts during recovery, so too little rest leads to overtraining (a build-up of fatigue that stops progress or causes injury).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.4] Readiness: the performer must be physically and mentally ready for the training, considering age, maturity, fitness base and skill.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.5] Reversibility: fitness gains are lost if training stops, so training must be kept up.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.6] Individual differences: programmes should suit the individual's fitness, goals and response to training, because two people given the same programme will not adapt in the same way.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.4.7] Variety (avoiding tedium): varying training keeps motivation high and reduces overuse injuries, which come from repeating the same movements.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.b] Each principle helps performance, but each has a cost if it is applied badly. Evaluating means giving the benefit and the risk together.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.0] Specificity transfers training directly to competition, but too narrow a focus can neglect general fitness and cause overuse injuries.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.1] Overload drives improvement, but progressing too fast raises injury and overtraining risk.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.2] Rest and recovery allow adaptation, but too much rest causes reversibility (the loss of fitness gained).
[pe-performance-demands-s5.5.3] Readiness protects young athletes, who should not be given adult training loads before they are physically mature, because their bodies are still developing.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.b] FITT is a way to describe a training programme: Frequency (how often), Intensity (how hard), Time (how long) and Type (what kind of exercise). Changing frequency, intensity or time is how overload is applied. The values below are typical guidance, not fixed rules.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.0] Cardio-respiratory endurance: 3 to 5 times a week, 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate, at least 20 minutes, continuous running, cycling or swimming.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.1] Muscular endurance: 2 to 3 times a week, light loads, 15 or more repetitions in 2 to 3 sets, circuits or body-weight exercises.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.2] Strength: 2 to 3 times a week with rest days between, heavy loads, 3 to 6 repetitions in 3 to 5 sets, free weights or machines.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.3] Flexibility: 3 to 5 times a week, stretch to mild tension, hold each stretch about 30 seconds, static, dynamic or PNF stretching.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.6.4] Body composition: most days, moderate intensity, longer sessions of 30 minutes or more, aerobic exercise combined with a balanced diet.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.b] The same four letters apply to the performance-related components. Again, the values are typical guidance.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.0] Speed: 2 to 3 times a week, maximal efforts, short sprints with full recovery, sprint intervals.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.1] Agility: 2 to 3 times a week, high intensity, short drills with recovery, ladder and cone drills.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.2] Power: 2 to 3 times a week, explosive maximal efforts, low repetitions with full recovery, plyometrics or jump training.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.3] Balance: 3 or more times a week, progressively harder positions, short holds, single-leg and unstable-surface exercises.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.4] Co-ordination: regular short practices, increasing complexity, skill drills combining body parts.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.7.5] Reaction time: regular short practices, maximal responses, reaction drills to sounds or visual signals.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.b] Intensity is the hardest FITT letter to judge by eye, so performers use heart rate to set it. Heart rate zones show whether a session is training the aerobic system (using oxygen) or the anaerobic system (working without enough oxygen). These figures are estimates.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.0] Maximum heart rate is estimated as 220 minus age.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.1] Aerobic training zone: about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate. Anaerobic training zone: about 80 to 90%.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.2] Aerobic threshold: the lower edge of the aerobic zone, below which training gives little cardio-respiratory benefit because the heart and lungs are not being challenged enough.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.3] Anaerobic (lactate) threshold: the intensity above which lactic acid builds up faster than it can be removed, which is why exercise above it cannot be kept going for long.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.4] Example: a 17-year-old's estimated maximum heart rate is 220 − 17 = 203 beats per minute, so the aerobic zone is about 122 to 162 beats per minute.
[pe-performance-demands-s5.8.5] Intensity is monitored by heart rate or by the rating of perceived exertion (RPE), the performer's own score of how hard they are working.

CANDIDATES pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3: 3. Aesthetic and artistic considerations
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.b] Some activities are won by a clock or a scoreboard, but others are decided by how the performance looks and what it communicates. Two related words describe this, and they are not the same.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.0] Aesthetic means concerned with the beauty and quality of movement as perceived by the observer, in other words how a movement looks and feels to watch.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.1] Artistic means concerned with creative expression, where the performer deliberately communicates an idea, mood, story or emotion through choreographed movement (movement planned in advance).
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.2] A performance can be aesthetically pleasing without being artistic, because the performer need not be communicating an idea. A graceful dive is lovely to watch but is not telling a story.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.0.3] Aesthetic and artistic criteria are judged qualitatively, by people, rather than by an objective outcome such as time, distance or score.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.b] The first group of criteria asks how well the body moves and holds its shape. Judges look for clean, controlled, flowing movement that is matched to the music where there is any.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.0] Body line, shape and extension: the position the body makes, such as pointed toes, straight legs and full extension of the arms. Stretched, clear shapes look more polished than bent or half-finished ones.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.1] Control, poise and balance: positions are held steadily and landings are controlled, such as a balanced finish at the end of a turn on a beam. Wobbling or stumbling shows a lack of control.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.2] Fluency and flow: smooth transitions between elements with no pauses or jerky changes, so the routine looks like one continuous movement rather than a string of separate skills.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.1.3] Timing, rhythm and musicality: movements are matched to the accents (the strongest beats) of the music, so the movement and the sound seem to belong together.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.b] The second group of criteria is about what the performance says and how it is put together. These matter most in dance and other activities where the performer is deliberately communicating something.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.0] Expression and communication: a mood or story is conveyed through facial expression and body language, and projected to the audience so that even those far away can read it.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.1] Creativity and originality: the choreography uses new or unexpected movements and combinations, so the routine stands out from others rather than repeating familiar patterns.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.2] Use of space and variety: levels (high, medium and low), pathways (straight, curved or zigzag) and direction (forwards, sideways or backwards) are varied across the routine, which keeps it interesting to watch.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.3] Synchronisation and relationships: in group or pair work, performers move with matched timing and shape, and their relationships (such as leading, following, mirroring or contrasting) convey the theme.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.2.4] Presentation: costume, props, entrance and finish, where the activity allows. A strong start and finish frame the routine and leave an impression on the judges.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.b] Even though judging is done by people, activities such as gymnastics use a structured scoring system. The system splits a routine into what was attempted and how well it was done.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.0] Difficulty value: awarded for the elements attempted, separately from how well they are performed. Harder skills earn a higher value.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.1] Execution score: starts from a maximum and has deductions for faults such as bent legs, loss of balance or a poor landing, so a cleaner performance keeps more of the marks.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.2] The final mark combines the difficulty value and the execution score, so a hard routine performed poorly can score less than an easier one performed well.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.3] Diving is scored in the same way, on the difficulty of the dive and its execution.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.3.4] Dance is scored by judges against criteria such as expression and musicality, rather than by a difficulty value plus deductions.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.b] Beauty is not limited to activities with judges. Games, athletics and swimming races are decided by objective outcomes, yet good performers often show aesthetic qualities along the way.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.0] Games, athletics and swimming races are judged on objective outcomes, but they contain aesthetic qualities that are present and not scored.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.1] Example: a fluent passing move in a game is aesthetically pleasing, but the score depends on the result, such as a goal.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.2] Example: an elegant hurdling technique in athletics is aesthetically pleasing, but the race is decided by time.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.4.3] Coaches and spectators notice these qualities informally, so they shape how people enjoy and talk about the activity, but they are not recorded on a scoring sheet.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.b] Exam questions ask you to set two activities side by side, with at least one being artistic or aesthetic. A good comparison covers what each is judged on, then where they overlap and where they differ.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.0] Activities judged mainly on aesthetic or artistic criteria include gymnastics, dance, diving, synchronised swimming and figure skating.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.1] A comparison covers what each activity is judged on, how its scoring works, which criteria the two share and where they differ.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.2] Shared criteria: body line, control and timing appear in both dance and gymnastics, because both reward well-held shapes and movement matched to the music or rhythm.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.3] Difference: dance emphasises expression and musicality, while gymnastics adds a difficulty value to the execution score.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.5.4] Surfing is a borderline case: it is judged on criteria including speed, power and flow, so its criteria mix performance qualities with aesthetic ones. Ski jumping is similar, because style marks are written into its scoring.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.b] Evaluating means judging a performance against the criteria and then using the result to improve. Because the criteria are qualitative, a clear method keeps the judgement fair and repeatable.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.0] 1. Video the performance so it can be replayed and scored more than once against the same criteria.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.1] 2. Score each criterion with a checklist or rating scale, so every criterion gets its own mark.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.2] 3. Gather self, peer and teacher evaluations, and compare the scores with a model performance (an example of the standard to aim for).
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.3] 4. Identify strengths, such as clean body lines, and areas to improve, such as transitions that lose their flow.
[pe-planning-optimum-performance-s3.6.4] 5. For each area to improve, state how it will be improved, such as a drill that rehearses the transition.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s7: 7. Psychological preparation
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.b] Skill alone does not decide a performance; what the performer believes about their skill matters too. Confidence shapes how hard they try, what they attempt and how they cope under pressure.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.0] Confidence is a performer's belief in their ability to perform successfully. Self-efficacy is confidence in one specific situation, such as taking a penalty in a final, so a player can be confident in training yet doubtful in that moment.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.1] Albert Bandura's theory gives four sources of self-efficacy. The first is performance accomplishments (past success).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.2] The second source is vicarious experiences (watching similar others succeed), which tells the performer 'someone like me can do this'. The third is verbal persuasion (encouragement from others).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.3] The fourth source is emotional arousal: how the performer interprets their body's state, for example reading a racing heart as readiness rather than as fear.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.4] High confidence leads to more effort and persistence, and to willingness to attempt difficult skills and to take responsibility at key moments, because the performer expects to succeed.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.5] Low confidence leads to avoiding the ball or difficult moves, hesitation, and more anxiety, because the performer expects to fail. Over-confidence can also harm, causing complacency (relaxing too much) and poor preparation.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.6] Strategies to build confidence: set achievable goals so success builds up; recall or watch video of past successes; use positive self-talk; use imagery (picturing yourself performing well); watch demonstrations by similar performers; and receive specific praise from the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.b] Before anyone can understand anxiety they need to know about arousal, the body and mind's level of activation. Performers need some arousal to perform well, but the right amount depends on the task and the person.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.0] Arousal is the level of physical and mental activation or readiness, on a scale from deep sleep to extreme excitement. It is neutral: it is simply how switched on the performer is, not a good or bad feeling.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.1] The inverted-U hypothesis (based on the Yerkes-Dodson law) says performance improves as arousal rises to an optimal level, then declines if arousal keeps rising. Plotted on a graph this makes an upside-down U.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.2] Under-aroused, the performer is bored, slow to react and makes careless errors, because they are not alert enough for the task.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.3] Over-aroused, muscles tense, attention narrows, and decisions and co-ordination suffer, because the performer is overloaded.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.4] The optimal level is lower for complex and fine skills and for beginners, such as a golf putt or a beginner's handspring, because these need precise control and a lot of thinking.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.5] The optimal level is higher for simple and gross skills and for experts, such as a rugby tackle or a weightlift, because these need force and speed and the expert has them well learned.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.b] Anxiety is what arousal can turn into when a performer feels threatened. It has a mental side and a physical side, and each affects performance in its own way.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.0] Anxiety is a negative emotional state of worry and apprehension (a fearful sense that something will go wrong), linked to high arousal.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.1] Cognitive anxiety is the mental side: worry, negative thoughts and self-doubt, for example dwelling on missing a free.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.2] Somatic anxiety is the physical side: a raised heart rate, sweating, muscle tension and butterflies in the stomach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.3] State anxiety is anxiety felt in a particular situation, such as before a penalty. Trait anxiety is a person's general tendency to be anxious across situations, so a high-trait performer feels it more often.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.4] Effects on performance: attention is narrowed or distracted, muscles tense, and co-ordination and decision-making break down. A performer may choke under pressure (fail at a skill they can normally do).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.5] Some anxiety can help if the performer interprets the feelings as readiness rather than threat, which is how nerves can become useful energy.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.6] Strategies: breathing control; progressive muscular relaxation (tensing and relaxing muscle groups in turn); positive self-talk and reframing nerves as excitement; imagery; a pre-performance routine; and simulating competition pressure in practice so it feels familiar.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.7] Match the strategy to the problem: relaxation and breathing lower somatic anxiety, while self-talk and reframing tackle the worry of cognitive anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.b] Motivation is what gets a performer to the training session and keeps them going when it is hard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.0] Motivation is the drive to begin, direct and keep going with an activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.1] Intrinsic motivation comes from within, such as enjoyment, mastery and satisfaction. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside, such as trophies, praise, selection and rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.2] Effects on practice: a motivated performer attends, works hard, keeps the quality of repetitions high when no one is watching and persists when progress is slow.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.3] Effects on performance: persistence when losing, effort to the final whistle, and willingness to train through difficult phases.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.4] Strategies: SMART goal setting; variety in training so it stays interesting; recording progress so improvement is visible; and specific praise and positive feedback.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.5] More strategies: give the performer choice and ownership, so they feel the training is theirs; set team targets; and use appropriate rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.6] Relying too much on extrinsic rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, because the performer starts to train for the prize rather than the enjoyment. Rewards should therefore recognise effort and improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.b] Concentration means putting your attention on the right thing at the right time. Different moments in a game need different kinds of focus, which Nideffer's model describes.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.0] Concentration is the ability to focus attention on the relevant cues (the information that matters), ignore distractions, and keep that focus over time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.1] Robert Nideffer described attentional focus as broad or narrow and internal or external. Broad takes in lots of information; narrow takes in one thing; internal is inside the performer; external is the outside world.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.2] Broad-external is reading the whole game, such as a quarterback or midfielder scanning the field. Narrow-external is focusing on one cue, such as watching the ball onto the racket.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.3] Broad-internal is planning tactics or analysing performance in the mind. Narrow-internal is focusing on one's own body or thoughts, such as a pre-shot routine or a key cue word.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.4] Effects of lapses: errors, missed cues and poor decisions, especially late in a contest or during long waits between efforts, such as between attempts in a field event.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.5] Strategies: pre-performance routines; cue words (short trigger words); focusing on process goals (what to do, not the result); and imagery.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.6] Further strategies: thought stopping (recognising a negative thought and replacing it with a positive one); simulation training with crowd noise and distractions, so distractions lose their effect; and breaking a long event into short segments.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.b] Feedback is how a performer finds out what happened, and it drives improvement. Feedback is classified in several ways, and exam questions often ask you to identify the type.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.0] Feedback is information a performer receives about their performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.1] Intrinsic feedback comes from within, through the senses and the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic feedback), such as feeling a clean strike. Extrinsic feedback comes from outside, such as a coach, video or the scoreboard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.2] Knowledge of results (KR) is information about the outcome, such as whether the shot went in or the time achieved.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.3] Knowledge of performance (KP) is information about the quality of the movement or technique, such as the elbow position at release. Do not swap KR and KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.4] Positive feedback tells the performer what went well and motivates. Negative feedback tells them what went wrong and helps correct it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.5] Concurrent feedback is given during the performance; terminal feedback is given after it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.6] Beginners need frequent, simple, positive, extrinsic feedback and KR, because they cannot yet judge their own movement. Experienced performers rely more on intrinsic feedback and detailed KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.7] Good feedback is specific, timely and constructive. Too much feedback overloads the learner and creates dependence on the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.b] Mental preparation is not only a pre-match job. Strategies are used at three stages, and each strategy has strengths and limits worth knowing.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.0] Before: goal setting for the performance; imagery of key moments; a pre-performance routine; relaxation and breathing; positive self-talk; and preparing equipment and a game plan, so nothing is left to worry about.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.1] During: cue words and self-talk; a refocusing routine after errors, such as a deep breath and a reset word; breathing control in breaks in play; and focusing on the process, one point at a time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.2] After: reflection and performance review; constructive feedback; relaxation and recovery; and resetting goals. A poor result is reframed (viewed differently) as information for improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.3] Evaluating imagery: it builds confidence and rehearses skills without physical fatigue, but it needs practice and a clear, controllable image to work.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.4] Evaluating goal setting: it directs effort and raises motivation, but unrealistic or outcome-only goals can increase anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.5] Evaluating relaxation techniques: they lower somatic anxiety quickly, but too much relaxation before a power event can leave the performer under-aroused.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.b] A personal action plan turns the psychological factors into a plan you could actually follow. It starts from the performer's own weaknesses and ends with a check on whether it worked.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.0] Step 1: identify the psychological factors to target from self-assessment, such as an anxiety questionnaire or a reflective diary.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.1] Step 2: set goals for those factors.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.2] Step 3: choose one or two strategies for before, during and after performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.3] Step 4: give a rationale for each strategy, explaining how it affects the targeted factor. A rationale is the reason the strategy was chosen.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.4] Step 5: say how progress will be monitored and reviewed, such as rating confidence after each match.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.5] Illustrative plan for a basketball free-throw shooter with high anxiety: before, breathing control and imagery of successful shots to lower somatic anxiety and build confidence.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.6] During, the same pre-shot routine and a cue word at every free throw to narrow focus. After, a review of video and a diary rating of anxiety to track progress.
