TARGET pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2: 2. Physical activity participation
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.0.b] A physical activity biography is the story of how a person has been active so far and what they do now. Writing one shows what has shaped a person's habits, which is the first step in understanding participation.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.0.0] History content: the activities taken up and given up, the age at each, and the settings used (school, club, home, outdoors), so you can see how a person's activity changed over time.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.0.1] History content: the influences on participation, such as family, school, friends, coaches and facilities, because these often explain why someone started, kept going or stopped.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.0.2] History content: the reasons for starting and stopping each activity, and the high and low points, such as a first team win or an injury that ended a sport.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.0.3] Presentation: a timeline by life stage (for example childhood, early teens, senior cycle) that marks the turning points, where a person's activity changed direction.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.0.4] Current profile: the activities done now, how often, for how long and at what intensity (how hard the effort is). This is the person's activity profile today.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.0.5] Comparison: set the biography beside someone whose profile differs, such as a non-participant, an older adult or someone with a disability, and explain the differences in influences, opportunities and motivation.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.b] A support is something that makes it easier to be active and a barrier is something that makes it harder. School shapes activity for most young people, so it has both.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.0] Support: PE classes taught by qualified teachers and coaches, who make activity enjoyable and accessible so pupils build skill and confidence.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.1] Support: school teams and extra-curricular activities (those outside timetabled classes), such as after-school clubs, which give pupils regular chances to take part.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.2] Support: lunchtime leagues and activity breaks during the school day, which fit activity into time pupils are already in school.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.3] Support: facilities and equipment that are available to pupils outside class time, so activity is not limited to the PE timetable.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.4] Support: the Active School Flag, a Department of Education initiative that recognises schools that promote physical activity and PE, and a positive peer culture that values activity.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.5] Barrier: limited time for PE on the timetable, and exam pressure, especially in senior cycle.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.6] Barrier: a narrow range of activities, with a focus on competitive sports that some pupils do not enjoy.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.1.7] Barrier: lack of facilities or limited access to them, changing-room and kit concerns that discourage some pupils, and low confidence in one's own skill or body.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.b] Outside school, whether a person is active depends on what is available locally and whether they can afford and reach it. The community also offers supports and barriers.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.0] Support: local clubs and sports partnerships offering organised activity, which give people a place to train and play with others.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.1] Support: parks, greenways (traffic-free routes for walking and cycling) and walking routes that are free to use, so cost is not an obstacle.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.2] Support: free events, such as parkrun (a free weekly 5 km timed event on Saturday mornings) and fun runs.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.3] Support: family involvement, such as parents driving children to sessions and attending them.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.4] Support: accessible and affordable facilities close to home, which make regular activity realistic.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.5] Barrier: cost of membership, fees and equipment.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.6] Barrier: transport, especially where public transport is limited or travel to facilities is long, and lack of local facilities.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.7] Barrier: safety concerns, such as poorly lit paths, opening hours that do not suit people who work or study, and weather, which stops outdoor activity.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.2.8] Barrier: lack of programmes for some groups, so those people have nothing suitable to join.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.3.b] Every school or community factor falls into one of four types. Sorting factors this way helps you see what kind of action would fix a problem.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.3.0] Personal: time, motivation, confidence, skill level, injury and body image, which all depend on the individual.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.3.1] Social: family, friends and culture, which can either encourage or discourage participation depending on whether they value activity.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.3.2] Environmental: facilities, transport, safety and weather, which are about the places and conditions around a person.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.3.3] Economic: membership fees, equipment and travel costs, which decide whether a person can afford to take part.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.4.b] To understand participation you need evidence, and no single method records everything. Each method collects a different type of information and has its own strength and limitation.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.4.0] Pedometers record steps per day. Strength: cheap and objective (not based on opinion). Limitation: they do not record cycling, swimming or intensity.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.4.1] Activity diaries or logs record the type, duration and intensity of each activity. Strength: detailed. Limitation: they depend on honesty and memory, so entries may be wrong.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.4.2] Questionnaires and surveys record self-reported activity and attitudes from many people quickly. Strength: wide reach. Limitation: self-report bias, because people may overstate or forget their activity.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.4.3] Interviews and focus groups record reasons and attitudes in depth. Strength: they explain why people do or do not take part. Limitation: time-consuming and limited to small numbers.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.4.4] Wearable trackers and smartphone apps record steps, heart rate and distance. Strength: automatic and continuous, so nothing relies on memory. Limitation: cost and accuracy limits.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.4.5] Heart-rate monitors record the intensity of activity. Strength: they show whether activity is moderate or vigorous. Limitation: cost, and the need to wear them correctly.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.4.6] Observation and attendance records count numbers using a facility or club. Strength: objective counts that do not rely on memory. Limitation: they show whether people attend, but not why or how active they are.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.b] A specific group in the school community, such as a year group or girls in senior cycle, can be analysed in five steps. Once you have the steps, you can also check the results against patterns that are commonly found.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.0] Step 1: collect data on the group, using a questionnaire, activity diary or attendance record.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.1] Step 2: present the data in tables and bar charts so that comparisons between activities and groups are clear.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.2] Step 3: describe the patterns, such as which activities are most popular, how often students take part and any drop-off (fall in participation) with age.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.3] Step 4: explain the likely reasons, such as exam pressure, interests, cost, facilities or gender.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.4] Step 5: suggest actions the school could take, linked to the reasons found, so the suggestions fit the problem.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.5] Common pattern: participation falls through adolescence, especially in senior cycle.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.6] Common pattern: team-sport participation is often higher among boys, while drop-out is often higher among girls. This is one observed pattern only.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.5.7] Common pattern: popular activities reflect local facilities and traditions, such as strong Gaelic games participation where clubs are local, and low swimming where there is no pool.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.b] Promotion means encouraging more people to be active. Different approaches work at different levels, from national campaigns to a single school.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.0] National media campaigns reach large audiences through television, radio and social media.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.1] School-based programmes, such as the Active School Flag (a Department of Education initiative recognising schools that promote physical activity and PE), daily activity breaks and PE.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.2] Community events, such as parkrun (a free weekly 5 km timed event on Saturday mornings) and fun runs.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.3] Targeted programmes for groups with low participation, which aim to help where it is most needed.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.4] Making environments active, such as building greenways and cycle lanes, so being active becomes the easy choice.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.5] Incentives and free access, such as free taster sessions or free entry to events, which remove cost as a barrier.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.6] Role models, such as athletes and coaches, who inspire others to take part.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.6.7] Wearable technology and challenges, such as step challenges that track progress and keep people motivated.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.7.b] Evaluating an approach means judging how well it works. Five questions help you decide.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.7.0] Reach: how many people take part, and whether they are the people the approach targeted.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.7.1] Cost: the money, staff and facilities needed, compared with the number of people reached, which shows whether it is good value.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.7.2] Sustainability: whether activity continues once the programme ends, so the benefit lasts.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.7.3] Inactive groups: whether the approach reaches people who are not active, not only those already keen.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.7.4] Measuring success: participation records, questionnaires or trackers used before and after the programme to show change.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.8.b] A school can act directly to get more pupils taking part. The actions below are ways to widen who takes part and how.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.8.0] Offer a wider range of activities, so pupils with different interests can find one they enjoy.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.8.1] Provide non-competitive options, such as fitness classes or walking groups, for pupils who dislike competitive sport.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.8.2] Run lunchtime and after-school clubs, which give extra chances to be active outside class.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.8.3] Let students have a say in which activities are offered, so they are more likely to take part in them.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.8.4] Link with local clubs, so pupils can carry on being active outside school.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s2.8.5] Use positive role models, such as older students, former pupils or coaches, to encourage others.

CANDIDATES pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1: 1. Benefits of physical activity participation
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.0.b] Physical activity is the broad idea, and it runs on a continuum, from healthy-lifestyle activity at one end to competing to win at the other. Play, leisure and recreation, and physical education each have their own purpose.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.0.0] Physical activity is any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles (the muscles attached to your bones) that uses energy, so it covers everything from a walk to school to a professional match.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.0.1] Play is spontaneous, freely chosen activity done for its own sake, with flexible or self-made rules, and it is mainly associated with children. Examples: chasing games in a playground; kicking a ball around a park with self-made goals.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.0.2] Leisure and recreation is activity chosen in free time for enjoyment, relaxation and wellbeing, with little emphasis on competition, because the point is to feel good rather than to win. Examples: a walk; a leisurely cycle; a swim.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.0.3] Physical education is planned, structured learning in, through and about physical activity within the school curriculum, taught by a qualified teacher. Examples: a PE class learning the technique of a striking action; a lesson on how the body responds to exercise.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.1.b] Mass-participation sports, outdoor and adventure activities, and sport differ in their purpose: personal challenge, adventure in nature, or winning.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.1.0] Mass-participation sports are organised events in which large numbers of people of mixed ability take part at the same time, usually for personal challenge or charity rather than to win. Examples: city marathons; fun runs; sportives; parkrun.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.1.1] parkrun is a free, weekly, timed 5 km event held on Saturday mornings in many locations and open to everyone, which makes it a clear example of many people of mixed ability taking part together.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.1.2] Outdoor and adventure activities take place in the natural environment and involve challenge, managed risk (risk that is assessed and controlled) and uncertainty.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.1.3] Land-based outdoor and adventure activities: hill walking, orienteering, rock-climbing and mountain biking.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.1.4] Water-based outdoor and adventure activities: canoeing, kayaking, sailing and surfing.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.1.5] Sport is organised, competitive physical activity governed by formal rules and a governing body, with an emphasis on performance and winning. Examples: a club league in Gaelic football; a national championship in swimming.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.2.b] Regular activity keeps the heart, bones and body systems working well. This is why it protects against many long-term illnesses and makes daily life easier.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.2.0] Lower risk of heart disease and stroke, because activity keeps the heart and circulation working well.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.2.1] Lower risk of type 2 diabetes.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.2.2] Lower risk of obesity, which supports weight management because activity uses energy.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.2.3] Lower risk of some cancers.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.2.4] Stronger bones and a lower risk of osteoporosis (a condition in which bones become weak and break easily).
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.2.5] Better fitness, which makes everyday physical tasks easier.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.2.6] Better sleep.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.3.b] Physical activity helps the mind as well as the body. It affects how people feel, think and stay motivated.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.3.0] Improved mood and self-esteem (how you value yourself).
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.3.1] Reduced stress and anxiety.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.3.2] Reduced symptoms of depression.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.3.3] Better concentration.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.3.4] Enjoyment and a sense of achievement, which sustain motivation to keep taking part, so people carry on being active.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.4.b] Physical activity can build relationships, skills and communities as well as fitness.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.4.0] Making friends and belonging to a group, which come from regularly taking part alongside the same people.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.4.1] Teamwork, communication and leadership skills develop through group activity, because people must work together, talk to each other and sometimes take charge.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.4.2] A social outlet and a positive use of free time.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.4.3] Reduced isolation, because activity brings people together regularly.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.4.4] Community cohesion (people in a community getting along and sticking together), as people from different backgrounds meet through shared activity.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.b] Economic benefits are about money. They fall on society as a whole and on the individual, and a student should keep the two apart.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.0] For society: lower healthcare costs, because fewer people need treatment.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.1] For society: fewer sick days and higher productivity at work, because healthier workers are absent less.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.2] For society: employment in the sport and leisure industry.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.3] For society: income from tourism and events.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.4] For society: the value of volunteering, which keeps clubs and events running.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.5] For the individual: lower medical costs, since fewer treatments are needed.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.6] For the individual: career opportunities in coaching, officiating (acting as a referee or umpire), teaching and the leisure industry.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.5.7] For the individual: free activities such as walking cost less than health treatment for conditions that inactivity can cause.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.6.b] The benefits of activity change with age. In childhood and the teenage years it helps the body develop and shapes habits and confidence.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.6.0] Children: motor-skill development, such as running, jumping, throwing and catching.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.6.1] Children: healthy growth, supported by play and daily activity.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.6.2] Children: social skills learned through play with others.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.6.3] Adolescents (teenagers): self-esteem and body image, which are formed during the teenage years.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.6.4] Adolescents: stress relief during the pressures of school and exams.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.6.5] Adolescents: friendships and belonging to a peer group (people of the same age).
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.6.6] Adolescents: lifelong habits that can carry into adult life.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.b] Adults use activity to protect their health and manage pressure. For older adults it also protects independence and the ability to move safely.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.0] Adults: weight and disease prevention, which protects health across working life.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.1] Adults: stress relief from work and family demands.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.2] Adults: social contact outside work.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.3] Adults: work productivity, which keeps people able to meet job demands.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.4] Older adults: balance and strength, which help prevent falls.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.5] Older adults: bone density, which helps protect against fractures.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.6] Older adults: independence in daily life for longer.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.7.7] Older adults: social contact and reduced loneliness, and cognitive health (thinking and memory).
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.8.b] Get Ireland Active sets the national activity guidelines. They say how much activity each age group needs.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.8.0] Children and young people: at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity every day.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.8.1] Adults: at least 30 minutes of moderate activity on five days a week, which is 150 minutes a week.
[pe-promoting-physical-activity-s1.8.2] Older adults: the adult target of 30 minutes of moderate activity on five days a week, plus balance and strength activities, which target the falls and fracture risks of older age.

CANDIDATES pe-gender-physical-activity-s1: 1. Gender, sport and physical activity
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.b] Differences in participation are best understood through gender, the social side of being male or female, rather than through biology alone. So the first step is to separate the two words, which are often used as if they meant the same thing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.0] Sex: the biological characteristics, such as chromosomes (the genetic material that helps determine sex), hormones (chemical messengers in the body) and physical make-up, that define a person as male or female.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.1] Gender: the socially constructed roles, behaviours and expectations (in other words, ideas made by society, not by biology) that a society attaches to being male or female.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.2] Gender expectations differ between societies and change over time, so the activities seen as suitable for each sex are social rather than fixed.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.0.3] Gendered activities: physical activities that a society labels as suitable for boys or for girls, for example rugby as 'for boys' and dance as 'for girls'.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.b] Boys and girls do not take part in physical activity in the same way. The differences show up in how much they do, which activities they choose and when they stop, and they are described here in words, not percentages.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.0] How much: on average, girls are less active than boys in physical activity and sport.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.1] When: girls drop out of sport in greater numbers than boys during adolescence (the teenage years), especially in the senior cycle years (the last three years of secondary school).
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.2] Which activities: boys more often play competitive team and contact sports (where players physically tackle or collide), such as rugby and Gaelic football.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.3] Which activities: girls more often choose dance, gymnastics, individual fitness and non-contact activities (where players do not tackle or collide).
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.4] Many activities are labelled 'for boys' or 'for girls', which steers young people towards the activities seen as suitable for their gender.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.1.5] Participation changes across the life course (the stages of life from childhood to older age) and is shaped by many interacting influences rather than by biology alone.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.b] The family is the first place a child meets sport, and parents control much of what a child can try. Their attitudes can push boys and girls towards different activities.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.0] Parents' own activity and encouragement shape whether a child sees physical activity as normal.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.1] Parents decide what they will pay for and drive their children to, which controls access to clubs, coaching and training.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.2] For boys: parents may encourage sons into contact sports and competitive team games.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.3] For girls: parents may steer daughters towards activities they consider suitable, such as dance or gymnastics.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.2.4] Example: a parent pays a son's club fees and drives him to matches, while a daughter's wish to join the same club is not supported.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.b] Peers (friends and classmates of the same age) matter most in the teenage years, when fitting in feels important. What friends think of sport can decide whether a young person takes part.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.0] Friends' choices can decide whether a young person joins an activity or stays in it.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.1] Fear of being judged by peers stops young people trying new activities.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.2] For teenage girls: being sporty may be seen as uncool, so girls may downplay their interest in sport to fit in with friends.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.3.3] For boys: sport can be central to status among peers, so skill in team sports can bring respect, while boys who are not sporty may lose standing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.b] School is where most young people do most of their organised sport, so what a school offers and how it runs PE can widen or narrow the gender gap.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.0] The range of activities offered often centres on competitive team games, which draw boys more than girls.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.1] For girls: a narrow range of activities leaves those who prefer non-contact or individual activities with little to choose from.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.2] For boys: competitive team games give boys a visible route into school teams and inter-school competition (matches between schools).
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.3] PE kit and changing facilities can deter girls where changing space is shared, poorly kept or lacks privacy.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.4] Teacher expectations and attention: boys' teams often receive more of the teacher's time, and teachers may hold different expectations of girls' competitiveness.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.4.5] Timetabling decides which activities run and when, so a fixed timetable can limit the choices open to each group.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.b] Outside school, whether a young person can play depends on what local clubs and facilities offer and on who they see succeeding in sport.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.0] Provision: the teams, coaches, facility time and fixtures (scheduled matches) that let people take part, and how much of each is on offer.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.1] For boys: boys often have more teams, better facility slots and more fixtures, so they can train and play more regularly.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.2] For girls: fewer teams, coaches and facility slots limit how regularly they can train and play.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.3] Role models: fewer visible female athletes, coaches and administrators (people who run clubs and governing bodies) mean girls see fewer people like themselves in sport and its leadership.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.5.4] For boys: male role models dominate many visible team and contact sports, which reinforces the view that these sports belong to boys.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.b] What young people have to wear for an activity can decide whether they take part. Kit that shows the body can make teenagers, who are already conscious of how they look, want to avoid it.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.0] Tight, short or revealing kit, such as swimwear, leotards, athletics briefs and short skirts, can make teenagers self-conscious about their bodies.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.1] For girls: self-consciousness about kit can lead to avoiding an activity or dropping out, especially in adolescence.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.2] For boys: tight or revealing kit can produce the same self-consciousness, leading some boys to avoid the activity too.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.6.3] Comfortable kit that students can choose helps young people take part without feeling exposed.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.7.b] Gender shapes boys' participation too. Boys have some advantages, but they also face pressures that can limit what they feel free to do.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.7.0] Provision and pathways: boys often have more established routes from school teams into competitive club sport, so it is easier for them to keep progressing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.7.1] Pressure to be strong and competitive: boys may feel they must excel in contact and team sports to be respected.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.7.2] Ridicule: boys who choose activities labelled feminine, such as dance, may be mocked, which narrows their choices too.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.8.b] Some barriers are practical rather than social. They affect everyone, but they can weigh more heavily on girls.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.8.0] Time, cost and transport limit participation for all young people, especially where clubs and facilities are far from home.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.8.1] For girls: safety concerns about travelling home after evening training or matches can keep them from attending.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.8.2] For boys: the same time, cost and transport limits apply, but safety concerns weigh less heavily.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.9.b] Two further influences work through ideas and images rather than through facilities. Each has its own subsection, so only the main point of each is given here. There is more on these in Gender, media and body image and in Gender socialisation and its impact on participation.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.9.0] Body image and media: idealised thin, toned and muscular bodies in advertising and social media can make young people dissatisfied with their own bodies (their body image) and deter them from activity.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.9.1] Gender socialisation (learning the behaviour expected of your sex) and hegemonic ideals (the dominant, most-valued versions of masculinity and femininity): family, peers, school and media teach boys and girls what is appropriate for their sex, and these ideals steer them towards some activities and away from others.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.b] Once the causes are known, a school can act on each one. Each measure below removes a particular barrier, so match the fix to the problem.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.0] Broaden the activity offer with dance, fitness classes, aquatics (water-based activities such as swimming), outdoor pursuits and non-contact versions of games, and give students a choice, so girls who avoid competitive team games have options.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.1] Offer girls-only or friendship-group options and non-competitive sessions alongside competitive teams, which removes the fear of being judged in mixed or competitive settings.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.2] Provide flexible, comfortable PE kit and private, clean changing facilities, which removes self-consciousness about kit and changing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.3] Give girls' teams equal facility time, fixtures, coaching and recognition, shown in assemblies, on noticeboards and on social media, which removes unequal provision and invisibility.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.4] Bring in female role models and leaders, such as older students acting as leaders and visiting female athletes and coaches, which tackles the shortage of visible women in sport.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.5] Use student voice (asking students what they want): survey students and plan activities from the results, so provision matches what students want.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.10.6] Track participation by gender to see whether the gap is closing and which measures need changing.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.b] Clubs, local bodies and national campaigns can close the gap outside school, by giving girls and women somewhere to play and by making women's sport easier to see.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.0] Clubs offering girls' and women's teams at every age, with female coaches, give girls a team to join at each stage of their development.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.1] Social, beginner and return-to-sport programmes, such as women's recreational leagues and mothers-and-daughters sessions, bring in people who would not join competitive teams.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.2] Allocate pitches, halls and coaching fairly between girls' and boys' teams, so neither group has the better slots.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.3] Provide safe, accessible, well-lit facilities and transport, which tackles safety concerns about travelling after dark.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.4] Local Sports Partnership programmes (local bodies that promote sport and physical activity) targeting teenage girls reach girls through local provision.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.5] The Ladies Gaelic Football Association (LGFA) and the Camogie Association govern ladies' Gaelic football and camogie, giving girls and women their own pathway in those games.
[pe-gender-physical-activity-s1.11.6] The 20x20 campaign, launched in 2018, aims to increase the visibility of women's sport in Ireland, under the slogan 'If she can't see it, she can't be it'. Sport Ireland has a Women in Sport policy, and media coverage of women's sport adds to its visibility.
