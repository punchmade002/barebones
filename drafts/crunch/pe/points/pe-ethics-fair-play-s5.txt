TARGET pe-ethics-fair-play-s5: 5. Best practice for the use of supplements
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.0.b] Athletes are often tempted to look for an extra edge beyond ordinary meals. A dietary supplement is the kind of product they turn to, and it carries a risk that ordinary food does not.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.0.0] A dietary supplement is a product taken in addition to the normal diet, such as a powder, tablet, drink or gel.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.0.1] It supplies nutrients or other substances intended to improve health or performance, so it is meant to add to meals, not replace them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.0.2] Supplements are not regulated like medicines, so a product can contain substances that are not on its label. This is why athletes cannot simply trust what a tub says.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.1.b] The danger of an unregulated product is not just poor quality. For an athlete who is drug-tested, it can lead to a ban even if they never meant to cheat.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.1.0] Some supplements are contaminated with prohibited substances (banned drugs picked up during manufacture), or contain undeclared ones that are missing from the label.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.1.1] Under strict liability the athlete is responsible for a positive test, whatever the source of the substance. Blaming the supplement or the manufacturer is not a defence.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.1.2] Taking a supplement without intending to cheat can still produce an anti-doping violation; this is inadvertent doping (doping by accident).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.2.b] Because of these risks, athletes should work through three questions before taking anything: can food do the job, is there a real need, and does the evidence support it?
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.2.0] Food first: meet nutritional needs through a balanced diet before considering supplements, because a supplement is an addition to food, not a substitute for it.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.2.1] Needs assessment: a qualified professional, such as a sports dietitian or doctor, decides whether there is a genuine need and whether the supplement will help this athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.2.2] Evidence: use only supplements with good scientific evidence for the athlete's activity, so that the benefit is proven for that sport and not just claimed.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.3.b] Once an athlete and a professional decide a supplement is justified, the next job is to lower the chance that it contains a banned substance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.3.0] Choose batch-tested products (each production batch checked for banned substances) from third-party testing programmes, which are run independently of the manufacturer; this reduces the risk but does not remove it.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.3.1] Check every ingredient against the Prohibited List, the official list of substances and methods banned in sport.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.3.2] Keep the packaging, batch number and receipt, so the product can be traced if a problem arises.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.3.3] Never buy from unknown online sources, because there is no assurance about what the product contains.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.4.b] Even a safe, tested product can cause problems if it is used badly, so how and when it is taken matters as well as what it is.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.4.0] Use the correct dose and timing, because more is not better and a supplement works best when taken at the right moment.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.4.1] Trial the supplement in training, never for the first time in competition, so that any bad reaction happens when the result does not count.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.4.2] Monitor side effects (unwanted effects such as stomach upset) and stop if they appear.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.4.3] Young athletes: supplements are generally not recommended for under-18s, so diet should come first.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.5.b] Protein is the supplement most associated with building muscle. It comes as powders and bars that are quick to use.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.5.0] Function: muscle repair and growth after resistance (strength) or high-volume (large amounts of) training.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.5.1] Suits: athletes who do heavy resistance or high-volume training.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.5.2] Challenge: it is convenient, but often unnecessary when the diet already supplies enough protein.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.6.b] Creatine works on the energy supply of muscle for very short, powerful efforts, so it suits sports built from repeated bursts.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.6.0] Function: raises muscle phosphocreatine stores (phosphocreatine is a fuel store in muscle used for quick energy), improving repeated short maximal efforts and recovery between them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.6.1] Suits: sprinting, team games and weightlifting, because each involves short all-out efforts.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.6.2] Challenge: weight gain from water retention.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.6.3] Not prohibited: creatine is not a prohibited substance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.7.b] Caffeine is a familiar substance that also has effects useful to athletes. Its status in anti-doping rules is worth knowing precisely.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.7.0] Function: a stimulant (a substance that raises alertness and activity in the nervous system) that can reduce perceived effort (how hard exercise feels) and improve alertness and endurance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.7.1] Suits: endurance events and games that need sustained alertness.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.7.2] Challenge: side effects include anxiety, poor sleep and stomach upset.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.7.3] Not prohibited: caffeine is on WADA's monitoring programme, not the Prohibited List. WADA is the World Anti-Doping Agency, and monitored means it is watched, not banned.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.8.b] In long events the body loses fluid and runs low on fuel. Sports drinks and gels replace both while the athlete is still competing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.8.0] Function: they supply fluid, electrolytes (salts such as sodium lost in sweat) and carbohydrate (the body's main fuel) during long events.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.8.1] Suits: long endurance events.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.8.2] Challenge: sugar, calories and cost, and unnecessary for short or low-intensity activity, where water and a normal diet are enough.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.9.b] Vitamins and minerals are needed in small amounts, and normally a balanced diet provides them. A supplement has a narrow use here.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.9.0] Function: to correct a diagnosed deficiency (a shortage confirmed by a professional), such as iron or vitamin D.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.9.1] Use: only to correct a diagnosed deficiency, not as a routine addition to the diet, so a healthy athlete with a balanced diet does not need them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.b] The syllabus asks you to investigate supplement use in one activity you have chosen. The answer is built from the activity's demands, matched to the supplements that suit them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.0] Structure: describe the activity's demands, including its duration, intensity and main energy systems (the body's ways of releasing energy for movement).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.1] Structure: list the supplements used in the activity, with their claimed benefits, the evidence, the risks and the best-practice guidance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.2] Structure: end with a conclusion on whether and how a performer should use them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.3] Worked example, Gaelic football: a match is a long game of repeated sprints, changes of pace and physical contact.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.4] Link: the repeated sprints match creatine, whose benefit lies in repeated short maximal efforts.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.5] Link: the length of a match makes sports drinks useful for fluid and carbohydrate.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.6] Link: recovery after heavy training is where protein has its role.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s5.10.7] Conclusion: a player could use creatine for repeated sprints, protein for recovery and sports drinks in matches, but only after food-first habits are in place, a qualified professional has confirmed the need, and batch-tested products are chosen.

CANDIDATES pe-ethics-fair-play-s4: 4. Anti-doping rules
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.0.b] Anti-doping (the effort to stop athletes using banned drugs and methods) works on two levels. An international agency writes the rules, and a national body applies them in each country.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.0.0] The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) sets the World Anti-Doping Code, the single set of rules for the world, and the Prohibited List, the list of substances and methods that athletes are banned from using.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.0.1] In Ireland, Sport Ireland (the national sports body) runs the Irish Anti-Doping Programme and the Irish Anti-Doping Rules, so the world rules are put into practice at home.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.0.2] The Irish rules apply the World Anti-Doping Code to all athletes in sports governed by national governing bodies (NGBs, such as the association that runs a sport in Ireland) that Sport Ireland recognises.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.1.b] The rules do not ask what the athlete meant to do. They look only at what is found in the athlete's body, and this principle is called strict liability.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.1.0] Athletes are responsible for any prohibited substance found in their body, whether or not they intended to cheat, so claiming it was an accident is not a defence.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.1.1] Athletes must therefore check every medicine and supplement (such as a vitamin or protein product) they take before taking it, because anything they swallow could contain a banned substance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.b] An anti-doping rule violation is any act that breaks the anti-doping rules. The list goes well beyond failing a test, and it also covers people around the athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.0] Presence: a prohibited substance, its metabolites (the substances the body breaks it down into) or markers (signs that it was used) found in a sample taken from the athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.1] Use: use or attempted use of a prohibited substance or method, so an athlete can be in breach even if the attempt did not work.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.2] Refusal: evading, refusing or failing to submit to sample collection after being notified, so an athlete cannot avoid a test without consequences.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.3] Whereabouts failures: three missed tests and/or filing failures (not giving required location details on time) within 12 months. Whereabouts means the athlete's location, given in advance for testing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.4] Tampering: tampering or attempted tampering with any part of doping control (the testing process), such as interfering with a sample.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.5] Possession: possession of a prohibited substance or method.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.6] Trafficking: trafficking or attempted trafficking, including selling a prohibited substance, so selling a performance-enhancing drug (PED) is itself a violation.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.7] Administration: administering or attempting to administer a prohibited substance or method to an athlete, which can involve coaches or doctors rather than the athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.2.8] Complicity: assisting or covering up a violation. Prohibited association: working with a banned coach or doctor.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.b] Rules only work if they are enforced. Samples, advance location details and long-term monitoring are three ways Ireland checks that athletes follow them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.0] Testing: urine and blood samples are taken in competition and out of competition, often with no advance notice, so athletes cannot time their drug use around a known test.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.1] Each sample is sealed and split into an A and a B sample, which are analysed in WADA-accredited laboratories (labs approved by WADA).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.2] The B sample can be tested to confirm a positive A sample, which gives the athlete a second check on the result.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.3] Whereabouts: athletes in the registered testing pool (the group of athletes chosen for testing) give their location in advance, so testers know where to find them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.4] The whereabouts requirement includes a daily 60-minute slot when the athlete is available for testing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.3.5] Athlete biological passport: blood and steroid markers are monitored over time to detect the effects of doping, so doping can be spotted from changes even when no banned substance is found.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.b] Once a possible violation is found, a fixed process decides whether the athlete is guilty and what the penalty is. Each step is designed to be fair to the athlete.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.0] Sport Ireland notifies the athlete of the suspected violation.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.1] An independent disciplinary panel (a group not linked to Sport Ireland) hears the case, and appeals are possible, so a decision can be challenged.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.2] Sanctions (penalties) include disqualification of results and loss of medals and prize money.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.3] Sanctions include a period of ineligibility (a time when the athlete is banned from competing), normally four years for an intentional first violation.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.4.4] The decision is published.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.5.b] Enforcement is not only testing. Ireland also tries to prevent doping through teaching and to catch it by other means.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.5.0] Anti-doping education programmes are run for athletes and support staff (coaches, doctors and others who work with athletes), so that they know the rules and the risks.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.5.1] Investigations use information from sources other than sample tests, so doping can be uncovered even when an athlete has never failed a test.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.b] Some athletes have genuine medical conditions that need drugs which are on the Prohibited List. A therapeutic use exemption lets them take the medicine legally, provided strict conditions are met.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.0] A therapeutic use exemption (TUE) is permission to use a prohibited substance or method to treat a diagnosed medical condition. It is the circumstance in which an athlete can compete with a banned substance in their system.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.1] The athlete applies in advance, except in an emergency, to the relevant anti-doping organisation, with medical evidence.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.2] An independent panel of doctors decides the application.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.3] Condition 1: the athlete's health would be significantly impaired without the substance or method.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.4] Condition 2: it gives no additional performance enhancement beyond a return to normal health, so the athlete is treated but not given an advantage.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.5] Condition 3: there is no reasonable permitted alternative. Condition 4: the need is not a consequence of earlier use without a TUE.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.6] Examples: insulin for type 1 diabetes; certain asthma medications taken above the permitted doses; prescribed stimulant medication for diagnosed ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.6.7] A doctor's prescription alone does not make a prohibited medicine allowed; a TUE is needed, because a doctor's prescription does not by itself give permission under the anti-doping rules.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.7.b] Doping harms more than the athlete who is caught. The consequences fall on the performer and on the activity (the sport) as a whole.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.7.0] For the performer: a ban, disqualification of results, loss of medals, funding and sponsorship, damage to reputation, and the health risks of the drug itself.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s4.7.1] For the activity: lost credibility and public trust, withdrawal of sponsors and broadcasters, and young people and parents turning away from the sport, which means fewer people take part.

CANDIDATES pe-ethics-fair-play-s3: 3. Drugs and sport
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.0.b] Sport depends on athletes competing on equal terms. A drug that artificially lifts performance breaks that equal footing, so sport has a body and a list to control them.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.0.0] A performance-enhancing drug (PED) is a substance taken to improve performance artificially, which gives the user an unfair advantage over athletes who do not take it.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.0.1] The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) is the international body that fights drug use in sport. It publishes the Prohibited List, which is updated annually.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.0.2] The Prohibited List names substances and methods (ways of improving performance that do not involve a drug). Each is banned at all times, banned in competition only, or banned in particular sports.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.b] Anabolic agents are the drugs that build muscle. They are the PEDs most closely tied to strength and power, and they carry some of the widest health risks.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.0] Examples: anabolic steroids (synthetic versions of male hormones) such as nandrolone, testosterone and stanozolol.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.1] Effect: they increase protein synthesis (the body building new protein, which is what muscle is made of), so muscle mass, strength and power rise. This allows harder training and faster recovery.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.2] Misused in strength and power events, because muscle mass and recovery from hard training matter most there.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.3] Health risks: liver damage, heart disease, acne and aggression.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.1.4] Hormonal effects: infertility, and masculinisation in women (developing male characteristics).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.b] Hormones are chemical messengers made in the body. Peptide hormones and growth factors copy these messengers to change how the body performs and recovers. The two to know are EPO and HGH.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.0] Erythropoietin (EPO) raises red blood cell production and oxygen-carrying capacity. More oxygen reaches the muscles, which improves endurance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.1] EPO is misused in endurance events such as distance running and cycling, because these events depend on a steady oxygen supply.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.2] EPO health risks: thickened blood, which causes clots, stroke and heart attack.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.3] Human growth hormone (HGH) increases muscle and aids recovery.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.2.4] HGH health risks: abnormal growth and heart problems.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.b] Stimulants act on the body and brain to speed up and sharpen an athlete in the moment. They also hide the signals that would normally tell an athlete to slow down.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.0] Examples: amphetamines, cocaine and ephedrine.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.1] Effect: they increase alertness and aggression, and mask fatigue and pain (the athlete does not feel tiredness or pain that would normally force them to ease off).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.2] Misused in sports that demand sustained alertness and aggression, such as contact sports and endurance events.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.3.3] Health risks: high blood pressure, heart problems, overheating, addiction and poor judgement.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.b] These drugs work by changing what leaves the body rather than by building performance directly. A diuretic is a drug that makes the body produce more urine.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.0] Effect: they increase urine output. This allows rapid weight loss to make a weight category (the weight band an athlete must fit in to compete).
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.1] Masking agents work by diluting urine or otherwise hiding other drugs, so the athlete is less likely to be caught in a drug test.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.2] Misused in weight-category sports such as boxing, because losing weight fast helps an athlete make the limit.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.4.3] Health risks: dehydration, cramps and kidney problems.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.b] An analgesic is a painkiller. Narcotic analgesics are strong painkillers that let an athlete keep competing when the body is signalling injury.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.0] Example: morphine.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.1] Effect: it masks pain, so an athlete can compete while injured.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.2] Misused in sports where athletes compete through injury, such as contact sports.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.5.3] Health risks: worsening injury, because pain is the warning that stops an athlete using an injured body part, and addiction.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.6.b] Beta-blockers calm the body, which is the opposite of a stimulant. That helps in sports where a steady hand matters more than power.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.6.0] Effect: they lower heart rate and steady tremor (shaking of the hands), which helps precision performance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.6.1] They are prohibited in particular precision sports such as archery and shooting, where a steady hand decides the result.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.6.2] Health risks: fatigue and low blood pressure.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.7.b] The Prohibited List is longer than the main categories above. Four more classes are on it.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.7.0] Beta-2 agonists, hormone and metabolic modulators, cannabinoids and glucocorticoids are also on the Prohibited List.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.8.b] Not everything banned is a drug. Some banned methods change the body or the test result without a drug being taken.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.8.0] Blood doping: a transfusion of blood to raise the red blood cell count, which carries more oxygen.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.8.1] Tampering with samples, meaning interfering with the urine or blood sample given for testing.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.8.2] Gene doping: altering genes to enhance performance.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.b] Athletes know the health and career risks, yet some still use PEDs. The reasons come from pressure and from the benefits a drug seems to offer.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.0] Pressure to win, because success is what the athlete is judged on.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.1] Financial rewards and sponsorship, which go to winners.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.2] National pressure to succeed.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.3] Belief that rivals are doping, so the athlete feels they must do the same to compete on equal terms.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.4] Recovering from injury faster, so they can return to training and competition sooner.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.5] Making a weight category.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.9.6] Ageing athletes trying to stay competitive as their natural performance declines.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.b] An athlete who is caught, or who simply takes PEDs, faces consequences in their health, their results, their income and their name. Each is a separate implication you can use in an answer.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.0] Health: short- and long-term risks from the categories above, including death in extreme cases.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.1] Sanctions: a ban, normally four years for intentional doping, and disqualification of results.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.2] Losses: medals and records are taken away.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.3] Financial and career: loss of funding, prize money and sponsorship, and the career can end.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.10.4] Reputation and personal: public disgrace, damage to family, mental-health effects and loss of self-respect.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.b] Doping harms more than the individual. When rules are broken, the whole activity pays, from public trust to the clean athletes who compete against dopers.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.0] Credibility: public trust falls, and every outstanding performance is doubted.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.1] Commercial: sponsors, broadcasters and spectators withdraw, which reduces funding.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.2] Clean athletes (those who do not dope) lose medals, places and earnings, and records may be reallocated years later.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.3] Participation: young people and parents turn away, and others may feel pressure to dope.
[pe-ethics-fair-play-s3.11.4] Cost and exclusion: testing and education are expensive, and a country or team may be excluded from competition.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s8: 8. Diet, nutrition and hydration
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.b] Food supplies the fuel and the building materials a performer needs. Each nutrient does a different job, so knowing what each does and where it comes from lets you build a diet that supports training and competition.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.0] Carbohydrate is the body's main energy source for moderate and high-intensity exercise, and it is stored as glycogen (the body's stored form of carbohydrate) in the muscles and liver. Sources: bread, pasta, rice, potatoes, cereals and fruit.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.1] Protein is used for growth and repair of muscle and other tissue, which is why it matters after hard training damages muscle fibres. Sources: meat, fish, eggs, dairy and pulses (beans, peas and lentils).
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.2] Fat is a concentrated energy source for low-intensity, long-duration exercise. It also insulates the body and carries fat-soluble vitamins (vitamins that need fat to be absorbed). Sources: oils, nuts, dairy and oily fish.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.3] Iron carries oxygen in red blood cells, so a deficiency (not having enough) causes tiredness and reduces endurance because muscles receive less oxygen.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.4] Calcium is needed for strong bones and for muscle contraction.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.5] B vitamins help release energy from food, so they support the energy supply for training.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.6] Fibre keeps the digestive system healthy, but large amounts just before exercise can cause discomfort because it is slow to digest.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.0.7] A balanced diet for a performer is mostly carbohydrate-based, with enough protein, limited saturated fat, plenty of fruit and vegetables, and energy intake matched to the training load.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.b] What you eat before an event decides how much fuel you start with and whether your stomach copes with it. The aim is full energy stores without digestive problems.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.0] Eat a meal about 3 to 4 hours before: high in carbohydrate, moderate in protein, and low in fat and fibre, such as pasta with chicken. This tops up glycogen stores and leaves time for digestion to be complete.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.1] If needed, have a small carbohydrate snack about 1 to 2 hours before, such as a banana or toast, to add energy without a heavy stomach.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.2] Eat familiar foods only on competition day, to avoid stomach upsets from something your body is not used to.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.3] Drink plenty of fluid in the hours beforehand so you start properly hydrated.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.1.4] Carbohydrate loading is for endurance events lasting more than about 90 minutes. Carbohydrate intake is increased over the days before while training tapers (is reduced), to maximise glycogen stores.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.b] During an event the goal is to keep energy and fluid levels up. Afterwards the goal switches to recovery, which means refuelling, repairing and rehydrating.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.0] During short events, water is usually enough because stored glycogen lasts the whole effort.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.1] During events lasting more than about an hour, take carbohydrate from a sports drink, gel or banana, with fluid. This maintains blood glucose and delays fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.2] After exercise, carbohydrate restores muscle glycogen and protein repairs muscle, ideally within about 30 to 60 minutes, such as chocolate milk or a chicken and rice meal.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.3] After exercise, rehydrate with fluid and electrolytes (salts such as sodium) to replace sweat losses.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.2.4] Match-day example for Gaelic football: a pasta lunch three to four hours before throw-in; a banana an hour before; water and a sports drink at half-time; chocolate milk straight after and a chicken and rice dinner that evening.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.b] Water is involved in almost everything the body does during exercise, and sweating drains it quickly. Even a small fluid shortage harms performance, so performers need to know why water matters, when needs rise and how to check their hydration.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.0] Water regulates body temperature through sweating, transports nutrients and maintains blood volume, so losing it affects cooling and circulation.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.1] Effects of dehydration (not having enough water in the body): raised heart rate and body temperature, fatigue, headache, cramp, reduced concentration and poorer skill.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.2] A fluid loss of about 2% of body mass can impair performance, so waiting until you feel very dehydrated is too late.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.3] Fluid needs are higher in long or high-intensity activities, in hot or humid conditions, in indoor halls, when wearing heavy kit, and at altitude. Swimmers also sweat and need fluid, even though they are in water.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.4] Monitor hydration with urine colour (pale straw is good), by weighing before and after exercise, and by thirst, which is a late sign because it appears after dehydration has begun.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.5] Replace about 1.5 times the fluid lost: 1 kg of body mass lost needs about 1.5 litres.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.3.6] Drinking large amounts of plain water in very long events can dilute blood sodium (hyponatraemia, dangerously low sodium in the blood), so drinks should include electrolytes.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.b] Sports drinks come in three types, defined by how concentrated they are compared with body fluid. The concentration decides how fast the drink is absorbed and what it is best used for.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.0] Hypotonic drinks have a lower concentration than body fluid, so they are absorbed fastest and suit rapid rehydration.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.1] Isotonic drinks have about 6 to 8% carbohydrate, the same concentration as body fluid. They provide fluid and energy together and are the most common drink during exercise.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.2] Hypertonic drinks have a higher carbohydrate concentration, so they provide energy and are usually used after exercise.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.3] Benefits: they replace fluid, electrolytes (sodium and potassium) and carbohydrate in one convenient drink.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.4.4] Disadvantages: sugar and calories (dental decay, and weight gain for inactive users), cost, and they are unnecessary for short or low-intensity activity, where water is enough.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.b] Supplements are products taken in addition to food to help performance or recovery. Each has a specific role, but each also brings risks, which is why a balanced diet comes first.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.0] Protein powders help muscle repair and are a convenient way to get protein after training.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.1] Creatine raises phosphocreatine stores (a quick energy store) in muscle, improving repeated short, maximal efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.2] Caffeine is a stimulant that can reduce perceived effort and improve alertness and endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.3] Energy gels give rapid carbohydrate during long events.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.4] Vitamin and mineral supplements correct a diagnosed deficiency, such as iron.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.5] Challenge: products can be contaminated with prohibited substances, and under strict liability the athlete is responsible for anything found in their sample. Batch-tested products reduce but do not remove this risk.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.6] Challenge: side effects, such as stomach upset from caffeine or weight gain from creatine.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.5.7] Challenges: cost, unregulated claims and possible dependence. Supplements are also usually unnecessary when the diet is balanced, so the advice is food first.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.b] A dietary plan applies everything above to one chosen activity. A good plan shows what the performer eats and drinks, and gives a reason for each choice.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.0] Cover a training day and a competition day, with meals and snacks before, during and after activity and a fluid plan.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.1] Give a rationale (reason) for each choice, linked to the activity's duration, intensity and main energy system, so the food matches the demands of the sport.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.2] Aspect 1, pre-event carbohydrate: maximises glycogen for high-intensity efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.3] Aspect 2, post-event carbohydrate and protein: restores glycogen and repairs muscle for the next session.
[pe-performance-demands-s8.6.4] Aspect 3, hydration plan: prevents dehydration and keeps concentration and skill levels high.
