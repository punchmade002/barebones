TARGET pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3: 3. Safe practice
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.0] Safe practice: procedures and behaviours that reduce the risk of injury or harm before, during and after physical activity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.1] In training and performance: a warm-up and cool-down, correct technique, and suitable progression and loads.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.2] Supervision by a qualified coach, with rules followed and participants matched by size, age or ability where relevant.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.3] Intrinsic risk factors come from the performer: fitness level, previous injury, technique and fatigue.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.0.4] Extrinsic risk factors come from outside the performer: equipment, playing surface, weather, opponents and coaching.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.0] Risk assessment: a systematic check of the venue and activity to identify hazards and the people who could be harmed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.1] First aid cover and an emergency action plan, so casualties are treated and moved quickly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.2] Trained stewards and officials, and checks of facilities and equipment before the event starts.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.3] Clear rules, and a briefing for participants and officials.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.4] Crowd and traffic management for spectators and vehicles.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.5] A weather contingency, with hydration points and shelter for participants.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.6] Communication systems between organisers, first aid and emergency services.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.1.7] Pre-competition safety checks by officials are set out in the notes on The role of official.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.0] Phase 1, pulse raiser: light activity, such as jogging, that gradually increases in intensity to raise the heart rate.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.1] Phase 2, mobility: dynamic stretching through the ranges of movement the activity uses.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.2] Phase 3, activity-specific drills: skill rehearsal that builds up to game intensity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.3] Mental preparation, such as rehearsing the first passages of play, can be included.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.2.4] Illustrative soccer warm-up of about 15 minutes: five minutes of jogging, five minutes of dynamic stretches, then five minutes of passing and shooting drills at increasing speed.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.0] Raises heart rate and the temperature of the body and muscles.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.1] Increases blood flow and oxygen delivery to the muscles.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.2] Improves muscle elasticity and joint mobility, reducing injury risk.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.3.3] Rehearses skills and prepares the performer mentally.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.0] Content: light activity, such as easy jogging or walking, that gradually reduces in intensity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.1] Content: static stretching of the muscles used in the activity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.2] Gradually returns heart rate and breathing to rest.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.3] Prevents blood pooling and dizziness that can follow stopping suddenly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.4] Speeds the removal of lactic acid from the muscles.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.5] May reduce delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS).
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.4.6] Helps flexibility and gives time to reflect on the performance.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.0] Footwear suited to the surface: studs for grass and court shoes for indoor courts.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.1] Clothing suited to the conditions, with layers for cold or wet weather.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.2] Jewellery removed before activity, because it can injure the wearer or others.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.3] Helmets with faceguards are compulsory in hurling under GAA rules, and helmets are worn in camogie.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.4] Gumshields protect the teeth and mouth in rugby.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.5] Shin guards protect the lower leg in soccer and hockey.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.6] Buoyancy aids are worn in water activities.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.7] Selection: equipment must be the correct size and fit, and meet approved standards.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.5.8] Selection by level: a racket's grip size and head size are chosen to suit the performer's ability.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.0] Regular inspection and repair of nets, posts and goals, which must be secured in place.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.1] Surfaces are checked for holes, debris and other hazards before use.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.2] Pool water quality is tested and maintained.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.3] Proper storage keeps equipment clean, dry and undamaged.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.4] Maintenance prevents injury, keeps equipment effective and extends its life.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.6.5] Example: an unsecured goal can topple onto a player, so securing it before each session prevents that injury.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.0] Acute injury: sudden onset, such as a sprain, strain, fracture, dislocation, concussion or cut.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.1] Chronic (overuse) injury: develops gradually from repeated stress, such as tendinitis, shin splints, stress fracture or tennis elbow.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.2] Sprain: damage to a ligament, the tissue that joins bone to bone.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.7.3] Strain: damage to a muscle or tendon, the tissue that joins muscle to bone.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.0] Soccer: ankle sprain, caused by landing awkwardly.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.1] Soccer: hamstring strain, caused by sprinting without a warm-up.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.2] Rugby: concussion, caused by a collision or tackle.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.8.3] Rugby: shoulder dislocation, caused by a heavy tackle or a fall onto the shoulder.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.0] PRICE stands for Protect, Rest, Ice, Compression and Elevation, and is used in the first 48 to 72 hours after a soft-tissue injury.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.1] Protect: remove the player from the activity and guard the injured area from further damage.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.2] Rest: stop using the injured part, since movement can make the injury worse.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.3] Ice: a cold pack wrapped in cloth and applied in short periods, never directly on the skin, to reduce pain and swelling.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.4] Compression: an elastic bandage applied firmly, but not so tight that it cuts off circulation.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.5] Elevation: raise the injured limb above heart level to reduce swelling.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.6] Avoid heat on a fresh soft-tissue injury, because it increases swelling.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.9.7] Refer for medical assessment if the injury is severe.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.0] SALTAPS stands for See, Ask, Look, Touch, Active movement, Passive movement and Strength.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.1] See: watch how the player fell and how they move.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.2] Ask: ask what happened and where it hurts.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.3] Look: check for swelling, bruising or deformity.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.4] Touch: feel the injured area for tenderness.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.5] Active movement: the player moves the joint themselves.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.6] Passive movement: the examiner moves the joint while the player relaxes.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.7] Strength: the player pushes or pulls against resistance.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.10.8] Pain, weakness or instability at any step means the player should not return to play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.0] Concussion signs: confusion, headache, dizziness, loss of consciousness and balance problems.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.1] Concussion can occur without loss of consciousness.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.2] Remove the player from play immediately and do not return them that day. Guidance: if in doubt, sit them out.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.3] Medical clearance is needed before a graduated return to play.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.4] Cuts and bleeding: apply pressure with a clean dressing, wearing gloves.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.11.5] Blood rule: the player leaves the field until the bleeding stops.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.0] Overtraining: training too hard or too often without enough recovery, so performance declines.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.1] Symptoms: persistent fatigue, a raised resting heart rate and poor sleep.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.2] Symptoms: frequent illness and injury, and loss of motivation.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.12.3] Symptoms: irritability, falling performance and muscle soreness that does not go away.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.0] Progress gradually: a common rule of thumb is to raise weekly load by about 10%, which is guidance rather than a law.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.1] Build in rest days and easy weeks so the body can recover.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.2] Periodisation, alternating harder and easier blocks of training, is one way to plan recovery into a schedule.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.3] Vary intensity so that not every session is maximal.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.4] Monitor load with session RPE multiplied by session duration, heart rate or a wellness questionnaire.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.5] Adequate sleep and nutrition support recovery between sessions.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.6] Listen to the athlete: reduce load when they report fatigue or pain.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.7] Evaluating a schedule: check where weekly load rises sharply, since a sudden jump increases injury and overtraining risk.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.8] Evaluating a schedule: check whether it includes rest days and easy weeks, and whether intensity varies.
[pe-structures-strategies-roles-conventions-s3.13.9] Evaluating a schedule: propose changes that smooth the jump, add rest or vary the intensity.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s6: 6. Designing a fitness plan
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.0] Continuous training: steady, moderate-intensity exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes in the aerobic zone (about 60 to 80% of maximum heart rate). Develops cardio-respiratory and muscular endurance through the aerobic system. Suits distance running, swimming and cycling.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.1] Interval training: periods of work alternated with periods of rest or recovery, set by the work-to-rest ratio, the number of repetitions and the number of sets.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.2] Short, high-intensity intervals with longer recovery develop speed and anaerobic capacity; longer intervals at lower intensity develop aerobic fitness. Interval training suits intermittent sports such as hurling, soccer and basketball.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.0.3] Fartlek (speed play): continuous running with changes of pace and terrain, such as jogging, sprinting between lamp posts and running uphill. Trains the aerobic and anaerobic systems together and suits games players.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.0] Circuit training: a series of stations worked in turn with set work and rest times; can be designed for muscular endurance, strength, cardio-respiratory endurance or sport skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.1] Resistance (weight) training: working against a load such as free weights, machines or body weight, with exercises such as squats, lunges, presses, rows and curls.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.2] Heavy loads with few repetitions build strength; light loads with many repetitions build muscular endurance.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.3] Plyometric training: jumping, bounding and hopping in which a lengthening contraction on landing is immediately followed by a forceful shortening contraction on take-off. Develops power for jumping and sprinting.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.4] Plyometrics needs a strength base, a forgiving surface and low volume, so it is not for beginners.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.5] Static stretching: a stretch held still at mild tension; best in the cool-down.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.6] Dynamic stretching: controlled movement through the full range, such as leg swings; used in the warm-up.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.1.7] PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) stretching: stretch the muscle, contract it against resistance from a partner, relax, then stretch further; gives the greatest gains in range.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.0] Hurling: interval training and fartlek suit the stop-start game better than continuous training, with resistance and plyometric work for power in striking and jumping.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.1] Distance swimming: continuous and long-interval training develop the aerobic system the event depends on.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.2] Gymnastics: flexibility training and body-weight strength work matter more than long aerobic sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.2.3] The best method depends on the energy systems and fitness components the activity demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.0] Training in the activity: conditioned games and sport-specific drills that are specific, motivating and train skill under fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.1] Training outside the activity: gym-based resistance training, plyometrics, swimming or cycling as cross-training, and yoga or Pilates for flexibility and core strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.2] Benefits of training outside the activity: overloads specific components precisely, builds a fitness base, adds variety, and reduces overuse of the sport's repeated movements.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.3] Limitation of training outside the activity: it is less specific, so it must be linked back to the activity's movements and demands.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.3.4] Example for a hurler: resistance training builds the strength and power for long striking and winning dropping balls; yoga improves hip and shoulder range for the swing.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.0] Step 1: use the fitness test results to identify the weakest components that matter most for the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.1] Step 2: set goals for those components.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.2] Step 3: select training methods suited to each goal.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.3] Step 4: apply FITT and the principles of training, planning sessions per week across several weeks with progression, a warm-up and cool-down in each session, and rest days.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.4] Step 5: re-test at the end to evaluate the programme and adjust it.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.4.5] Illustrative six-week plan for a soccer player whose tests show weak speed and agility: two speed and agility sessions a week (sprints and cone drills with full recovery), one resistance session for leg strength, one fartlek run; repetitions rise every two weeks; week four is lighter; re-test in week six.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.0] Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the session: if recovery is adequate, the body rebuilds slightly above its previous level (supercompensation).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.1] Cool-down: light activity and stretching help remove waste products and return the body to rest.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.2] Rehydration: replaces fluid and electrolytes lost in sweat.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.3] Refuelling soon after exercise: carbohydrate restores muscle glycogen and protein repairs muscle tissue.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.4] Sleep: most tissue repair and growth takes place during sleep.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.5] Active recovery: a light session, such as an easy swim the day after a match, keeps blood flowing to the muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.6] Massage and foam rolling: reduce muscle tightness and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.7] Cold-water immersion: reduces inflammation and soreness after intense exercise.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.8] Compression garments: may reduce swelling and soreness.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.5.9] Planned rest days and lighter weeks prevent overtraining (its signs are in the notes on safe practice).
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.0] Periodisation: dividing a training plan into planned phases or cycles, each with a specific aim, so that volume, intensity and recovery vary systematically to bring the performer to peak performance at target competitions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.1] Macrocycle: the long-term plan, often a year or a season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.2] Mesocycle: a block of several weeks, often 4 to 6, with one focus, such as building strength.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.3] Microcycle: usually one week of sessions.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.4] Preparation phase: general fitness first, then specific fitness and skills.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.5] Competition phase: fitness is maintained and the performer peaks for key events; tapering before the main event reduces training volume while keeping intensity, so the performer arrives fresh.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.6] Transition phase: active rest and recovery after the season.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.7] Why coaches use it: the performer peaks at the right time, develops qualities in a logical order, avoids overtraining and injury, and stays motivated through variety.
[pe-performance-demands-s6.6.8] Peaking for a single major event, such as an Olympic Games every four years, uses a long-term plan built from these cycles, with each year's macrocycle building towards the target.
