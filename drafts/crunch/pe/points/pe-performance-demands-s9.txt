TARGET pe-performance-demands-s9: 9. Energy systems
[pe-performance-demands-s9.0.0] ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the only direct source of energy for muscle contraction.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.0.1] Muscles store only enough ATP for about 2 to 3 seconds of work, so it must be constantly resynthesised by three energy systems.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.0] Fuel: phosphocreatine (PC) stored in the muscle; no oxygen is needed (anaerobic).
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.1] Intensity and duration: maximal intensity, for about 8 to 10 seconds.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.2] By-products: none that cause fatigue; fatigue comes from PC running out.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.3] Recovery: PC stores are about half restored in 30 seconds and almost fully restored in about 3 minutes of rest.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.1.4] Sporting examples: 100 m sprint, long jump, shot-put, a tackle, a jump to catch a ball.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.0] Fuel: carbohydrate (glycogen and glucose) broken down without oxygen.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.1] Intensity and duration: high intensity, from about 10 seconds to about 2 minutes.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.2] By-product: lactic acid, which lowers the pH in the muscle and causes fatigue and a burning feeling.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.3] Recovery: removing lactic acid takes much longer, up to about an hour, and is faster with an active cool-down.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.2.4] Sporting examples: 400 m race, 100 m swim, a long sustained attack in a team game.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.0] Fuel: carbohydrate and fat (protein only in extreme cases), broken down with oxygen.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.1] Intensity and duration: low to moderate intensity, from about 2 minutes to several hours.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.2] By-products: carbon dioxide and water, which are easily removed and do not cause fatigue.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.3] It produces far more ATP from each molecule of fuel than the anaerobic systems, but more slowly.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.4] Sporting examples: marathon, 10 km open-water swim, the background work of a Gaelic football match.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.3.5] It also powers recovery between high-intensity efforts, such as restoring PC between sprints.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.0] All three systems work at all times; the one that predominates depends on the duration and intensity of the activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.1] Short, maximal activities rely mainly on ATP-PC; high-intensity efforts lasting up to about 2 minutes rely mainly on the lactic acid system; longer, moderate efforts rely mainly on the aerobic system.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.2] Main system by event: long jump (3 to 5 seconds, high intensity) ATP-PC; 400 m (45 seconds or more, high intensity) lactic acid; marathon (2 hours or more, low to medium intensity) aerobic.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.3] Intermittent team games use all three: ATP-PC for sprints, jumps and tackles; lactic acid for repeated efforts with short recovery; aerobic for the overall duration and for recovery between efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.4.4] On an energy-system graph of contribution against time, the ATP-PC line peaks first and falls within about 10 seconds, the lactic acid line peaks next, and the aerobic line rises steadily and dominates after about 2 minutes.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.0] Preparation: train the predominant system (specificity). For ATP-PC, short maximal sprints with full recovery; for the lactic acid system, repeated high-intensity intervals with short recovery to build tolerance of lactic acid; for the aerobic system, continuous or long-interval training.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.1] Work-to-rest ratios match the system: for ATP-PC, about 1:3 or longer so PC is restored before the next effort; for the lactic acid system, shorter recovery; aerobic work is near-continuous.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.2] Performance: pacing strategy matters; going too hard early in a 400 m or 1500 m race floods the muscles with lactic acid.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.3] Performance: substitutions and breaks in play let team players restore PC between efforts.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.4] Recovery: an active cool-down removes lactic acid faster; carbohydrate refuels glycogen for the lactic acid and aerobic systems; full rest between maximal efforts restores PC.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.5.5] Diet link: carbohydrate fuels the lactic acid and aerobic systems; creatine supplementation aims to raise PC stores for the ATP-PC system.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.0] 100 m backstroke: about one minute at very high intensity, so the ATP-PC system powers the start and the lactic acid system supplies most of the race. Preparation focuses on maximal starts and turns with full recovery and on high-intensity intervals with short recovery.
[pe-performance-demands-s9.6.1] 10 km open-water swim: about two hours at moderate intensity, so the aerobic system supplies almost all the energy, using carbohydrate and fat. Preparation focuses on long continuous swims and long intervals, pacing practice and feeding with carbohydrate during the race.

CANDIDATES pe-performance-demands-s2: 2. Health-related fitness
[pe-performance-demands-s2.0.b] Health-related fitness is the set of fitness components that matter for staying healthy as well as for sport. The first is cardio-respiratory endurance, which is about how well your body keeps supplying oxygen to working muscles.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.0.0] Definition: the ability of the heart, lungs and blood vessels to take in, transport and use oxygen to sustain whole-body activity over a long period. It is also called aerobic fitness or stamina.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.0.1] It depends on the aerobic energy system (the way the body releases energy from fuel using oxygen), which is why oxygen delivery by the heart, lungs and blood vessels decides how long you can keep going.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.0.2] It is important in distance running, distance swimming, soccer and Gaelic football, because players in these activities are active for over an hour.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.0.3] Health benefit: it lowers the risk of heart disease, stroke and high blood pressure.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.1.b] Muscular endurance sounds like cardio-respiratory endurance but is a different component. It is about how long one muscle or muscle group can keep working, not the whole body.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.1.0] Definition: the ability of a muscle or muscle group to perform repeated contractions (the tightening of a muscle as it produces force) over a period of time without tiring.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.1.1] It is local to a muscle group, whereas cardio-respiratory endurance involves the whole body. This is the key distinction to state when comparing them.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.1.2] It matters for the legs of a rower or cyclist, the arms in repeated tackles, and the core and legs of a dancer holding positions through a routine, because each of these muscle groups must keep working over and over.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.1.3] Health benefit: it makes everyday tasks such as climbing stairs and carrying shopping easier and supports good posture.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.2.b] Strength is the force a muscle can produce. It comes in several forms depending on how fast, how often and whether the body moves while the force is applied.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.2.0] Definition: the maximum force a muscle or muscle group can exert against a resistance in a single contraction.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.2.1] Maximal strength is the greatest force you can produce in one all-out effort, such as pushing in a rugby scrum (the forwards driving against the opposing pack).
[pe-performance-demands-s2.2.2] Explosive (elastic) strength is force applied quickly, such as a jump take-off. It is the basis of power, so it is the link to the next subsection.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.2.3] Strength endurance is repeated sub-maximal force (below your maximum, so it can be repeated) over time, such as a rower's stroke.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.2.4] Static strength is used without movement, such as holding a position on the rings (the hanging gymnastics apparatus). Dynamic strength is used with movement, such as lifting a weight.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.2.5] In terms of training intensity, strength is built with heavy loads and few repetitions, while muscular endurance is built with lighter loads and many repetitions.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.2.6] Health benefit: it protects joints, supports bone density and makes daily lifting safer.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.3.b] Flexibility is about how far your joints can move. A joint is where two bones meet, so more range at the joints means the body can reach and stretch further.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.3.0] Definition: the range of movement possible at a joint or series of joints.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.3.1] Static flexibility is the range a joint can reach and hold at rest, such as a held split.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.3.2] Dynamic flexibility is the range used during movement, such as a hurdler's lead leg (the first leg to go over the hurdle) or a high kick in dance.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.3.3] It is important in gymnastics, dance, hurdles, goalkeeping and tennis, where the body must stretch to reach the ball.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.3.4] Benefits: better technique through a full range of movement, less risk of muscle strains (muscles overstretched or torn), and easier everyday movement.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.4.b] Body composition looks at what the body is made of rather than how much it weighs. It is the last of the five health-related fitness components.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.4.0] Definition: the relative proportions of fat mass and lean (fat-free) mass (muscle, bone and organs) in the body, usually given as a body-fat percentage (the share of total body weight that is fat).
[pe-performance-demands-s2.4.1] It is not the same as body weight: two people of the same weight can have very different body composition, because one may carry more fat and the other more muscle.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.4.2] Different activities favour different compositions: a low body-fat percentage suits distance running, while more lean mass suits a rugby forward.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.4.3] Somatotypes (body-shape types) are ectomorph (lean and linear), mesomorph (muscular) and endomorph (rounder, with more fat). Most people are a mix.
[pe-performance-demands-s2.4.4] Health benefit: a healthy body-fat percentage lowers the risk of type 2 diabetes (a condition affecting how the body handles blood sugar), heart disease and joint problems.
