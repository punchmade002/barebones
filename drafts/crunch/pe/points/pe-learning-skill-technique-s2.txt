TARGET pe-learning-skill-technique-s2: 2. Analysing skill: the biomechanical perspective
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.0.b] To analyse a skill from the biomechanical perspective you need two things: a science that explains how forces shape movement, and an agreed starting posture so that everyone describes movement the same way.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.0.0] Biomechanics is the study of the forces acting on and produced by the body and their effects on movement. A force is a push or pull, so this covers both what moves you and what you push against.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.0.1] In analysing skill, biomechanics is used to improve technique, to reduce the risk of injury and to inform the design of equipment.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.0.2] The anatomical position is the reference for describing movement: standing upright, facing forward, arms by the sides and palms facing forward. Every movement is described as a change from this position.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.0.3] A plane is a flat surface passing through the body in which a movement takes place. Think of an imaginary sheet of glass slicing through you.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.0.4] An axis is an imaginary line about which the body or a limb rotates, like the rod a wheel spins on. Each plane pairs with the axis at right angles to it.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.1.b] Planes and axes are matched to movements, so you first need the names of the basic joint actions (the ways a joint can move). Each name is paired below with a sporting example.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.1.0] Flexion: bending at a joint so the angle between the bones decreases, such as bending the elbow.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.1.1] Extension: straightening at a joint so the angle increases, such as straightening the knee to kick.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.1.2] Abduction: movement away from the midline of the body (the imaginary vertical line down the centre of the body), such as raising the arm sideways.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.1.3] Adduction: movement towards the midline, such as bringing the arm back to the side.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.1.4] Rotation: turning a body part around its long axis, such as turning the trunk (the torso) in a golf swing.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.1.5] Plantar flexion: pointing the toes, as in rising onto the toes. Dorsiflexion: pulling the toes up towards the shin, the opposite action.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.2.b] The body has three planes, and each one is paired with one axis at right angles to it. Each plane is linked to particular joint actions, which is how you work out which plane a movement happens in.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.2.0] Sagittal plane: divides the body into left and right sides. Its movements are flexion and extension. Its axis is the transverse axis (also called the frontal or medial-lateral axis), which runs from side to side.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.2.1] Sagittal plane examples: the leg action in running, a biceps curl, a squat, kicking a ball straight ahead, a forward roll and a front somersault. All of these bend and straighten joints in a forward-and-back direction.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.2.2] Frontal (coronal) plane: divides the body into front and back. Its movements are abduction, adduction and lateral flexion (bending sideways). Its axis is the sagittal axis (also called the antero-posterior axis), which runs from front to back.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.2.3] Frontal plane examples: a cartwheel, a star jump, a side-step and a side bend. Each moves the body or limbs out to the side or bends sideways.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.2.4] Transverse (horizontal) plane: divides the body into upper and lower halves. Its movement is rotation. Its axis is the longitudinal (vertical) axis, which runs from head to toe.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.2.5] Transverse plane examples: the turns of a discus or hammer thrower, a golf swing, a pirouette (a spin on one foot in dance) and a full twist in trampolining. Each is a turn about a vertical line.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.2.6] The pairs never change: sagittal plane with transverse axis; frontal plane with sagittal axis; transverse plane with longitudinal axis.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.3.b] Exam questions give you a movement and ask for its plane and axis. The method is to decide which joint action the movement is, then use the fixed pairing from the last block.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.3.0] Hammer throw: the thrower's turns in the circle are rotation, so they are in the transverse plane about the longitudinal axis.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.3.1] Discus: the spin that builds speed before release is also rotation in the transverse plane about the longitudinal axis. A pirouette in dance occurs in the same plane and axis because it is also a turn about a vertical line.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.3.2] Gymnastics: a forward roll is flexion of the spine and hips, so it is in the sagittal plane about the transverse axis. A cartwheel is in the frontal plane about the sagittal axis.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.3.3] To justify an answer, name the movement (flexion, abduction, rotation) and match it to its plane and axis.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.4.b] A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a fixed point. The class of a lever depends on which part is in the middle: 1st class fulcrum, 2nd class load, 3rd class effort (memory aid 1, 2, 3 = F, L, E).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.4.0] In the body the bone is the lever (the bar), the joint is the fulcrum (the pivot it turns about) and muscle contraction provides the effort (the force that does the moving).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.4.1] The load (the resistance) is the weight of the body part plus anything held or struck, such as a ball.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.4.2] To identify the class, ask which of the three parts sits between the other two: fulcrum in the middle is 1st class, load in the middle is 2nd class, effort in the middle is 3rd class.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.4.3] Most levers in the body are third class.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.4.4] Diagram: draw the bar, mark the fulcrum as a triangle, the effort as an arrow showing the muscle's pull, the load as an arrow showing the weight, and the direction of movement.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.b] Each class has standard body examples. Learn which part is the fulcrum, effort and load in each, because exams ask you to identify them and to name the joint action.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.0] First-class lever (fulcrum in the middle): nodding the head, with the neck joint as fulcrum, the neck muscles as effort and the weight of the head as load.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.1] First-class lever: extension of the elbow by the triceps (the muscle at the back of the upper arm), with the elbow as fulcrum, the triceps pulling behind it as effort, and the forearm, hand and ball as load.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.2] That first-class elbow extension is the arm action in a soccer throw-in or the follow-through of a basketball set shot.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.3] Second-class lever (load in the middle): plantar flexion of the ankle when rising onto the toes, with the ball of the foot as fulcrum and body weight through the ankle as load.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.4] In that second-class example the effort comes from the calf muscle (gastrocnemius) pulling on the heel through the Achilles tendon, the tendon at the back of the heel.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.5] Second-class sporting examples: the push-off in a sprint start and the take-off in a jump.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.6] Third-class lever (effort in the middle): elbow flexion by the biceps (the muscle at the front of the upper arm), with the elbow as fulcrum, the biceps attached just below it as effort and the forearm, hand and any weight as load, as in a biceps curl.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.5.7] Third-class lever: knee extension by the quadriceps (the muscle group at the front of the thigh) when kicking a ball, with the knee as fulcrum, the quadriceps pulling just below the knee as effort, and the lower leg, foot and ball as load.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.6.b] The class of a lever decides whether it favours force or speed. This comes from how far the effort and the load are from the fulcrum, and it explains why different skills use different levers.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.6.0] Effort arm: the distance from the fulcrum to the point where the effort is applied. Load arm: the distance from the fulcrum to the load.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.6.1] Mechanical advantage (effort arm longer than load arm, typical of second-class levers): a large load can be moved with a small effort, but only through a small range and at low speed.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.6.2] Mechanical disadvantage (load arm longer than effort arm, typical of third-class levers): more effort is needed, but the end of the lever moves through a large range at high speed, which suits throwing and striking.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.6.3] First-class levers can give either advantage or disadvantage, depending on where the fulcrum lies.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.6.4] Comparing second- and third-class levers: the second class has the load in the middle and favours force (rising onto the toes to push off); the third class has the effort in the middle and favours speed and range (the arm action in a throw).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.6.5] Lengthening the lever, with a straight arm or by adding a racket, hurley, bat or club, increases the speed at the end of the lever and so the speed of release or impact, but needs more effort.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s2.6.6] Using levers to improve a skill: a fuller arm extension at contact in a tennis serve, or at release in the javelin, lengthens the lever and adds speed to the ball or implement.
