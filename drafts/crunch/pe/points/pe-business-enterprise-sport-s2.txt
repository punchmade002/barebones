[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.b] Sport is a big business, and many different kinds of company make money from it. Each sector (a branch of the industry) earns in its own way, so knowing the sectors helps you place any business you investigate.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.0] Sportswear and equipment manufacturers and retailers make and sell kit, footwear and equipment, so every player and supporter is a potential customer.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.1] Media and broadcasters sell coverage of sport to audiences and buy the rights to show events (the legal permission to broadcast them).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.2] Stadiums and venues host matches, concerts and other events, and earn from ticketing (selling entry tickets) and hire (renting the venue out).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.3] Gyms and leisure centres sell memberships, classes and access to facilities, which means they earn from people who want to be active.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.4] Sports nutrition businesses manufacture and sell foods and supplements for athletes and active people.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.5] Event organisers plan and run competitions, races and festivals.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.6] Tourism and travel businesses carry and house visitors who travel to take part in or watch sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.7] Betting companies take bets on sporting events and sponsor (pay money to be linked with) teams and competitions.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.8] Sports clubs and NGBs (national governing bodies, the organisations that run a sport nationally) operate as businesses, earning income from members, events, sponsors and merchandise (branded goods for fans).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.b] A revenue stream is a way of bringing money in. Sport has several, and most businesses and organisations in it rely on a mix of them.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.0] Broadcasting rights are sold to television and online platforms, which pay for the right to show events to their audiences.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.1] Sponsorship is business money or products given to a team or event in return for association and exposure (the brand being seen by fans). There is more on this in Sponsorship and advertising.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.2] Ticketing and hospitality means sales of match and event tickets, and packages of food and seats for businesses and guests.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.3] Merchandising is the sale of branded kit, scarves and souvenirs, so fans pay to show their support.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.4] Membership and participation fees are regular payments by club members and people taking part.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.5] Facility hire means renting pitches, halls and pools to clubs and groups, so a facility earns even when no match is on.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.6] Events bring in income from hosting competitions, concerts and festivals.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.b] Enterprise is how new sport businesses begin. Someone spots something people want and builds a way to provide it.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.0] Enterprise means identifying a need in sport and meeting it with a product (something made), a service (something done for people) or an event.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.1] Examples include a coaching camp, a sports app or a fitness class.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.2] An enterprise plan weighs the target market, the value offered (what the customer gains), the costs, the expected revenue (money coming in) and the risk (the chance it loses money or fails).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.3] The target market is the group of customers the product is designed for, such as young players, parents or older adults, so the offer can be shaped to suit them.

CANDIDATES pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1: 1. Sponsorship and advertising
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.0.b] Sport is big business, and money flows into it in several different ways. Each way has its own name, and the exam expects you to define them separately.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.0.0] Sponsorship: a business provides money, products or services to a sport, team, event or performer in return for association (being linked with it in the public mind), exposure and commercial benefit (a gain in sales or profit).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.0.1] Title sponsorship: the sponsor's name is attached to a competition, such as the Guinness Six Nations, the men's rugby championship that Guinness has sponsored since 2019. It is also an example of alcohol sponsorship in sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.0.2] Other sponsorship forms: a logo on a team's jersey, and naming rights (the right to put the sponsor's name on a venue or competition).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.0.3] Endorsement: a performer is paid to publicly promote and use a product or brand (the name and identity a company sells under).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.0.4] Merchandising: selling branded goods, such as jerseys, scarves, equipment and souvenirs, connected with a sport, team, event or performer.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.0.5] Sport-related advertising: advertising that uses sport, sports settings or athletes to sell products, including products unrelated to sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.1.b] The three are easy to mix up because all involve a brand and sport. The difference is who has the relationship and who receives the income.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.1.0] Sponsorship is a business relationship with a sport, team or event, and the income goes to that organisation.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.1.1] Endorsement is a performer promoting a product, and the income goes to the performer.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.1.2] Merchandising is the sale of branded goods, and the income goes to the club, NGB (national governing body, the organisation that runs a sport nationally) or event that owns the brand.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.1.3] Endorsement example: a performer is paid to appear in an advert for a brand of running shoes they wear.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.1.4] Merchandising example: a club sells replica jerseys and scarves in its shop, and each sale brings income to the club.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.2.b] A business only sponsors sport if it gets something back. The benefits explain why companies pay, and why sport can rely on them.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.2.0] Brand awareness and exposure to a large audience, because crowds, television viewers and online followers all see the sponsor's name.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.2.1] A positive image through association with success and with health, because the brand is linked in people's minds with winning teams and fit athletes.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.2.2] Access to target markets (the groups of customers the business wants to reach), such as the young people and families who follow a sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.2.3] Hospitality for clients and staff at matches and events, such as tickets and entertainment, which builds business relationships.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.2.4] Higher sales of the sponsor's products, which is the final commercial benefit of the other four.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.b] Each type of commercial deal changes sport and the people in it, for better and for worse. Take them one at a time.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.0] Sponsorship funds grassroots programmes (sport for local clubs and beginners), facilities, coaching, events and broadcasting (getting matches onto television and online).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.1] Sponsorship allows elite athletes (those competing at the top level) to train full-time, because the money replaces income from another job.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.2] Endorsement brings large income to star performers and raises their profile.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.3] Endorsement obligations, such as personal appearances and filming, take time away from training.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.4] A performer's image is tied to the brand, so a problem with the brand can damage the performer's reputation, and the reverse.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.5] Merchandising brings major income to clubs and NGBs.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.6] Merchandising builds fan identity with a team, a sport or an event, because fans who wear the kit feel they belong to it.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.3.7] Replica kits (copies of the team's playing jersey) change often and are expensive for families.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.4.b] An NGB runs its sport nationally, organising competitions, development and the national teams. It needs money to do this, and sponsors supply a good deal of it.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.4.0] Income: sponsorship money pays for development programmes (bringing new players into the sport) and high-performance programmes (preparing top athletes for elite competition).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.4.1] Facilities: sponsor funds can build or improve facilities, so the NGB does not have to find that money elsewhere.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.4.2] Coach education: sponsor funds pay for the training of coaches, so standards of coaching can improve.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.4.3] Expertise: sponsors bring marketing expertise (skill in promoting and selling) that helps the NGB promote the sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.4.4] Recognition: the NGB's name and competitions gain public recognition through sponsors' advertising.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.5.b] Sponsorship money comes with strings. For an NGB the risks are mainly about depending on the sponsor and about who the sponsor is.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.5.0] Dependence: relying on one sponsor leaves the NGB exposed, because the whole budget rests on one company.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.5.1] Withdrawal: the NGB loses income if the sponsor withdraws, so programmes it paid for may have to stop.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.5.2] Influence: a sponsor may try to influence scheduling (when matches are played) and decisions.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.5.3] Reputation: an unsuitable sponsor can damage the NGB's reputation.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.5.4] Ethics: brands such as alcohol or gambling can conflict with the values the NGB promotes to young members.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.6.b] For an individual athlete, sponsorship can decide whether a sporting career is affordable. Answer these separately from the NGB and the sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.6.0] Income: sponsorship money lets the performer train full-time.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.6.1] Equipment and travel: sponsors may provide free equipment and travel, which removes costs the athlete would otherwise pay.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.6.2] Profile: a higher profile can open a career after sport, because the performer is already known to the public and to businesses.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.7.b] A sponsorship deal is a contract, and the performer has to keep their side of it. That creates several pressures.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.7.0] Obligations: contracts set appearances, filming and other demands on the performer's time.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.7.1] Pressure: the performer must perform and win to keep the deal.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.7.2] Injury or poor form: the deal can end after an injury or a run of poor results, because the sponsor's benefit depends on a successful athlete.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.7.3] Privacy: the performer gives up some privacy when their image is used commercially, because the sponsor wants them seen.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.7.4] Conduct: the performer must not do anything that damages the brand.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.7.5] Conflict: the performer's personal deal can clash with the sponsorship of their team, for example if the two sponsors are rivals.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.7.6] Unwanted products: the performer may be linked to a product they would not choose.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.8.b] Here the question is about the sport as a whole, not the NGB or any one athlete. Sponsorship raises a sport's profile, but it can also change it.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.8.0] Advantage, profile: sponsorship brings higher profile and more media coverage.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.8.1] Advantage, events: sponsors help to fund better events.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.8.2] Advantage, facilities: sponsors help to fund better facilities.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.8.3] Advantage, participation: a higher profile can attract more participants.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.8.4] Disadvantage, inequality: sponsors favour popular sports, men's sport and elite sport, so other sports struggle to attract money.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.8.5] Disadvantage, commercialisation (turning the sport into a product to be sold): changes are made to suit sponsors and television rather than the sport's own needs.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.8.6] Disadvantage, image: the sport's image is linked to unhealthy products.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.9.b] Advertisers use sport because young people watch it and admire its stars. They reach them through several routes.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.9.0] Television and online adverts featuring athletes.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.9.1] Social media and influencer content (posts by people with large online followings who promote products).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.9.2] Shirt and stadium advertising, which is seen by everyone at the ground and on television.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.9.3] Product placement in sport (a brand deliberately shown within the game or its coverage).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.9.4] Branded events and competitions.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.10.b] Sport-related advertising can send young people messages that support taking part in sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.10.0] Being active is enjoyable and achievable.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.10.1] Effort and perseverance pay off.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.10.2] Role models of diverse backgrounds and abilities show that sport is open to everyone.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.10.3] Healthy lifestyles are presented as normal.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.11.b] The same advertising can also harm young viewers, who are still forming their ideas about health, bodies and success.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.11.0] Sport is linked with unhealthy products such as sugary drinks, fast food, alcohol and gambling.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.11.1] Unrealistic body ideals are presented as the norm.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.11.2] Success appears to depend on expensive gear, which can make sport seem out of reach for families who cannot afford it.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.11.3] Winning at all costs is presented as the goal.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.11.4] Betting adverts shown during matches normalise gambling, meaning it comes to seem an ordinary part of watching sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.12.b] When an exam shows or describes an advert, work through the same five steps in order. They turn a vague impression into marked points.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.12.0] 1. Who is shown: the age, gender, body type and ability of the people in the advert.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.12.1] 2. What product is sold, and whether it is linked to health or to sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.12.2] 3. What message the advert gives about sport, bodies and success.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.12.3] 4. Whether that message is positive or negative for young viewers, and why.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.12.4] 5. The likely effect on young viewers: whether it encourages activity, unhealthy choices or unrealistic expectations.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.b] A code of practice is a set of rules a club agrees to follow when choosing sponsors. Each criterion below carries a justification, so the committee can explain its choice to parents and members.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.0] Criterion 1: no sponsors whose products are unsuitable for young people, such as alcohol, gambling, tobacco and vaping, or high-fat and high-sugar food and drink.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.1] Justification for criterion 1: a logo on a child's jersey is a constant endorsement to the child and everyone watching, so an unhealthy brand contradicts the club's health message.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.2] Criterion 2: the sponsor's values and conduct must match the club's code of ethics, child safeguarding (protecting children from harm), inclusion and fair play.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.3] Justification for criterion 2: a sponsor shown on the club's kit is associated with its values, so a sponsor whose conduct breaks them damages the club's credibility with young players and parents.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.4] Criterion 3: no use of children's images, names or data in marketing without parental consent. Justification: children cannot judge how a commercial company will use their image, so parents must decide.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.5] Criterion 4: sponsorship benefits the whole club fairly, including girls' and boys' teams and all age groups, not only elite teams. Justification: a deal that funds one team alone leaves other young members without coaching, facilities or equipment.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.6] Criterion 5: the sponsor has no say over team selection, coaching or club decisions. Justification: selection and coaching should reflect players' ability and development, not what suits a commercial partner.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.7] Criterion 6: a transparent written agreement, approved by the committee, with a fixed term and a review and exit clause (a condition allowing the club to leave the deal).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s1.13.8] Justification for criterion 6: a written, time-limited agreement lets the club check what it receives, review the deal and leave it if the sponsor no longer meets the criteria.
