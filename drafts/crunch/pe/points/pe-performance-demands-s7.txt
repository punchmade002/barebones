TARGET pe-performance-demands-s7: 7. Psychological preparation
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.b] Skill alone does not decide a performance; what the performer believes about their skill matters too. Confidence shapes how hard they try, what they attempt and how they cope under pressure.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.0] Confidence is a performer's belief in their ability to perform successfully. Self-efficacy is confidence in one specific situation, such as taking a penalty in a final, so a player can be confident in training yet doubtful in that moment.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.1] Albert Bandura's theory gives four sources of self-efficacy. The first is performance accomplishments (past success).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.2] The second source is vicarious experiences (watching similar others succeed), which tells the performer 'someone like me can do this'. The third is verbal persuasion (encouragement from others).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.3] The fourth source is emotional arousal: how the performer interprets their body's state, for example reading a racing heart as readiness rather than as fear.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.4] High confidence leads to more effort and persistence, and to willingness to attempt difficult skills and to take responsibility at key moments, because the performer expects to succeed.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.5] Low confidence leads to avoiding the ball or difficult moves, hesitation, and more anxiety, because the performer expects to fail. Over-confidence can also harm, causing complacency (relaxing too much) and poor preparation.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.0.6] Strategies to build confidence: set achievable goals so success builds up; recall or watch video of past successes; use positive self-talk; use imagery (picturing yourself performing well); watch demonstrations by similar performers; and receive specific praise from the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.b] Before anyone can understand anxiety they need to know about arousal, the body and mind's level of activation. Performers need some arousal to perform well, but the right amount depends on the task and the person.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.0] Arousal is the level of physical and mental activation or readiness, on a scale from deep sleep to extreme excitement. It is neutral: it is simply how switched on the performer is, not a good or bad feeling.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.1] The inverted-U hypothesis (based on the Yerkes-Dodson law) says performance improves as arousal rises to an optimal level, then declines if arousal keeps rising. Plotted on a graph this makes an upside-down U.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.2] Under-aroused, the performer is bored, slow to react and makes careless errors, because they are not alert enough for the task.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.3] Over-aroused, muscles tense, attention narrows, and decisions and co-ordination suffer, because the performer is overloaded.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.4] The optimal level is lower for complex and fine skills and for beginners, such as a golf putt or a beginner's handspring, because these need precise control and a lot of thinking.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.1.5] The optimal level is higher for simple and gross skills and for experts, such as a rugby tackle or a weightlift, because these need force and speed and the expert has them well learned.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.b] Anxiety is what arousal can turn into when a performer feels threatened. It has a mental side and a physical side, and each affects performance in its own way.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.0] Anxiety is a negative emotional state of worry and apprehension (a fearful sense that something will go wrong), linked to high arousal.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.1] Cognitive anxiety is the mental side: worry, negative thoughts and self-doubt, for example dwelling on missing a free.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.2] Somatic anxiety is the physical side: a raised heart rate, sweating, muscle tension and butterflies in the stomach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.3] State anxiety is anxiety felt in a particular situation, such as before a penalty. Trait anxiety is a person's general tendency to be anxious across situations, so a high-trait performer feels it more often.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.4] Effects on performance: attention is narrowed or distracted, muscles tense, and co-ordination and decision-making break down. A performer may choke under pressure (fail at a skill they can normally do).
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.5] Some anxiety can help if the performer interprets the feelings as readiness rather than threat, which is how nerves can become useful energy.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.6] Strategies: breathing control; progressive muscular relaxation (tensing and relaxing muscle groups in turn); positive self-talk and reframing nerves as excitement; imagery; a pre-performance routine; and simulating competition pressure in practice so it feels familiar.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.2.7] Match the strategy to the problem: relaxation and breathing lower somatic anxiety, while self-talk and reframing tackle the worry of cognitive anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.b] Motivation is what gets a performer to the training session and keeps them going when it is hard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.0] Motivation is the drive to begin, direct and keep going with an activity.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.1] Intrinsic motivation comes from within, such as enjoyment, mastery and satisfaction. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside, such as trophies, praise, selection and rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.2] Effects on practice: a motivated performer attends, works hard, keeps the quality of repetitions high when no one is watching and persists when progress is slow.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.3] Effects on performance: persistence when losing, effort to the final whistle, and willingness to train through difficult phases.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.4] Strategies: SMART goal setting; variety in training so it stays interesting; recording progress so improvement is visible; and specific praise and positive feedback.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.5] More strategies: give the performer choice and ownership, so they feel the training is theirs; set team targets; and use appropriate rewards.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.3.6] Relying too much on extrinsic rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, because the performer starts to train for the prize rather than the enjoyment. Rewards should therefore recognise effort and improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.b] Concentration means putting your attention on the right thing at the right time. Different moments in a game need different kinds of focus, which Nideffer's model describes.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.0] Concentration is the ability to focus attention on the relevant cues (the information that matters), ignore distractions, and keep that focus over time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.1] Robert Nideffer described attentional focus as broad or narrow and internal or external. Broad takes in lots of information; narrow takes in one thing; internal is inside the performer; external is the outside world.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.2] Broad-external is reading the whole game, such as a quarterback or midfielder scanning the field. Narrow-external is focusing on one cue, such as watching the ball onto the racket.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.3] Broad-internal is planning tactics or analysing performance in the mind. Narrow-internal is focusing on one's own body or thoughts, such as a pre-shot routine or a key cue word.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.4] Effects of lapses: errors, missed cues and poor decisions, especially late in a contest or during long waits between efforts, such as between attempts in a field event.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.5] Strategies: pre-performance routines; cue words (short trigger words); focusing on process goals (what to do, not the result); and imagery.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.4.6] Further strategies: thought stopping (recognising a negative thought and replacing it with a positive one); simulation training with crowd noise and distractions, so distractions lose their effect; and breaking a long event into short segments.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.b] Feedback is how a performer finds out what happened, and it drives improvement. Feedback is classified in several ways, and exam questions often ask you to identify the type.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.0] Feedback is information a performer receives about their performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.1] Intrinsic feedback comes from within, through the senses and the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic feedback), such as feeling a clean strike. Extrinsic feedback comes from outside, such as a coach, video or the scoreboard.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.2] Knowledge of results (KR) is information about the outcome, such as whether the shot went in or the time achieved.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.3] Knowledge of performance (KP) is information about the quality of the movement or technique, such as the elbow position at release. Do not swap KR and KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.4] Positive feedback tells the performer what went well and motivates. Negative feedback tells them what went wrong and helps correct it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.5] Concurrent feedback is given during the performance; terminal feedback is given after it.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.6] Beginners need frequent, simple, positive, extrinsic feedback and KR, because they cannot yet judge their own movement. Experienced performers rely more on intrinsic feedback and detailed KP.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.5.7] Good feedback is specific, timely and constructive. Too much feedback overloads the learner and creates dependence on the coach.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.b] Mental preparation is not only a pre-match job. Strategies are used at three stages, and each strategy has strengths and limits worth knowing.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.0] Before: goal setting for the performance; imagery of key moments; a pre-performance routine; relaxation and breathing; positive self-talk; and preparing equipment and a game plan, so nothing is left to worry about.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.1] During: cue words and self-talk; a refocusing routine after errors, such as a deep breath and a reset word; breathing control in breaks in play; and focusing on the process, one point at a time.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.2] After: reflection and performance review; constructive feedback; relaxation and recovery; and resetting goals. A poor result is reframed (viewed differently) as information for improvement.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.3] Evaluating imagery: it builds confidence and rehearses skills without physical fatigue, but it needs practice and a clear, controllable image to work.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.4] Evaluating goal setting: it directs effort and raises motivation, but unrealistic or outcome-only goals can increase anxiety.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.6.5] Evaluating relaxation techniques: they lower somatic anxiety quickly, but too much relaxation before a power event can leave the performer under-aroused.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.b] A personal action plan turns the psychological factors into a plan you could actually follow. It starts from the performer's own weaknesses and ends with a check on whether it worked.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.0] Step 1: identify the psychological factors to target from self-assessment, such as an anxiety questionnaire or a reflective diary.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.1] Step 2: set goals for those factors.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.2] Step 3: choose one or two strategies for before, during and after performance.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.3] Step 4: give a rationale for each strategy, explaining how it affects the targeted factor. A rationale is the reason the strategy was chosen.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.4] Step 5: say how progress will be monitored and reviewed, such as rating confidence after each match.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.5] Illustrative plan for a basketball free-throw shooter with high anxiety: before, breathing control and imagery of successful shots to lower somatic anxiety and build confidence.
[pe-performance-demands-s7.7.6] During, the same pre-shot routine and a cue word at every free throw to narrow focus. After, a review of video and a diary rating of anxiety to track progress.
