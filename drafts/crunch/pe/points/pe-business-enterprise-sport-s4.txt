TARGET pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4: 4. Tourism and sport
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.0.b] Sport can be used to sell things, and it can also draw people to a place. Sports marketing and sports tourism have different definitions, and sports tourists fall into three types according to why they travel.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.0.0] Sports marketing means using sport to promote and sell products, services or places. It also means promoting sport itself to attract participants, spectators, sponsors (businesses that pay to be linked with a sport) and broadcasters (television and media companies that show it).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.0.1] Sports tourism is travel to take part in or watch sport, or to visit sporting attractions. The sport is the reason for the trip, not an extra.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.0.2] An active sports tourist travels to take part, for example in golf, surfing, cycling, walking or running events.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.0.3] An event sports tourist travels to watch, for example international matches, golf tournaments or horse racing.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.0.4] A nostalgia sports tourist travels to visit stadiums, museums and halls of fame (places that honour great players and past achievements), so the draw is sporting history.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.1.b] One way sports marketing builds tourism is to market Ireland as a place to do a particular activity. Each activity is matched to a setting Ireland can offer.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.1.0] Golf: Ireland is marketed as a destination for golf, so visitors travel specifically to play.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.1.1] Surfing and water sports are marketed on the Wild Atlantic Way, a tourism route along Ireland's west coast, where the Atlantic provides the waves.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.1.2] Walking and cycling are marketed on greenways, which are off-road routes such as the Great Western Greenway in Co. Mayo and the Waterford Greenway.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.1.3] Visitors who come specifically for an activity stay longer and spend more, which is why marketing aims at them: a longer stay means more spending in the local area.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.2.b] Countries compete to host big events, and Ireland can bid for them (make a formal offer to host). A hosted event brings people and attention that marketing alone cannot.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.2.0] Bidding for and hosting major international events brings visiting teams, media and spectators to Ireland, all of whom spend money while they are here.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.2.1] Hosting an event shows Ireland to a worldwide television audience, which works as advertising for the country as a whole.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.2.2] The 2006 Ryder Cup (a golf contest between teams from Europe and the United States) was held at the K Club, Co. Kildare. The 2027 Ryder Cup is scheduled for Adare Manor, Co. Limerick.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.2.3] The Tour de France (the famous cycling race) began in Dublin in 1998.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.2.4] Six Nations matches (the annual rugby championship) are played at the Aviva Stadium, Dublin.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.2.5] American college football games have been played at the Aviva Stadium, Dublin.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.3.b] Not all tourism comes from one-off events. Yearly fixtures, dramatic settings, sporting history and famous names all keep Ireland in view.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.3.0] Annual events attract visitors every year, such as the Galway Races, an annual horse-racing festival in Galway, so the benefit repeats instead of happening once.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.3.1] The Irish Open, a professional golf championship held in Ireland, attracts visitors every year.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.3.2] Spectacle events in scenic locations market the landscape: the Red Bull Cliff Diving World Series has held events on Inis Mór in the Aran Islands, so the scenery is shown to viewers along with the sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.3.3] Sporting heritage attractions draw nostalgia tourists: the GAA Museum and stadium tours at Croke Park, Dublin.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.3.4] Famous athletes and teams are used in tourism campaigns to publicise Ireland, because people follow and trust sporting stars.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.b] Visitors who come for sport bring money, a better reputation and local energy. The benefits fall into three groups: economic, image and legacy, and community.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.0] Economic: visitor spending on accommodation, food, transport and retail (shops).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.1] Economic: jobs in tourism and hospitality (hotels, restaurants and similar services), created by that spending.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.2] Economic: taxes raised from visitor spending.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.3] Economic: benefits spread to rural and coastal areas outside the main tourist season, because activities such as surfing and walking happen outside cities and outside the summer peak.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.4] Image and legacy (what is left behind after an event): a positive international image for Ireland.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.5] Image and legacy: improved facilities and infrastructure that local people can use afterwards.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.6] Image and legacy: repeat visits by people who first came for a sporting event.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.4.7] Community: pride in hosting events; volunteering to run events; and increased local participation, inspired by events.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.b] Sports tourism is not all gain. Some costs fall on the public and on residents, and some things about Ireland limit which events it can host.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.0] There is a public cost of bidding, hosting and security, so taxpayers' money is spent before any benefit arrives.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.1] Money can leave the area through international companies, which means the profits of an event may not stay with local businesses.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.2] Congestion and overcrowding in host areas make travel and daily life harder.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.3] Rising prices and disruption for residents are a cost to the people who live in the host area.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.4] Environmental damage is caused by visitors.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.5] Facilities built for one event may be under-used afterwards, which wastes the money spent building them.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.6] Ireland's weather limits some events.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.5.7] Ireland has limited large venues, which restricts the size of some events, and transport links limit some events too.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.6.b] To discuss the potential of sports marketing for tourism, weigh the benefits against the costs and limits. The overall judgement is that the potential is high, but it depends on conditions.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.6.0] High potential for activity tourism, given Ireland's landscape and Irish sporting culture.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.6.1] Heritage tourism also has high potential, rooted in Irish sporting culture.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.6.2] The potential depends on investment in facilities and transport, because limited venues and transport links hold back some events.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s4.6.3] The benefits depend on being spread locally, beyond the venue and the event weekend, so that rural and coastal communities gain as well.

CANDIDATES pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2: 2. The business dimension
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.b] Sport is a big business, and many different kinds of company make money from it. Each sector (a branch of the industry) earns in its own way, so knowing the sectors helps you place any business you investigate.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.0] Sportswear and equipment manufacturers and retailers make and sell kit, footwear and equipment, so every player and supporter is a potential customer.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.1] Media and broadcasters sell coverage of sport to audiences and buy the rights to show events (the legal permission to broadcast them).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.2] Stadiums and venues host matches, concerts and other events, and earn from ticketing (selling entry tickets) and hire (renting the venue out).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.3] Gyms and leisure centres sell memberships, classes and access to facilities, which means they earn from people who want to be active.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.4] Sports nutrition businesses manufacture and sell foods and supplements for athletes and active people.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.5] Event organisers plan and run competitions, races and festivals.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.6] Tourism and travel businesses carry and house visitors who travel to take part in or watch sport.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.7] Betting companies take bets on sporting events and sponsor (pay money to be linked with) teams and competitions.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.0.8] Sports clubs and NGBs (national governing bodies, the organisations that run a sport nationally) operate as businesses, earning income from members, events, sponsors and merchandise (branded goods for fans).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.b] A revenue stream is a way of bringing money in. Sport has several, and most businesses and organisations in it rely on a mix of them.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.0] Broadcasting rights are sold to television and online platforms, which pay for the right to show events to their audiences.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.1] Sponsorship is business money or products given to a team or event in return for association and exposure (the brand being seen by fans). There is more on this in Sponsorship and advertising.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.2] Ticketing and hospitality means sales of match and event tickets, and packages of food and seats for businesses and guests.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.3] Merchandising is the sale of branded kit, scarves and souvenirs, so fans pay to show their support.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.4] Membership and participation fees are regular payments by club members and people taking part.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.5] Facility hire means renting pitches, halls and pools to clubs and groups, so a facility earns even when no match is on.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.1.6] Events bring in income from hosting competitions, concerts and festivals.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.b] Enterprise is how new sport businesses begin. Someone spots something people want and builds a way to provide it.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.0] Enterprise means identifying a need in sport and meeting it with a product (something made), a service (something done for people) or an event.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.1] Examples include a coaching camp, a sports app or a fitness class.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.2] An enterprise plan weighs the target market, the value offered (what the customer gains), the costs, the expected revenue (money coming in) and the risk (the chance it loses money or fails).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.2.3] The target market is the group of customers the product is designed for, such as young players, parents or older adults, so the offer can be shaped to suit them.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.3.b] When you investigate a business in sport, you work from a fixed set of questions and gather answers from real sources. The questions cover what the business does, how it earns, and whether its role in sport is good or bad.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.3.0] Question 1: what does the business sell, and to whom?

[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.3.5] Question 6: what ethical issues does it raise (questions of what is fair or right)?
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.3.6] Question 7: what challenges does it face?
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s2.3.7] Sources: the company's website and annual reports (yearly summaries a company publishes about its business), news articles, interviews, visits to the business and its social media.

CANDIDATES pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3: 3. Mass participation in sport
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.0.b] Some sporting events are built for the crowd taking part rather than for the winner. Knowing exactly what the term means is the starting point for the business and enterprise opportunities they create.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.0.0] Mass-participation sporting events are organised events in which large numbers of people of mixed ability take part at the same time, so beginners and experienced athletes share the same start line.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.0.1] The usual aim is personal challenge, health or charity, rather than winning, which is why finishing is the goal for most entrants.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.0.2] Types include city marathons (long-distance road races through a city), fun runs, parkrun (a free weekly timed run), cycling sportives (organised long-distance bike rides for all abilities), open-water swims, obstacle and colour runs, and triathlons (swim, cycle and run in one event).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.1.b] Ireland has well-known events that fit the definition. Naming real ones, with a fact about each, makes an answer specific.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.1.0] Dublin Marathon: a marathon (a long-distance road race) held in Dublin each year.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.1.1] Women's Mini Marathon: a women's mass-participation event in Dublin, so it is an example of a women-only format.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.1.2] parkrun: a free, weekly, timed 5 km event held on Saturday mornings, so anyone can take part at no cost.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.1.3] Great Limerick Run: a mass-participation running event in Limerick.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.2.b] The number of people entering these events has grown because several things have made taking part more appealing and easier. Each reason below is one cause you can use to explain the growth.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.2.0] Health and fitness awareness, promoted nationally, has encouraged more people to look for an activity to take up, and an organised event gives them one.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.2.1] Charity fundraising and personal challenge goals attract entrants, such as 'bucket list' events (events people want to complete at least once in their lives).
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.2.2] Social media, apps and wearable trackers (watches and devices that record your runs) make training and sharing results easy, so participants stay motivated and show others what they achieved.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.2.3] Inclusive formats widen who can enter: walk, jog or run options, shorter distances, women-only events and free weekly events.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.2.4] Sponsorship (companies paying to be linked with the event) and professional event organisers, who market events widely, make events better known and better run.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.2.5] Running and cycling clubs, couch-to-5k style programmes (plans that take a beginner from no running to a 5 km run) and workplace challenges give people a route into the events.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.b] A large crowd of participants and spectators is a market. For each opportunity, know what it is and who benefits.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.0] Entry fees: each participant pays to enter, which is income for the organiser and for charities.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.1] Title and partner sponsorship: a company's name is attached to the event (title sponsor) or it supports it as a partner, so brands gain exposure to thousands of participants and spectators.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.2] Merchandise and event products: branded T-shirts, medals and photographs are sold, so the organiser and the event brand benefit from sales.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.3] Retail: running shoes, clothing, watches, trackers and nutrition products are bought for training, so sports retailers benefit.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.4] Services: coaching, training plans and apps, physiotherapy, sports massage and timing companies (who record each runner's time) are needed, so these providers benefit.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.5] Local economy: hotels, restaurants, pubs, shops and transport are busy on event weekends with visitors from outside the area, so local businesses benefit.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.6] Media and advertising: live and online coverage carries sponsors' brands to viewers beyond the start line, so broadcasters and advertisers benefit.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.3.7] Charities raise large sums through participants' fundraising, because entrants ask friends, family and colleagues to sponsor them. Enterprise: new businesses are set up to organise events, sell event photography, provide timing chips (devices that record a runner's time) or run training groups, and their founders benefit.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.4.b] Events do more than make money. They also bring gains for the people taking part and the communities around them.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.4.0] Health: participants gain fitness and wellbeing from the training and from taking part.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.4.1] Goal-setting: a fixed event date gives participants a training target to work towards.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.4.2] Community spirit: people train, cheer and volunteer together.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.4.3] Inclusivity: events welcome people of mixed ability, so beginners are not shut out.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.4.4] Charity income: money is raised for good causes through fundraising.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.5.b] Large events also have downsides. These are the costs and problems that organisers and communities have to deal with.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.5.0] Road closures cause disruption to residents, because roads along the route are shut for the event.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.5.1] Policing and stewarding costs (stewards guide participants and manage the route) must be met, which adds to the cost of running the event.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.5.2] Litter and environmental impact, since thousands of people on a route leave waste behind.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.5.3] Rising entry fees exclude lower-income groups, which works against the inclusive aim of these events.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.5.4] Injury risk for participants who are unprepared, such as those who take on a distance without enough training.
[pe-business-enterprise-sport-s3.5.5] Events can be dominated by commercial interests rather than by participants, so the business side outweighs the participants' needs.
