TARGET pe-learning-skill-technique-s1: 1. Defining a skilled performance
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.0.b] Three terms are easy to mix up: skill, technique and skilled performance. Each describes a different thing, and the exam asks you to define them.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.0.0] Skill is a learned ability to bring about a predetermined result (one decided in advance) with maximum certainty and the minimum outlay of time and/or energy.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.0.1] Barbara Knapp (1963) defined skill as the learned ability to bring about pre-determined results with maximum certainty, often with the minimum outlay of time or energy or both.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.0.2] A skill is learned through practice and is goal-directed, which means the performer knows the result they want before they start.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.0.3] Technique is the way a skill is executed, meaning the specific movement pattern used. Two players can both have the skill of a basketball free throw (an uncontested shot at the basket) but use different techniques, such as a one-handed or two-handed release.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.0.4] The specification defines a skilled performance as one in which a sequence of movements is performed in a fluent (smooth) and controlled manner, the right options are selected, and the skills and techniques used fully reflect the performer's ability and experience.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.0.5] A skilled performance therefore includes decision-making, not only movement quality, because choosing the right option is part of the definition. A perfectly struck pass to a marked team-mate (one closely guarded by a defender) is not a skilled performance.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.b] You can recognise a skilled performance by a set of features. Each can be seen in a named skill such as the basketball jump shot (a shot taken while jumping), the Gaelic football solo (running while dropping the ball onto the foot and kicking it back to the hand) or the tennis serve.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.0] Consistent: the outcome can be repeated reliably, even under pressure late in a match. A skilled free-taker scores from similar positions again and again.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.1] Accurate and precise: the intended outcome is achieved, such as a tennis serve landing in the chosen corner of the service box.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.2] Fluent and well co-ordinated: movements flow smoothly from one phase to the next with good timing, such as the run-up, gather and release of a jump shot.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.3] Controlled: the performer is in control of the body and any implement (equipment such as a racket or hurley) throughout, such as keeping the ball close while soloing at speed.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.4] Efficient: no wasted movement or energy is used, which is called economy of movement. There is more on this in the notes on movement and quality perspectives.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.5] Aesthetically pleasing: the movement looks good to an observer, which matters most in judged activities such as gymnastics and dance.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.6] Adaptable: the right option is chosen and the skill is adjusted to the situation, using anticipation (predicting what will happen next) and reading of the play, such as switching from a shot to a pass when a defender closes in.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.7] Learned and goal-directed: the movement results from practice and has a clear intended outcome.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.1.8] A novice (beginner) shows the opposite features: movements are jerky and inconsistent, errors are frequent, and decisions are slow or wrong.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.2.b] Skill depends on something the performer already has before any practice: ability. Ability is a different thing from skill.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.2.0] Ability is an inherited (innate, meaning born with), stable and enduring trait that a person brings to the learning of skills. It is not how good someone is at a sport, because that is skill level.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.2.1] Abilities underpin skills (skills are built on them) and set a person's potential in an activity.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.2.2] Edwin Fleishman classified motor abilities (abilities used in movement) into two groups: perceptual-motor abilities, such as reaction time, co-ordination, balance and spatial awareness, and physical proficiency abilities, such as strength, flexibility and stamina.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.2.3] Each skill draws on several abilities. A tennis serve, for example, needs co-ordination, timing, power and flexibility.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.2.4] One ability supports many skills. Good hand-eye co-ordination, for example, helps with catching, striking a sliotar and returning a serve.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.3.b] Skill and ability are linked but they are not the same thing. Comparing them on origin, change and relationship gives a clear answer to the question of whether they are the same.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.3.0] Origin: skill is learned, whereas ability is largely inherited.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.3.1] Change: skill can develop quickly with practice, whereas ability is relatively stable and changes only slowly, influenced by genetics and early development.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.3.2] Relationship: skills are built on abilities, and ability decides how quickly and how far a skill can be developed.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.3.3] Example: a young basketball player with excellent co-ordination and speed (abilities) still has to practise to develop a reliable lay-up (a close-range shot played off the backboard, a skill).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.3.4] Natural ability alone is not enough to succeed. Without practice, coaching, feedback and psychological preparation, ability is never turned into a skilled performance.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.3.5] A less gifted performer who practises well and is well coached can outperform a more gifted performer who does not, because practice develops the skill that ability only makes possible.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.b] Skills can be sorted into types, and most skills sit somewhere between the two extremes of each type, so each is a continuum (a scale with two ends). The types decide which practice methods suit a skill.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.0] Open skill: performed in an unpredictable environment that the performer must react to, and usually externally paced, such as passing in a game.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.1] Closed skill: performed in a stable, predictable environment, usually self-paced, such as a basketball free throw or a gymnastics vault.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.2] Discrete skill: has a clear beginning and end, such as a penalty kick.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.3] Serial skill: a series of discrete elements linked in order, such as the hop, step and jump of a triple jump.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.4] Continuous skill: has no clear beginning or end, and each movement cycle runs into the next, such as running or swimming.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.5] Gross skill: uses large muscle groups and whole-body movement, such as a shot-put (throwing a heavy metal ball).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.6] Fine skill: uses small muscle groups for precise, delicate movement, such as a snooker shot or a dart throw.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.4.7] Self-paced skill: the performer decides when to start and how fast to go, such as a tennis serve. Externally paced skill: the timing is set by the environment or opponents, such as receiving a serve.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.5.b] Exam questions ask you to take one activity and explain why particular skills help performance. Basketball gives three clear examples, each linked to its benefit.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.5.0] Jump shot: scores points from mid-range and over a defender, so the team can score when the defence is set (organised and in position).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.5.1] Bounce pass: keeps possession by passing below a defender's reach to a team-mate cutting to the basket (running towards it to receive the ball).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s1.5.2] Defensive slide: lets a defender stay between the attacker and the basket without crossing the feet, preventing penetration (the attacker getting through to the basket).
