CANDIDATES pe-learning-skill-technique-s4: 4. Skill acquisition
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.b] A skill (a learned movement or action, such as a shot or a throw) is not mastered all at once. Fitts and Posner (1967) described three stages in order: cognitive, associative, then autonomous. What the performer does, how the movement looks and what the coach should do all change at each stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.0] Cognitive (beginner) stage: the performer forms a mental picture of the skill, usually from a demonstration, and works out what to do.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.1] Cognitive stage: movements are slow, jerky and inconsistent. Errors are frequent and large and the performer often cannot detect them, because full conscious attention (deliberate thinking) is on the movement.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.2] Cognitive stage coaching: simple, unopposed practice (no defender or opponent), clear demonstrations and frequent, specific feedback from the coach, because the performer cannot yet spot errors alone. Progress is rapid but fragile, meaning it is easily lost.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.3] Associative (intermediate) stage: the basic movement pattern is in place, so movements are smoother and more consistent. Errors are fewer and smaller, and the performer begins to detect some through the feel of the movement (kinaesthetic sense).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.4] Associative stage coaching: add pressure, speed and simple opposition, and make feedback more detailed. Most learners spend the longest time at this stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.5] Autonomous (expert) stage: the skill is performed automatically, consistently and fluently with little conscious attention, so attention is free for tactics, opponents and decisions.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.0.6] Autonomous stage coaching: the performer detects and corrects their own errors. Practice keeps the skill grooved (so well learned it repeats the same way) under competitive conditions, and feedback is fine-tuning.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.b] Exams ask you to describe one named skill at each stage. The basketball jump shot (shooting while jumping) shows how the same skill looks different as the learner improves.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.0] Cognitive: the feet are not set, the elbow flares out, the ball is pushed from the chest and the release point varies. The player can shoot only when stationary and unopposed.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.1] Associative: the set-up and release are mostly correct and the success rate rises, but the shot breaks down when a defender closes or the player is tired.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.2] Autonomous: the shot is released quickly and consistently off the dribble (while bouncing the ball) or the catch, under pressure, while the player reads the defence.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.1.3] Identifying a stage from evidence: an elite athlete winning national titles with a consistent technique is at the autonomous stage. Someone new to a sport who is inconsistent and needs instructions is at the cognitive stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.b] Learners improve faster when the coach gives them the right kind of help. Guidance can be visual, verbal, manual or mechanical, and it works best alongside feedback, mental rehearsal and motivation.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.0] Visual guidance and demonstration: accurate demonstrations, video and model performers (someone who performs the skill well) give the learner a clear picture, which matters most at the cognitive stage.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.1] Verbal guidance: short, clear key teaching points, because too much information overloads a beginner.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.2] Manual guidance: physically moving the learner through the movement, such as a coach guiding the arm through a swing.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.3] Mechanical guidance: equipment that supports the learner, such as a float in swimming or a harness on a trampoline.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.4] Manual and mechanical guidance build safety and confidence, but over-use creates dependence and the learner may not develop the feel of the movement.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.5] Feedback after attempts tells the learner what to change. There is more on this in Psychological preparation, which covers the types of feedback.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.6] Mental rehearsal (imagery): imagining performing the skill between physical attempts strengthens the mental picture.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.2.7] Motivation (the drive to keep trying), clear goals and practice matched to the learner's stage keep learning effective.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.b] A coach must decide whether to teach a skill as one piece or in sections. Whole practice, part practice, whole-part-whole and progressive part are the four ways of breaking a skill down.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.0] Whole practice: the skill is practised in its entirety. It suits skills that are fast and fluid or cannot easily be broken into parts, such as a golf swing or a javelin throw.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.1] Part practice: one sub-routine (a section of the skill) is practised in isolation, such as the ball toss in a tennis serve. It suits complex serial skills (skills made of several linked parts) and dangerous elements.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.2] Whole-part-whole: the learner tries the whole skill, a weak part is isolated and practised, then the whole skill is performed again.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.3.3] Progressive part (chaining): the first part is learned, then the second is added, and so on, such as the hop, step and jump of a triple jump.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.b] Practice can also be organised by how much rest it includes and by whether conditions stay the same. Massed or distributed is about rest, and fixed or variable is about conditions.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.0] Massed practice: continuous practice with little or no rest. It suits simple, closed (predictable, in a stable setting), discrete (a short skill with a clear start and end) skills and motivated, fit, experienced performers, such as repeated basketball free throws.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.1] Distributed practice: practice broken up by rest or other activities. It suits beginners and complex or tiring skills, and lowers fatigue and injury risk.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.2] Fixed practice: the skill repeated in unchanging conditions to groove the movement. It suits closed skills such as a free throw or a penalty kick.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.3] Variable practice: the skill practised in changing situations. It suits open skills (skills where the situation keeps changing) because it builds adaptability, such as passing under different levels of defensive pressure.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.4.4] The pairs are separate choices: whole or part (how the skill is broken down), massed or distributed (rest), fixed or variable (conditions).
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.b] Good practice follows a set of principles, which are the rules a coach uses to plan and judge a session. Exam answers use these seven.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.0] Specificity: practice reproduces the demands of the competitive situation, including the same skills, speeds, conditions and fatigue, so learning transfers to performance.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.1] Progression: practice moves from simple to complex, unopposed to opposed, slow to game speed and closed to open, raising the demand only when the previous level is secure.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.2] Appropriate distribution: the balance of practice and rest (massed or distributed) suits the learner and the skill.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.3] Variety: different drills and conditions keep motivation high and build adaptability.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.4] Quality of repetitions: correct technique is repeated, not just volume, and practice stops before fatigue breaks down technique.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.5] Clear goals and regular feedback direct each session.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.5.6] Practice is matched to the individual's stage of learning and ability, is safe and begins with a warm-up.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.b] A practice schedule applies the methods and principles to one skill. It plans a single session and then the weeks of work, and every choice is justified.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.0] Session structure: warm-up; introduction and demonstration with key teaching points; progressive drills from simple to complex; a conditioned game (a game with rules changed to focus on the skill) or competitive scenario; cool-down; review and reflection.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.1] Over several weeks: set the number of sessions per week and their length, raise the difficulty week by week, choose practice methods and record progress, such as a success rate out of ten attempts.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.2] Justify each choice by the principles and the type of skill: fixed, massed whole practice suits a closed free throw; variable practice suits an open pass.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.3] Illustrative six-week plan for a basketball lay-up (a shot taken close to the basket after running): weeks 1 and 2 standing footwork then one-dribble lay-ups unopposed; weeks 3 and 4 lay-ups from the wing at game speed off either hand.
[pe-learning-skill-technique-s4.6.4] Weeks 5 and 6 of the same illustrative lay-up plan: lay-ups against a passive then an active defender, with success recorded each session.
