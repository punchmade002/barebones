TARGET geo-option-geoecology-s1: 1. Soil composition and characteristics
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.b] Soil is the thin layer of loose material on the earth's surface in which plants grow. Its formation is called pedogenesis, and it starts with the rock or deposit the soil forms from, known as the parent material.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.0] Soil is made of four components: mineral matter (particles of weathered rock), organic matter (living and dead material), water and air. Every later block looks at how one of these shapes the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.1] Residual soils form from rock weathered in situ, meaning in place, directly beneath them, so the soil and the rock below are closely related.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.2] Transported soils form on redeposited weathered material that has been carried and laid down by ice, water, wind or volcanic activity, so they can differ from the rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.3] Transported parent materials include glacial till (boulder clay), fluvio-glacial sands and gravels (laid down by meltwater from ice), alluvium (river deposits), loess (wind-blown silt), wind-blown sand and volcanic ash.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.4] Weathering (the breakdown of rock at the surface) produces regolith, the loose layer that soil develops from. Freeze-thaw, carbonation and hydrolysis are among the processes involved. There is more on this in Weathering processes, in Rocks, Weathering and Mass Movement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.b] Named places make the residual and transported types concrete. Remember that the same area can look residual on a map of rock types but be transported in fact.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.0] Residual examples: the limestone of the Burren and the granite of the Wicklow Mountains, which gives acidic, sandy soils. Most Midland soils formed on boulder clay over the limestone, so they are transported, not residual.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.1] Transported examples: the boulder clay (till) plains of the Irish Midlands, and alluvium on the floodplain of the Shannon Callows, meadows beside the River Shannon that flood in winter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.2] Loess (wind-blown silt), called limon in France, covers plateaux of the Paris Basin, such as the Beauce. There is more on this in A Core European Region: the Paris Basin.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.b] Soils take time to develop, and in Ireland the clock was reset by the ice. This is why Irish soils are young and why they often do not match the rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.0] Most Irish soils formed on glacial deposits laid down during the last (Midlandian) glaciation, which ended about 10,000 years ago. There is more on this in Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.1] So most Irish soils have developed in about 10,000 years or less, since the ice melted, and they often differ from the solid rock beneath them because the glacial deposits came from elsewhere.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.b] A soil profile is a vertical section from the surface down to bedrock, divided into layers called horizons. A soil pit or road cutting shows it, and the horizons are named by letters.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.0] O horizon: organic litter (undecomposed plant and animal remains) and humus (the dark, decomposed organic material) on the surface.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.1] A horizon: topsoil, with mineral matter mixed with humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.2] B horizon: subsoil, which receives material washed down from above.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.3] C horizon: weathered parent material, the rock or deposit the soil forms from.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.4] R horizon: bedrock, the solid rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.5] There is more on the full profiles of named soils in Soil processes and the global pattern of soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.b] Soil is mostly mineral matter, with small shares of organic matter, water and air. The figures below are the textbook values for a good loam, a balanced, fertile soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.0] A typical good loam has about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water and 25% air, by volume.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.1] These proportions describe a well-structured loam. They are not fixed values for every soil, so don't present them as true of all soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.2] Water and air share the pore spaces (the gaps between particles), so when one rises the other falls. A wet soil is poorly aerated for this reason.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.3] Diagram: a pie chart of the four components.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.b] Mineral matter is the largest component, and the parent rock decides what kind of mineral matter a soil gets. It is the first of the four components to know for an essay on soil development.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.0] Mineral matter is particles of weathered rock, from stones down to clay. It forms the soil's skeleton and decides its texture (the mix of particle sizes).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.1] As it weathers, mineral matter releases nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium, which plants then take up through their roots.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.2] Parent rock controls the result: limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils (bases are the chemicals that neutralise acid).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.3] Granite gives sandy, acidic soils with quartz grains and clay from weathered feldspar.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.4] Sandstone gives sandy, acidic, free-draining soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.5] Clay particles are the chemically active part of the mineral matter, because they hold on to nutrients and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.b] Organic matter is a small share of the soil by volume, but it gives soil its dark colour, its crumbly structure and much of its fertility. The key is the difference between litter and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.0] Organic matter is living organisms (bacteria, fungi, earthworms, insects and roots) plus dead plant and animal remains.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.1] Litter is undecomposed material. Decomposers (organisms that feed on dead material) break it down into humus, a dark, sticky, jelly-like substance, which is the end product of decomposition.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.2] Humus darkens the soil, binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients, releases nutrients slowly, and is the main store of nitrogen in the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.3] Earthworms mix and aerate the soil, drag litter down into it, and their casts (droppings) improve structure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.4] Charles Darwin's last book (1881), The Formation of Vegetable Mould through the Action of Worms, showed how earthworms mix and enrich soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.5] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (bacteria that turn nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use), such as those in the root nodules of legumes, add nitrogen. Clover is the standard example of a legume in Irish grassland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.b] Humus comes in different forms depending on the vegetation above and the organisms below. The forms are named mull, moder and mor, and each is linked to a soil type.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.0] Mull humus is mild, crumbly, well decomposed and well mixed by earthworms. It is slightly acidic to neutral and forms under deciduous woodland and grassland, giving brown earths.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.1] Mor humus is acidic, fibrous and poorly decomposed, with few earthworms because the acidity does not suit them. It forms under coniferous forest and heath, giving podzols.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.2] Moder is intermediate between mull and mor.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.b] Water enters the soil as precipitation and is held in its pores. It exists in three forms, and only some of it is any use to plants.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.0] Water dissolves nutrients to form the soil solution, from which roots absorb them.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.1] Gravitational water drains down through large pores after rain and carries nutrients away. This is the cause of leaching (the washing of nutrients down out of the soil).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.2] Capillary water is held in small pores by surface tension. It is available to plants and can move up as well as down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.3] Hygroscopic water is a thin film bound tightly to particles, so plants cannot use it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.4] Field capacity is the water a soil holds after gravitational water has drained away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.5] Wilting point is reached when only water that plants cannot extract is left, so the plants wilt.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.b] Which way water moves depends on whether rain or evaporation wins. This decides what the soil loses or collects, and it leads into the soil processes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.0] Where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration (water lost by evaporation and plant transpiration), water moves down and leaches the soil. This is the situation in Ireland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.1] Where evaporation exceeds precipitation, capillary action raises water to the surface and leaves salts and lime there, as in deserts and grasslands.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.2] Too much water fills all the pores and causes waterlogging, which leads to gleying (the grey, rusty-mottled soil of waterlogged ground). There is more on this in Soil processes and the global pattern of soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.b] Air fills whatever pore space water does not. Living things in the soil need it, and whether it is plentiful or scarce changes how the soil forms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.0] Air fills the pores not occupied by water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.1] Soil air supplies oxygen for root respiration and for the aerobic organisms (those that need oxygen) that decompose litter, and nitrogen for nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.2] Soil air is richer in carbon dioxide than the atmosphere. This forms weak carbonic acid in soil water, which aids chemical weathering.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.3] Poorly aerated (anaerobic, meaning without oxygen) soils decompose slowly, so organic matter builds up as peat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.4] In poorly aerated soils iron is reduced (it gains electrons and changes chemical form), giving grey colours.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.b] The four components do not act separately: each changes how the others behave. Showing this interaction is part of a full answer on soil development.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.0] Humus improves crumb structure, which opens pores for both air and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.1] Clay holds water but reduces air, because its tiny pores fill with water and leave little room for air.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.2] Organisms need both air and water to work, so a soil that is too wet or too dry supports less life and less decomposition.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.b] Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay in a soil. It is set by the size of the mineral particles, so it is one of the most basic characteristics of a soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.0] Sand has particles 2 to 0.05 mm across; silt 0.05 to 0.002 mm; clay under 0.002 mm. Particles over 2 mm are gravel or stones. Some textbooks put the sand and silt limit at 0.06 mm, which is also acceptable.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.1] Clay is the smallest particle and sand the largest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.2] Field test by feel: sand is gritty, silt is smooth and silky, and clay is sticky and can be rolled into a ribbon when moist.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.3] Texture is shown on a soil texture triangle, a triangular graph. There is more on this method in Figure interpretation, in Tables, Graphs and Data Skills.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.b] Particle size decides how water, air and nutrients move through a soil and how easy it is to farm. Each texture has strengths and weaknesses, and loam balances them.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.0] Sandy soils are light, free-draining, well aerated, warm quickly in spring and are easy to plough. They hold little water and few nutrients, and they are easily leached.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.1] Clay soils are heavy and hold water and nutrients. They drain slowly, become waterlogged and cold, crack and harden when dry, and are hard to work.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.2] A loam is a balanced mix, roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay. It combines good drainage with good water and nutrient retention, and is the best for farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.3] Silty soils are fertile but form a surface crust and erode easily.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.b] Structure is the way particles are grouped into clumps called peds. Two soils with the same texture can behave very differently if their peds differ.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.0] Crumb: small rounded peds, typical of humus-rich topsoil, and the best structure for farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.1] Granular: a further type of ped structure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.2] Platy: flat, horizontal plates that hinder drainage and root growth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.3] Blocky: angular cubes, common in clay subsoils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.4] Prismatic or columnar: vertical columns, found in some clay subsoils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.5] Structureless soils are single-grain (loose sand) or massive (solid clay), because their particles are not grouped into peds.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.b] Structure matters because it sets the pore spaces that water, air and roots use. It can be built up by natural agents and destroyed by pressure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.0] Structure controls the size and number of pores, so it decides drainage, aeration and how easily roots penetrate.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.1] Humus, clay, earthworms, roots and freeze-thaw build peds.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.2] Compaction (squashing the soil together) by heavy machinery, and trampling by livestock on wet ground (called poaching in Ireland), destroy peds and so close up the pores.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.b] Colour is a clue to a soil's composition and conditions, so reading it tells you what the soil contains and how well drained it is. Each colour below is linked to a cause and a named soil type.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.0] Black or dark brown: rich in humus, as in chernozems and peat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.1] Brown: humus plus iron compounds, as in brown earths.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.2] Red, orange or yellow: iron oxides in a well-aerated soil, as in tropical latosols and terra rossa.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.3] Ash-grey or bleached: heavily leached (washed of its nutrients and colouring), as in the E horizon of a podzol.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.4] Grey or blue-grey with orange (rusty) mottles: waterlogged, with reduced iron, as in gley.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.5] White: lime or salt crusts, as in desert soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.6] Dark soils absorb more heat, so colour also affects soil temperature.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.b] How much water a soil can hold, and how fast it passes through, depends on its other characteristics. Two similar-sounding terms, porosity and permeability, must be kept apart.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.0] Water retention depends on texture (clay retains water, sand drains), structure and organic content. Humus holds several times its own weight of water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.1] Porosity is how much pore space a soil has. Permeability is how easily water passes through it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.2] Clay soils have high porosity but low permeability, because their pores are tiny, so they drain slowly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.b] Organic content is the amount of humus in a soil. It varies a lot between soil types and affects several other characteristics.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.0] It is high in chernozems and peat, moderate in brown earths, and low in desert soils, which have little vegetation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.1] Tropical latosols are surprisingly low in humus, because litter decays so fast that little humus builds up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.2] Organic content affects colour, structure, water retention and fertility.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.b] pH tells you how acidic or alkaline a soil is, which affects which organisms and crops can live in it. It is shaped by climate, parent rock and vegetation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.0] pH is the measure of acidity or alkalinity, on a scale of 0 to 14, with 7 neutral.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.1] Soils become acidic where heavy rain leaches out bases, and where acid litter such as conifer needles or heather builds up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.2] Soils are alkaline on limestone and chalk and in dry climates.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.3] Most crops and earthworms prefer slightly acidic to neutral soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.4] Teagasc, the Irish agriculture and food development authority, advises a soil pH of about 6.3 for grassland and about 6.5 for tillage crops. Liming corrects lower values.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.5] Farmers spread lime, which is ground limestone, to raise pH.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.b] A fertile soil holds nutrients in a form that roots can reach and rain cannot wash away. This depends on tiny charged particles in the clay and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.0] Clay particles and humus form the clay-humus complex. Its tiny, negatively charged particles, called colloids, hold positively charged nutrient ions (charged atoms) such as calcium, magnesium and potassium.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.1] Held nutrients are not washed away, and they are exchanged with roots, so plants can take them up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.2] Base-rich soils are fertile; base-poor, leached soils are not.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.3] NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium) are the main nutrients farmers add.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.b] The characteristics above work together to decide which soil processes dominate and which soil forms. Each link below is one step of the argument an essay needs.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.0] Texture and structure control drainage, so they decide whether leaching, gleying or capillary rise dominates.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.1] pH controls which organisms live in the soil, so it decides whether mull or mor humus forms and how fast humification (the breakdown of litter into humus) happens.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.2] Organic content controls structure and fertility, so it decides how well the soil supports the vegetation that feeds it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.3] Water retention and aeration together decide whether decomposition is aerobic, as in fertile brown soils, or anaerobic, as in peat and gley.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.4] Use brown earth, podzol, gley, latosol and chernozem as illustrations by name.

CANDIDATES geo-rivers-s1: 1. Fluvial processes, patterns and associated landforms
[geo-rivers-s1.0.b] Before looking at how rivers shape the land, you need the vocabulary for the parts of a river system. These words are used to label maps and diagrams.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.0] Source: where the river rises, meaning where it begins. The River Shannon, Ireland's longest river, rises at the Shannon Pot, Co. Cavan.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.1] Mouth: where the river ends by entering the sea, a lake or another river, so it is the opposite end from the source.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.2] Tributary: a smaller river that flows into a larger river, so it adds its water to the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.3] Confluence: the point where a tributary joins the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.4] Drainage basin (catchment): the whole area of land drained by a river and its tributaries, so all rain falling in it ends up in that river system.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.5] Watershed: the high ground that separates one drainage basin from the next, so water on either side flows to a different river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.6] Distributary: a channel that leaves the main river and flows away from it, found on deltas (fan-shaped landforms of deposited material at a river mouth).
[geo-rivers-s1.0.7] Estuary: the tidal lower course of a river, where fresh water meets salt water. The Shannon enters the Atlantic through the Shannon Estuary below Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.b] The long profile is the smooth, concave slope of a river from its source to its mouth. Because the slope changes along the way, the river is divided into three courses, and each course has its own landforms.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.0] The gradient (steepness of the river's slope) is steep near the source and gentle near the mouth, which is what makes the profile concave.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.1] Upper (youthful) course: steep, with V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs (ridges that overlap like the teeth of a zip), waterfalls, rapids and potholes.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.2] Middle (mature) course: meanders (looping bends) develop as the valley widens, because the gentler gradient lets the river swing from side to side.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.3] Lower (old) course: a wide floodplain (flat land beside the river), levees (natural banks built up beside the channel), meanders with oxbow lakes (cut-off loops), and a delta or estuary at the mouth.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.b] A river can only erode or carry material if it has energy. How much it has, and where it uses it, explains why the upper course looks so different from the lower course.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.0] A river's energy depends on its volume (discharge, the amount of water flowing past a point) and its gradient.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.1] Energy left after overcoming friction with the bed and banks is used for erosion (wearing away the land) and transport (carrying material, called the load).
[geo-rivers-s1.2.2] Volume increases downstream because tributaries keep joining the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.3] Vertical erosion (deepening the bed) dominates in the upper course, so valleys there are cut downwards.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.4] Lateral erosion (widening the valley sideways) dominates in the middle and lower courses, so the valley floor there becomes wide.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.5] Headward erosion lengthens a valley at its source, cutting back upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.b] Rivers wear away their bed and banks in four ways. Each works differently, and each is a named process you may be asked to explain.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.0] Hydraulic action: the force of moving water dislodges loose material and forces air into cracks in the bed and banks, widening them until rock breaks away.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.1] Abrasion (corrasion): the load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks like sandpaper. Pebbles swirled in eddies (small whirlpools) drill potholes into the bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.2] Attrition: particles of the load collide with each other and with the bed, so they become smaller and more rounded downstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.3] Solution (corrosion): slightly acidic water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone, carrying it away in solution (dissolved in the water).
[geo-rivers-s1.4.b] Once material has been eroded, the river carries it downstream. The method depends on the size of the particles and the speed of the flow.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.0] Traction: the largest boulders are rolled along the bed, mainly during floods when the flow is strongest.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.1] Saltation: pebbles and sand are too heavy to stay in the flow, so they bounce along the bed in a series of hops.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.2] Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried within the flow, giving the water its brown colour.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.3] Solution: dissolved minerals are carried invisibly in the water.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.4] Competence: the largest particle a river can carry. It rises sharply in flood because the faster, bigger flow can move larger particles.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.5] Capacity: the total load a river can carry. It also rises sharply in flood, so a flooded river carries far more material overall.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.b] A river drops its load when it no longer has the energy to carry it. Understanding why it drops it, and in what order, explains the shape of most depositional landforms.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.0] Deposition (dropping of the load) happens when velocity (speed of flow) falls: on entering a lake or the sea, on the inside of a bend, when floodwater recedes, or on a gentler gradient.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.1] Deposition also happens when the load becomes too great for the river's energy to carry, so the excess is dropped even if the flow has not slowed.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.2] Heavier material is deposited first and the finest last, so deposits are sorted by size. This contrasts with glacial till (material dropped by ice), which is unsorted and angular.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.b] A drainage pattern is the arrangement of a river and its tributaries, shaped by the underlying rock and relief (the height and shape of the land). Each pattern tells you something about the rock or land beneath it.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.0] Dendritic: tree-like, with tributaries joining at acute angles (sharp angles); forms on rock of uniform resistance, so the rivers have no reason to favour any direction.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.1] Trellised: tributaries meet the main rivers at right angles; forms where bands of hard and soft rock alternate. In the ridge-and-valley landscape of Munster, rivers follow the soft limestone valleys and tributaries flow off the sandstone ridges at right angles.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.2] Radial: rivers flow outward from a central high point like the spokes of a wheel, because the ground slopes away in every direction from the high point.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.3] Deranged: no clear pattern; forms on poorly drained glacial deposits (material left by ice), e.g. the drumlin belt of Co. Cavan, with many small lakes and marshes between the drumlins.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.4] Centripetal: rivers flow inward to a central lake or basin, e.g. Lough Neagh, into which rivers drain from all sides.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.5] On an Ordnance Survey map, dendritic streams branch like a tree at sharp angles, trellised streams run parallel with tributaries meeting them at right angles, radial streams spread out from a high point, and centripetal streams converge on a lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.b] In the upper course the river is steep and powerful, so it cuts downwards into its bed. This shapes a narrow valley with a V-shaped cross-section.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.0] 1. The steep gradient gives the river high energy, so it erodes vertically by hydraulic action and abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.1] 2. Weathering (breakdown of rock in place) and mass movement (downhill movement of loose material) loosen the valley sides and move material into the channel, widening the top of the valley.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.2] 3. The result is a narrow floor and steep sides in a V-shape, because the river cuts down faster than it widens the valley.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.3] 4. The river swings around areas of harder rock, leaving spurs (ridges of land) that interlock like the teeth of a zip.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.4] Interlocking spurs are fluvial (made by rivers) and are not to be confused with truncated spurs, which are glacial.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.5] On an Ordnance Survey map, the contours (lines joining points of equal height) form a V that points upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.6] Example: the upper course of the River Liffey in the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.7] Diagram: cross-section of a V-shaped valley; plan view of interlocking spurs with the river winding between them.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.b] A waterfall forms where a river crosses rock of different hardness. Two erosion processes, hydraulic action and abrasion, do the work in the undercutting and plunge-pool stages.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.0] 1. A waterfall forms where a band of resistant rock (rock that wears away slowly) lies over less resistant rock.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.1] 2. Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft rock below faster, undercutting the hard rock and leaving an overhang.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.2] 3. Falling water and its load scour a deep plunge pool at the base by hydraulic action and abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.3] 4. The unsupported overhang collapses, and the waterfall retreats upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.4] 5. Repeated retreat leaves a steep-sided gorge downstream of the falls, because the river has cut back through the rock the falls once occupied.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.5] Example: Niagara Falls, USA-Canada border, with a resistant cap of dolomite over weaker shale; the retreat of the falls has cut the Niagara Gorge below them.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.6] Irish waterfalls: Powerscourt Waterfall, River Dargle, Co. Wicklow, Ireland's highest waterfall at about 121 m; and Torc Waterfall, near Killarney, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.7] Diagram: cross-section showing hard rock cap, soft rock beneath, overhang, plunge pool and direction of retreat.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.b] Rapids and potholes are smaller features of the steep upper course, both made by erosion of the river bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.0] 1. Rapids form where alternating bands of hard and soft rock lie across the river bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.1] 2. Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft bands faster, leaving the hard bands standing as steps.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.2] 3. Fast, turbulent water tumbles over the steps, so the surface is broken and white, forming rapids.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.3] Potholes: hollows drilled into the bed by pebbles swirled in eddies, the grinding action of abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.b] A meander is a looping bend in a river. It is formed by both erosion and deposition, so it is classified under both.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.0] 1. Water flows fastest on the outside of a bend, where it erodes the bank by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a steep river cliff.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.1] 2. Water is slowest on the inside of the bend, where it deposits sand and gravel, forming a gently sloping point bar (slip-off slope).
[geo-rivers-s1.10.2] 3. Helicoidal (corkscrew-shaped) flow carries eroded material from the outer bank to the inner bank of the next bend.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.3] 4. Continued lateral erosion makes the bends more pronounced, so that only a narrow neck of land eventually separates them.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.4] Example: meanders on the River Shannon's floodplain, the Shannon Callows, between Athlone and Portumna.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.5] Diagram: plan view of a meander with the fastest-flow line, river cliff and point bar.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.b] If meanders keep growing, the loops can eventually be cut off from the river. This leaves a lake that later disappears.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.0] 1. During a flood the river cuts through the neck of a meander and takes the straighter, shorter course.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.1] 2. Deposition seals off the old bend, so the water left behind forms a crescent-shaped oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.2] 3. The oxbow lake slowly fills with silt and becomes a meander scar (a dry, curved hollow where the loop used to be).
[geo-rivers-s1.11.3] Example: oxbow lakes on the lower Mississippi, USA.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.4] Diagram: plan sequence showing the cut-off and the formation of the oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.b] Floodplains and levees are built by deposition during floods. They are among the main depositional landforms of the lower course.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.0] Lateral erosion by migrating meanders widens the valley floor, so there is a broad flat area for floodwater to spread over.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.1] When the river floods, it spreads across the valley, loses velocity and deposits a layer of fine silt called alluvium.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.2] Repeated floods build a wide, flat, fertile floodplain; on an Ordnance Survey map, widely spaced contours show it. Example: the Shannon Callows, a wide floodplain of seasonally flooded meadows.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.3] Levees: the coarsest material is dropped first, right beside the channel, because the water slows as soon as it leaves it. Each flood therefore raises the banks a little.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.4] Over time these build into natural embankments called levees, and the river bed itself may rise above the level of the floodplain.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.5] Example: levees along the lower Mississippi, USA, which have been artificially raised for flood control.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.6] Diagram: cross-section of a floodplain showing alluvium layers, levees and the raised channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.b] A delta is a landform of deposition at a river mouth. It builds up when the river drops its load faster than the sea or lake can remove it.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.0] A delta forms where a river carrying a heavy load enters a sea or lake and its velocity drops sharply.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.1] In salt water, fine clay particles clump together (flocculation) and sink faster.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.2] Deposits build up until they block the channel, forcing the river to split into smaller channels called distributaries.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.3] Conditions needed: a large load, and calm water with a small tidal range and weak currents, so that deposits are not removed.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.b] A delta is built up in layers, and deltas take different shapes depending on how the deposits spread. You should know the layers and the named types.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.0] Topset beds: flat, coarser deposits laid down on top of the delta, because the heavier material drops first.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.1] Foreset beds: steeply sloping deposits at the front of the delta, built out into the water so the delta grows forward.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.2] Bottomset beds: fine, flat layers laid down beyond the front of the delta, because the finest material is carried furthest.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.3] Arcuate (fan-shaped) delta, e.g. the Nile delta, Egypt.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.4] Bird's-foot delta: long fingers of deposits along distributaries, e.g. the Mississippi delta, USA.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.5] Estuarine delta: fills a drowned river mouth (a river mouth flooded by the sea).
[geo-rivers-s1.14.6] Lake delta, e.g. the delta between the Upper and Lower Lakes at Glendalough, Co. Wicklow, which divided what was once a single lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.7] Diagram: plan view of a delta with distributaries; cross-section showing topset, foreset and bottomset beds.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.b] Two more depositional features occur near the mouth, or where the load is large: the estuary and the braided channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.0] Estuary: in the tidal river mouth, fresh and salt water meet, and deposition builds mudflats and salt marsh.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.1] Example: the Shannon Estuary below Limerick, where the Shannon enters the Atlantic.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.2] Braided channel: the river splits into several channels around islands of deposited sand and gravel, called eyots or braid bars.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.3] Braided channels form where the load is large and discharge varies, so the river cannot carry all its sediment and drops it as islands in the channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.b] Every fluvial landform is formed by erosion, by deposition, or by both. Sorting them this way is a skill that exams test.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.0] Erosion: V-shaped valley, interlocking spurs, waterfall, gorge, potholes, rapids.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.1] Erosion and deposition: meander, oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.2] Deposition: floodplain, levee, delta, point bar, estuary mudflats.
