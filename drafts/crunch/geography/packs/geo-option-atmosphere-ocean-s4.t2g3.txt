[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.b] An anticyclone is the opposite of a depression: a large area of sinking air and high pressure. Its sinking air explains its settled weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.0] An anticyclone is an area of high pressure with widely spaced, closed isobars. It has light winds blowing clockwise outwards in the northern hemisphere, and no fronts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.1] Anticyclones are large and slow-moving, so they can last for days or weeks.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.2] Air converging high in the atmosphere sinks, a process called subsidence. As it sinks it is compressed and warms adiabatically (by compression alone, without taking in heat), so its relative humidity falls and clouds evaporate.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.3] Dynamic (warm) highs form from sinking air in the general circulation. The Azores High is an example, and it often extends a ridge (a long narrow extension of high pressure) over Ireland in summer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.4] Thermal (cold) highs form when the ground cools intensely in winter. The Siberian High, a cold anticyclone over Central Asia, is an example.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.5] A temperature inversion (a layer where temperature rises with height instead of falling) often forms in the sinking air, trapping moisture and pollution beneath it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.b] Sinking, drying air means few clouds. In summer this gives hot, bright weather.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.2] Occasional thunderstorms, triggered by the heat, at the edges of the high.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.3] Sea breezes on coasts by day, because the land heats faster than the sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.4] Prolonged summer highs cause heatwaves and drought, because there is little cloud or rain for days.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.23.0] Clear nights let the ground lose heat rapidly. This causes hard frost and freezing fog in valleys (radiation fog, fog formed when the ground cools the air above it), which may persist all day.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.b] The two systems are opposites in almost every respect. Set them side by side, point for point.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.0] Pressure: a depression has low pressure at its centre and an anticyclone has high pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.1] Isobars: closely spaced in a depression, widely spaced in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.2] Wind: strong in a depression and light in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.3] Air movement: air rises in a depression and sinks in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.4] Cloud and rain: cloud and rain in a depression, clear skies in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.5] Typical weather: changeable, windy and wet in a depression, and settled, dry and calm in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.6] Movement: depressions travel from west to east, while anticyclones are slow-moving.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.b] Land and sea heat up at different rates. By day this difference sets up a small circulation that blows from the sea onto the land.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.0] Land heats faster than the sea under sunshine. Air over the land warms, expands and rises, creating lower pressure over the land, while the cooler sea has relatively higher pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.1] Cool air flows onshore from the sea to the land, because air moves towards the lower pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.2] The air that rose returns seaward at height and sinks over the sea, completing a circulation cell.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.3] Sea breezes are strongest on hot, calm, sunny summer afternoons, often under an anticyclone, and in the tropics they may blow most days.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.b] After dark the heating difference reverses, so the breeze reverses with it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.0] After sunset, land loses heat faster than the sea. Air over the land cools and becomes denser, creating higher pressure over the land and lower pressure over the warmer sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.1] Air flows offshore, from the land to the sea, because air moves towards the lower pressure over the sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.2] The land breeze is weaker than the sea breeze, because the night-time temperature difference is smaller.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.b] These local winds change the temperature, humidity and cloud along a coast, and people have used them for centuries.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.0] Sea breezes lower coastal temperatures in the afternoon and raise humidity.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.1] A sea-breeze front (the line where the cool sea air meets the warmer land air) can trigger a line of cumulus or showers inland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.2] Sea breezes can carry sea fog onshore and trap urban pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.3] Land breezes help clear coastal fog, because they blow the fog out to sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.4] Sailing fishermen traditionally went out at dawn with the land breeze and came back in the afternoon with the sea breeze.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.5] Lake breezes form in the same way on large lakes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.29.b] Slopes in the mountains heat and cool faster than the air above the valley. By day this makes air flow up the slopes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.29.0] Sunshine heats the valley sides. Air in contact with them warms and rises upslope.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.29.1] Cumulus may form over the peaks in the afternoon, because the rising air cools and its moisture condenses.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.b] At night the slopes cool and the process runs in reverse. Cold air drains downhill and collects in the valley.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.0] Slopes lose heat by radiation. Air in contact with them cools, becomes denser and drains downslope under gravity, collecting on the valley floor.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.1] Cold air pooling on the valley floor creates frost hollows and temperature inversions, so valley floors are colder than the slopes above.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.2] Orchards and vineyards are planted on slopes above the frost line to avoid frost hollows.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.3] Radiation fog forms in the valleys, because the cold pooled air chills moisture to saturation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.4] In Antarctica and Greenland, where the ice sheets are very cold, katabatic winds flowing off the ice sheets can reach gale force.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.5] Diagram: a day and night cross-section of a valley showing the anabatic and katabatic flows.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.b] A föhn is a warm, dry wind on the lee side of a mountain range (the side sheltered from the prevailing wind). Its temperature change comes from the difference between saturated and dry cooling and warming.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.0] The name föhn comes from the Alps, the original area where the wind was described, in the German-speaking Alpine valleys.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.1] Air rising on the windward side cools at the saturated rate (the slower rate) after condensation begins, because condensation releases latent heat.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.2] Air descending on the lee side warms at the faster dry rate, so it arrives warmer and drier than it started.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.3] The föhn is warm because of this difference in rates, not because the air comes from a warm place.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.4] Effects: rapid snowmelt, avalanche and fire risk, and sudden temperature rises.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.5] The Chinook is a föhn on the eastern (lee) side of the Rocky Mountains in Canada and the USA. It is called the snow eater because it melts snow rapidly.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.32.b] Where high ground meets the Mediterranean coast, cold air can pour through gaps in the mountains. Two such winds have local names.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.32.0] The mistral is a cold, dry northerly wind funnelled down the Rhône valley in southern France to the Mediterranean.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.32.1] The bora is a cold, dry north-easterly wind descending to the Adriatic coast, in Croatia and at Trieste in Italy.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.32.2] Both winds pour from high ground towards the sea, usually when high pressure lies inland and low pressure lies over the sea, because air flows from high to low pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.33.b] A thunderstorm is a violent storm of heavy rain, lightning and thunder, which grows in a tall cumulonimbus cloud. It needs three things to start.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.33.0] Thunderstorms need plentiful moisture, which fuels the cloud, and unstable air (air that keeps rising once it is lifted).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.33.1] They also need a trigger to lift the air: strong surface heating (convection), a cold front, mountains, or converging air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.34.b] A thunderstorm cell (one storm unit of the cloud) passes through three stages. Rising air builds it and falling air destroys it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.34.0] 1. Cumulus (developing) stage: warm, moist air rises in strong updrafts. Condensation releases latent heat, which keeps the air warmer than its surroundings so that it rises faster. Cloud grows above the freezing level, but there is no rain yet.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.34.1] 2. Mature stage: water drops and ice grow large enough to fall. They drag air down and create downdrafts beside the updrafts. Heavy rain, hail, lightning, thunder and gusty winds reach the surface, and the cloud top spreads into an anvil at the tropopause. This is the most violent stage.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.34.2] 3. Dissipating stage: downdrafts spread through the cloud and cut off the supply of warm, moist air. The rain becomes lighter and the cloud breaks up. A single cell lasts about an hour.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.34.3] Diagram: a cumulonimbus cell in its three stages, with updraft and downdraft arrows, the freezing level and the anvil at the tropopause.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.35.b] Lightning is a giant electric spark inside a storm cloud, and thunder is the sound it makes. Both come from the ice and water in the cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.35.0] Collisions between ice crystals, hailstones and supercooled droplets (water that is still liquid below freezing) separate electrical charges. The top of the cloud is usually positive and the base negative.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.35.1] A lightning discharge occurs within the cloud, between clouds, or to the ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.35.2] A lightning bolt heats the air along its path to around 30,000 °C, several times hotter than the surface of the sun.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.35.3] The heated air expands explosively. This expansion, not collisions between clouds, is the thunder.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.35.4] Light travels faster than sound, so the flash arrives first. Counting the seconds to the thunder gives the distance, at about 3 seconds per km.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.36.b] Thunderstorms are grouped by what lifts the air to start them, plus one especially violent type.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.36.0] Air-mass (convectional) thunderstorms form in hot, humid air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.36.1] Frontal thunderstorms form along vigorous cold fronts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.36.2] Orographic thunderstorms form over mountains, where the ground forces air upward.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.36.3] Supercells, such as those on the US Great Plains, are long-lived, rotating storms that can produce tornadoes and very large hail. They are common on the US Great Plains.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.37.b] Thunderstorms need heat and moisture, so they cluster in the warm, wet tropics and thin out towards the poles.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.37.0] Thunderstorms are most frequent over tropical land, because heat and moisture are plentiful, where they occur daily in the equatorial belt. They are less frequent in the mid-latitudes and rare at the poles.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.37.1] Lake Maracaibo, Venezuela, has the highest density of lightning on Earth. This is the Catatumbo lightning, measured by NASA satellites.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.37.2] The Congo Basin in Central Africa also has very frequent thunderstorms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.37.3] Ireland has relatively few thunderstorms, because the air is rarely hot and unstable, mainly in summer inland and with active cold fronts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.37.4] Impacts: flash flooding, hail damage to crops, lightning strikes that cause fires, power cuts and deaths, and downbursts (violent downdrafts) that are dangerous to aircraft.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.38.b] A tropical cyclone is a huge rotating storm that feeds on warm ocean water. It can only form where several conditions are met together.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.38.0] They form over sea-surface temperatures of at least about 26–27 °C, over a deep layer of ocean.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.38.1] They form at least about 5° from the equator, because the Coriolis force is needed to start the rotation and it is zero at the equator.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.38.2] Light winds aloft (low wind shear) and warm, moist, unstable air are also needed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.38.3] The North Atlantic hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.39.b] Warm sea feeds a tropical cyclone with moisture and heat. The result is a ring of violent storms round a calm centre.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.39.0] Clusters of thunderstorms form over warm ocean. Rising air and released latent heat lower the pressure at the centre.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.39.1] Inflowing air spirals in and rises in the eyewall (the ring of storm cloud round the centre). The calm, clear eye at the centre has sinking air, and spiral rain bands surround the eye.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.39.2] Cyclones weaken over land or cold water, because the evaporation from warm sea that fuels them is cut off.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.39.3] Diagram: a cross-section of a tropical cyclone showing the eye and the eyewall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.40.b] The same type of storm has different names in different oceans, and a scale rates its strength by wind speed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.40.0] For example, a hurricane forms in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.40.1] A typhoon forms in the north-west Pacific.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.40.2] A cyclone forms in the Indian Ocean and the South Pacific.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.40.3] The Saffir–Simpson scale has categories 1 to 5. Category 1 begins at sustained winds of 119 km/h (74 mph), and category 5 at 252 km/h (157 mph) or more.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.41.b] Tropical cyclones kill through wind, rain and, above all, the sea. Four events show the scale of the loss.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.41.0] Tropical cyclones bring destructive winds, torrential rain and flooding. Storm surge (a wall of sea water driven ashore by the storm) is usually the biggest killer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.41.1] Hurricane Katrina, August 2005: levee and floodwall failures flooded about 80% of New Orleans, and about 1,800 people died.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.41.2] Typhoon Haiyan, called Yolanda in the Philippines, November 2013: one of the strongest tropical cyclones ever recorded at landfall. Its storm surge devastated Tacloban on Leyte, and over 6,000 people died in the Philippines.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.41.3] Odisha super cyclone, October 1999: about 10,000 deaths.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.41.4] Hurricane Mitch, October 1998: about 11,000 people were killed in Central America, mainly in Honduras and Nicaragua, by floods and landslides.

CANDIDATES geo-weather-climate-s1: Weather maps and weather data
[geo-weather-climate-s1.0.b] Weather and climate describe the atmosphere (the layer of air around the Earth) over different lengths of time, and every weather map and table uses fixed units. Knowing the units lets you read a value correctly and give it with its unit.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.0.0] Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place at a given time, measured over hours and days. For example, a wet, windy afternoon is weather.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.0.1] Climate is the average weather of a place over a long period, usually calculated over 30 years. A single wet day does not change a place's climate.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.0.2] Pressure (the weight of the air pressing down on a surface) is measured in hectopascals (hPa); 1 hPa equals 1 millibar (mb), so the two units give the same value and are never converted.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.0.3] Average sea-level pressure is about 1013 hPa, which gives a reference point for judging whether a pressure reading is high or low.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.0.4] Units on weather charts and tables: temperature in °C, rainfall in mm, wind speed in km/h or knots (nautical miles per hour), sunshine in hours, cloud cover in oktas (eighths of the sky covered).
[geo-weather-climate-s1.1.b] A synoptic chart shows the weather over a large area at one moment, giving a general view of the whole (this is what 'synoptic' means).
[geo-weather-climate-s1.1.0] Observations are taken at the same time from many weather stations, ships, buoys (floating instrument platforms at sea) and satellites, and plotted onto one map, so the chart is a snapshot of one moment.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.1.1] A surface analysis chart shows the observed situation at a given time; a forecast chart shows the expected situation at a later time.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.1.2] Charts are issued at fixed times, stated in UTC (Coordinated Universal Time, the standard world time used so that observations from everywhere match).
[geo-weather-climate-s1.1.3] Met Éireann, Ireland's national meteorological (weather) service, produces Irish weather maps from its network of stations, combined with satellite and radar data. Forecasters combine charts with satellite and radar data to build up a full picture.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.1.4] Met Éireann stations include Valentia Observatory (Co. Kerry), Malin Head (Co. Donegal), Belmullet (Co. Mayo), Dublin Airport and Shannon Airport.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.b] An isoline joins places of equal value. Read the name of the line before its interval, because each name refers to one quantity and the names are easily swapped.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.0] Isobars join places of equal atmospheric pressure; they are usually drawn at fixed intervals, commonly every 4 hPa, so read the labels to confirm the interval.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.1] Isotherms join places of equal temperature, so they show where it is warmer or colder.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.2] Isohyets join places of equal rainfall.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.3] Isohels join places of equal sunshine.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.4] Isonephs join places of equal cloud cover, so a line labelled with a cloud amount is an isoneph.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.5] Shaded or banded maps, such as rainfall, sunshine, UV or temperature maps, use a colour key: read the class from the key and give the range it states when the map shows a band rather than a single value.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.6] To read a value between isolines, find the two lines either side of the point, note their values and the interval between them, then estimate in proportion to distance. A point halfway between two lines is about halfway between their values.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.7] A value at a closed centre (where an isoline forms a complete loop) is the figure written on it, such as 'L 976', or lies beyond the innermost isobar.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.b] High and low pressure are relative to the surrounding air, so no fixed value marks the boundary between them. Each system has its own marking on the chart and its own typical weather.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.0] A depression (also called a low or cyclone) is marked L or Low with a pressure figure; it has closed isobars with pressure falling towards the centre, and usually has fronts attached. Its isobars are usually tightly packed.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.1] Air rises in a depression, cools and condenses (turns from vapour into water droplets), so rain, cloud and strong winds are associated with depressions.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.2] An anticyclone (also called a high) is marked H or High; it has closed isobars with pressure rising towards the centre, widely spaced isobars, and no fronts.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.3] Air sinks in an anticyclone and warms, so clouds rarely form: summer brings warm, sunny days, and winter brings cold, clear nights with frost and fog.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.4] A trough is an elongated area of low pressure, often unsettled because air rises along it, sometimes drawn as a line.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.5] A ridge is an elongated extension of high pressure, bringing a short settled spell.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.6] A col is a slack area between two highs and two lows, with light winds, uncertain weather, and possible fog or thunderstorms.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.7] An air mass is a large body of air with fairly uniform temperature and humidity (moisture content), named from its source region (polar or tropical; maritime or continental). Ireland's usual air mass is maritime, from the Atlantic. There is more on this in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.b] The values in this example are invented for illustration and are not taken from any real chart. It shows how to answer the usual questions on pressure and wind from isobars.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.0] A depression marked 'L 976' is centred in the Atlantic about 500 km west of Ireland, with closed isobars at 980, 984, 988, 992, 996 and 1000 hPa packed closely over the west coast.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.1] An anticyclone marked 'H 1036' lies over central Europe, with widely spaced isobars at 1032, 1028 and 1024 hPa.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.2] Pressure at the depression's centre is 976 hPa, the figure written on the L, and the isobar interval is 4 hPa, the gap between neighbouring isobars such as 980 and 984.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.3] A point midway between the 988 and 992 hPa isobars is about 990 hPa, because it lies halfway between the two values.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.4] Winds are stronger over the west of Ireland than over central Europe, because the isobars are closer together over the west.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.5] Ireland lies under the depression, so it is likely to be wet and windy; central Europe lies under the high, so it is likely to be calm and dry.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.b] Wind is named from the direction it blows from, and its strength follows the spacing of the isobars. Once you know these rules you can judge the wind anywhere on a chart.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.0] Wind direction is named by the direction the wind blows from: a westerly blows from the west.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.1] Air moves from high to low pressure; closely spaced isobars mean a steep pressure gradient (a big pressure change over a short distance) and strong winds, while widely spaced isobars mean light winds or calm.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.2] In the northern hemisphere the wind blows anticlockwise around a depression and clockwise around an anticyclone. There is more on the cause of this pattern in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.3] Near the surface the wind blows nearly parallel to the isobars, angled slightly in towards a low and out from a high.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.4] Buys Ballot's law: stand with your back to the wind, and low pressure is on your left in the northern hemisphere.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.5] The prevailing wind is the wind that blows most frequently over an area; in Ireland it is south-westerly, from the Atlantic.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.6] The Beaufort scale rates wind force from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane force), based on observed effects, such as how trees and the sea look.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.b] A front is the boundary between two air masses of different temperature and humidity. Frontal symbols point in the direction the front is moving.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.0] Warm front: a line with semicircles on the side it moves towards (red on colour charts). Warm air rises gently over cold air, bringing a long period of steady rain and thickening cloud, then rising temperature after it passes.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.1] Cold front: a line with triangles on the side it moves towards (blue on colour charts). Cold air undercuts warm air (pushes underneath it), bringing short heavy showers, possible thunder and falling temperature; clearer, brighter showery weather follows.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.2] Occluded front: triangles and semicircles alternating on the same side (purple on colour charts). It forms where a cold front catches up with a warm front, lifting the warm air off the ground, and it brings cloud and rain.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.3] An occluded front is usually found near the centre of a mature depression (one that is fully developed).
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.4] Stationary (quasi-stationary) front: triangles and semicircles alternating on opposite sides.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.5] Worked example W3 (drawing practice): in a box, draw each of the four symbols above, placing the symbol on the side the front moves towards.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.6] Draw a curved line with three triangles for cold and three semicircles for warm. Alternate them on one side for occluded and on opposite sides for stationary.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.7] Diagram cue: a plan of a depression shows the warm front ahead, the warm sector (the area of warm air between the fronts), the cold front behind and the occlusion near the centre.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.8] The isobars are kinked where they cross the fronts. There is more on the weather as a depression passes in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.7.b] A station model is the cluster of symbols plotted at each weather station on a synoptic chart. Each part gives one reading, so you can pull the cloud, wind, temperature and weather out of one small drawing.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.7.0] The circle at the station shows cloud cover in oktas: one okta is one eighth of the sky covered by cloud, so an empty circle is clear sky (0 oktas) and a fully shaded circle is overcast (8 oktas).
[geo-weather-climate-s1.7.1] The wind arrow's shaft points from the station towards the direction the wind is coming from, not where it is going.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.7.2] Barbs (the short lines on the shaft) give wind speed: a half barb is 5 knots, a full barb 10 knots and a pennant (filled triangle) 50 knots; add them together.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.7.3] A circle around the station with no shaft means calm, because there is no wind to point from.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.7.4] Other values around the circle are temperature (°C), dew point (the temperature at which the air is saturated and water vapour condenses), pressure and a present-weather symbol such as rain, drizzle, showers, snow, fog or thunderstorm; read them from the chart's key.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.7.5] Worked example W2 (illustrative): a circle three-quarters shaded is 6 oktas (three-quarters of 8); a shaft pointing towards the south-west with two full barbs and one half barb is a south-westerly wind of 25 knots (20 + 5); the temperature is 12 °C and the present weather is rain.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.8.b] Television, websites and apps use simplified symbols for the same features that appear on a synoptic chart. A series of maps at set times can then show how the weather moves.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.8.0] Media weather symbols include the sun, cloud, sun-and-cloud, rain drops, showers, snow, thunder (a lightning bolt) and fog.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.8.1] Wind arrows on media maps show speed in km/h, for example 20 km/h, and temperatures appear in coloured boxes.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.8.2] On rainfall radar (which detects rain in the air), heavier rain is shown in darker or warmer colours.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.8.3] A rainfall forecast sequence is a series of maps at set times; compare the position of the rain band on successive maps to give its direction of movement, and read each map rather than assuming a direction.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.8.4] Worked example W5 (illustrative): at 06:00 a rain band covers Munster and Connacht; at 12:00 it covers the midlands; at 18:00 it is over Leinster and the east coast. The rain moves from west to east, and Leinster's rain arrives last.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.9.b] Forecasts and warnings turn the charts into advice for the public. Knowing what a forecast contains and what the warning colours mean lets you read and use them.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.9.0] Met Éireann forecasts reach viewers through TV, radio, the website and app, as national, regional and city forecasts.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.9.1] Sea area and coastal forecasts give wind and sea state for shipping and fishing; farming, UV and pollen forecasts are also issued.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.9.2] A forecast describes the synoptic situation (what pressure systems and fronts are doing), expected rain, the highs and lows of temperature, wind direction and speed, cloud and sunshine, and how conditions change across the country and through the day.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.9.3] Warnings are colour-coded Yellow, Orange and Red by expected impact; Red is the most severe, and people should act to protect themselves and property.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.9.4] Warning types include wind, rain, snow-ice, low temperature, high temperature, fog and thunderstorm.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.10.b] Naming storms makes warnings easier to communicate, and the UV index gives a simple scale for the strength of the sun. Both appear in media reports.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.10.0] Storms have been named jointly by Met Éireann and the UK Met Office since 2015, and by the Dutch service KNMI since 2019; names make warnings easier to communicate.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.10.1] Ex-hurricane Ophelia reached Ireland on 16 October 2017. It had been a major hurricane south of the Azores, unusually far east for an Atlantic hurricane, and arrived as a post-tropical storm; Met Éireann issued Red warnings and three people died in Ireland.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.10.2] Storm Éowyn, a named storm, crossed Ireland on 24 January 2025, when Met Éireann issued Red wind warnings.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.10.3] The UV index measures the strength of the sun's ultraviolet radiation (the part of sunlight that burns skin); World Health Organization categories are Low 1-2, Moderate 3-5, High 6-7, Very high 8-10 and Extreme 11 and above.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.10.4] UV maps are read with isolines or a colour key, like any other map, so give the class or range the key states.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.11.b] Station tables and graphs record daily, hourly or monthly figures, and each figure comes from a particular instrument. Here you need to name each instrument and say what it measures.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.11.0] Rain gauge: measures rainfall in mm, so it is the instrument to name for rainfall.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.11.1] Thermometer: measures air temperature in °C (the instrument to name for temperature), and is kept in a Stevenson screen (a white, louvred box about 1.25 m above the ground) so that it measures shaded air temperature.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.11.2] Maximum-minimum thermometer: records the highest and lowest temperature in a period.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.11.3] Barometer: measures pressure in hPa; barograph: gives a continuous record of pressure.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.11.4] Anemometer: measures wind speed; wind vane: shows wind direction.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.11.5] Hygrometer or wet-and-dry bulb thermometer: measures humidity (the amount of water vapour in the air); sunshine recorder: measures hours of sunshine.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.12.b] Once you can name the instrument and read the value, you calculate with the figures in the units given. Exams set a table or graph of a station's daily, hourly or monthly figures.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.12.0] Total: add the values, for example rainfall over three days or three months.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.12.1] Mean (the average): total divided by the number of values; mean daily temperature = (maximum + minimum) ÷ 2.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.12.2] Range: highest minus lowest, for a day or for a year, such as 14 minus 6 for one day.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.12.3] Other tasks: find the difference between two places; count the months or days above a threshold; find the highest or lowest month. Always give units.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.12.4] Hourly graphs: read times carefully on the horizontal axis (00:00 is midnight), read the value at a given hour, and count the hours with rain.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.12.5] Climate graphs (a temperature line with rainfall bars by month) at an Irish station typically show mild winters, cool summers and rain all year, with the wettest months usually in autumn and winter.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.12.6] This links to the cool temperate oceanic climate in The Concept of a Region. There is more on the graph method in Figure interpretation.

[geo-weather-climate-s1.13.0] Monday: maximum 14 °C, minimum 6 °C, rainfall 4 mm, mean wind 20 km/h.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.13.1] Tuesday: maximum 12 °C, minimum 5 °C, rainfall 11 mm, mean wind 32 km/h.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.13.2] Wednesday: maximum 15 °C, minimum 8 °C, rainfall 0 mm, mean wind 14 km/h.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.13.3] Total rainfall over the three days is 15 mm (4 + 11 + 0); Monday's temperature range is 8 °C (14 - 6); Monday's mean temperature is 10 °C ((14 + 6) ÷ 2).

[geo-weather-climate-s1.14.b] A two-mark 'state one reason' answer links the pattern to the moist Atlantic air and to the relief (the height and shape) of the land.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.14.0] The west is wetter than the east: moist air from the Atlantic is forced to rise over the western mountains, where it cools and condenses, producing relief (orographic) rain. There is more on this in Exchanges of water between oceans and atmosphere.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.14.1] The east and south-east are sunnier and drier because they lie in the rain shadow of the western and central uplands: descending air warms and its cloud evaporates. The south-east is the sunniest part of Ireland.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.14.2] Coastal areas are milder in winter than the interior, because the sea warms the coast, including through the North Atlantic Drift (a warm ocean current); inland areas have a larger temperature range.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.14.3] Long-term averages from Met Éireann: most of the east gets about 750-1,000 mm of rain a year, the west about 1,000-1,250 mm, and many mountain areas over 2,000 mm.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.14.4] The highest rainfall totals are on mountains near the west coast, because that is where the Atlantic air is forced to rise, and the lowest are around Dublin and the east.
