TARGET geo-region-european-s1: A Core European Region: the Paris Basin
[geo-region-european-s1.0.b] The Paris Basin is a large, low sedimentary lowland (a basin floored by layers of rock laid down as sediment) in northern France, centred on Paris. Knowing exactly where it starts and stops matters, because the edging hills are not part of it.
[geo-region-european-s1.0.0] It is open to the English Channel in the north-west, so the basin has a sea outlet on one side and is closed in by higher land elsewhere.
[geo-region-european-s1.0.1] Older uplands ring the basin and mark its edge, and they are not part of the lowland: the Armorican Massif (west), the Massif Central (south), the Vosges (east) and the Ardennes (north-east).
[geo-region-european-s1.0.2] The basin covers several French administrative regions (the main divisions used to govern France). Île-de-France, the region containing Paris, is its core.
[geo-region-european-s1.0.3] France has 13 regions in mainland France (since 2016); Île-de-France is one of them.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.b] A core region is one where people, wealth and decision-making are concentrated. The Paris Basin earns that status from the concentration of people, wealth and functions around Paris, and from the links that radiate out from it.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.0] Paris is the capital, so government, finance and head offices (the headquarters of large firms) are concentrated there.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.1] The region holds the largest concentration of population, wealth, industry and services in France.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.2] Fertile farmland surrounds the city and supplies its food, so the city does not depend on distant supplies.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.3] The national transport network is focused on the region, so routes across France lead in and out of it.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.4] The basin has a central position in the EU.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.5] The reasons reinforce each other: the large market draws industry and services, these draw migrants, and the transport links carry goods and people in and out.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.b] Exams ask for an outline map of a European region with named features on it. These are the items you can draw and name, and where each one belongs.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.0] River: draw the Seine and name it; the Marne, Oise and Loire may be added as named rivers.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.1] Urban centres: name Paris with two others from Rouen, Le Havre, Reims, Orléans, Troyes, Chartres or Amiens.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.2] Placement on rivers: Paris, Rouen and Le Havre lie on the Seine, with Le Havre at its mouth and Rouen upstream of it. Troyes is on the Seine upstream of Paris. Orléans is on the Loire. Épernay, not Reims, is on the Marne.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.3] Placement elsewhere: Chartres lies in the Beauce (a lowland plain) and Amiens is on the Somme.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.4] Relief: shade the Beauce (lowland plain) and the Brie plateau, and mark the edging uplands on the rim, labelled as bounding the region.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.5] Agricultural area: the Beauce for cereals, and Champagne for vines.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.6] Industrial area: the Lower Seine between Rouen and Le Havre.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.b] The climate is transitional, between cool temperate oceanic conditions in the west (mild, wet, moderated by the sea) and more continental conditions in the east (bigger swings between summer and winter). This shapes what can be grown.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.0] The annual temperature range (the gap between the warmest and coldest month) increases from west to east, so inland winters are colder and summers warmer.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.1] Paris: average January temperature about 5°C; average July temperature about 20°C.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.2] Paris: annual rainfall about 600–650 mm, spread fairly evenly through the year, so rainfall is moderate and there is no dry season.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.3] Champagne lies near the northern limit of commercial vine growing, so its warm summers are essential to the crop.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.b] The basin is a saucer of sedimentary rock layers, with the youngest rocks at the centre and the oldest at the rim. The resulting low, gentle relief matters for land use.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.0] Layers of limestone, chalk, clay and sand dip (slope) gently towards Paris, which sits near the centre on the youngest rocks.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.1] The land is mostly below about 300 m, so the relief is low and gently rolling, which suits large-scale farming.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.2] On the eastern side, resistant layers form escarpments (long ridges with a steep face) that face outwards (east), while their dip slopes (the gentle back slopes) fall back in towards Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.3] Clay vales (low, wet valleys cut in soft clay) lie between the escarpments.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.4] Only the relief and its effect on land use are needed here. There is more on how cuestas (escarpments) form in Landforms from sedimentary structures.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.b] Soil type decides what a farmer can grow, and the basin has a clear pattern: rich loam on the plateaux, thin chalk soil in Champagne, and heavy clay in the vales.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.0] Limon, a fine, wind-blown loam, covers the Beauce, Brie and Picardy plateaux.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.1] Limon is deep, fine, well-drained, fertile and easily worked, so it is among the most fertile soils in Europe.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.2] Champagne's chalk gives thin calcareous (lime-rich) soils. The dry chalk plain east of Reims was once called 'Champagne pouilleuse' (poor Champagne).
[geo-region-european-s1.5.3] Fertilisers and modern farming turned that dry chalk plain into productive cereal land, so soil weakness was overcome by human effort.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.4] Heavier, wetter clay soils in the vales and in the west are suited to grass rather than crops, because they are hard to work and waterlogged.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.b] The Seine and its tributaries (rivers that flow into it) form the main drainage system, and they converge on Paris. The gentle land that makes them useful also makes them a flood risk.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.0] The Seine rises on the Langres plateau in Burgundy and flows about 775 km north-west through Troyes, Paris and Rouen to the English Channel at Le Havre.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.1] Its tributaries are the Aube, Yonne, Marne and Oise.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.2] Gentle gradients make the rivers navigable, so boats and barges can use them.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.3] The lower Seine meanders (winds in loops) between Paris and Rouen.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.4] The Loire crosses the south of the region and flows through Orléans.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.5] Permeable chalk and limestone (rock that lets water soak in) store groundwater in aquifers (underground water stores) and give dry valleys, which are valleys with no river in them now.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.6] The gentle gradient and low relief let floodwater spread slowly across the flat valley floor. The Great Flood of Paris (January 1910) and a major flood in June 2016 (which closed the lower galleries of the Louvre) both show this risk.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.7] Upstream reservoirs regulate the flow of the rivers; Lac du Der-Chantecoq regulates the Marne.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.b] The Paris Basin is the main arable region (growing crops rather than keeping animals) of France, and its physical conditions give it a head start in farming.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.0] France is the EU's largest agricultural producer and its largest wheat producer.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.1] Low, gently rolling relief allows large fields to be worked with machinery.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.2] Limon soils are fertile and easily worked, giving high yields.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.3] Warm summers ripen cereals and grapes, and the long growing season extends the time crops have to develop.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.4] Dry harvest weather lets crops be gathered in good condition, because wet grain is harder to store.
[geo-region-european-s1.8.b] The Beauce is the best example of the basin's arable farming, where flat land and limon soil are put to full use.
[geo-region-european-s1.8.0] The Beauce, south-west of Paris around Chartres, is known as the granary of France (a granary is a store for grain).
[geo-region-european-s1.8.1] Crops: wheat, barley and maize, with sugar beet and oilseed rape grown on the plateaux.
[geo-region-european-s1.8.2] Many cereal farms exceed 100 hectares, which is large enough for big machines to work efficiently.
[geo-region-european-s1.8.3] Large, highly mechanised farms achieve high yields on the flat limon land, because flat ground suits big machines.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.b] Viticulture (growing grapes for wine) is the basin's best-known specialist farming. It depends on chalk soils, slopes and a protected name.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.0] Vineyards lie on the chalk slopes around Reims and Épernay; Épernay is on the Marne.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.1] The vines grow on the slopes of the escarpment, on thin chalk soils.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.2] Only sparkling wine from this protected (AOC) area may be called Champagne. AOC is a legal label that protects a product's name and place of origin.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.3] The Champagne houses (the firms that make and sell the wine) store wine in chalk cellars. Moët & Chandon is based in Épernay.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.4] The houses add value: grapes grown on the slopes become a branded product sold under the protected name, which links farming to manufacturing.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.b] Where the soil is too wet and heavy for crops, farmers turn to grass and animals. Near Paris, the huge city market favours fresh produce instead.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.0] Wetter clay soils and grassland support dairy farming (milk production) in Normandy (west), which produces Camembert cheese.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.1] Brie, east of Paris, is also a dairy area, so it gives its name to Brie cheese.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.2] Market gardening (growing vegetables and flowers for sale) is practised on the edge of Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.3] Proximity to Paris lets fresh produce reach a very large urban market quickly, which matters because fresh goods spoil.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.b] Physical conditions are only part of the story. Changes to farm layout, organisation and EU money also explain why the basin farms so productively.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.0] Remembrement: after the Second World War, fragmented holdings (many small scattered plots) were consolidated into larger farms.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.1] Mechanisation allows fewer workers to farm larger areas, which is also why the rural population falls.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.2] Farm co-operatives (farmers' groups) and agribusiness firms (large commercial food companies) buy, process and market farm produce.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.3] France receives the largest share of CAP spending (the EU's Common Agricultural Policy) of any member state, which supports French farm incomes.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.4] The Paris market is a large outlet for farm produce within reach of the basin.
[geo-region-european-s1.12.b] Forestry and fishing are small activities here compared with farming.
[geo-region-european-s1.12.0] Large forests grow on the poorer sandy soils near Paris, which are less useful for crops, so trees took the land. They include the Forests of Fontainebleau (on sands and sandstone), Compiègne and Rambouillet.
[geo-region-european-s1.12.1] These state forests are used for timber and recreation.
[geo-region-european-s1.12.2] Fishing is minor in this region and no port is named for it.
[geo-region-european-s1.13.b] France has few local fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas), so it relies on nuclear power, and the basin's rivers and coast host many of the stations.
[geo-region-european-s1.13.0] Nuclear power supplies about two-thirds of France's electricity (recent years).
[geo-region-european-s1.13.1] Nuclear stations sit on rivers and the coast, which supply the cooling water they need: Nogent-sur-Seine on the Seine; Dampierre and Saint-Laurent on the Loire near Orléans; Paluel and Penly on the Normandy Channel coast.

[geo-region-european-s1.31.4] Urban sprawl: development spreads outward into the surrounding countryside, because high housing costs push people out.
[geo-region-european-s1.32.b] Away from Paris, the countryside has changed as farming has mechanised and as the city's influence has spread outward.
[geo-region-european-s1.32.0] Fewer farmers on larger holdings leave the outer basin with a declining rural population.
[geo-region-european-s1.32.1] Rural depopulation in the outer basin forms part of the 'diagonale du vide' (empty diagonal), a band of low population density from north-east to south-west France.
[geo-region-european-s1.32.2] Commuter villages near Paris are growing as residents travel to jobs in the city.
[geo-region-european-s1.32.3] Second homes and rural tourism bring visitors and money to villages in the Champagne and countryside areas.
[geo-region-european-s1.33.b] Population is very unevenly spread: packed into Paris and its region, thinly spread across the outer basin.
[geo-region-european-s1.33.0] Île-de-France has about 1,000 people per km² (2020s).
[geo-region-european-s1.33.1] The City of Paris has about 2.1 million people (2020s) on about 105 km², about 20,000 people per km²; it is divided into 20 arrondissements (administrative districts of the city).
[geo-region-european-s1.33.2] Densities are much lower in rural Champagne and across the outer basin, where mechanised farming needs few workers.
[geo-region-european-s1.33.3] The dense core exists because jobs, services and transport access are concentrated in and around Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.34.b] Population change in the basin has been driven by movement of people into Paris, from the French countryside and from abroad.
[geo-region-european-s1.34.0] Jean-François Gravier's Paris et le désert français (Paris and the French desert), 1947, argued that Paris drained people and wealth from the rest of France.
[geo-region-european-s1.34.1] Rural-to-urban migration into Paris continued because jobs and services concentrated there.
[geo-region-european-s1.34.2] The Paris region has a young age structure, because in-migrants are mostly young adults seeking work.
[geo-region-european-s1.34.3] Older residents move out of the region on retirement, so the age structure stays young.
[geo-region-european-s1.34.4] After the Second World War, immigrant labour arrived from Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia and Portugal.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s4: 4. Socio-economic regions
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.b] Geographers group places into regions by how wealthy and developed they are, not just by landscape. A socio-economic region is defined by its level of wealth and development, judged by several indicators at once, because no single figure tells the whole story.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.0] The indicators include income or GDP per head (the value of what an area produces, divided by its population), unemployment, the mix of primary, secondary and tertiary jobs, migration, age structure, education and services.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.1] Primary jobs take materials from the land and sea (farming, fishing, forestry, mining); secondary jobs make goods in factories; tertiary jobs provide services. The mix of these three is a clue to how developed a region is.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.2] The core–periphery idea: wealth, people and investment (money put into businesses and infrastructure) concentrate in a core, while the periphery (the outer, distant area) loses people and capital to it.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.3] Cumulative causation means each advantage in the core attracts more, so the gap tends to widen unless governments or the EU intervene.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.b] A core region has a strong, growing economy, high incomes, and a concentration of population, investment and decision-making. Everything else about it follows from that economic strength.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.0] It has high GDP per head and low unemployment, because jobs are plentiful.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.1] It has a large tertiary (services) sector, including finance, company headquarters and high-tech industry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.2] Most foreign investment goes to the core, because firms from abroad want its markets, workers and transport links.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.3] Net in-migration (more people arriving than leaving) of young, educated workers gives it a young population, because they move to where the jobs are.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.4] It has dense, well-developed infrastructure (the transport networks that move people and goods): motorways, airports, ports and rail lines.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.5] It has universities and research institutions, which supply skilled workers, and usually the capital city with the seat of government.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.6] Causes: accessibility, a large market and labour force, an early start, government and capital functions, and agglomeration, where firms attract more firms to the same place because they gain from being near suppliers, customers and workers.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.7] Problems of success: congestion, high house prices, urban sprawl (the city spreading outwards over farmland) and pressure on services.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.b] Three examples show what a core looks like at national and European scale. Each is a place where population, government and business cluster.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.0] Greater Dublin Area: Dublin, Kildare, Meath and Wicklow. County Dublin had about 1.45 million people in Census 2022, when the State's population was about 5.1 million.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.1] Dublin, the capital, has the seat of government, Ireland's main port (Dublin Port) and busiest airport (Dublin Airport). Its docklands host the European headquarters of many technology firms, e.g. Google. There is more on this in the Irish Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.2] Paris Basin, France: Île-de-France, the region around Paris at the basin's core, has about 12 million people. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.3] The Blue Banana: the curved belt of Europe's main core, running from south-east England and London through the Benelux countries (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg) and the Rhine valley to northern Italy (Milan).
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.b] A peripheral region is distant, physically or economically, from the core, with a weaker economy and fewer opportunities. Its problems tend to feed each other, so people leave and the region falls further behind.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.0] Remote location and poor access to markets and services, so it is costly to sell goods or reach shops, hospitals and schools.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.1] Difficult physical conditions: mountains, high rainfall, poor soils and a short growing season, which limit what farmers can produce.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.2] Dependence on primary activity (taking materials from land and sea): small farms, fishing and forestry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.3] Low incomes and higher unemployment, so people look elsewhere for work, or underemployment (people working fewer hours or in lower-skilled jobs than they want).
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.4] Net out-migration of young adults, so the people left behind are older and rural populations fall.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.5] Fewer services, and small, scattered settlements, which means services costly to provide.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.b] Because the periphery tends to fall further behind, governments and the EU act to slow the loss of people and jobs.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.0] EU regional and cohesion funds (money meant to narrow the gap between richer and poorer regions) and state agencies channel investment into the periphery.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.1] Better roads and broadband, and decentralisation of government offices (moving them out of the capital), help peripheral areas keep people and jobs.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.2] Tourism builds on the scenery and culture of peripheral areas, so it brings jobs without needing a nearby core.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.3] There is more on each example in the case-study chapters.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.b] These four examples are far from their country's or Europe's core, in the west, south and far north.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.0] West of Ireland: the Northern and Western regional assembly area. Co. Leitrim has the smallest population of any Irish county.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.1] The Mezzogiorno, southern Italy. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.2] Northern Norway: Arctic, remote and reliant on fishing.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.3] North-west Scotland: the Highlands and Islands.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.b] A less-developed region is defined by how far its development falls below an average, and the EU uses a precise cut-off to decide which regions get the most help.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.0] A less-developed region is one whose level of development is well below the national or EU average.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.1] In EU regional policy, a less-developed region has GDP per head below 75% of the EU average, and receives the most EU aid.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.2] Most less-developed regions in Europe are also peripheral: the Mezzogiorno, and much of eastern Europe such as Romania and Bulgaria.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.3] Peripheral is not the same as less-developed, because a peripheral region can lie in a rich country, such as the West of Ireland or northern Norway.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.4] Ireland was a single Objective 1 region (the category receiving the highest level of EU aid) until 1999. From 2000 to 2006 only the BMW (Border, Midland and Western) region kept Objective 1 status.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.5] At a global scale, less-developed regions are developing countries. India is the continental example. There is more on India in its own chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.6] The Mezzogiorno's development history began with the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno (Fund for the South) in 1950.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.b] A region of industrial decline grew on heavy, traditional industry and has since lost much of it. The loss then spreads through the local economy.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.0] Its traditional industries include coal mining, iron and steel, shipbuilding and textiles.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.1] Causes: coal seams exhausted or too costly to work; cheaper imports and competition from newly industrialised countries (countries that built up their factory industry more recently, often with lower costs).
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.2] Further causes: coal replaced by oil and gas; out-of-date plant (old machinery and factories); and automation (machines doing work people once did), which needs fewer workers.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.3] Effects: high unemployment, especially among older male workers; derelict factories, mines and spoil heaps (waste rock dumped from mining); and polluted land.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.4] Further effects: out-migration, falling services, and a poor image that deters new investment.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.5] Responses: retraining; clearing and cleaning derelict land; attracting new light industry and services; universities and technology parks; reuse of industrial heritage for tourism and culture; EU funds.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.b] Most examples are old coal and steel regions of north-west Europe. The Irish examples are port and shipbuilding areas.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.0] Sambre–Meuse valley, Wallonia, Belgium: coal mining and steel along the Sambre and Meuse, e.g. Charleroi on the Sambre and Liège on the Meuse.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.1] Ruhr, Germany (North Rhine-Westphalia): Europe's largest coal and steel region, so its decline was large, with cities such as Essen, Dortmund and Duisburg.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.2] Prosper-Haniel in Bottrop, the last hard-coal mine in Germany, closed in 2018. The Zollverein coal mine complex in Essen became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001 and is now a cultural centre, an example of reusing industrial heritage.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.3] South Wales coalfield, including the Rhondda valleys: coal mining collapsed in the late 20th century.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.4] Nord–Pas-de-Calais, France, is a further European example.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.5] Belfast, for example: the Harland and Wolff shipyard, which built the Titanic (launched 1911), declined greatly in the late 20th century.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.6] Dublin docklands: port jobs fell with containerisation (goods shipped in standard metal containers, which need far fewer dock workers). The International Financial Services Centre (IFSC), established in 1987 on derelict dockland, regenerated the area.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.7] There is more on Irish economic evidence in the Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Economic Activities chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.b] The clearest difference between the two region types is the kind of work people do. Compare them sector by sector: primary, secondary and tertiary.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.0] Primary, for example: large, commercial, efficient farms on good land in the core; small, fragmented farms on poor land in the periphery, with more fishing and forestry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.1] Secondary: concentrations of manufacturing and multinational investment (factories owned by firms based in other countries) in the core; few, small and often grant-aided (helped by state money) factories in the periphery.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.2] Tertiary: finance, headquarters, government, universities, and major ports and airports in the core; tourism and public services in the periphery.

CANDIDATES geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4: 4. Landforms from sedimentary structures
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.0.b] Sedimentary rocks are built up in layers, and the boundaries between those layers decide how the rock later breaks and wears away. That is why landforms in layered rock start with strata and bedding planes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.0.0] Sedimentary rocks are laid down in layers called strata, or beds, formed by deposition (sediment settling out) followed by compaction (squeezing together) and cementation (minerals gluing the grains).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.0.1] A bedding plane is the boundary between one layer and the next; when the layers are laid down, bedding planes are roughly horizontal.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.0.2] A bedding plane marks a pause or change in deposition, which is why neighbouring layers can differ.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.0.3] Bedding planes divide the rock into layers, and each layer may differ in thickness and resistance (how well it stands up to erosion).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.1.b] Layers are cut by cracks as well as by planes between them. These cracks give water and ice a way into the rock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.1.0] A joint is a crack in rock along which no movement has taken place, so the rock on either side has not slid past.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.1.1] Joints in sedimentary rock are at right angles to the bedding planes, so they are vertical (across the layers) where the strata are horizontal.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.1.2] Joints form as sediment dries and shrinks, and by stress and the removal of overlying rock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.1.3] Bedding planes and joints together divide the rock into blocks.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.2.b] Earth movements can tilt layers that were laid down flat. The landform that results depends on how far and in which direction the strata tilt.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.2.0] Dip is the angle at which strata tilt from the horizontal; the dip direction is the way they tilt.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.2.1] Strike is the direction along a tilted layer's surface, at right angles to the dip.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.2.2] Strata are horizontal, gently dipping or steeply dipping, and the landform depends on which, so you must state the attitude (tilt) of the layers when explaining a landform.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.3.b] Bedding planes and joints are gaps in otherwise solid rock. Erosion does not attack the rock evenly but follows them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.3.0] Water, ice and weathering (the breakdown of rock in place) exploit bedding planes and joints as lines of weakness, because they can get into the rock there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.3.1] Rock breaks into blocks along these planes, so erosion is concentrated there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.4.b] Layers in a rock are not equally tough. Differential erosion means different layers wear away at different rates.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.4.0] Where resistant layers, such as sandstone and limestone, alternate with weak layers, such as shale and clay, the weak layers are worn away faster.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.4.1] The resistant layers are left standing out as cliffs and caps, because the soft rock around them has been removed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.5.b] Rock layers also differ in whether water can pass through them. This controls where water sinks underground and where it comes back to the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.5.0] Water passes down joints and bedding planes in permeable (or pervious) rock, which lets water through.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.5.1] Water flows over impermeable rock instead, because it cannot soak in.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.5.2] A spring emerges where a permeable layer lies on an impermeable one, because the water sinking through the top layer is stopped by the layer below. The line of springs is a spring line.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.6.b] A plateau (a flat-topped upland) forms in layered rock. It needs horizontal strata with a tough layer on top.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.6.0] 1. Horizontal strata have a resistant cap rock (the tough top layer) lying above weaker layers.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.6.1] 2. Erosion strips the weaker layers beneath the cap and wears back the edges of the upland, because weak rock wears away faster than the cap.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.6.2] 3. The cap protects the layers beneath, so the top stays flat and the edges stay steep.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.6.3] Diagram: cross-section of a flat-topped upland with a resistant cap, cliffed edges and weaker strata below.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.7.b] When horizontal layers of hard and soft rock alternate several times, the plateau edge is not one cliff but a staircase.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.7.0] Alternating hard and soft horizontal layers erode at different rates, because the hard rock resists erosion and the soft rock does not.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.7.1] Hard layers form cliffs; soft layers form gentler slopes, often covered by scree (loose broken rock fragments), because the soft rock wears down more easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.7.2] The profile of cliff and slope repeats for each pair of hard and soft layers.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.8.b] This is the Irish setting for landforms of horizontal strata. Near-horizontal Carboniferous strata (rocks laid down in the geological period of that name) of limestone, shale and sandstone form flat-topped uplands with steep cliffed edges and stepped slopes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.8.0] The Dartry Mountains and Cuilcagh span Co. Sligo, Co. Leitrim, Co. Cavan and Co. Fermanagh.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.8.1] Caves and karst features (the landscape of limestone shaped by water) lie in the limestone beneath the uplands.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.9.b] Benbulben, part of the Dartry Mountains, shows a resistant cap above weaker rock in one clear profile.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.9.0] Benbulben, at 526 m, is a flat-topped mountain of near-horizontal strata.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.9.1] A resistant limestone cap forms its vertical cliffs, because the cap resists erosion.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.9.2] Weaker shale beneath forms the lower, gentler slopes, partly covered in scree.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.9.3] Ice later steepened its sides.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.10.b] Cuilcagh has three rock types stacked on one another, so its profile is stepped rather than a single cliff.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.10.0] Cuilcagh, at 665 m, is the highest point in both Co. Cavan and Co. Fermanagh.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.10.1] A resistant sandstone cap lies over shale, which lies over limestone, giving a stepped profile and a flat summit ridge.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.10.2] The Shannon Pot, the source of the River Shannon, lies at the foot of Cuilcagh in Co. Cavan.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.10.3] The Marble Arch Caves, Co. Fermanagh, lie in the limestone beneath it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.11.b] If erosion keeps working at a plateau, the plateau does not stay in one piece. It is cut into smaller flat-topped hills.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.11.0] 1. Weathering and erosion work back the edges of a plateau.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.11.1] 2. The plateau is broken into separate flat-topped remnants (the parts left behind), each capped by the resistant rock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.11.2] 3. A mesa is wider than it is high; a butte is a smaller, narrower remnant.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.11.3] Example: Monument Valley, on the Arizona–Utah border, USA.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.11.4] Diagram: cross-section of a mesa and a butte, each with a resistant cap.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.12.b] When strata tilt gently rather than lie flat, a resistant layer makes a ridge with one steep side and one gentle side. This ridge is an escarpment, or cuesta.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.12.0] 1. On gently dipping strata, a resistant layer is exposed at the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.12.1] 2. Erosion cuts a steep scarp slope across the edges of the layers, because the cut ends of the resistant layer are exposed there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.12.2] 3. The layers continue along the tilt as a long, gentle dip slope.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.12.3] 4. The weaker rock between cuestas is eroded into a vale (a low valley floor).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.12.4] Example: the Cotswolds, England, a limestone cuesta with a steep west-facing scarp and a long dip slope to the east.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.12.5] Diagram: cross-section of a cuesta showing scarp slope, dip slope and vale.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.13.b] If the strata dip steeply instead of gently, the same process gives a different shape.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.13.0] A hogback forms on steeply dipping strata.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.13.1] It is a narrow ridge whose scarp and dip slopes are of nearly equal steepness, unlike a cuesta, where one slope is steep and the other gentle.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.13.2] Example: the Hog's Back, Surrey, England, a narrow chalk ridge.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.14.b] Bedding planes and joints also shape smaller features. These are named here as effects of the structures; how each forms is taught elsewhere.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.14.0] Joints and bedding planes in limestone give limestone pavement, with blocks called clints separated by grikes (the gaps between them).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.14.1] Caves form along bedding planes in limestone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.14.2] Waterfalls form over horizontal cap rock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.14.3] Sea caves and arches form along joints on the coast.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.15.b] The Paris Basin is the international example of gently dipping strata. It is a saucer-shaped basin of sedimentary layers of different resistance, dipping gently in towards Paris.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.15.0] The layers are limestone, chalk and clay.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.15.1] Paris lies near the centre of the basin.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.15.2] On the eastern side, resistant layers form outward-facing escarpments (cuestas), with dip slopes facing in towards Paris.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.15.3] Clay vales lie between the escarpments, because the weaker clay is worn away faster.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.16.b] The Brecon Beacons are a second international example of gently dipping strata, this time in sandstone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.16.0] Gently dipping Old Red Sandstone forms flat-topped summits.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.16.1] The summits have steep north-facing escarpments and gentle south-facing dip slopes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.16.2] Pen y Fan, at 886 m, is the highest peak in southern Britain.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4.16.3] The area was renamed Bannau Brycheiniog National Park in 2023.
