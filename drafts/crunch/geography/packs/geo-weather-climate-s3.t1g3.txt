TARGET geo-weather-climate-s3: Geographical information systems (GIS)
[geo-weather-climate-s3.0.b] A geographical information system (GIS) is a computer system that combines data linked to places on the earth's surface, so that data sources for one area can be studied together. Its power comes from combining: one map or photo on its own is not a GIS.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.0.0] A GIS captures, stores, analyses and displays spatial (geo-referenced) data, meaning data tied to a location, from maps, aerial photographs, satellite images, census and other statistics, and field surveys.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.0.1] Each data set is stored as a separate layer, such as roads, rivers, land use, population or protected sites, over the same base map (the common background map). Layers can be switched on and off and overlaid to reveal relationships.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.0.2] Geo-referencing ties every item to coordinates, such as latitude and longitude or a national grid, so layers from different sources line up. Irish maps use the Irish Transverse Mercator (ITM) grid and the older Irish Grid.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.0.3] Vector data are points, lines and polygons (closed shapes enclosing an area): a monument is shown as a point, a road as a line and a forest as a polygon.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.0.4] Raster data are a grid of cells or pixels (tiny squares, each holding one value), such as a satellite image or an aerial photograph.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.0.5] Each feature can carry attribute data, a table of information about it, such as a house's year of building or a field's crop.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.1.b] A GIS is useful because of what it can do with the layers once they are lined up. Every study follows the same sequence, so the analysis tools below are used in whichever option is chosen.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.1.0] Overlay combines layers to show where things coincide, such as new housing on former farmland.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.1.1] Buffering draws a zone of set distance around a feature, such as a zone around a river or monument.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.1.2] Querying selects the features that meet a condition, such as all Small Areas (the small census districts) whose population grew by more than a set amount.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.1.3] Change detection compares the same layer at different dates, so you can see what has altered.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.1.4] Network analysis finds the shortest or fastest route along roads or other links, such as for an ambulance.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.1.5] Choropleth maps shade each area by a statistic, such as census figures, so high and low areas stand out.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.1.6] Steps: 1. define the problem and area; 2. collect data layers; 3. geo-reference them to one base map; 4. overlay and analyse; 5. map and present the results; 6. draw conclusions and recommend planning action (decisions about how land should be used).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.2.b] Beyond the four studies, GIS is used across planning and services. Knowing its strengths, its limits and where Irish data come from lets you judge any GIS study.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.2.0] Wider uses of GIS: local authority planning and zoning, flood-risk mapping, emergency services, water and electricity networks, retail and service location, environmental monitoring and census mapping.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.2.1] Advantages: handles very large data sets, combines many sources, analyses and re-analyses quickly, updates easily, gives clear map output and models 'what if' scenarios (testing the effect of a possible change before it happens).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.2.2] Limitations: software and data are costly and need training; results are only as good as the data, which may be out of date or inaccurate; layers at different scales or dates may not match; personal data raise privacy issues.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.2.3] Tailte Éireann, set up in 2023 by bringing together Ordnance Survey Ireland, the Property Registration Authority and the Valuation Office, supplies maps and aerial photography. Either name for the mapping body is acceptable.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.2.4] The Central Statistics Office (CSO) supplies census data by area. Tailte Éireann is not a census body.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.2.5] Satellite images, such as Landsat and Copernicus Sentinel, are another source; government departments and agencies supply sector statistics. There is more on satellite images in Satellite imagery.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.3.b] Urban sprawl is the uncontrolled outward spread of low-density urban development, such as housing estates, retail parks and business parks, into the surrounding countryside, often along roads and into commuter towns (towns from which people travel to work in a city) and villages. This study uses aerial photographs, census material and maps.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.3.0] Aerial photographs of the same area at different dates show farmland replaced by housing estates, new roads, roundabouts and business parks, and ribbon development (building in a line along approach roads). Overlaying them shows exactly where and when land changed.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.3.1] Census material: population and housing counts by small area show where population grew fastest, the age of the housing stock, household size and commuting (distance and mode of travel to work).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.3.2] The CSO publishes census results for Small Areas, each generally of 50–200 dwellings, so growth can be mapped in fine detail.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.3.3] Ireland's censuses were held in 2011, 2016 and 2022; the 2021 census was postponed to 2022 because of COVID-19. Census 2022 counted about 5.1 million people in Ireland and about 1.45 million in Dublin county.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.3.4] Maps: Ordnance Survey and historic maps of different editions show the built-up area expanding; development plan zoning maps show land zoned (officially set aside) for housing; transport maps show new motorways and rail lines that encouraged growth.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.4.b] Once the layers are lined up, the analysis tests how and why the city spread. The Dublin findings then show the problems and the planning responses.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.4.0] Overlay the built-up area at each date to measure how much land was urbanised (turned into built-up land), then overlay census growth on it.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.4.1] Buffer motorways and rail stations to test whether growth followed transport, and link commuting data to show dependence on the car.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.4.2] Findings for Dublin: the city and its suburbs spread into the surrounding counties; commuter towns such as Navan, Drogheda, Naas, Newbridge and Portlaoise grew rapidly; many residents commute long distances into Dublin. The Greater Dublin Area comprises Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.4.3] Problems shown by the study: loss of farmland and green space; car dependence, long commutes and traffic congestion; services such as schools, transport and shops lagging behind new housing.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.4.4] Further problems: high cost of extending water, sewerage and public transport to low-density areas, and weakened community life in dormitory settlements (places where people mainly sleep and travel out to work).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.4.5] Responses: compact growth and higher density near public transport, promoted by the National Planning Framework (2018).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.4.6] Other responses: planned new communities with services built alongside housing, such as Adamstown, Co. Dublin, a planned Strategic Development Zone (SDZ); and greenbelts (protected rings of open land around a city).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.5.b] This study combines satellite images with statistics to measure how farmland has changed between dates.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.5.0] Satellite images from different years show changes in crops and grassland, field size, drainage, reclaimed marginal land (poorer land brought into use), and land lost to housing or forestry.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.5.1] Satellite images repeat regularly, so changes can be tracked within a single season.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.5.2] CORINE land cover is an EU inventory (a mapped record) made from satellite images that shows agricultural, forest, wetland and urban land at each date, so change can be measured. Ireland has been mapped for 1990, 2000, 2006, 2012 and 2018.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.5.3] Statistical information: the CSO Census of Agriculture (about 135,000 farms counted in 2020) and annual crop and livestock surveys give farm numbers, farm size, crop areas and livestock numbers by county.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.5.4] Department of Agriculture data on farm payments add the financial side of land use.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.6.b] The satellite layers are overlaid and joined to the statistics, so the pattern of change can be explained by soils, relief and rainfall.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.6.0] Overlay satellite-derived land use from two dates to map where tillage (growing crops), grassland, forestry and built land changed.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.6.1] Join county statistics to the map, then relate the change to soils, relief and rainfall layers. For example, tillage is concentrated on the drier, flatter, better-drained land of the east and south-east.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.6.2] Most Irish farmland is grassland for cattle and sheep; tillage is concentrated in the east and south-east.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.6.3] Dairy farming expanded after EU milk quotas (limits on how much milk farms could produce) ended in 2015.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.6.4] Some marginal land in the west has been planted with forestry, and farmland on the edge of cities has been lost to urban growth.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.6.5] Uses: checking farmers' land-use claims under the EU's Common Agricultural Policy (CAP, the EU farm payment scheme) by satellite, monitoring crop health and forecasting yields, and tracking wetland drainage, hedgerow loss and nitrate pressure on water.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.6.6] International illustration: satellite images of irrigated desert farming (centre-pivot circles) and of Amazon clearance show agricultural change on a large scale. There is more on this in Satellite imagery.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.7.b] This study combines aerial photographs, maps and forestry statistics to show where forest has been planted and cleared, and why planting went where it did.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.7.0] Aerial photographs of different dates show new plantations as dark, uniform blocks with straight edges and firebreaks (gaps that stop fire spreading), clear-felled areas as bare brown patches with brash (leftover branches), and replanting. Species and age can be distinguished by tone and texture.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.7.1] OS maps show forest areas by symbol (coniferous, deciduous or mixed) and can be compared across editions.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.7.2] Soil, peat and relief maps show where planting took place, on marginal upland, wet mineral soils and peat.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.7.3] Maps of protected areas, Special Areas of Conservation and Special Protection Areas (sites protected for habitats and birds), show where planting conflicts with conservation.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.7.4] Statistical information: the Forest Service's National Forest Inventory (forest area, species, ownership and age by county), annual afforestation (planting new forest) and felling figures, and grant data.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.7.5] GIS analysis: overlay forest cover at successive dates to map expansion; overlay soils, slope and altitude to explain where planting happened.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.7.6] GIS analysis, continued: overlay protected sites, rivers and archaeological sites, with buffers, to identify conflicts; query which counties had the largest increase.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.b] The study shows how much forest Ireland has gained, who planted it and what it has cost the environment.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.0] Forest cover was about 1% of Ireland at the start of the 20th century and is about 11% today (National Forest Inventory, 2022).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.1] Most planting has been of conifers, mainly Sitka spruce, which makes up about half of all forest area, largely on marginal land.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.2] Coillte, the state forestry company set up in 1988, manages most public forest; private, largely farm-based, planting grew with grants.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.3] Benefits: timber and jobs, farm income diversification (more than one source of farm income), carbon storage and recreation.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.4] Problems: dense conifer blocks change the landscape and reduce biodiversity; acidification and sediment in streams harm fish and the freshwater pearl mussel; open upland habitat for birds such as the hen harrier is lost.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.5] Further problems: planting on peat releases carbon; clear-felling (cutting all trees in an area) causes erosion; forestry replacing farms has caused local opposition, for example in Co. Leitrim.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.6] Ash dieback, a tree disease first found in Ireland in 2012, is a disease risk.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.8.7] Management: more broadleaf planting, buffer zones along rivers, environmental assessment of planting, and continuous-cover forestry (harvesting without clear-felling, so the ground is always covered by trees).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.9.b] The archaeological study uses the official record of known sites, old and new maps, and aerial photographs. The record is the starting point because it says which sites are known and protected.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.9.0] The Record of Monuments and Places (RMP) is the statutory (legally established) list of known archaeological monuments in each county, set up under section 12 of the National Monuments (Amendment) Act 1994.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.9.1] It is published as a written list with OS maps on which each site is circled; the database behind it, the Sites and Monuments Record, is viewable in the National Monuments Service's online Historic Environment Viewer.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.9.2] Anyone intending works at or near a recorded monument must give two months' notice before the works begin, so the site can be assessed.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.9.3] Types of site: ringforts (about 45,000 in Ireland, the most common field monument), megalithic tombs, standing stones, ecclesiastical (church) sites, castles and tower houses, crannógs (lake dwellings), fulachta fiadh (burnt-mound cooking sites), and buried sites known only from below-ground remains.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.10.b] Old maps show where sites once stood and aerial photographs reveal what survives, so overlaying them shows what has been lost.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.10.0] First edition OS six-inch maps of Ireland date from the 1830s–1840s and later editions record sites, for example ringforts drawn as circles. Comparing old maps, modern maps and the RMP shows which sites have disappeared.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.10.1] Aerial photographs reveal sites that are invisible on the ground. Cropmarks: crops over buried ditches grow taller and greener from extra moisture, while over buried walls they are stunted and parched.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.10.2] Other marks: parchmarks (dry patches) in dry summers; soil marks in ploughed fields; and shadow marks, where low sun shows earthworks (banks and mounds).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.10.3] Comparing photographs from different dates shows sites that have been levelled; LiDAR (laser scanning from an aircraft) reveals earthworks under trees.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.10.4] GIS analysis: overlay the RMP point layer on old and new maps and aerial photographs to record which sites survive, are damaged or are destroyed.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.10.5] Overlay development layers (new roads, housing, quarries, forestry and land drainage), buffer each monument to predict threats, and map destruction rates by area.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.b] The study identifies what destroys sites, uses named cases to show it, and supports the measures that protect monuments.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.0] Agricultural improvement (land reclamation, drainage, ploughing and removal of field boundaries) causes destruction.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.1] Other causes: road building, urban and housing development, quarrying, afforestation (ploughing, drains and tree roots), and natural erosion such as coastal erosion of promontory forts and middens (ancient rubbish heaps).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.2] Wood Quay, Dublin: a Viking-age site, excavated and largely removed for Dublin Corporation's civic offices after protests in the late 1970s.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.3] M3 motorway through the Tara–Skryne valley, Co. Meath: opened 2010, and passes through the valley near the Hill of Tara; a henge (a circular earthwork) was found at Lismullin during construction in 2007.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.4] Carrickmines Castle on the M50 route, Co. Dublin: a dispute in the early 2000s.
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.5] July 2018 drought: a henge at Brú na Bóinne, Co. Meath, was spotted from the air (by drone).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.6] Protection the study supports: the RMP and its notification system; archaeological assessment and excavation before development, which means preservation by record (recording the site before it is removed) or preservation in situ (keeping it in place).
[geo-weather-climate-s3.11.7] Further protection: planning conditions; rerouting roads; public awareness; and farm schemes that protect monuments.

CANDIDATES geo-urban-s5: 5. Urban problems in developed-world cities
[geo-urban-s5.0.b] Congestion occurs when the volume of traffic on a road network exceeds the space and capacity available, so vehicles move slowly or stop. Most causes come from how cities grew and how people travel in them.
[geo-urban-s5.0.0] Rising car ownership puts more vehicles onto streets that were laid out for far fewer, so the same road space has to carry more traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.0.1] Low-density suburbs (spread-out housing with few homes per hectare) and commuter towns (towns whose residents travel to work elsewhere) are poorly served by public transport, so most journeys are made by car.
[geo-urban-s5.0.2] Radial roads (roads that run out from the centre like spokes) converge on a historic centre with narrow streets and few river crossings, funnelling traffic into one place.
[geo-urban-s5.0.3] Journeys to work and school peak at the same times of day, so the network is filled all at once.
[geo-urban-s5.0.4] Delivery vehicles and heavy goods vehicles (HGVs, large lorries) share the same streets as commuters and shoppers.
[geo-urban-s5.0.5] Jobs and shopping have dispersed (spread out) to suburbs that can be reached only by car, so even short trips add to road traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.0.6] Historically weak investment in rail has left cities without a fast alternative to the car.
[geo-urban-s5.1.b] Congestion costs a city time, money and health. Several of its effects feed back into more congestion.
[geo-urban-s5.1.0] Workers lose time and productivity, and businesses face higher costs because deliveries and staff are delayed.
[geo-urban-s5.1.1] Commuters suffer stress and a reduced quality of life.
[geo-urban-s5.1.2] Stop-start traffic causes air and noise pollution, with carbon emissions from idling engines (engines left running while the vehicle is stationary).
[geo-urban-s5.1.3] Crowded routes lead to road accidents.
[geo-urban-s5.1.4] Buses are slowed by the same traffic, which makes public transport less attractive and so adds to car use.
[geo-urban-s5.1.5] Businesses and tourism are harmed by poor access to the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.1.6] There is less space for pedestrians and cyclists, because road space is given over to traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.2.b] Dublin is the Irish city the exams use for traffic congestion. Its congestion comes from the way it spread out and the late arrival of alternatives to the car.
[geo-urban-s5.2.0] Sprawl (the spread of low-density development at the city edge) and car dependence bring large numbers of commuters into the city every day by car.
[geo-urban-s5.2.1] Radial routes converge on the centre, and there are few crossings of the River Liffey, so traffic is squeezed onto a handful of bridges.
[geo-urban-s5.2.2] The light rail network (electric trams) arrived late: the Luas opened in 2004, after the city's roads had already become congested.
[geo-urban-s5.2.3] Port traffic formerly ran through the city centre until the Port Tunnel opened in 2006.
[geo-urban-s5.3.b] Cities tackle congestion in two ways: by giving people a better alternative to the car and by managing where and how vehicles travel. These measures are mostly drawn from Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.3.0] Public transport: DART (the electric commuter rail along the coast, opened 1984), Luas (opened 2004), commuter rail and BusConnects (a redesign of the bus network), plus the planned MetroLink, which has no date or cost yet.
[geo-urban-s5.3.1] Luas Cross City linked the Red and Green lines through the city centre in December 2017, so passengers could travel between the two lines.
[geo-urban-s5.3.2] Bus lanes and quality bus corridors give buses priority over cars, so buses are not slowed by general traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.3.3] Park and ride: car parks at the edge of the city where drivers can switch to public transport, so fewer cars enter the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.3.4] Cycle lanes and Dublin Bikes, the public bicycle scheme launched in 2009.
[geo-urban-s5.3.5] Integrated ticketing: the Leap card, introduced in 2011, allows one card to be used across different transport services.
[geo-urban-s5.3.6] Orbital roads and bypasses: the M50 orbital motorway, built in stages from 1990, takes traffic around the city instead of through it.
[geo-urban-s5.3.7] The Port Tunnel, opened in 2006, took port traffic out of the city centre; a 2007 restriction banned most five-axle heavy goods vehicles from the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.4.b] The second group of measures aims to cut the number of car journeys, by making driving dearer or by making journeys unnecessary.
[geo-urban-s5.4.0] Pedestrianisation: the busiest shopping streets are closed to traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.4.1] Parking charges and limits discourage commuters from driving into the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.4.2] Congestion charging: the London Congestion Charge, introduced in February 2003, is a daily charge to drive into central London on weekdays, and traffic in the zone fell in its early years.
[geo-urban-s5.4.3] Car sharing, remote working (working from home) and staggered working hours (different start times) spread traffic over time.
[geo-urban-s5.4.4] Land-use planning that puts homes close to jobs and transport reduces the need to travel by car.
[geo-urban-s5.5.b] Urban decay is the physical, economic and social decline of part of a city, usually the inner city (the older area close to the centre). It is decline inside the built-up area, not growth at the edge.
[geo-urban-s5.5.0] Old industries and port jobs close, removing the economic base of the inner city.
[geo-urban-s5.5.1] Better-off residents move to the suburbs, leaving a smaller and poorer population behind.
[geo-urban-s5.5.2] Buildings are left derelict (empty and falling into disrepair) and are poorly maintained, particularly social housing (homes provided by the local authority).
[geo-urban-s5.5.3] Reduced rates income (the local tax paid by businesses and property) means the local authority has less money to maintain buildings and services.
[geo-urban-s5.5.4] Dereliction attracts crime and vandalism, which deter investment and residents.
[geo-urban-s5.5.5] Road schemes and planning blight: the threat of future road building leaves property uncertain, so owners stop investing in repairs.
[geo-urban-s5.6.b] Once decay begins, its effects reinforce each other, so a run-down area is hard to recover without outside help.
[geo-urban-s5.6.0] Unemployment and poverty among residents of the affected area, because local jobs have gone.
[geo-urban-s5.6.1] Derelict sites stand empty for long periods, because owners and investors see little reason to rebuild.
[geo-urban-s5.6.2] Population decline as families leave, so fewer people remain to support local shops and services.
[geo-urban-s5.6.3] Drug and crime problems, which grow where there are empty buildings and few jobs.
[geo-urban-s5.6.4] Poor services, because fewer people and less money remain to support them.
[geo-urban-s5.6.5] Stigma: the reputation of the area discourages investment and visitors.
[geo-urban-s5.6.6] A cycle of decline, in which each problem makes the others worse.
[geo-urban-s5.7.b] Dublin's inner city shows how the loss of port jobs and the clearing of old housing left an area empty and run down.
[geo-urban-s5.7.0] Dublin's docks lost most of their jobs with containerisation (the shift to shipping goods in standard metal containers handled by machinery, needing far fewer workers) from the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-urban-s5.7.1] Inner-city tenements (old, overcrowded rented housing) were cleared, and their residents moved to the suburbs.
[geo-urban-s5.7.2] Dereliction of inner-city streets and buildings followed through the 1970s and 1980s. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal, which covers the renewal of these areas.
[geo-urban-s5.8.b] Limerick is an Irish example of decay concentrated in social-housing estates rather than in the old centre.
[geo-urban-s5.8.0] Disadvantage (poverty and lack of opportunity) was concentrated in social-housing estates: Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston.
[geo-urban-s5.8.1] After an arson attack on two children in Moyross in 2006, the Government commissioned John Fitzgerald's report, published in 2007.
[geo-urban-s5.8.2] The report led to the Limerick Regeneration programme for Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston. There is more on its results in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s5.9.b] Detroit, Michigan, USA, is known as 'Motor City', the home of Ford, General Motors and Chrysler. Its decline shows decay on a city-wide scale.
[geo-urban-s5.9.0] Population peaked at about 1.85 million at the 1950 US census and fell to about 640,000 at the 2020 US census.
[geo-urban-s5.9.1] Middle-class, largely white families moved to the suburbs from the 1950s, helped by freeways (high-speed urban motorways), which left the city with fewer taxpayers.
[geo-urban-s5.9.2] Car-industry jobs moved out of the city or were automated (done by machines), so the city lost its main employer base.
[geo-urban-s5.9.3] Tens of thousands of houses were abandoned, because families had left and there was no demand for them.
[geo-urban-s5.9.4] Detroit filed for bankruptcy in July 2013, the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history, and exited it in December 2014.
[geo-urban-s5.9.5] Downtown and Midtown have since seen new investment, although many areas remain derelict.
[geo-urban-s5.10.b] Urban sprawl is the uncontrolled outward spread of low-density housing and other development over rural land at the edge of a city. It is growth at the edge, unlike urban decay, which is decline inside the city.
[geo-urban-s5.10.0] Population growth, high prices in the inner city, and demand for houses with gardens push development outwards.
[geo-urban-s5.10.1] Car ownership and motorways make distant sites accessible, so people can live far from where they work.
[geo-urban-s5.10.2] Planning that zoned greenfield land (land never built on before) for development, and developers' preference for cheaper greenfield sites, drive building at the edge.
[geo-urban-s5.10.3] Tax incentives during the Celtic Tiger boom (the period of rapid Irish economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s) encouraged building.
[geo-urban-s5.11.b] Sprawl spreads people thinly over a large area, which is costly for the people who live there and for the city they left behind.
[geo-urban-s5.11.0] Long commutes and congestion, and car dependence, because distances are too long to walk or cycle and public transport is poor.
[geo-urban-s5.11.1] Loss of farmland and habitats, because rural land is built on. There is more on this in Urban land use and planning.
[geo-urban-s5.11.2] Costly services, such as roads, water and schools, spread thinly over a large area, so each household costs more to serve.
[geo-urban-s5.11.3] Dormitory towns, whose residents sleep there and work elsewhere, leaving little local activity.
[geo-urban-s5.11.4] Decline of the city centre as people and spending move out, and higher carbon emissions from longer car journeys.
[geo-urban-s5.12.b] Dublin is the Irish example of sprawl, and its sprawl left a visible legacy when the property market collapsed.
[geo-urban-s5.12.0] Dublin's sprawl has extended into the commuter counties beyond the city boundary.
[geo-urban-s5.12.1] A Government survey in 2010 found almost 3,000 unfinished housing estates, known as ghost estates, after the 2008 property crash (the sudden collapse in house prices and building).
[geo-urban-s5.13.b] Solutions either stop the city spreading outwards or make better use of land already built on.
[geo-urban-s5.13.0] Green belts: a protected ring of open land around the city to stop its spread.
[geo-urban-s5.13.1] Compact growth and brownfield development (reusing land that was built on before), set out in the National Planning Framework, to build on land within the city.
[geo-urban-s5.13.2] Higher densities of housing near public transport, so that fewer people need cars.
[geo-urban-s5.13.3] New towns planned as self-contained settlements with their own services, so residents do not have to travel far.
[geo-urban-s5.13.4] Regeneration of inner-city sites that are vacant or derelict, so growth is directed inwards.
[geo-urban-s5.14.b] A community is a group of neighbours who know each other and share local life. In some urban areas the way housing was planned or built stops this from forming.
[geo-urban-s5.14.0] New estates are built quickly without schools, shops, community centres or public transport, so residents have nowhere to meet.
[geo-urban-s5.14.1] High-rise flats have no shared space where neighbours can meet.
[geo-urban-s5.14.2] Long commutes leave little time for local life.
[geo-urban-s5.14.3] Transient renting populations (tenants who move often) do not put down roots.
[geo-urban-s5.14.4] Old inner-city communities were demolished when tenements were cleared, breaking up established networks of family and neighbours.
[geo-urban-s5.14.5] Social segregation separates people by income and class into different areas.
[geo-urban-s5.15.b] When a place has no shared life, the people in it feel the lack, and the area itself suffers.
[geo-urban-s5.15.0] Isolation and loneliness among residents who do not know their neighbours.
[geo-urban-s5.15.1] Anti-social behaviour, which is harder to deal with where neighbours do not know each other.
[geo-urban-s5.15.2] Little sense of belonging or local organisation, so there are few residents' groups or clubs to take action on local problems.
[geo-urban-s5.16.b] Examples come from Dublin and from Paris. The responses aim to build the facilities and involvement that were missing.
[geo-urban-s5.16.0] Ballymun's tower blocks in Dublin: seven 15-storey towers built 1966 to 1969, demolished 2004 to 2015.
[geo-urban-s5.16.1] Commuter estates and the ghost estates of Dublin's commuter counties.
[geo-urban-s5.16.2] Paris banlieues (the suburbs on the edge of Paris): large grands ensembles (estates built in the 1950s to 1970s) such as Sarcelles, with high unemployment and the riots that began in Clichy-sous-Bois in October 2005.
[geo-urban-s5.16.3] Responses: community facilities built alongside housing, with phasing conditions (planning rules) so that schools and shops come before the homes are occupied.
[geo-urban-s5.16.4] Responses: regeneration with resident participation, mixed tenure (a mix of owned, rented and social homes in one area), and sports clubs and family resource centres.
[geo-urban-s5.17.b] Urban heritage includes historic buildings, streetscapes (the look of a street as a whole), archaeological sites and monuments, together with their setting. It is valuable in three ways.
[geo-urban-s5.17.0] Identity: heritage gives a city and its residents a sense of their history and place.
[geo-urban-s5.17.1] Tourism: historic buildings and monuments attract visitors and the income they bring.
[geo-urban-s5.17.2] Economic value: restored heritage buildings can support shops, hotels and cultural venues.
[geo-urban-s5.18.b] Heritage is easily lost because the pressures of a growing city work against old buildings.
[geo-urban-s5.18.0] Demolition for offices and roads, as when historic buildings are cleared for new development.
[geo-urban-s5.18.1] Neglect and dereliction of old buildings, so that without repair they decay and may have to be demolished.
[geo-urban-s5.18.2] Inappropriate new buildings, with heights that are out of scale with the historic streetscape.
[geo-urban-s5.18.3] Traffic vibration and pollution, which damage old buildings and monuments.
[geo-urban-s5.18.4] Tourist pressure, which can wear down historic sites and crowd out local residents.
[geo-urban-s5.19.b] Dublin has both losses and successes. The losses show the threats above, and the reuse examples show heritage put to work.
[geo-urban-s5.19.0] Lower Fitzwilliam Street: the ESB (Ireland's electricity company) demolished a row of 16 Georgian houses in 1965 to build its head office. That office block has itself since been demolished and replaced.
[geo-urban-s5.19.1] Wood Quay: one of the largest Viking-age sites in Europe. Despite protests and court cases in the late 1970s, Dublin Corporation (the city council) built its civic offices on the site.
[geo-urban-s5.19.2] 14 to 17 Moore Street, where the 1916 leaders spent their last hours before surrender, was declared a national monument in 2007.
[geo-urban-s5.19.3] Positive reuse: Temple Bar was regenerated in the 1990s, and Docklands warehouses have been reused for new purposes.
[geo-urban-s5.20.b] Heritage is protected by law, by voluntary groups and by international listing.
[geo-urban-s5.20.0] The Planning and Development Act 2000 created the Record of Protected Structures (a list of buildings the law protects) and Architectural Conservation Areas (whole areas protected for their character).
[geo-urban-s5.20.1] National monuments are protected by the State.
[geo-urban-s5.20.2] An Taisce (the National Trust for Ireland), founded in 1948, campaigns for the protection of heritage.
[geo-urban-s5.20.3] UNESCO World Heritage listing (international recognition of sites of outstanding value): Ireland's two World Heritage Sites are Brú na Bóinne, listed in 1993, and Skellig Michael, listed in 1996. Neither is urban.
[geo-urban-s5.20.4] International urban examples: the Old and New Towns of Edinburgh, listed by UNESCO in 1995, and the Historic Centre of Rome, listed in 1980.
[geo-urban-s5.21.b] Heritage attracts visitors in smaller cities as well as in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.21.0] Kilkenny's Medieval Mile attracts heritage tourism to a smaller Irish city. There is more on its medieval history in Pre-historic and historic settlement.
[geo-urban-s5.22.b] Environmental quality is how clean, quiet and pleasant an urban area is. Air quality is the first aspect, and Dublin's ban on smoky coal is the Irish example of a response to air pollution.
[geo-urban-s5.22.0] Sources of urban air pollution: smoke from coal fires, and traffic emissions of nitrogen dioxide and particulates (tiny solid particles in the air).
[geo-urban-s5.22.1] Dublin's severe winter smog (smoke and fog mixed) in the 1980s led to the ban on smoky coal in Dublin from 1 September 1990.
[geo-urban-s5.22.2] Research after the ban found that black smoke levels and winter death rates fell.
[geo-urban-s5.22.3] The ban on smoky coal was extended nationwide in October 2022.
[geo-urban-s5.23.b] Cities produce large amounts of sewage and polluted runoff, which must be treated before it reaches rivers and the sea.
[geo-urban-s5.23.0] The Ringsend wastewater treatment plant treats most of Dublin's sewage and discharges into Dublin Bay.
[geo-urban-s5.23.1] The Ringsend plant is being upgraded, so that it can keep treating the sewage of a growing city.
[geo-urban-s5.23.2] Storm overflows: heavy rain can send mixed sewage and surface water into urban rivers and the bay, because the drains cannot carry the extra volume.
[geo-urban-s5.23.3] Urban rivers carry pollution from paved surfaces, roads and drains.
[geo-urban-s5.24.b] Waste that is not recycled or treated becomes litter or goes to landfill, which harms the look and quality of an urban area. Ireland has used charges, recycling and energy recovery to cut it.
[geo-urban-s5.24.0] Litter and landfill: waste that is not recycled or treated goes to landfill (buried in the ground).
[geo-urban-s5.24.1] Plastic bag levy: introduced in March 2002 at 15 cent, after which plastic bag use fell sharply, showing a charge can change behaviour.
[geo-urban-s5.24.2] Recycling schemes reduce the amount of waste sent to landfill, because materials are reused instead of buried.
[geo-urban-s5.24.3] Poolbeg (Dublin Waste-to-Energy) incinerator, operating since 2017, burns residual waste (what is left after recycling) to generate energy.
[geo-urban-s5.25.b] Not all pollution is chemical. Noise and an ugly environment also lower the quality of life in a city.
[geo-urban-s5.25.0] Noise from traffic, construction and night-time activity affects residents, for example by disturbing sleep.
[geo-urban-s5.25.1] Visual pollution: graffiti, poor signage and unsightly derelict buildings reduce the quality of the streetscape.
[geo-urban-s5.25.2] Derelict sites: the Derelict Sites levy is a charge on owners of derelict property to encourage its reuse.
[geo-urban-s5.26.b] Green space gives wildlife a home and gives residents somewhere to relax. Dublin has several examples.
[geo-urban-s5.26.0] Parks provide habitats and recreation: Phoenix Park, about 700 hectares, established 1662.
[geo-urban-s5.26.1] Canals provide green corridors (strips of greenery that wildlife can move along) through the city: the Royal Canal and the Grand Canal.
[geo-urban-s5.26.2] North Bull Island, in Dublin Bay, is a green space beside the city.
[geo-urban-s5.26.3] Loss of green space to infill: small gaps between buildings are built on, reducing open space for wildlife and residents.
[geo-urban-s5.27.b] Building over land changes how water and heat behave in a city, which causes two problems.
[geo-urban-s5.27.0] Urban flooding: paved surfaces stop rain soaking into the ground, so runoff (water flowing over the surface) rises quickly into rivers and drains.
[geo-urban-s5.27.1] Hurricane Charley, in August 1986, caused severe flooding of the River Dodder in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.27.2] Urban heat island: built-up areas are warmer than the surrounding countryside because buildings and paving absorb and hold heat.

CANDIDATES geo-urban-s1: 1. Pre-historic and historic settlement
[geo-urban-s1.0.b] Geographers describe where and why a settlement exists with three terms: site, situation and function. Keeping them apart lets you explain a settlement's location precisely.
[geo-urban-s1.0.0] Site is the exact ground a settlement is built on, such as a dry point (a patch of dry ground in wet land), a wet point (a water source), a defensive hill, a river loop, a bridging point (where a river can be crossed) or a sheltered harbour.
[geo-urban-s1.0.1] Example of site: Mount Sandel, near Coleraine, Co. Derry, on a bluff (a steep bank) above the River Bann.
[geo-urban-s1.0.2] Situation is where the settlement lies relative to the land around it, including its routes, resources and hinterland (the surrounding area it serves). Example: Clonmacnoise, Co. Offaly, beside the River Shannon where the Esker Riada routeway (a ridge of glacial gravel used as a road) crosses it.
[geo-urban-s1.0.3] Function is the economic and social purpose of a settlement, what people there do. Example: Glendalough, Co. Wicklow, where a monastery (a community of monks) served a religious function.
[geo-urban-s1.1.b] People in every period chose where to settle using the same practical factors. Later blocks apply these factors to each period in turn, and they are the reasons an exam answer on why a town developed at its location should give.
[geo-urban-s1.1.0] Water supply: a river, lake or spring gave drinking water, fishing and a means of transport.
[geo-urban-s1.1.1] Defence: high ground, cliffs, river loops, islands and marsh edges made a site harder to attack.
[geo-urban-s1.1.2] Fertile, well-drained land: soil that supported crops and grazing without flooding.
[geo-urban-s1.1.3] Shelter: sheltered valleys, bays and estuaries (wide river mouths where the river meets the sea) protected people and boats from the weather.
[geo-urban-s1.1.4] Routeways and fords or bridging points: places where roads, valleys or rivers could be crossed easily, so people and trade passed through.
[geo-urban-s1.1.5] Resources: fish, timber and stone, which were needed for food, fuel and building.
[geo-urban-s1.1.6] Religious or political importance: a holy site or a seat of power attracted people and the services they needed.
[geo-urban-s1.1.7] Finding and naming historic settlements on an OS map is a separate skill. There is more on this in Map interpretation.
[geo-urban-s1.2.b] The Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age) people were the first to live in Ireland. They were hunter-gatherers, meaning they lived by hunting, fishing and collecting wild food rather than farming, so they had no reason to stay in one place.
[geo-urban-s1.2.0] They made temporary camps on coasts, lakeshores and river banks, where they could fish and gather food. This is why their sites are camps, not farms.
[geo-urban-s1.2.1] Mount Sandel, near Coleraine, Co. Derry, is the earliest known house site in Ireland. It is Mesolithic and over 9,000 years old, such as a camp beside a river.
[geo-urban-s1.2.2] The people of Mount Sandel fished for salmon and eels and gathered hazelnuts, which shows how they lived off wild food.
[geo-urban-s1.3.b] Neolithic (New Stone Age) farmers cleared forest for fields and lived in permanent farmsteads, so settlement became fixed in place. They left large stone tombs and field walls that still survive.
[geo-urban-s1.3.0] Megalithic tombs (tombs built of huge stones) were built on high, visible ground, so they could be seen across the landscape. The main types are court, portal (dolmen), passage and wedge tombs.
[geo-urban-s1.3.1] Poulnabrone, in the Burren, Co. Clare, is a portal tomb (dolmen), such as the Neolithic tombs built on high, visible ground.
[geo-urban-s1.3.2] Brú na Bóinne, Co. Meath, lies in a bend of the River Boyne and holds the passage tombs of Newgrange, Knowth and Dowth.
[geo-urban-s1.3.3] Newgrange is about 5,000 years old (about 3200 BC). Brú na Bóinne has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site (a place protected as important to all humanity) since 1993.
[geo-urban-s1.3.4] Céide Fields, near Ballycastle, north Co. Mayo, is a Neolithic field system of stone walls preserved under blanket bog (a thick layer of peat that grew over the land). It is over 5,000 years old.
[geo-urban-s1.3.5] The walls of the Céide Fields show organised farming in the Neolithic period, because the fields were planned and divided by walls.
[geo-urban-s1.4.b] In the Bronze Age and Iron Age people learned to work metal, and defence and royal power began to shape where they settled. Some sites were for ceremony or kingship rather than everyday living.
[geo-urban-s1.4.0] Metalworking developed in the Bronze Age and Iron Age, so tools and weapons improved. Standing stones and stone circles survive from this period.
[geo-urban-s1.4.1] Fulachta fia were Bronze Age cooking sites beside streams, so water was close at hand.
[geo-urban-s1.4.2] Hillforts and promontory forts (forts on headlands jutting into the sea) gave defence on high ground and headlands.
[geo-urban-s1.4.3] Dún Aonghasa, on Inis Mór in the Aran Islands, Co. Galway, is a stone fort on the edge of a sheer sea cliff, so the cliff itself defends one side.
[geo-urban-s1.4.4] Crannógs were artificial islands in lakes, used over a long period. The water around them made them hard to attack.
[geo-urban-s1.4.5] The Hill of Tara, Co. Meath, was the traditional seat of the High Kings of Ireland. This is why it was a ceremonial site, not a town.
[geo-urban-s1.4.6] Navan Fort (Emain Macha), Co. Armagh, was the royal site of Ulster.
[geo-urban-s1.4.7] The Hill of Tara and Navan Fort were royal and ceremonial sites, not towns.
[geo-urban-s1.5.b] From the 5th century, in the early Christian (early medieval) period, many farmers lived in defended farmsteads called ringforts. Unlike the later towns, these were spread across the countryside.
[geo-urban-s1.5.0] Ringforts (raths and lios; stone versions are cashels, cahers and dúns) were dispersed (spread out, not clustered), defended farmsteads, enclosed by a bank and ditch or a stone wall.
[geo-urban-s1.5.1] Ringforts were built on well-drained hillsides, which kept the farmstead dry and gave a view of approaching danger.
[geo-urban-s1.5.2] Souterrains were underground stone passages built alongside ringforts, so they are found at ringfort sites.
[geo-urban-s1.5.3] Over 40,000 ringforts survive in Ireland.
[geo-urban-s1.5.4] Place-name evidence: kil or cill (church), rath or lios (ringfort) and dun (fort) mark early Christian sites, so a townland name can show an old settlement.
[geo-urban-s1.5.5] Ringforts are early medieval. They are not prehistoric, Viking or Norman.
[geo-urban-s1.6.b] Monasteries were the first centres of people and trade in the early Christian period. They were proto-towns (early forms of town), not true towns, because they lacked the full range of town functions.
[geo-urban-s1.6.0] Monasteries were sited on routeways, rivers, eskers (long ridges of glacial gravel that made dry routes) or remote islands, so they were either easy to reach or secluded.
[geo-urban-s1.6.1] Glendalough, Co. Wicklow, was founded by St Kevin in the 6th century in a glaciated valley (a valley shaped by ice).
[geo-urban-s1.6.2] Clonmacnoise, Co. Offaly, was founded by St Ciarán in the mid-6th century beside the River Shannon.
[geo-urban-s1.6.3] Skellig Michael, Co. Kerry, is an island monastery and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1996.
[geo-urban-s1.6.4] Kells, Co. Meath, and Armagh are towns that grew from early monasteries.
[geo-urban-s1.6.5] Round towers served as bell towers and refuges, places to shelter from attack.
[geo-urban-s1.6.6] High crosses (tall carved stone crosses) and holy wells also mark early Christian sites.
[geo-urban-s1.7.b] The Vikings founded Ireland's first true towns, which began as fortified bases for their ships. They chose sites for trade and access by sea.
[geo-urban-s1.7.0] From the 9th century, Viking raids were followed by longphorts, fortified ship bases.
[geo-urban-s1.7.1] The Vikings sited their settlements on sheltered estuaries and harbours at river mouths, for trade and access by sea.
[geo-urban-s1.7.2] Dublin was founded as a longphort in 841, on the Liffey near its mouth, so ships could come and go by sea.
[geo-urban-s1.7.3] Waterford was founded by Vikings in 914, on the Suir estuary, so ships could reach the sea.
[geo-urban-s1.7.4] Limerick was a Viking settlement from about 922, on an island in the Shannon, which made it easy to defend.
[geo-urban-s1.7.5] Wexford and Cork were also Viking towns. Like Dublin, they were coastal or estuary sites suited to trade by sea.
[geo-urban-s1.7.6] Norse (Viking) place names include Wexford, Waterford, Wicklow and Arklow. Leixlip, Co. Kildare, comes from Old Norse for salmon leap.
[geo-urban-s1.7.7] Wood Quay, Dublin: excavations in the 1970s uncovered a large Viking town site. There is more on this in Urban problems in developed-world cities.
[geo-urban-s1.8.b] The Normans (Anglo-Normans) took over and extended existing Viking towns rather than founding them. They also built castles to hold the land they conquered.
[geo-urban-s1.8.0] The Normans landed at Bannow Bay, Co. Wexford, in 1169.
[geo-urban-s1.8.1] Strongbow (Richard de Clare) took Waterford in 1170, a Viking town, which shows the Normans captured towns rather than founding them.
[geo-urban-s1.8.2] Motte and bailey castles came first. A motte was an earth mound topped by a wooden tower, with a fortified enclosure (the bailey) beside it. Stone castles followed.
[geo-urban-s1.8.3] Trim Castle, Co. Meath, on the River Boyne, is the largest Anglo-Norman castle in Ireland, a stone castle of the kind that followed the mottes. Hugh de Lacy began it in the 1170s.
[geo-urban-s1.8.4] Carrickfergus Castle, Co. Antrim, was built by John de Courcy from 1177.
[geo-urban-s1.9.b] Norman towns were sited at bridging points and on navigable rivers in fertile lowlands, for defence and control of land and trade. They were planned places with a set of recognisable features.
[geo-urban-s1.9.0] Walled towns had gates, market squares, charters (documents granting the town rights), burgage plots (long narrow strips of land rented by townspeople), parish churches and abbeys. The walls gave defence and the market square supported trade.
[geo-urban-s1.9.1] Manor villages grew up around a lord's manor (his estate and house), with tenants farming the surrounding land.
[geo-urban-s1.9.2] Athenry, Co. Galway, and Fethard, Co. Tipperary, have among the most complete medieval town walls in Ireland, which survive as evidence of Norman defence.
[geo-urban-s1.10.b] Kilkenny shows how a Norman town became a centre of power. Its castle, and later laws and assemblies, tie it to the history of Norman Ireland.
[geo-urban-s1.10.0] Kilkenny is a Norman town on the River Nore. A stone castle was built there by William Marshal around 1200.
[geo-urban-s1.10.1] The Statutes of Kilkenny, 1366, tried to stop the Normans adopting Irish language and customs.
[geo-urban-s1.10.2] The Confederation of Kilkenny was formed in 1642.
[geo-urban-s1.11.b] After the first Norman phase, lords built fortified homes of their own. Outside the Norman towns, much of the country was run on a different pattern.
[geo-urban-s1.11.0] Tower houses were fortified homes of local lords, built in the 15th and 16th centuries, so lords could defend themselves.
[geo-urban-s1.11.1] Gaelic Ireland (the parts ruled by Irish lords, not the Normans) was largely without towns.
[geo-urban-s1.12.b] A plantation was the official settling of land with new settlers loyal to the Crown. The Laois and Offaly, Munster and Ulster plantations each produced planned towns.
[geo-urban-s1.12.0] Plantation of Laois and Offaly, 1556: Maryborough (now Portlaoise) and Philipstown (now Daingean) were planned as plantation towns.
[geo-urban-s1.12.1] Munster Plantation, from the 1580s: Bandon, Co. Cork, is a plantation town.
[geo-urban-s1.12.2] Plantation towns had a central square or diamond, a wide main street, a market house and an Established (Protestant) church. Donegal town is an Ulster example.
[geo-urban-s1.12.3] Ulster Plantation, from 1609: Derry was renamed Londonderry in 1613.
[geo-urban-s1.12.4] Derry's city walls were built 1613–1618. They are the only complete city walls in Ireland.
[geo-urban-s1.13.b] Improving landlords (estate owners who invested in their land) laid out planned towns beside their estates, with wide streets, squares, malls (tree-lined walks) and market houses. Georgian (18th-century) towns followed the same orderly pattern.
[geo-urban-s1.13.0] Westport, Co. Mayo, was laid out in the 18th century by the Browne family of Westport House. It has the Octagon and a tree-lined Mall along the Carrowbeg River.
[geo-urban-s1.13.1] Birr, Co. Offaly, is a Georgian planned town of the Parsons family (Earls of Rosse), built beside Birr Castle, so the landlord's home and his town sit together.
[geo-urban-s1.13.2] The Leviathan telescope at Birr, built in 1845, was the largest telescope in the world for decades.
[geo-urban-s1.13.3] Kenmare, Co. Kerry, was laid out in 1775 by the first Marquess of Lansdowne.
[geo-urban-s1.13.4] Adare, Co. Limerick, is an estate village of the Dunraven estate, with thatched cottages.
[geo-urban-s1.13.5] The Wide Streets Commissioners were set up in Dublin in 1757. Georgian Dublin has wide streets, squares such as Merrion Square and Fitzwilliam Square, and red-brick terraces (rows of joined houses).
[geo-urban-s1.13.6] Limerick's Georgian Newtown Pery was laid out in the late 18th century on land of Edmund Sexton Pery.
[geo-urban-s1.13.7] The full development of Georgian Dublin is a larger story. There is more on this in A Core Irish Region.
[geo-urban-s1.14.b] In the 19th century transport and industry created new towns, while poverty and famine emptied parts of the countryside.
[geo-urban-s1.14.0] Canals and railways created railway junction towns, where lines met and traffic changed, and seaside resorts, which people could now reach.
[geo-urban-s1.14.1] Mills and industrial villages were a feature of the 19th century.
[geo-urban-s1.14.2] The first railway in Ireland, Dublin–Kingstown (Dún Laoghaire), opened in 1834.
[geo-urban-s1.14.3] The Irish Poor Law Act of 1838 built a workhouse (an institution housing the poor) in each Poor Law union (a district grouped for poor relief).
[geo-urban-s1.14.4] The Great Famine (1845–49) and emigration emptied much of the rural west. This is why the west had fewer people afterwards.
[geo-urban-s1.14.5] Clonmel, Co. Tipperary: Charles Bianconi began Ireland's first public horse-drawn passenger car service there in 1815, so people could travel between towns.
[geo-urban-s1.14.6] Belfast grew on linen and shipbuilding, such as Harland and Wolff, who built the Titanic, launched in 1911.
[geo-urban-s1.15.b] In the modern period the State began to plan new towns, and cities spread outward.
[geo-urban-s1.15.0] Shannon, Co. Clare, was Ireland's first planned new town. It was built from the 1960s for workers at the Shannon Free Zone (an area set up in 1959 to attract industry) beside Shannon Airport.
[geo-urban-s1.15.1] The Wright Report of 1967 set out new towns for Dublin at Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown.
[geo-urban-s1.15.2] Suburban expansion and commuter towns (towns whose residents travel out to work) grew outward from cities.
[geo-urban-s1.16.b] A modern Irish town keeps the features of each historic phase in its street pattern and buildings. Kilkenny is the worked example.
[geo-urban-s1.16.0] The street plan links Kilkenny Castle and the cathedral. It is promoted as the Medieval Mile. This is why the old castle and church still shape the town.
[geo-urban-s1.16.1] St Canice's Cathedral, with its round tower beside it, marks the early Christian monastery that gave the city its name, Cill Chainnigh, meaning church of Canice.
[geo-urban-s1.16.2] Kilkenny has city status.
[geo-urban-s1.16.3] In an Irish town, walls and gates date from the Norman period, the market square and market house from the plantation and landlord towns, Georgian terraces from the 18th century, and mills and the railway station from the 19th century.
