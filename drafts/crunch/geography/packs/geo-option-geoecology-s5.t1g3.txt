TARGET geo-option-geoecology-s5: 5. Human alteration of biomes
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.0.b] A biome (a large natural community of plants and animals, shaped by climate) has a natural state. People have changed almost every one, and the exam asks how.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.0.0] Natural (climax) vegetation is what would grow if the land were left undisturbed. Very little of the earth's land remains untouched, so most biomes now show some human influence.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.0.1] Humans alter biomes by removing, replacing or degrading vegetation, changing soils and water, introducing or removing species, and polluting.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.0.2] The effects reach every part of the system, because the parts are linked: climate, soil, plants, animals and people all change together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.1.b] Before people changed it, Ireland's natural vegetation was forest. The forest spread as the ice retreated.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.1.0] After the last glaciation (the last ice age) ended about 10,000 years ago, Ireland was colonised first by birch and hazel, then by pine, oak, elm and ash.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.1.1] By about 6,000 years ago forest covered almost all of the island. This was the temperate deciduous forest biome (broadleaved trees that lose their leaves in winter).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.2.b] The first big change came when people stopped hunting and gathering and began to farm. Farming needs open land, so forest had to go.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.2.0] Mesolithic hunter-gatherers (the Middle Stone Age, people who lived by hunting and gathering) had little impact on the forest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.2.1] Farming (the Neolithic, the New Stone Age) reached Ireland about 6,000 years ago, about 4000 BC. Farmers cleared forest with stone axes and fire to grow cereals and graze animals. This was the start of human alteration of the Irish biome.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.2.2] A sharp fall in elm pollen (found in layers of old soil and bog) around this time, the elm decline, is seen across Ireland and Britain. Clearance by farmers and elm disease are both thought to have contributed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.3.b] The Céide Fields show what clearance did to Irish soils. Farmland created by clearance was later buried by bog.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.3.0] Neolithic farmers built stone-walled fields here, about 5,500 years ago. The fields are among the oldest known field systems in the world.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.3.1] Blanket bog (a thick peat cover over wide areas of upland and west-coast land) later covered the fields. Forest clearance, a wetter climate and leaching (nutrients washed out of the soil by rain) turned the forest soils into bog, and peat grew over the walls.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.3.2] A local teacher, Patrick Caulfield, found the stone walls under the bog while cutting turf in the 1930s. His son, the archaeologist Séamas Caulfield, later studied the site.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.b] Clearance went on for thousands of years, but the greatest loss of Irish forest came much later, under conquest and trade.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.0] Earlier clearance continued through the Bronze and Iron Ages and in early Christian and medieval farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.1] The great clearance came in the 16th and 17th centuries, during the Tudor conquest and plantations. Woods were felled for timber exports, shipbuilding, barrel staves and charcoal for ironworks.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.2] Woods were also felled to remove cover used by Irish resistance, because forest gave fighters places to hide.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.3] By 1900 Ireland was among the least wooded countries in Europe, with about 1% of the island forested.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.4] Forest cover is now about 11%, mostly conifer plantations (cone-bearing evergreen trees grown in rows), so most new forest is not the original woodland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.5.b] Clearance did not just remove trees. It changed the land cover, the wildlife and the soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.5.0] Native deciduous forest was replaced by farmland, hedgerows, bog and grassland, which is why little of the original forest remains.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.5.1] Woodland species declined because their habitat was lost. The wolf was hunted to extinction, the last one generally said to have been killed in the late 1700s.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.5.2] Soils under heath (open land of low shrubs such as heather) were leached and podzolised (made acidic and infertile, with a pale layer where nutrients have washed out), and blanket bog spread across the uplands.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.6.b] Ireland has tried to bring trees back, but the new forests are not the old ones.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.6.0] State afforestation (planting trees on land that had none) has mostly used fast-growing, non-native conifers, chiefly Sitka spruce (native to western North America), because they grow quickly for timber.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.6.1] Sitka plantations form a new, species-poor habitat, with few kinds of plant and animal, and acidify soils and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.6.2] Native woodland survives in remnants, such as the oakwoods of Killarney National Park, Co. Kerry, and the Reenadinna yew wood on the Muckross peninsula, a rare pure yew wood.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.b] Forests around the Mediterranean Sea have been cut since ancient times, so the damage there is a clear European example.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.0] Deforestation since ancient times, for shipbuilding, fuel, farming and goat grazing, degraded the evergreen oak and pine forest to maquis (dense scrub) and garrigue (low, open scrub on thin soils).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.1] Soil erosion on the cleared slopes followed, because tree roots no longer held the soil. There is more on this in A Peripheral European Region: the Mezzogiorno.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.2] The cedars of Lebanon, prized by the ancient Egyptians and Phoenicians for shipbuilding and temples, survive only in small groves, such as the Cedars of God grove, a UNESCO World Heritage site.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.3] Global example, Iceland: birch woodland was cleared and grazed after settlement. There is more on this in Human interference with soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.8.b] The Amazon in Brazil is the main case study for felling (cutting down) tropical rainforest. Satellites measure how fast it is being cleared, and the rate has risen and fallen sharply.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.8.0] Brazil's space agency, INPE, measures annual deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon by satellite (the PRODES programme).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.8.1] Clearance peaked at about 27,800 km² in 2004, fell to about 4,600 km² in 2012, rose to about 13,000 km² in 2021, and fell again from 2023 under renewed enforcement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.8.2] Close to a fifth of the original Amazon forest has been cleared, so a large share of the biome is already gone.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.b] Forest is cleared to make money from the land. Farming and timber are the first group of causes, and cattle ranching is the biggest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.0] Cattle ranching is the largest single cause and accounts for most of the cleared land in the Brazilian Amazon. It is the cause, not soya or logging, to name first.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.1] Soya farming, for example, spread north from Mato Grosso. Much of the soya is exported as animal feed, including to Europe and China, which links the forest to distant demand.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.2] Logging of hardwoods: selective logging (taking only chosen valuable trees) opens roads and gaps in the canopy that lead to further clearance.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.3] Government policy and debt, together with global demand for beef, soya, timber and minerals, drive clearance.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.b] Big building projects open the forest up. Roads bring settlers, mines need power and fuel, and dams flood land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.0] Road building and planned settlement: the Trans-Amazonian Highway (BR-230), begun in the early 1970s by the military government, opened the Amazon to settlers from the poor, drought-prone north-east.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.1] BR-364 into Rondônia, paved in the 1980s with World Bank support (the Polonoroeste programme), led to a fishbone pattern of clearings along its side roads, which shows up on satellite images.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.2] Mining: Carajás, Pará state, is one of the world's largest iron-ore mines, operated by Vale and opened in the 1980s. A railway carries the ore to the coast.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.3] Charcoal-fuelled pig-iron smelters (furnaces making crude iron) near Carajás used forest wood, so mining cleared forest beyond the mine itself.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.4] Illegal gold mining (garimpo) on Yanomami land pollutes rivers with mercury.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.5] Hydroelectric dams: the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River, Pará, completed in 2019, is one of the world's largest hydroelectric schemes by capacity. It flooded forest and land, diverted the river past the Volta Grande (Big Bend), and displaced thousands of people.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.6] The Kayapó and other indigenous groups strongly opposed Belo Monte. There is more on river impacts in Human interaction: river processes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.b] Removing the trees affects the animals, the soil, the water cycle and the climate. The effects feed into each other.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.0] Loss of habitat and species, many of them endemic to the region (found nowhere else), so animals that depend on the forest lose their homes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.1] Fragmentation of the remaining forest (breaking it into patches) creates edge effects, as the forest edge is drier, hotter and more exposed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.2] The nutrient cycle is broken: cleared latosols (the deep, nutrient-poor red tropical soils) lose their fertility within a few years, and pasture is abandoned.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.3] Soil erosion and a hard laterite crust (an iron-rich layer that bakes hard) form on the exposed land, and rivers silt up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.4] Less evapotranspiration (water given off by plants into the air) means less rainfall downwind and longer dry seasons, and fires become more frequent.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.5] The forest stores huge amounts of carbon; burning and decay release it to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.6] Scientists, notably Carlos Nobre and Thomas Lovejoy, have warned that losing about 20 to 25% of the forest could push parts of the Amazon past a tipping point (a threshold after which change cannot easily be reversed) towards degraded savanna.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.12.b] People live in the rainforest, and clearance harms indigenous peoples (the original inhabitants) through loss of land, disease and conflict.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.12.0] Indigenous peoples lose their land, suffer disease, and face violence and conflict with ranchers and miners, for example the Yanomami and the gold miners.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.12.1] Chico Mendes, a rubber tapper and union leader who campaigned to protect the forest, was murdered by a rancher's family at Xapuri, Acre, on 22 December 1988.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.12.2] In 2023 the Brazilian government declared a public health emergency in Yanomami territory after illegal gold mining caused malnutrition and disease.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.b] Brazil has laws, markets and monitoring to slow clearance. How well they work has changed with national policy.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.0] Brazil's Forest Code requires landowners in the Amazon to keep 80% of their land as forest, the legal reserve. The code was revised in 2012.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.1] The Amazon Soy Moratorium (2006): major traders agreed not to buy soya grown on newly deforested Amazon land, which removes the market for that soya.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.2] The Amazon Fund (2008), with Norway the largest donor, pays for forest protection, so money from abroad supports enforcement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.3] INPE's PRODES annual figures and DETER near-real-time alerts guide enforcement by IBAMA, the federal environment agency, so illegal clearing can be found and acted on.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.4] Protected areas, indigenous territories and extractive reserves, where local people harvest rubber and nuts sustainably, were set up after Chico Mendes's death; the Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve in Acre is one example.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.5] Evaluation: the falls and rises in clearance show that protection depends on political will and enforcement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.b] Rainforest in Borneo and Sumatra is a second example. Here the main driver is palm oil, a vegetable oil from oil palm trees.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.0] Indonesia and Malaysia together produce about 85% of the world's palm oil, so these two countries dominate world supply.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.1] Lowland rainforest and peat swamp forest (waterlogged forest growing on thick peat) in Borneo and Sumatra are cleared for oil palm plantations and timber.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.2] Drained and burned peat causes regional haze (smoke pollution), notably in 1997 and 2015, affecting Singapore and Malaysia.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.3] The Bornean orangutan was listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN in 2016, making it the flagship threatened species (the animal that stands for the wider loss) of this clearance.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.15.b] European consumers drive some of the clearance, and the EU has begun to act on it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.15.0] Europe's demand for beef, soya (as animal feed), timber and palm oil drives felling.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.15.1] The EU Deforestation Regulation (adopted 2023) will require firms selling beef, soya, palm oil, cocoa, coffee, rubber and timber in the EU to prove they were not grown on land deforested after 2020. Its application has been delayed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.16.b] Intensive farming tries to get as much food as possible from each patch of land. This changes the habitats on and around the farm.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.16.0] Intensive farming uses high inputs of capital (money), machinery, fertiliser, pesticides and water to get maximum output per hectare.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.16.1] It creates monocultures (one crop grown over a large area), larger fields and drained wetlands, so habitats become fewer and simpler.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.17.b] Irish farming has intensified in both dairying (milk production) and tillage (growing crops on ploughed land). The changes removed habitats and polluted water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.17.0] EU milk quotas (limits on how much milk each farm could produce) ended on 1 April 2015, after which Irish dairy herds expanded.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.17.1] Hedgerows were removed and fields enlarged; wetlands and callows (wet meadows beside a river) were drained; species-rich meadows were reseeded with ryegrass, a single grass grown for feed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.17.2] Silage (grass cut and stored for animal feed) replaced hay. Earlier, mechanised cutting destroys the nests of ground-nesting birds such as the corncrake.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.17.3] The corncrake has declined to a small population mainly in north-west Donegal, west Mayo and the Shannon Callows.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.17.4] Wild bees and farmland birds have declined, because the hedgerows and meadows that fed and sheltered them were lost.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.18.b] Heavy use of fertiliser and slurry (liquid animal manure) affects water and the atmosphere. Policies now try to reduce it and help wildlife.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.18.0] Heavy use of fertiliser and slurry raises nitrate levels in rivers and estuaries, especially in the south and south-east.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.18.1] The extra nutrients cause eutrophication of lakes (an overgrowth of algae that uses up the oxygen in the water).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.18.2] Agriculture is the largest source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, over a third of the total, according to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.18.3] Responses: the Nitrates Directive (1991); agri-environment schemes (payments to farmers for farming that helps nature) REPS (1994), GLAS (2015) and ACRES (2023); and the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, begun in 2015.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.19.b] The EU's Common Agricultural Policy (CAP, the EU's system of farm payments) shows how policy can drive intensification across a continent.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.19.0] The Common Agricultural Policy began in 1962. Guaranteed prices encouraged farmers to remove hedgerows, enlarge fields and drain wetlands, notably in the arable east of England (East Anglia).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.19.1] Food surpluses, the butter mountains and wine lakes, followed in the 1980s, because guaranteed prices encouraged farmers to produce more than was needed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.19.2] Later reforms, from 1992 onwards, began paying farmers for environmental farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.20.b] Intensive farming has also replaced grassland biomes, and it overlaps with rainforest clearance.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.20.0] The tallgrass prairie of North America (temperate grassland) has been almost entirely converted to farmland; only a few per cent survives.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.20.1] The Dust Bowl, when ploughed grassland soils blew away, is an example of the soil loss that followed. There is more on this in Human interference with soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.20.2] Cattle and soya in the Amazon are also intensive agriculture; the impacts are those listed under the felling of tropical rainforests.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.b] Irrigation (supplying water to crops by channels) can destroy a whole lake. The Aral Sea in Central Asia is the case study.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.0] The Aral Sea lies on the border of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. In 1960 it was the world's fourth-largest lake.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.1] From the 1960s Soviet planners diverted its feeder rivers, the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, to irrigate cotton and rice in the desert, so less water reached the sea.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.2] The sea shrank to a fraction of its former size and split into separate parts. Its salinity rose and the fishing industry collapsed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.3] Former fishing ports such as Moynaq (Muynak), Uzbekistan, were left far from the shore, with ships stranded on the dry seabed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.4] Dust storms carry salt and pesticide residues from the exposed seabed onto farmland and people, with health effects.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.5] Salinisation (build-up of salt in the soil) and waterlogging damaged the irrigated soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.6] The Kok-Aral Dam (2005), built by Kazakhstan with World Bank funding, raised the level of the small North Aral Sea, and fishing has partly recovered there.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.b] Factories and power stations release gases that make rain acidic. The pollution travels, so it damages forests and lakes far from its source.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.0] Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from coal- and oil-burning power stations, industry and vehicles form acids that are carried downwind, often across national borders.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.1] Acid rain acidified thousands of lakes in southern Norway and Sweden from the 1960s to the 1980s; fish died out in many of them. Its source was largely the emissions of other countries, so the countries that suffered were not the ones that caused it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.2] Effects on coniferous forests include needle loss, dieback (trees dying from the top down) and nutrient leaching from the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.3] Effects on lakes include fish being killed and aluminium being released into the water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.4] The Black Triangle, where the borders of Germany, Poland and the Czech Republic meet, had lignite-burning power stations, so large amounts of acid gases were released (lignite is a low-grade, dirty brown coal).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.5] Large areas of spruce forest in the Ore Mountains (Erzgebirge) and neighbouring ranges died in the 1970s and 1980s, a process known as Waldsterben (forest death).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.23.b] Because acid rain crosses borders, countries had to act together. The same fixes were applied at home.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.23.0] Responses: the Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution (1979); EU limits on power-station emissions; flue-gas desulphurisation (removing sulphur from chimney gases); and liming of lakes in Sweden and Norway, which neutralises their acidity.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.23.1] Sulphur dioxide emissions in Europe have since fallen by well over half, and many forests and lakes have partly recovered.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.23.2] Moneypoint, Co. Clare, on the Shannon Estuary, was Ireland's only coal-fired power station, opened in the 1980s.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.23.3] Flue-gas desulphurisation was later fitted to cut its sulphur emissions, so less acid rain formed. ESB ended coal-burning there in 2025.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.24.b] Industry changed the raised bogs of the Midlands. A raised bog is a dome of peat that formed over thousands of years, and it was harvested for fuel.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.24.0] Bord na Móna, a state company set up in 1946, harvested milled peat on a large scale from the Midland raised bogs for power stations and briquettes (peat fuel blocks).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.24.1] The peat-burning power stations at Shannonbridge (West Offaly) and Lanesborough (Lough Ree) closed in December 2020. Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in 2021, under its Brown to Green plan.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.24.2] Its cutaway bogs (bogs where the peat has been removed) are being rehabilitated and rewetted. Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly, is cutaway bog converted to wetlands, woodland and amenity land, so land damaged by harvesting gains new habitats.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.24.3] Clara Bog, Co. Offaly, is one of the largest relatively intact raised bogs in Ireland, and is a nature reserve and Special Area of Conservation, so it shows what an undamaged raised bog looks like.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.24.4] Peatlands cover about a fifth of Ireland, so changes to them alter a large share of the country's land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.25.b] Getting oil out of sand needs huge areas to be dug up, so the boreal forest above is destroyed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.25.0] The Athabasca oil sands, around Fort McMurray in northern Alberta, Canada, are mined by surface (open-pit) methods that strip away boreal forest (northern coniferous forest) and muskeg (peatland) to reach bitumen-soaked sand.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.25.1] The mining produces large tailings ponds (ponds of polluted waste water) and high greenhouse gas emissions per barrel.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.25.2] Companies must reclaim mined land (restore it), but little of it has been certified as reclaimed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.26.b] Oil extraction can pollute a whole delta, where creeks, mangroves and farms are close together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.26.0] Oil has been produced in the Niger Delta, Nigeria, since the late 1950s, by Shell and others.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.26.1] Repeated spills from pipelines, and theft, have polluted creeks, mangrove forest (coastal forest growing in salt water) and farmland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.26.2] A UNEP study of Ogoniland (2011) found pollution so widespread that a clean-up could take 25 to 30 years.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.27.b] A nuclear accident killed a forest, and then the absence of people let wildlife return.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.27.0] The Chernobyl nuclear power station in Ukraine, then part of the USSR, exploded on 26 April 1986.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.27.1] Radiation killed a nearby pine forest, because the trees absorbed it, whose needles turned reddish-brown, the Red Forest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.27.2] A 30 km exclusion zone was evacuated. With people gone, so that wildlife is undisturbed, wildlife such as wolves, elk, wild boar and the reintroduced Przewalski's horse has become common there.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.28.b] Industry affects biomes beyond the factory site, through the atmosphere and through the spread of towns and factories.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.28.0] Greenhouse gases from industry shift climate belts and stress biomes, through coral bleaching, thawing tundra permafrost (ground that stays frozen all year), and more drought and fire. There is more on global warming in Option: Global Interdependence.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.28.1] Urban and industrial sprawl (the spread of towns and factories over open land) replaces habitats and seals the soil beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.29.b] Human activity can also repair biomes, so a balanced answer weighs benefits against losses.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.29.0] Not all alteration is negative. Protected areas, rewilding, rewetting of bogs, results-based farming (farmers paid for the wildlife results they achieve, as in the Burren) and pollution controls can restore biomes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.29.1] The central evaluation is the balance between economic gain and the loss of biomes.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s1: 1. Renewable and non-renewable resources in the economy
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.b] A resource is anything in the environment that people use to meet their needs. Resources are classed by whether nature can replace them, and this decides whether a country can keep using them for ever.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.0] Resources include energy, minerals, water, soil, forests, fish, land and scenery, so the word covers far more than fuels.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.1] Non-renewable (stock, finite) resources formed over millions of years, so they are used up far faster than they form and will eventually run out. Examples are coal, oil, natural gas, peat, metal ores and uranium.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.2] Renewable (flow) resources are replaced naturally, so they will not run out however much is used. Examples are wind, solar, hydro (running water), wave, tidal and geothermal heat (heat from inside the Earth).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.3] Some resources are renewable only if managed sustainably (used no faster than they are replaced): forests, fish stocks, soils, groundwater and biomass (plant material used as fuel). Overuse turns them into exhausted resources, as happens with fish stocks. There is more on this in Sustainable economic development.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.b] Three kinds of fuel are non-renewable. Fossil fuels and peat formed from once-living material, and uranium is a finite mineral.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas and peat) formed from the remains of plants or marine organisms buried and compressed over millions of years. Burning them releases the carbon they stored.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.1] Peat is non-renewable because a bog (wet ground where dead plants build up as peat) grows only about 1 mm a year, far slower than it is cut.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.2] Nuclear energy from uranium is an alternative to fossil fuels with low carbon emissions, but it is not renewable because uranium is finite.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.b] Energy is the resource that the rest of the economy runs on. This is why a country's energy supply matters so much to its wealth and security.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.0] Primary energy is energy in its raw form, such as oil, gas or wind. Electricity is a secondary form made from primary sources, for example by burning gas or by turning turbines with wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.1] Renewables give a far larger share of electricity than of total energy, because much of total energy is oil used for transport and heating, which renewables supply little of.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.2] Energy powers industry, transport, farming and homes, so resources are central to the economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.3] Energy security means a secure and affordable supply that is not disrupted by price shocks (sudden large rises in price) or supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.4] A country that imports its energy pays a large import bill (the money spent buying energy from abroad) and is exposed to price shocks and supply cuts.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s7: 7. Human interaction: mass movement
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.b] Mass movement is the downhill movement of soil and rock under gravity. People can make it happen more often and faster, or reduce it, depending on how they treat slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.0] Humans make mass movement more frequent and faster in three ways: by removing the vegetation that binds soil and absorbs water, by adding water or weight to slopes, and by removing support at the base of slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.1] Managing slopes can reduce the risk, because the same three factors can be reversed: keep vegetation, keep water out and keep the base supported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.2] Removing vegetation also exposes soil to rain splash (raindrops knocking soil particles loose), sheet wash (thin water flow over the whole surface), gullying (water cutting channels) and wind, which strip it away. Slopes then become prone to creep, slides and mudflows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.3] Answers may cover soil erosion as well as mass movement, provided the link to slopes and gravity is made.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.b] Overgrazing means keeping more animals on land than it can feed. It removes the plant cover that holds a slope together, so it is one of the three human activities the syllabus names.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.0] Too many animals for the land's carrying capacity (the number it can support without damage) eat the vegetation down to the roots and trample the ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.1] Hooves compact the soil and break the surface of peat, leaving bare patches.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.2] Without roots, soil and peat lose cohesion (the way the particles stick together). Rain runs off the compacted surface or saturates the broken peat, so the ground is both weaker and wetter.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.3] Sheet and gully erosion increase, soil creep (slow downhill movement of soil) speeds up as terracettes (small step-like ridges on a slope) deepen, and peat slides occur.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.4] Sediment from the eroded slopes silts up rivers and damages fish spawning beds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.b] In Ireland, overgrazing occurred on the hill commonage of the west, where grazing land is shared. A payment system encouraged farmers to keep far more sheep than the hills could carry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.0] From the 1980s, EU (CAP) headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage (shared mountain grazing) in western Ireland, including Connemara, Co. Galway, and the mountains of Co. Mayo, Co. Donegal and Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.1] Overgrazing stripped heather and grass and exposed and eroded blanket peat (a thick layer of peat covering upland ground). Silt then entered rivers, harming salmon and trout spawning grounds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.2] Response 1: payments were changed from per-head to per-area, so farmers no longer gained by adding animals.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.3] Response 2: Commonage Framework Plans, from the early 2000s, set lower stocking rates (fewer animals per area).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.4] Response 3: REPS (the Rural Environment Protection Scheme, introduced in 1994) paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.3.b] Each management method tackles the cause of overgrazing: too many animals, or animals staying too long in one place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.3.0] Destocking: reducing the number of animals to the land's carrying capacity, so the vegetation is no longer eaten down to the roots.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.3.1] Rotational grazing, moving animals between areas so that vegetation can recover.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.3.2] Fencing off degraded ground to let vegetation recover, because the roots can then bind the soil again.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.b] Overcropping means growing crops on the same land too intensively, so the soil is worn out. Weak, bare soil is then easily carried away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.0] The same land is cropped year after year without fallow periods (rest years with no crop) or fertiliser. Soil nutrients and organic matter are used up, and soil structure breaks down.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.1] Crops grow poorly, the ground is left bare between harvests, and marginal (poor-quality) and sloping land is ploughed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.2] Loose, dry, structureless soil is washed downslope by rain or blown away by wind, forming rills (tiny channels) and gullies (larger, deeper channels).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.3] On slopes, ploughing up and down the slope channels water and soil downhill, because the furrows act like drains.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.b] The Dust Bowl shows how ploughing up natural cover and then meeting a drought can strip a whole region of its soil.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.0] On the Great Plains of the USA, including Oklahoma and Kansas, natural grassland was ploughed up for wheat in the 1930s.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.1] Severe drought then left bare, dry soil, which wind erosion removed in huge dust storms, because there were no grass roots to hold it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.2] Many farming families were forced to leave, because the land could no longer grow crops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.3] The US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935. Shelter belts of trees (rows of trees that break the wind), contour ploughing (ploughing along the slope) and crop rotation (changing the crop grown each year) were promoted.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.b] The Sahel is an international example in which overgrazing and overcropping act together, with fuelwood cutting added.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.0] The Sahel is the semi-arid (very dry) belt along the southern edge of the Sahara.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.1] Population growth led to more cattle, goats and sheep concentrated around wells and boreholes, so the land around the water was grazed bare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.2] Population growth also led to shorter fallow periods and more cash crops (crops grown to sell), which is overcropping, and to more fuelwood cutting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.3] With droughts, notably in the 1970s and 1980s, the bare soil was eroded by wind and by the heavy rain of the short wet season. This process is called desertification (land turning into desert).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.b] Management either keeps the soil fed and covered, or stops water and wind from carrying it away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.0] Crop rotation and fallow periods, so that soil nutrients recover.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.1] Contour ploughing, ploughing along the slope rather than up and down it, so furrows hold water instead of channelling it downhill.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.2] Strip cropping (growing crops in alternate strips), terracing of steep slopes (cutting level steps into them), and shelter belts against wind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.3] Mulching (covering the soil with plant material) and cover crops keep the soil covered between harvests.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.4] Example: the rice terraces of Banaue, Luzon, Philippines, show terracing of steep slopes that is over 2,000 years old.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.b] Deforestation is the clearing of forest. Trees hold a slope together and manage the water that falls on it, so removing them makes slopes much less stable.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.0] Trees are removed for farmland, timber, fuelwood, roads and building, so forest is replaced by bare or thinly covered ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.1] Tree roots bind soil and anchor it to bedrock. The canopy breaks the force of rain, and trees take up water (interception, where leaves catch rain, and transpiration, where plants release water vapour).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.2] Without trees, more rain reaches and saturates the soil. Roots rot and no longer hold the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.3] Landslides and mudflows become more frequent, especially in heavy tropical rain. More sediment and faster run-off reach rivers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.b] Two international examples show how deforested slopes turn heavy rain into deadly floods and mudflows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.0] Haiti has lost almost all its natural forest, mainly for farmland and charcoal. Neighbouring Dominican Republic has far more forest cover.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.1] In September 2004 Tropical Storm Jeanne caused floods and mudslides off the bare hills around Gonaïves, killing about 3,000 people.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.2] Hurricane Mitch, October 1998: torrential rain over Honduras and Nicaragua caused floods and landslides, made worse by deforested slopes. About 11,000 people were killed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.3] A mudflow from the Casita volcano, Nicaragua, buried villages during Hurricane Mitch.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.10.b] Ireland has little woodland left after centuries of clearance, and modern forestry can still destabilise upland slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.10.0] Historic forest clearance left Ireland with very little woodland. About 1% of Ireland was forested around 1900.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.10.1] Forest cover is now about 11%, so it has risen since 1900, mostly in conifer plantations.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.10.2] Clear-felling (cutting down all the trees in an area at once) of conifer plantations on upland peat, and building forestry and wind-farm roads, can destabilise peat slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.11.b] These methods keep tree roots and canopy in place on the slopes that need them most.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.11.0] Afforestation (planting trees where there were none) and reforestation (replanting where trees were cut) of steep slopes, so roots bind the soil again.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.11.1] Buffer strips of trees beside rivers, so that roots hold the banks and trap sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.11.2] Selective felling (removing only some trees) instead of clear-felling, and restricting felling on steep or peaty slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.b] Building on upland blanket bog can cause peat slides, because the work disturbs a wet, weak slope. Exams accept this as a human cause of mass movement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.0] Wind farms and roads on upland blanket bog involve excavation, dumping of spoil (waste material dug out) on the bog surface and interference with drainage.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.1] Derrybrien, Slieve Aughty Mountains, Co. Galway, 16 October 2003: during construction of a wind farm on a forested blanket-bog hillside, a peat slide of about 450,000 cubic metres moved into the Owendalulleegh River, killing large numbers of fish.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.2] In 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland, a lump sum of €5 million plus €15,000 a day, because no proper environmental impact assessment (a study of likely damage before building) had been carried out.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.3] Meenbog wind farm, near Ballybofey, Co. Donegal, November 2020: a peat slide during construction flowed into the Mourne Beg River and crossed into Northern Ireland, polluting a river system important for salmon.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.b] Anything that steepens a slope, loads it, wets it or removes its support raises the risk of mass movement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.0] Road and rail cuttings steepen slopes or remove support at their base, so the slope above is left unsupported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.1] Building weight on top of a slope adds to the downslope force.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.2] Leaking pipes, drainage and irrigation saturate slopes, because extra water adds weight and reduces friction between particles.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.3] Reservoirs saturate valley sides. Vajont is the example.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.4] Mining spoil heaps (piles of waste rock) are unstable. Aberfan is the example.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.b] Slope risk can be cut by physically strengthening slopes, and by planning so that people avoid or are warned about the danger.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.0] Slope engineering: retaining walls, gabions (wire cages of stones), rock bolts, wire netting and rock-catch fences on cliffs, all of which hold or catch loose material.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.1] Regrading slopes to a gentler angle, and drainage pipes to lower the water content.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.2] Planning: hazard mapping, avoiding building on unstable slopes, and environmental impact assessments before building on peat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.3] Monitoring and warning systems, so that people can leave before a slope fails.
