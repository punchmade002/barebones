TARGET geo-option-geoecology-s3: 3. Human interference with soils
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.0.b] Soil is the thin layer on which almost all land plants and farming depend. People can protect it or damage it, and the damage is hard to undo.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.0.0] Soil forms very slowly but can be lost quickly, so on a human timescale it is a non-renewable resource: once it is gone, it will not be replaced within our lifetimes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.0.1] The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO, the UN food agency) has stated that it can take up to 1,000 years to form 1 cm of soil. This was quoted for the International Year of Soils, 2015.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.0.2] Human activity can degrade soil, which is a negative impact, or conserve and improve it, which is a positive impact.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.b] Overcropping happens when farmers take more from the soil than they give back. It changes the soil's characteristics (its nutrients, humus, structure and water-holding ability) in a series of steps.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.0] Overcropping is growing crops on the same land too intensively, without fallow (leaving land unplanted to recover), rotation (changing the crop each year) or enough organic matter returned, so the soil is exhausted.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.1] Nutrients are removed faster than they are replaced, so humus content (the dark, decayed organic matter in soil) falls and crumb structure (the small stable clumps of soil particles) breaks down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.2] The soil becomes dusty or compacted, holds less water, and is easily eroded by wind and rain, so yields fall.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.3] Monoculture, one crop grown year after year, takes the same nutrients each time and encourages pests.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.4] In the Sahel, cash crops (crops grown for sale) and shorter fallow periods exhaust soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.b] The Dust Bowl shows how ploughing up natural vegetation, followed by drought, can strip away a whole region's soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.0] Place and time: the Great Plains of the USA, including Oklahoma and Kansas, in the 1930s.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.1] The natural grassland was ploughed up for wheat, which removed the roots that had held the soil together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.2] Severe drought then left bare, dry soil that strong winds blew away in huge dust storms. Many farming families were forced to leave.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.3] The prairie soils are chernozems (deep, dark, fertile grassland soils).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.4] Response: the US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935. Shelter belts of trees, contour ploughing and crop rotation were promoted.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.b] Overgrazing damages soil by removing the plant cover that protects it. Animals add a second problem by trampling the ground.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.0] Overgrazing is keeping more animals on land than its vegetation can support, which exceeds its carrying capacity (the number of animals the land can feed in the long term).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.1] Vegetation is eaten down and roots die, so the soil is exposed to rain-splash (the impact of raindrops that knocks soil particles loose) and wind.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.2] Trampling compacts the soil. On wet Irish ground this is called poaching. It reduces infiltration (water soaking in) and increases runoff (water flowing over the surface).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.3] Sheet, rill and gully erosion (washing away of a thin layer, then small channels, then deep channels) remove topsoil and humus. Less litter (fallen leaves and plant remains) means less humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.4] On peat, bare surfaces erode quickly, because loose, light peat is easily washed and blown away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.5] International examples: in the Sahel, livestock concentrated around wells, and in Iceland, sheep grazing prevented birch regrowth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.b] A change in farm payments, not a change in the land, caused overgrazing of Irish hills. Commonage is hill land that farmers use together for grazing.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.0] From the 1980s, EU (CAP) headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in the west of Ireland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.1] The affected areas include Connemara, Co. Galway, and the mountains of Co. Mayo, Co. Donegal and Co. Kerry.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.2] Overgrazing stripped heather and grass, exposed and eroded blanket peat (a thick peat layer covering hills), and sent silt into rivers.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.3] The silt harmed salmon and trout spawning grounds, because it smothers the gravel where the fish lay eggs.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.4] Response: payments changed from per-head to per-area. Commonage Framework Plans, from the early 2000s, set lower stocking rates (fewer animals per hectare).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.5] Response: REPS, the Rural Environment Protection Scheme introduced in 1994, paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.b] Desertification is often misunderstood. It is about land losing its productive soil, and it has both natural and human causes that usually work together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.0] Desertification is land degradation in arid (very dry), semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas, resulting from climatic variations and human activities.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.1] It is the degradation of land on desert margins, not the advance of sand dunes from an existing desert.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.2] Physical causes are drought, unreliable rainfall, high temperatures and evaporation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.3] Human causes are population growth, overgrazing, overcropping, cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood, and poorly managed irrigation causing salinisation (salts building up in the soil as irrigation water evaporates).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.4] The two usually act together. Human pressure leaves the soil bare, then drought and storms remove it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.b] Desertified land loses the qualities that make soil useful, and the people who farm it lose their living.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.0] Loss of topsoil and humus, and lower fertility, because the nutrient-rich surface layer is the part that is removed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.1] Surface crusting, so rain runs off rather than soaking in, and water retention is reduced.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.2] Wind-blown sand and dust, because bare, dry soil has nothing to hold its particles down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.3] Salt crusts where irrigation is poorly managed (salinisation). In extreme cases the ground becomes bare and sterile.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.4] Human effects: falling yields, food shortages and famine, loss of livestock, and migration to cities and abroad.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.7.b] The Sahel is the main example of desertification. Its climate explains why its soils are so vulnerable.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.7.0] The Sahel is the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara. It stretches across Africa from Senegal and Mauritania in the west, through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad, to Sudan.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.7.1] Annual rainfall is roughly 200 to 600 mm. It falls in one short summer wet season, when the ITCZ, the rain belt, moves north.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.7.2] Rainfall is highly unreliable from year to year, so a run of dry years can strip the plant cover.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.b] In the Sahel, human pressure and drought combined: people left the soil bare, then drought and heavy rain removed it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.0] Population growth led to more cattle, goats and sheep concentrated around wells and boreholes (drilled water sources), shorter fallow periods, more cash crops and more fuelwood cutting.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.1] Cash crops such as groundnuts (peanuts) in Senegal and cotton took the best land, pushing food crops and herders onto marginal land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.2] Nomadic herders, whose movements once let pasture recover, became more settled, so land around their camps was grazed continuously.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.3] Droughts, notably in the 1970s and 1980s, left bare soil that was eroded by wind and by the heavy rain of the short wet season.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.9.b] The Sahel shows the soil damage of desertification and its effect on water supply.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.9.0] The soil effects are loss of topsoil and humus, lower fertility, surface crusting, reduced water retention, wind-blown sand and dust, and salt crusts where irrigation is poor.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.9.1] Lake Chad, on the Sahel's border of Chad, Niger, Nigeria and Cameroon, has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s, through drought and water use for irrigation. A widely quoted UN figure gives a shrinkage of about 90%.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.10.b] Because desertification crosses borders, governments have tried to respond together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.10.0] The UN Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD) was adopted in 1994. 17 June is World Day to Combat Desertification.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.10.1] The Great Green Wall was launched by the African Union in 2007. It is a planned band of restored land about 8,000 km long across Africa, from Senegal to Djibouti, aiming to restore 100 million hectares of degraded land by 2030.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.10.2] Progress has been slow and well short of target. The project has shifted from a line of planted trees towards a mosaic of local land-restoration projects.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.11.b] Some of the most effective responses come from farmers using low-cost methods that fit dry soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.11.0] In Burkina Faso, in the Yatenga region of northern Burkina Faso, farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo revived the traditional zaï technique.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.11.1] Zaï are small pits dug in hard, crusted soil and filled with manure or compost. They catch rainwater and concentrate nutrients around the seed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.11.2] Farmers also built stone lines, or bunds, along the contours (lines of equal height) to slow runoff and trap soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.12.b] In Niger, farmers rebuilt tree cover without planting. They simply protected what was already growing.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.12.0] In Niger, farmer-managed natural regeneration (FMNR) means farmers protect and prune tree shoots that regrow from old stumps and roots in their fields.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.12.1] FMNR has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, especially in the Maradi and Zinder regions.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.12.2] Trees shade the soil, cut wind speed and add leaf litter. Species such as Faidherbia albida also add nitrogen.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.13.b] Comparing the Sahel's responses shows what works.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.13.0] Local methods have restored land at scale in Niger, but the Great Green Wall has been slow to meet its targets.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.13.1] Conservation works best when farmers gain from it and take part, as the Niger FMNR and the Burren results-based payments show.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.b] Beyond overcropping, overgrazing and desertification, several other human activities damage soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.0] Deforestation exposes soil to erosion and stops the supply of litter, which is the source of humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.1] Heavy machinery and livestock on wet ground compact the soil and destroy its structure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.2] Excess fertiliser and slurry let nitrates and phosphates wash out of the soil into rivers and lakes, causing eutrophication (nutrient enrichment that makes algae overgrow). Some fertilisers also acidify the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.3] Salinisation and waterlogging (soil saturated with water) occur under badly managed irrigation. Canal irrigation in Punjab and Haryana, India, has caused both.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.4] Peat extraction removes an entire soil, as in Irish bogs, because the peat itself is the soil being cut away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.5] Urban sprawl and roads seal soil under concrete and tarmac, so it can no longer grow plants or absorb rain.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.b] On slopes, water and wind carry soil away. These methods slow the water or wind so the soil stays in place.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.0] Contour ploughing means ploughing across a slope along the contours, so that furrows trap water instead of channelling it downhill.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.1] Terracing cuts steep slopes into level steps, such as the rice terraces of Banaue, Luzon, Philippines, which are over 2,000 years old.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.2] Strip cropping alternates strips of crops and grass. The grass traps soil washed or blown from the cropped strip.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.3] Shelter belts and windbreaks of trees and hedges cut wind speed and reduce wind erosion.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.16.b] These methods keep nutrients and humus in the soil and keep the surface covered.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.16.0] Crop rotation, fallow, cover crops and green manure, such as clover, restore nutrients and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.16.1] Adding manure and compost builds up humus, so the soil holds more nutrients and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.16.2] Reduced or zero tillage (little or no ploughing), and mulching, leave crop residue on the surface to protect the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.17.b] Protecting soil from grazing animals means letting plants recover and using roots to hold the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.17.0] Controlled stocking (limiting animal numbers), rotational grazing (moving animals between fields), fencing and reseeding allow vegetation to recover.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.17.1] Afforestation (planting trees where there were none) and replanting bind the soil with roots.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.18.b] Dry regions need methods that capture scarce rain and protect soil from wind.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.18.0] Stone lines (bunds) along contours, zaï planting pits, and farmer-managed natural regeneration of trees are the dryland methods used in the Sahel, because they capture scarce rain and protect soil from wind.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.b] Conservation protects soil that already exists. These methods go further and improve poor soils, or even create soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.0] Liming raises the pH of acid soils, so they become less acidic and suit more crops.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.1] Field drainage and arterial drainage improve gleys (waterlogged soils). The Arterial Drainage Act, 1945, began state schemes to deepen and straighten Irish rivers to drain wet farmland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.2] Deep ploughing breaks an iron pan (a hard layer in the soil), as in podzols.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.3] Irrigation with proper drainage avoids salinisation, because the drainage carries away the salts instead of letting them build up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.4] Building soil where none existed: on the bare limestone of the Aran Islands, Co. Galway, islanders layered sand and seaweed, and later manure, on the rock inside stone-walled fields.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.20.b] Government schemes pay farmers to protect soil, and EU rules limit pollution from farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.20.0] REPS was followed by GLAS (Green, Low-Carbon, Agri-Environment Scheme), from 2015, and ACRES (Agri-Climate Rural Environment Scheme), from 2023.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.20.1] The EU Nitrates Directive (1991) limits how much organic nitrogen can be spread per hectare and bans spreading slurry during a closed period in winter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.b] The Burren is the syllabus's Irish example of human interference with soil. It is thought that its bare rock is partly a result of early farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.0] The Burren, in north-west Co. Clare, lies on Carboniferous limestone, with extensive limestone pavement of clints (blocks) and grikes (gaps between them).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.1] Its soils are thin, dark, lime-rich rendzinas, found mainly in the grikes and hollows.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.2] Plants from Arctic-alpine and Mediterranean environments grow side by side, e.g. spring gentian and mountain avens.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.3] Poulnabrone, a Neolithic portal tomb (dolmen) over 5,000 years old, is evidence of early farming communities.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.4] It is thought that the Burren once had thicker soil and woodland cover. Clearance and grazing by early farmers, followed by erosion of soil into the grikes and underground, helped expose the bare pavement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.5] The Burren was also glaciated.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.b] On the Burren, grazing is part of the solution. The problem today is too little grazing.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.0] Winterage means cattle are grazed on the upland limestone in winter, because the rock stores heat and the ground is dry.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.1] Grazing keeps grass and scrub down and maintains the flower-rich grassland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.2] From the late 20th century fewer farmers grazed the uplands in winter, because of farm changes, supplementary feeding and labour shortage.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.3] Hazel and blackthorn scrub (low woody bushes) then spread over grassland and pavement, because nothing grazed it back.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.4] On the Burren the main threat is undergrazing, not overgrazing.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.b] Conservation projects on the Burren led to a payment system that rewards farmers for the quality of their land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.0] BurrenLIFE (2005 to 2010) was an EU LIFE-funded project led by Dr Brendan Dunford, with local farmers, the National Parks and Wildlife Service and Teagasc.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.1] It tested scrub removal, better feeding systems and grazing plans.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.2] It was followed by the Burren Farming for Conservation Programme (2010 to 2015) and the Burren Programme (from 2016), funded by the Department of Agriculture.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.3] These pay farmers by results. Each field is scored for habitat quality, e.g. on a scale of 1 to 10, and a higher score earns a higher payment, so farmers decide how best to manage their land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.4] The model has been copied in other Irish agri-environment schemes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.b] Iceland shows how settlers can strip a landscape of trees. Their cutting and their sheep removed the cover that protected the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.0] Iceland was settled by Norse people from about AD 874.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.1] At settlement, birch woodland and scrub covered a large part of the island, an estimated quarter or more. Today native birch woodland covers only about 1 to 2%.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.2] Settlers cut and burned birch for fuel, for charcoal used in iron working, and for grazing land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.3] They kept large numbers of sheep, which grazed the year round and prevented regrowth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.b] Iceland's soil and climate made the damage worse, because the soil is easily blown away and plants recover slowly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.0] Iceland's soils are andosols formed from volcanic ash. They are light, loose and low in clay.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.1] Once the vegetation cover is broken, they are easily blown away by strong winds and washed out by rain.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.2] Erosion escarpments (rofabörð in Icelandic) are steep banks of soil being eaten away from their edges.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.3] Volcanic eruptions add ash that smothers vegetation, so plant cover is lost again after each eruption.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.4] A cold climate and a short growing season mean vegetation recovers very slowly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.b] Iceland also shows the positive side: over a century of work to hold and rebuild its soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.0] The Soil Conservation Service of Iceland (Landgræðslan, now part of Land and Forest Iceland) was founded in 1907. It is one of the oldest soil conservation services in the world.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.1] Methods include fencing off eroding land to keep sheep out, and sowing lyme grass (Leymus arenarius) to stabilise drifting sand.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.2] Other methods are sowing grasses with fertiliser from the air, and replanting birch.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.3] Nootka lupin fixes nitrogen (turns nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use) and binds soil, but it has become an invasive plant that crowds out native species, so its use is controversial.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.4] Farmer-led reclamation projects, called Farmers Heal the Land, began in 1990.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.5] Sheep numbers have fallen from their peak in the late 1970s, which means less grazing pressure on the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.6] Iceland shows both sides: over a century of positive impact through conservation and reclamation, after centuries of negative impact through deforestation, overgrazing and erosion.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.7] Positive and negative impacts can occur on the same land at different times.

CANDIDATES geo-option-geoecology-s1: 1. Soil composition and characteristics
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.b] Soil is the thin layer of loose material on the earth's surface in which plants grow. Its formation is called pedogenesis, and it starts with the rock or deposit the soil forms from, known as the parent material.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.0] Soil is made of four components: mineral matter (particles of weathered rock), organic matter (living and dead material), water and air. Every later block looks at how one of these shapes the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.1] Residual soils form from rock weathered in situ, meaning in place, directly beneath them, so the soil and the rock below are closely related.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.2] Transported soils form on redeposited weathered material that has been carried and laid down by ice, water, wind or volcanic activity, so they can differ from the rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.3] Transported parent materials include glacial till (boulder clay), fluvio-glacial sands and gravels (laid down by meltwater from ice), alluvium (river deposits), loess (wind-blown silt), wind-blown sand and volcanic ash.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.4] Weathering (the breakdown of rock at the surface) produces regolith, the loose layer that soil develops from. Freeze-thaw, carbonation and hydrolysis are among the processes involved. There is more on this in Weathering processes, in Rocks, Weathering and Mass Movement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.b] Named places make the residual and transported types concrete. Remember that the same area can look residual on a map of rock types but be transported in fact.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.0] Residual examples: the limestone of the Burren and the granite of the Wicklow Mountains, which gives acidic, sandy soils. Most Midland soils formed on boulder clay over the limestone, so they are transported, not residual.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.1] Transported examples: the boulder clay (till) plains of the Irish Midlands, and alluvium on the floodplain of the Shannon Callows, meadows beside the River Shannon that flood in winter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.2] Loess (wind-blown silt), called limon in France, covers plateaux of the Paris Basin, such as the Beauce. There is more on this in A Core European Region: the Paris Basin.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.b] Soils take time to develop, and in Ireland the clock was reset by the ice. This is why Irish soils are young and why they often do not match the rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.0] Most Irish soils formed on glacial deposits laid down during the last (Midlandian) glaciation, which ended about 10,000 years ago. There is more on this in Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.1] So most Irish soils have developed in about 10,000 years or less, since the ice melted, and they often differ from the solid rock beneath them because the glacial deposits came from elsewhere.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.b] A soil profile is a vertical section from the surface down to bedrock, divided into layers called horizons. A soil pit or road cutting shows it, and the horizons are named by letters.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.0] O horizon: organic litter (undecomposed plant and animal remains) and humus (the dark, decomposed organic material) on the surface.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.1] A horizon: topsoil, with mineral matter mixed with humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.2] B horizon: subsoil, which receives material washed down from above.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.3] C horizon: weathered parent material, the rock or deposit the soil forms from.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.4] R horizon: bedrock, the solid rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.5] There is more on the full profiles of named soils in Soil processes and the global pattern of soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.b] Soil is mostly mineral matter, with small shares of organic matter, water and air. The figures below are the textbook values for a good loam, a balanced, fertile soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.0] A typical good loam has about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water and 25% air, by volume.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.1] These proportions describe a well-structured loam. They are not fixed values for every soil, so don't present them as true of all soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.2] Water and air share the pore spaces (the gaps between particles), so when one rises the other falls. A wet soil is poorly aerated for this reason.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.3] Diagram: a pie chart of the four components.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.b] Mineral matter is the largest component, and the parent rock decides what kind of mineral matter a soil gets. It is the first of the four components to know for an essay on soil development.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.0] Mineral matter is particles of weathered rock, from stones down to clay. It forms the soil's skeleton and decides its texture (the mix of particle sizes).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.1] As it weathers, mineral matter releases nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium, which plants then take up through their roots.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.2] Parent rock controls the result: limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils (bases are the chemicals that neutralise acid).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.3] Granite gives sandy, acidic soils with quartz grains and clay from weathered feldspar.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.4] Sandstone gives sandy, acidic, free-draining soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.5] Clay particles are the chemically active part of the mineral matter, because they hold on to nutrients and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.b] Organic matter is a small share of the soil by volume, but it gives soil its dark colour, its crumbly structure and much of its fertility. The key is the difference between litter and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.0] Organic matter is living organisms (bacteria, fungi, earthworms, insects and roots) plus dead plant and animal remains.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.1] Litter is undecomposed material. Decomposers (organisms that feed on dead material) break it down into humus, a dark, sticky, jelly-like substance, which is the end product of decomposition.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.2] Humus darkens the soil, binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients, releases nutrients slowly, and is the main store of nitrogen in the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.3] Earthworms mix and aerate the soil, drag litter down into it, and their casts (droppings) improve structure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.4] Charles Darwin's last book (1881), The Formation of Vegetable Mould through the Action of Worms, showed how earthworms mix and enrich soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.5] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (bacteria that turn nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use), such as those in the root nodules of legumes, add nitrogen. Clover is the standard example of a legume in Irish grassland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.b] Humus comes in different forms depending on the vegetation above and the organisms below. The forms are named mull, moder and mor, and each is linked to a soil type.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.0] Mull humus is mild, crumbly, well decomposed and well mixed by earthworms. It is slightly acidic to neutral and forms under deciduous woodland and grassland, giving brown earths.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.1] Mor humus is acidic, fibrous and poorly decomposed, with few earthworms because the acidity does not suit them. It forms under coniferous forest and heath, giving podzols.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.2] Moder is intermediate between mull and mor.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.b] Water enters the soil as precipitation and is held in its pores. It exists in three forms, and only some of it is any use to plants.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.0] Water dissolves nutrients to form the soil solution, from which roots absorb them.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.1] Gravitational water drains down through large pores after rain and carries nutrients away. This is the cause of leaching (the washing of nutrients down out of the soil).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.2] Capillary water is held in small pores by surface tension. It is available to plants and can move up as well as down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.3] Hygroscopic water is a thin film bound tightly to particles, so plants cannot use it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.4] Field capacity is the water a soil holds after gravitational water has drained away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.5] Wilting point is reached when only water that plants cannot extract is left, so the plants wilt.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.b] Which way water moves depends on whether rain or evaporation wins. This decides what the soil loses or collects, and it leads into the soil processes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.0] Where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration (water lost by evaporation and plant transpiration), water moves down and leaches the soil. This is the situation in Ireland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.1] Where evaporation exceeds precipitation, capillary action raises water to the surface and leaves salts and lime there, as in deserts and grasslands.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.2] Too much water fills all the pores and causes waterlogging, which leads to gleying (the grey, rusty-mottled soil of waterlogged ground). There is more on this in Soil processes and the global pattern of soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.b] Air fills whatever pore space water does not. Living things in the soil need it, and whether it is plentiful or scarce changes how the soil forms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.0] Air fills the pores not occupied by water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.1] Soil air supplies oxygen for root respiration and for the aerobic organisms (those that need oxygen) that decompose litter, and nitrogen for nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.2] Soil air is richer in carbon dioxide than the atmosphere. This forms weak carbonic acid in soil water, which aids chemical weathering.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.3] Poorly aerated (anaerobic, meaning without oxygen) soils decompose slowly, so organic matter builds up as peat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.4] In poorly aerated soils iron is reduced (it gains electrons and changes chemical form), giving grey colours.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.b] The four components do not act separately: each changes how the others behave. Showing this interaction is part of a full answer on soil development.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.0] Humus improves crumb structure, which opens pores for both air and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.1] Clay holds water but reduces air, because its tiny pores fill with water and leave little room for air.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.2] Organisms need both air and water to work, so a soil that is too wet or too dry supports less life and less decomposition.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.b] Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay in a soil. It is set by the size of the mineral particles, so it is one of the most basic characteristics of a soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.0] Sand has particles 2 to 0.05 mm across; silt 0.05 to 0.002 mm; clay under 0.002 mm. Particles over 2 mm are gravel or stones. Some textbooks put the sand and silt limit at 0.06 mm, which is also acceptable.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.1] Clay is the smallest particle and sand the largest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.2] Field test by feel: sand is gritty, silt is smooth and silky, and clay is sticky and can be rolled into a ribbon when moist.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.3] Texture is shown on a soil texture triangle, a triangular graph. There is more on this method in Figure interpretation, in Tables, Graphs and Data Skills.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.b] Particle size decides how water, air and nutrients move through a soil and how easy it is to farm. Each texture has strengths and weaknesses, and loam balances them.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.0] Sandy soils are light, free-draining, well aerated, warm quickly in spring and are easy to plough. They hold little water and few nutrients, and they are easily leached.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.1] Clay soils are heavy and hold water and nutrients. They drain slowly, become waterlogged and cold, crack and harden when dry, and are hard to work.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.2] A loam is a balanced mix, roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay. It combines good drainage with good water and nutrient retention, and is the best for farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.3] Silty soils are fertile but form a surface crust and erode easily.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.b] Structure is the way particles are grouped into clumps called peds. Two soils with the same texture can behave very differently if their peds differ.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.0] Crumb: small rounded peds, typical of humus-rich topsoil, and the best structure for farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.1] Granular: a further type of ped structure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.2] Platy: flat, horizontal plates that hinder drainage and root growth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.3] Blocky: angular cubes, common in clay subsoils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.4] Prismatic or columnar: vertical columns, found in some clay subsoils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.5] Structureless soils are single-grain (loose sand) or massive (solid clay), because their particles are not grouped into peds.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.b] Structure matters because it sets the pore spaces that water, air and roots use. It can be built up by natural agents and destroyed by pressure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.0] Structure controls the size and number of pores, so it decides drainage, aeration and how easily roots penetrate.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.1] Humus, clay, earthworms, roots and freeze-thaw build peds.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.2] Compaction (squashing the soil together) by heavy machinery, and trampling by livestock on wet ground (called poaching in Ireland), destroy peds and so close up the pores.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.b] Colour is a clue to a soil's composition and conditions, so reading it tells you what the soil contains and how well drained it is. Each colour below is linked to a cause and a named soil type.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.0] Black or dark brown: rich in humus, as in chernozems and peat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.1] Brown: humus plus iron compounds, as in brown earths.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.2] Red, orange or yellow: iron oxides in a well-aerated soil, as in tropical latosols and terra rossa.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.3] Ash-grey or bleached: heavily leached (washed of its nutrients and colouring), as in the E horizon of a podzol.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.4] Grey or blue-grey with orange (rusty) mottles: waterlogged, with reduced iron, as in gley.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.5] White: lime or salt crusts, as in desert soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.6] Dark soils absorb more heat, so colour also affects soil temperature.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.b] How much water a soil can hold, and how fast it passes through, depends on its other characteristics. Two similar-sounding terms, porosity and permeability, must be kept apart.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.0] Water retention depends on texture (clay retains water, sand drains), structure and organic content. Humus holds several times its own weight of water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.1] Porosity is how much pore space a soil has. Permeability is how easily water passes through it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.2] Clay soils have high porosity but low permeability, because their pores are tiny, so they drain slowly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.b] Organic content is the amount of humus in a soil. It varies a lot between soil types and affects several other characteristics.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.0] It is high in chernozems and peat, moderate in brown earths, and low in desert soils, which have little vegetation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.1] Tropical latosols are surprisingly low in humus, because litter decays so fast that little humus builds up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.2] Organic content affects colour, structure, water retention and fertility.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.b] pH tells you how acidic or alkaline a soil is, which affects which organisms and crops can live in it. It is shaped by climate, parent rock and vegetation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.0] pH is the measure of acidity or alkalinity, on a scale of 0 to 14, with 7 neutral.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.1] Soils become acidic where heavy rain leaches out bases, and where acid litter such as conifer needles or heather builds up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.2] Soils are alkaline on limestone and chalk and in dry climates.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.3] Most crops and earthworms prefer slightly acidic to neutral soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.4] Teagasc, the Irish agriculture and food development authority, advises a soil pH of about 6.3 for grassland and about 6.5 for tillage crops. Liming corrects lower values.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.5] Farmers spread lime, which is ground limestone, to raise pH.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.b] A fertile soil holds nutrients in a form that roots can reach and rain cannot wash away. This depends on tiny charged particles in the clay and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.0] Clay particles and humus form the clay-humus complex. Its tiny, negatively charged particles, called colloids, hold positively charged nutrient ions (charged atoms) such as calcium, magnesium and potassium.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.1] Held nutrients are not washed away, and they are exchanged with roots, so plants can take them up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.2] Base-rich soils are fertile; base-poor, leached soils are not.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.3] NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium) are the main nutrients farmers add.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.b] The characteristics above work together to decide which soil processes dominate and which soil forms. Each link below is one step of the argument an essay needs.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.0] Texture and structure control drainage, so they decide whether leaching, gleying or capillary rise dominates.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.1] pH controls which organisms live in the soil, so it decides whether mull or mor humus forms and how fast humification (the breakdown of litter into humus) happens.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.2] Organic content controls structure and fertility, so it decides how well the soil supports the vegetation that feeds it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.3] Water retention and aeration together decide whether decomposition is aerobic, as in fertile brown soils, or anaerobic, as in peat and gley.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.4] Use brown earth, podzol, gley, latosol and chernozem as illustrations by name.

CANDIDATES geo-option-geoecology-s2: 2. Soil processes and the global pattern of soils
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.0.b] A soil is shaped by its surroundings, so five factors decide what it becomes. The first three are the rock it forms from, the climate above it and how long it has had to develop.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.0.0] Parent material (the rock or loose deposit the soil forms from) supplies the mineral matter and sets the texture, the base content (the supply of nutrient bases such as calcium) and the starting pH (acidity). There is more on this in Soil composition and characteristics.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.0.1] Climate is the most important factor at a global scale, because it controls both how fast rock breaks down and which plants grow.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.0.2] Temperature controls the speed of weathering (the breakdown of rock) and of decomposition (the rotting of dead plant matter). Warmth speeds both, while cold slows both.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.0.3] The balance of precipitation and evapotranspiration (water lost from the soil by evaporation and from plants by transpiration) decides whether water moves down through the soil (leaching) or up (capillary rise, water drawn up through tiny pores). This is why soil belts follow climate belts.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.0.4] Time: a mature soil has had long enough to develop distinct horizons (layers), while an immature soil has thin or missing horizons.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.1.b] The shape of the land and the living things on it also change a soil. Relief (the height and slope of the ground) decides where water and material end up, and organisms add or mix the organic matter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.1.0] Steep slopes have thin, well-drained soils, because material moves downslope.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.1.1] Hollows and valley floors collect water and have deep, wet soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.1.2] A catena is the sequence of soils down a slope developed from the same parent material, so the differences between them come from slope and drainage.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.1.3] Altitude brings cooler, wetter conditions, which mean more leaching (washing of nutrients down through the soil) and more peat. Aspect (the direction a slope faces) affects soil temperature and moisture.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.1.4] Vegetation supplies litter (fallen leaves and plant debris) and decides humus type (humus is the dark, decomposed organic matter in soil): deciduous woodland gives mull, a mild, well-mixed humus, while coniferous forest and heath give mor, an acid humus that builds up in a layer.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.1.5] Roots bind soil and recycle nutrients, and animals mix the soil. For example, earthworms mix the soil as they burrow.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.1.6] Humans change soils. There is more on this in Human interference with soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.2.b] If you dig a pit, a soil shows distinct layers called horizons. Together they make the soil profile, and each is named by a letter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.2.0] O horizon: litter and humus, which may be split into L, F and H layers. It sits at the top because it is made of fallen plant matter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.2.1] A horizon: dark topsoil, with humus mixed with minerals. It is dark because of the humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.2.2] E horizon: eluvial (material has been washed out of it), a pale, washed-out layer found in leached soils such as podzols, because leaching has stripped its colour.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.2.3] B horizon: illuvial (material has been washed into it) subsoil, where washed-down iron, aluminium, clay or humus accumulate.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.2.4] C horizon: weathered parent material. R horizon: bedrock. The C horizon sits below the B horizon, so it is the least altered layer.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.2.5] Eluviation is the washing out of material from an upper horizon. Illuviation is its deposition in a lower one. This is why a leached soil has a pale E horizon above a richer B horizon.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.2.6] Diagram: a labelled soil profile showing depth and horizon letters.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.3.b] Weathering is the breakdown of rock where it lies, and it is the first step in making soil. How fast it works sets how deep a soil can get.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.3.0] Physical and chemical weathering break parent material down into mineral particles and release nutrients. This is why weathering is the first step in making soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.3.1] Hydrolysis (a reaction between minerals and water) forms clay, and oxidation (a reaction with oxygen) gives red and brown colours.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.3.2] The rate of weathering sets soil depth. Soils are deep in the hot, wet tropics, where chemical weathering is fastest, and thin in cold or dry regions.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.3.3] There is more on the processes themselves in Weathering processes, in Rocks, Weathering and Mass Movement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.4.b] Soil can be lost as well as made. Erosion removes it, and the loss changes the characteristics of what remains.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.4.0] Soil erosion is the removal of soil, especially topsoil, faster than it forms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.4.1] Erosion by water takes the forms of rain-splash (raindrops knocking soil particles loose), sheet wash (a thin sheet of water carrying soil away), and rills and gullies (small and large channels cut into the soil).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.4.2] Erosion by wind is deflation of dry, loose, bare soil, which means the wind lifts the particles away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.4.3] Natural geological erosion is slow and balanced by soil formation. Accelerated erosion follows the removal of vegetation, which no longer protects the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.4.4] Erosion thins the profile, removes the humus-rich A horizon and its nutrients, and exposes infertile subsoil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.4.5] It also worsens structure (how the particles clump together) and water retention. The eroded material is redeposited elsewhere, as alluvium (river-laid deposits) or loess (fine wind-blown silt).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.4.6] There is more on human causes and responses in Human interference with soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.5.b] Where rain is heavy, water carries nutrients out of the upper soil. This washing is leaching, and it makes soil more acid and less fertile.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.5.0] Leaching happens where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, so there is surplus water. Gravitational water (water pulled down by gravity) moves down through the soil. For example, it is common where rainfall is heavy.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.5.1] The water dissolves soluble minerals and nutrient bases (calcium, magnesium, potassium and sodium) and carries them to lower horizons or into groundwater.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.5.2] The upper soil becomes more acidic and less fertile, and material may be redeposited in the B horizon.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.5.3] Leaching is common in Ireland's wet climate. Mild leaching gives brown earths, and intense leaching gives podzols.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.6.b] Humification is the making of humus, the dark organic part of soil. It depends on how quickly the organisms that rot litter can work.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.6.0] Humification is the decomposition of litter by bacteria, fungi and soil animals into humus. It has nothing to do with humidity. The nutrients in dead matter are locked up until it is broken down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.6.1] It is fast in warm, moist, well-aerated, base-rich conditions, which produce mull.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.6.2] It is slow in cold, acid or waterlogged conditions, where partly decomposed matter builds up as mor or peat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.6.3] It releases nutrients for plants, as part of the nutrient cycle (the repeated passing of nutrients between soil and living things).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.7.b] Podzolisation is an intense form of leaching that produces the podzol. It strips iron and aluminium from the upper soil and deposits them lower down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.7.0] Conditions: a cool, wet climate where precipitation is well above evapotranspiration; acid parent material such as granite or sandstone; coniferous forest or heath vegetation giving acid mor humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.7.1] 1. Acid litter releases organic acids into percolating water (water seeping down through the soil).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.7.2] 2. The acids combine with iron and aluminium (chelation, or cheluviation) and carry them down, along with bases and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.7.3] 3. The A/E horizon is left as a bleached, ash-grey, sandy layer of quartz, because the coloured minerals have been removed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.7.4] 4. Iron, aluminium and humus are deposited in the B horizon, which turns dark reddish-brown.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.7.5] 5. A thin, hard iron pan (a cemented layer) may form in the B horizon. It blocks drainage and roots, so water lies above it and peat may form on top.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.7.6] Result: the podzol, which is acidic and infertile, with sharply defined horizons. It is infertile because bases and nutrients have been washed out of the upper soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.b] Laterisation occurs in hot, wet tropical climates. Intense weathering and leaching remove silica and leave a soil rich in iron and aluminium.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.0] 1. High temperatures and heavy rain cause intense chemical weathering to great depth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.1] 2. Silica and bases are leached out, since silica is soluble in warm water under these conditions.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.2] 3. Insoluble iron and aluminium oxides (sesquioxides) are left behind and concentrated, giving a deep red or yellow colour.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.3] 4. Litter decomposes so quickly that little humus accumulates.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.4] 5. Where the forest is removed and the soil dries out, the iron-rich layer can harden into a brick-like crust called laterite. A laterite crust forms only when the soil is exposed and dries out, not under intact forest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.5] Extreme laterisation of aluminium-rich rock forms bauxite, the ore of aluminium (the rock aluminium is extracted from).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.6] Bauxite is mined from laterised rock in tropical countries such as Guinea, Jamaica and Australia (north Queensland).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.8.7] Result: latosols (oxisols). It is infertile because the silica and bases have been leached away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.9.b] Calcification occurs in dry continental climates where evapotranspiration roughly equals or exceeds precipitation, as in temperate grasslands and semi-arid areas. Little water moves down, so calcium stays in the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.9.0] 1. Rain penetrates only part of the way down, so leaching is weak.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.9.1] 2. Calcium carbonate is dissolved and carried a short distance down, then deposited in the B horizon as nodules (small lumps) or a lime-rich layer.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.9.2] 3. In dry periods, capillary action draws water and calcium back up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.9.3] 4. Deep grass roots take up calcium and return it to the surface in litter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.9.4] Result: an alkaline or neutral, base-rich, fertile soil with a thick, humus-rich A horizon, the chernozem. It is fertile because calcium and humus stay in the topsoil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.10.b] Gleying happens when a soil is saturated with water and has no air. The lack of oxygen changes the colour and behaviour of the iron in it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.10.0] Gleying happens in waterlogged soils: heavy clay, impermeable till (boulder clay that water cannot pass through), a high water table (the level below which the ground is saturated), hollows, or soils above an iron pan.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.10.1] Pores fill with water, air is excluded, and conditions become anaerobic (without oxygen).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.10.2] Bacteria reduce iron from its red ferric form to its grey or blue-grey ferrous form, which is why the soil turns grey.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.10.3] Where air enters along roots or cracks, iron is re-oxidised and forms orange or rusty mottles (patches).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.10.4] Result: a gley soil, which is grey, mottled, sticky and cold, slow to warm, and slow to decompose.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.11.b] In hot, dry areas, water moves up through the soil rather than down, and it leaves salts behind.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.11.0] In hot, dry areas, evaporation draws water up by capillary action and leaves soluble salts at or near the surface, sometimes as a white crust.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.11.1] Poor irrigation (watering land artificially) makes salinisation worse. There is more on this in Human interference with soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.11.2] Salt crusts link this process to desert soils. This is why desert soils often carry a white crust of salt.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.12.b] Soils are classified by how much of their character comes from climate, from a local factor, or from lack of time.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.12.0] Zonal soils are mature soils whose character is set by climate and vegetation, so they form broad belts matching the world's climate and biome (major plant and animal community) belts.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.12.1] Intrazonal soils are dominated by one local factor, such as drainage (gley, peat) or parent material (rendzina, a thin lime-rich soil on limestone).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.12.2] Azonal soils are immature, with poorly developed horizons. Examples are alluvium, scree, sand dunes, fresh volcanic ash and recent glacial deposits. They are immature because they have had too little time to develop.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.13.b] Because climate and vegetation set zonal soils, the world's soils form belts. Going from the poles to the equator, each belt has its own soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.13.0] Tundra soils are thin and gleyed, with permafrost (permanently frozen ground) below.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.13.1] Podzols lie in the boreal (taiga) coniferous forest belt of northern Canada, Scandinavia, Finland and Siberia (Russia).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.13.2] Brown earths lie under temperate deciduous forest in Ireland, Britain, much of western and central Europe (e.g. France and Germany), and the eastern USA.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.13.3] Chernozems and chestnut soils lie under temperate grassland. Chestnut soils form on the drier margins of the grasslands.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.13.4] Red Mediterranean soils, called terra rossa, form on limestone in Mediterranean lands, e.g. southern Italy. There is more on this in A Peripheral European Region: the Mezzogiorno.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.13.5] Aridisols, or desert soils, lie in the hot desert belt, including the Sahara, Arabian, Australian and south-western USA deserts.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.13.6] Latosols lie under the tropical rainforest of the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia (e.g. Indonesia, Malaysia).

[geo-option-geoecology-s2.14.b] Climate changes with height, so soils on a mountain change in the same way as they do across the world.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.14.0] Soils change with altitude as climate does, a pattern called vertical zonation. Upslope they become thinner, more leached and peatier. This is because altitude brings cooler, wetter conditions.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.15.b] Ireland's soils follow relief and drainage. Lowlands, uplands, wet clay and limestone each have their own soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.15.0] Brown earths and grey-brown podzolics dominate the lowlands. Mild leaching under woodland gives brown earths.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.15.1] Podzols and peaty podzols occur on the uplands.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.15.2] Gleys are found on poorly drained clay till, especially in the drumlin belt. Water cannot drain through the clay, so the soil is gleyed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.15.3] Peatland covers about a fifth of Ireland's land area. Peat is intrazonal: blanket bog lies in the west and on mountains, such as Co. Mayo and Connemara, and raised bog lies in the Midlands, such as Co. Offaly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.15.4] Thin rendzinas, which are dark and lime-rich, occur on the limestone of the Burren.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.16.b] Brown earth is the default choice for an Irish soil. It forms under mild leaching beneath woodland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.16.0] Brown earth is Ireland's main lowland soil, and the most widespread soil of the Irish lowlands, especially in the south, east and Midlands.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.16.1] Fertile grassland and tillage (crop-growing) soils of the Golden Vale (Co. Tipperary and Co. Limerick) and the south-east are brown earths. The soil is fertile, which is why it suits farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.16.2] Grey-brown podzolics, which are slightly more leached relatives with clay washed into the B horizon, are typical of the limestone-rich till of the east Midlands, e.g. Co. Meath and Co. Kildare.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.16.3] It formed under Ireland's mild, wet, cool temperate oceanic climate and the deciduous woodland of oak, ash, elm and hazel that covered most of Ireland after the Ice Age. There is more on the climate in Physical regions.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.16.4] Annual rainfall is roughly 750 to 1,000 mm in the east and over 1,200 mm in much of the west.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.16.5] Parent material is usually glacial till, and the soil is slightly acidic, at roughly pH 5.5 to 6.5.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.16.6] Processes: mild leaching carries some iron and clay down, and mull humus is mixed into the soil by earthworms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.b] The brown earth profile is mixed by earthworms, so it has no bleached layer. That makes it fertile and easy to farm.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.0] O: a thin mull humus layer.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.1] A: dark brown, loamy, with crumb structure (small, rounded crumbs), roughly 20 to 30 cm deep.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.2] B: lighter brown, with some iron and clay washed in. The boundary with A is gradual because earthworms mix the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.3] C: weathered till or rock.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.4] There is no bleached E horizon, because earthworms mix the soil. Depths are rough illustrative values only.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.5] Characteristics: loamy texture, crumb structure, slightly acidic and fertile, because mull humus and earthworm mixing keep nutrients in the topsoil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.6] Uses: grassland and tillage in the Golden Vale and the south-east, because the soil is fertile.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.17.7] Improvement: lime is applied where pH falls below the Teagasc targets. There is more on this in Fertility and nutrients, in Soil composition and characteristics.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.18.b] Podzols are the soil of Irish uplands. Cool, wet, acid conditions there drive intense leaching.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.18.0] Podzols occur on Irish uplands such as the Wicklow Mountains, which are granite, and on other mountains with heather moorland and high rainfall.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.18.1] Many lie under conifer plantations, notably Sitka spruce, whose needle litter feeds the acid mor humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.18.2] Many Irish upland podzols are peaty podzols, with a peaty O horizon above an iron pan.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.18.3] Formation follows the podzolisation steps given earlier, under acid mor humus from heather or conifers in a cool, wet climate.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.b] The podzol profile shows the effect of leaching clearly. A bleached layer sits above a dark, enriched layer, so the horizons are sharply defined.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.0] O: thick mor or peat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.1] A/E: ash-grey, bleached sand. It is bleached because iron and humus have been washed out.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.2] A thin, black iron pan.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.3] B: reddish-brown, enriched with iron and humus. It is reddish-brown because the iron is deposited here.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.4] C: parent material below.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.5] Characteristics: strongly acidic, at about pH 4 to 4.5. Acid mor humus and leached bases cause this.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.6] Uses: rough grazing for sheep, and forestry, because the soil is too acid and infertile for crops.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.19.7] Improvement: deep ploughing to break the iron pan, heavy liming and fertiliser.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.20.b] Gleys are the wet soils of Ireland. They form where water cannot drain away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.20.0] Gleys occur in the drumlin belt of Co. Cavan, Co. Monaghan and Co. Leitrim, on impermeable boulder clay in the wet hollows between drumlins.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.20.1] Heavy clay soils of the north Midlands and Co. Clare are also gleys, because the clay holds water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.20.2] Formation follows the gleying steps given earlier: waterlogging, reduction of iron, and grey, mottled colours.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.21.b] Water sits in a gley for much of the year, which decides how the land can be farmed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.21.0] Profile: a waterlogged upper layer that is grey or blue-grey with orange mottles, sitting on impermeable clay.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.21.1] Characteristics: wet, sticky and cold, with a short grazing season because wet ground is easily poached (trampled into mud by animals).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.21.2] Uses: mainly grass for cattle.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.21.3] Improvement: field drains and arterial drainage (large-scale drainage of rivers and channels). There is more on this in Human interference with soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.22.b] The latosol is the tropical red soil. It is a choice for the continental soil in an essay.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.22.0] Latosols form under the tropical rainforest of the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia (e.g. Indonesia, Malaysia).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.22.1] The climate is hot, about 27 °C all year round, with over 2,000 mm of rain. There is more on this in One major biome in detail.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.22.2] Formation is laterisation, as given earlier.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.23.b] Latosols look rich because of the lush forest above them, but they are infertile. The nutrients are in the living plants, not the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.23.0] Latosols are deep, often many metres, much deeper than Irish soils. This is because chemical weathering is fastest in the hot, wet tropics.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.23.1] Profile: a thin O layer; a thin, leached A horizon; a very deep red B horizon of sesquioxides; deep weathered rock below (C).
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.23.2] They are red or yellow from iron and aluminium oxides, clay-rich, acidic, and have very little humus. They have little humus because litter decomposes so quickly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.23.3] They are low in nutrients. Nearly all nutrients are held in the living vegetation and recycled quickly from the thin litter layer.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.23.4] Shifting cultivation (slash and burn) traditionally used the soil for a few years before moving on.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.23.5] When the forest is cleared, the soil loses fertility within a few years, erodes under heavy rain, and can bake into laterite crust. Without the forest, nothing recycles the nutrients or shields the soil from rain.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.24.b] The chernozem is the soil of the temperate grasslands. Its climate and grass cover explain why it is so fertile.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.24.0] Chernozem is the Russian name for black earth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.24.1] It lies on the steppes of Ukraine and southern Russia, the Prairies of North America (the Great Plains of the USA and Canada), and the Pampas of Argentina.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.24.2] The climate is continental, with hot summers, very cold winters and low rainfall of roughly 250 to 500 mm a year, mostly in late spring and summer.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.24.3] The natural grassland of steppe or prairie has dense, deep roots that die back each year, supplying large amounts of organic matter. This is why the soil is so rich in humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.25.b] Calcification, along with burrowing animals, builds a deep black soil with a lime-rich layer below.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.25.0] Snow melt in spring gives a short burst of downward water movement. Calcium is moved within the soil as in calcification.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.25.1] Burrowing animals mix the soil: ground squirrels, called susliks in the steppe, and prairie dogs in North America.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.25.2] Their filled-in burrows show as darker patches called krotovinas.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.25.3] A: very thick, black, humus-rich topsoil, commonly about 1 m deep, with crumb structure. It is black and thick because of the humus from the grass roots.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.25.4] B: calcium-rich, with white lime nodules. It is calcium-rich because calcification deposits dissolved calcium carbonate there.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.25.5] C: loess, fine wind-blown silt, in much of the steppe and prairie.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.26.b] The chernozem is among the most fertile soils on earth, so it supports intensive farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.26.0] Neutral to slightly alkaline, and very fertile. It is fertile because of its humus and calcium.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.26.1] Uses: some of the world's main wheat and maize lands, with sunflowers in Ukraine. They suit these crops because the soil is fertile.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.26.2] Ukraine has been called the breadbasket of Europe.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.26.3] Ploughing the prairie soils for wheat, followed by drought in the 1930s, caused the Dust Bowl. There is more on this in Human interaction: mass movement, in Rocks, Weathering and Mass Movement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.27.b] Two other soils are valid choices for the continental soil. Each has its own full treatment elsewhere.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.27.0] India's black soil, or regur, of the Deccan is formed from Deccan Traps basalt. It is clay-rich, so it holds moisture, cracks when dry, and is used for cotton. There is more on this in India.
[geo-option-geoecology-s2.27.1] The aridisol of the hot desert is another valid choice. There is more on this in One major biome in detail.
