CANDIDATES geo-option-culture-identity-s2: 2. Language as a cultural indicator
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.0.b] A cultural indicator is a feature that helps you identify and map a cultural group. Language is one of the strongest, because it is both part of a group's identity and easy to measure.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.0.0] Language carries a group's history, literature, songs, place names and ways of thinking, so losing a language means losing a store of the group's culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.0.1] Language marks who belongs to a group, because sharing a language means sharing a way of speaking and thinking that outsiders do not have.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.0.2] Language is easy to map from census questions on language (a census is the official population count, held at regular intervals), so it is used to define cultural regions (areas with a shared culture).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.b] Nationalism (the belief that a people with a shared culture should govern themselves) has often rested on language. Governments have therefore either promoted a language to build a nation or suppressed one to weaken a group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.0] Many nation states (states whose people share one national identity) were built around a single language, because a common language helps unite the population.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.1] The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge), founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill, led a language revival that fed the Irish independence movement, though it was founded as a non-political body.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.2] Suppressing a language has been used to weaken a group. Basque and Catalan were banned from public life and schools under Franco (1939–1975).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.3] The Welsh Not was a wooden board hung round the neck of children caught speaking Welsh in some 19th-century schools, so children were punished for using their own language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.4] Kurdish was banned in public life and broadcasting in Turkey until the early 1990s, which is an example of a state weakening a group by silencing its language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.2.b] Languages carry power as well as culture. The language of the rulers or the wealthy tends to spread, and smaller languages lose ground.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.2.0] Colonial languages (those of the empires that ruled a territory) became official languages of former colonies: English in India and Nigeria, French in Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire, Spanish across most of Latin America, and Portuguese in Brazil.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.2.1] The language of the powerful brings jobs and status, so speakers of minority languages shift to it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.2.2] The Organisation internationale de la Francophonie, founded in 1970, links French-speaking states, which shows how a colonial language can keep a group of countries connected.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.3.b] Place names preserve the language spoken in an area long after it has been replaced. Reading them is a way of tracing a region's linguistic history.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.3.0] Irish place-name elements show the Irish-speaking past of areas that are now English-speaking: baile (townland or homestead; a townland is a small traditional land division), cill (church), dún (fort), inis (island) and cluain (meadow).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.3.1] Examples: Clonmel (Cluain Meala), Kilkenny (Cill Chainnigh), Dundalk (Dún Dealgan) and Ennis (Inis). Each English name is a version of the older Irish one, which is why the Irish elements are still visible in them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.4.b] Language can bind people together or split them apart, and it pays no attention to state borders.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.4.0] A shared national language can unite a state, but language borders can divide it, as Belgium's does. There is more on this in The complexity of regions (ii).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.4.1] French is spoken in France, Belgium (Wallonia), Switzerland (west) and Quebec (Canada), so one language links four different places.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.4.2] Languages cross borders: French is spoken in several states, and Basque is spoken in both Spain and France, so a language community can be split between two states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.5.b] Languages are grouped by descent, from the largest unit to the smallest. This lets us show how languages that look different are related.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.5.0] A language family is a set of languages descended from a common ancestor. Each family divides into branches, and each branch contains languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.5.1] A dialect is a regional variety of a single language, so it is smaller than a language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.5.2] About 7,000 languages are spoken in the world today, for example across the families below.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.b] The world's languages fall into a small number of large families. Know each family's main languages and the regions where it is spoken.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.0] Indo-European is the largest family, spoken by nearly half the world's people. It covers Europe, the Americas, Iran and South Asia.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.1] Sino-Tibetan: Mandarin and other Chinese languages, Tibetan and Burmese.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.2] Afro-Asiatic: Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic and Hausa.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.3] Niger-Congo: most of sub-Saharan Africa (Africa south of the Sahara), e.g. Swahili, Yoruba and Zulu.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.4] Austronesian: Malay/Indonesian, Filipino, Malagasy, Māori and Hawaiian.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.5] Dravidian: southern India, e.g. Tamil and Telugu.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.6] Turkic and Uralic are further families.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.b] Counting speakers depends on whether you count only native speakers (those who learned it as a first language) or second-language speakers as well. That is why different languages come first on different lists.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.0] Mandarin Chinese has the most native speakers.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.1] English has the most speakers in total, about 1.5 billion counting second-language speakers.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.2] Spanish has the second-most native speakers. Hindi is also very widely spoken.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.3] Arabic is the language of the Qur'an (the holy book of Islam) and an official language in over 20 states of the Middle East and North Africa.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.8.b] Languages move when people move or when one group gains power over another. Four main routes explain the present world map of languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.8.0] Migration, conquest and empire spread languages: Latin with the Roman Empire, and English, Spanish, French and Portuguese with colonialism.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.8.1] Trade and religion spread languages too: Arabic spread with Islam, because the Qur'an is in Arabic.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.8.2] Today globalisation (the growing links between countries) and the media spread dominant languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.b] Most European languages belong to the Indo-European family, which divides into branches. A sketch map of the branches is a useful diagram in an exam answer.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.0] Germanic: English, German, Dutch (including Flemish, the Dutch spoken in Belgium), Danish, Swedish, Norwegian and Icelandic.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.1] Romance, from Latin (the language of the Roman Empire, so these languages descend from it): French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Romanian and Catalan.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.2] Slavic: Russian, Polish, Czech, Slovak, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Croatian and Slovene.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.3] Celtic: Goidelic (Irish, Scottish Gaelic and Manx) and Brythonic (Welsh, Breton and Cornish).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.4] Hellenic: Greek. Baltic: Latvian and Lithuanian. Albanian is a separate branch.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.5] Non-Indo-European languages in Europe: Finnish, Estonian and Hungarian (Uralic); Turkish (Turkic); Maltese (Semitic, written in the Latin alphabet).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.6] Basque is an isolate (a language with no known relatives), unrelated to any other language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.10.b] The EU has to manage many languages in one union. Irish has gained official status there, while English has become the language most Europeans learn as a second language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.10.0] The EU has 24 official languages, so documents and debates may be needed in each.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.10.1] Irish became an official EU language in 2007, with full working-language status from 1 January 2022. Do not say 1973: that is when Ireland joined, not when Irish became official.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.10.2] English is Europe's main second language, and language learning is supported by programmes such as Erasmus exchanges (study periods abroad).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.b] The mass media (television, film, the internet and other means of reaching large audiences) now spread dominant languages, and English is the language most carried by them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.0] Television, film, music, the internet, social media and streaming are dominated by English, through Hollywood films and US and UK music.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.1] About half of all websites are in English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.2] Young people adopt English words and English-language culture, because that is what they see and hear online.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.3] Minority-language speakers consume most media in the majority language at home, so even in a Welsh or Irish-speaking household, most of what is watched is in the majority language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.12.b] The spread of English through the media and business changes other languages and puts pressure on them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.12.0] English loanwords (words borrowed from another language) enter other languages, such as the French le weekend (a feature of Franglais, French mixed with English).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.12.1] English is the language of business, science and the internet, so people need it for work and study.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.12.2] The pressure falls on small national languages as well as on minority languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.b] Some states have used law to protect their language from English and other dominant languages. France, Quebec and Iceland are the examples.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.0] France: the Académie française, founded in 1635, works to protect the French language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.1] Toubon Law (1994): French must be used in advertising, product labels, workplace documents and public signage.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.2] French music radio stations must play at least 40% French-language songs (a quota, or fixed minimum; law of 1994, applied from 1996).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.3] Quebec's Charter of the French Language (Bill 101, 1977) made French the sole official language of Quebec, a French-speaking province of Canada, and requires most immigrant children to attend French-language schools.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.4] Iceland coins new Icelandic words for new things, e.g. tölva (computer), so it does not need to borrow English words.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.b] A minority language survives better if people can hear and see it every day. Broadcasting in the language gives it a public presence and helps young people use it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.0] Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972, so Irish was heard on the radio every day.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.1] TG4, originally Teilifís na Gaeilge, launched on 31 October 1996 and is based in Baile na hAbhann, Conamara.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.2] S4C, the Welsh-language television channel, launched on 1 November 1982, after Plaid Cymru's former leader Gwynfor Evans threatened a hunger strike in 1980 when the government backed away from its promise.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.3] BBC Alba, the Scottish Gaelic television service, launched in 2008, so Scottish Gaelic gained its own screen presence.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.4] EITB, the Basque public broadcaster, began television in 1982.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.5] Social media and online content also let small languages reach young people.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.15.b] Many languages are under threat, and it is the minority languages that are most at risk.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.15.0] A minority language is spoken by a minority of a state's population, e.g. Irish, Welsh, Breton, Basque, Scottish Gaelic and Sámi.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.15.1] About 40% of the world's languages are endangered, meaning they may stop being spoken.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.b] Languages rarely die for one reason. Pressure from outside combines with decisions made inside families, and each cause below is a separate point you can use in an essay.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.0] Conquest and official suppression, because a ruling power can ban a language from schools and public life.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.1] A dominant language is needed for jobs, education and emigration, so people see the minority language as less useful.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.2] Famine and emigration, and the in-migration (arrival) of majority-language speakers, reduce the number of speakers in the heartland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.3] Urbanisation (movement to cities), mass media and globalisation all expose people to the dominant language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.4] Parents do not pass the language on, so inter-generational transmission (passing a language from one generation to the next) breaks.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.17.b] Extinct languages can sometimes be brought back, which shows that decline is not always final. Manx and Cornish are the examples.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.17.0] Manx: the last native speaker, Ned Maddrell, died in 1974. The language has since been revived, with a Manx-medium primary school (a school that teaches through Manx).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.17.1] Cornish: the last traditional speaker is usually given as Dolly Pentreath, who died in 1777. Cornish was revived in the 20th century.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.17.2] The Cornish were recognised as a national minority in 2014.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.b] A language survives when the state, schools, media, employers and families all support it. Exams ask for these strategies by heading, so learn each one as a heading you can develop.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.0] Official status in law, such as Article 8 for Irish, which gives the language public standing.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.1] Education through the language: immersion and medium-of-instruction schools (where lessons are taught in the language), and compulsory study of the language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.2] Media, such as broadcasting and online content, so the language is heard daily.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.3] Jobs and economic development in the language's heartland (the area where it is strongest), and planning and housing controls, so speakers can afford to stay.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.4] Language commissioners (officials who enforce language rights) and public-service rights.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.5] Community and family support, culture and festivals, because a language lives only if people use it socially.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.6] Cross-border and EU recognition, so the language has support beyond one state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.7] Possible future measures, such as language conditions for housing in the Gaeltacht or more online content, count alongside measures already in place.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.b] Irish is Ireland's national setting for this topic. Its legal status is strong, but the number of people who use it every day is small.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.0] Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution makes Irish the national language and the first official language; English is the second official language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.1] Census 2022: about 1.87 million people (about 40%) said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.2] Census 2022: the Gaeltacht (the Irish-speaking regions) had a population of about 106,000, of whom about 20,000 spoke Irish daily outside education.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.3] Census 1851, the first count: about 1.5 million Irish speakers (about 23%), which shows how far the language has fallen.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.4] Gaeltacht areas lie in seven counties: Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford and Meath.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.5] Main areas: Gaoth Dobhair (Co. Donegal); Conamara and the Aran Islands (Co. Galway, the largest); Corca Dhuibhne (Co. Kerry); Múscraí and Oileán Chléire (Co. Cork); An Rinn (Co. Waterford); Ráth Chairn and Baile Ghib (Co. Meath).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.b] The State has created a set of bodies, laws and plans to support Irish, especially in the Gaeltacht. Each is a point you can use under the strategies of official status, commissioners, planning and economic development.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.0] The Gaeltacht Areas Order 1956 and the Department of the Gaeltacht (1956) are the founding state measures for the Gaeltacht.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.1] Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980) is the Gaeltacht development authority. It grant-aids firms in the Gaeltacht, such as on the Gaoth Dobhair industrial estate, and supports Irish-language community projects, so that jobs exist locally.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.2] Official Languages Act 2003 (amended 2021): the public has the right to deal with public bodies in Irish. The 2021 amendment sets a target that 20% of new public-service recruits be competent in Irish by 2030.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.3] An Coimisinéir Teanga (Language Commissioner), set up in 2004, enforces the Act, so the public can complain if their right to Irish is ignored.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.4] Foras na Gaeilge is an all-island body, so it works in both parts of Ireland, set up in 1999 under the Good Friday Agreement to promote Irish.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.5] Gaeltacht Act 2012: 26 Gaeltacht Language Planning Areas, each with a language plan; Gaeltacht Service Towns; Irish Language Networks outside the Gaeltacht.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.6] The 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language 2010–2030 aims to raise daily speakers to 250,000.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.7] Northern Ireland's Identity and Language (Northern Ireland) Act 2022 gave official recognition to Irish and Ulster-Scots and created an Irish Language Commissioner.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.b] Schools and cultural events give people the chance to learn and use Irish. They matter most for children, because a language survives only if the young learn it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.0] Irish is a compulsory subject, so all pupils learn it, through primary and post-primary school, with exemptions.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.1] Gaelscoileanna are Irish-medium schools (schools teaching through Irish) outside the Gaeltacht; their umbrella body was founded in 1973.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.2] Gaeltacht summer colleges (coláistí samhraidh) bring learners to live with Irish-speaking families, so they hear Irish in daily life.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.3] Seachtain na Gaeilge is an annual Irish-language festival around St Patrick's Day. The Oireachtas na Gaeilge festival was first held in 1897.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.4] TG4 broadcasts Irish-language drama, sport and music programmes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.b] Despite state support, Irish is weak as an everyday community language. The 2007 study set the figure that shows how fragile the Gaeltacht is.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.0] The Comprehensive Linguistic Study of the Use of Irish in the Gaeltacht (2007) found that Irish is viable as a community language only where at least 67% speak it daily.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.1] Its 2015 update warned that Irish could cease to be the community language in most Gaeltacht districts within about ten years.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.2] Evaluation: Irish has high ability, meaning many people can speak it, but low daily use.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.3] The history of Gaeltacht boundaries since 1850: there is more on this in The complexity of regions (ii).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.b] Welsh is the international setting for this topic. Its history shows how law, industry and a government decision each weakened the language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.0] The Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 annexed (took over) Wales to England and barred Welsh speakers from public office unless they used English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.1] The 1847 government reports on education in Wales, known as the Treachery of the Blue Books, blamed Welsh for the people's backwardness.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.2] Coal and iron industries in the 19th century drew large numbers of English and Irish workers into south Wales, spreading English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.3] The flooding of the Welsh-speaking village of Capel Celyn in the Tryweryn valley in 1965, for a reservoir to supply Liverpool, went ahead despite almost all Welsh MPs opposing it, and became a symbol of Welsh powerlessness.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.24.b] Welsh speakers did not accept decline passively. Campaigners pressed the government for rights and services in Welsh.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.24.0] Cymdeithas yr Iaith Gymraeg (the Welsh Language Society) was founded in 1962 after Saunders Lewis's radio lecture Tynged yr Iaith (The Fate of the Language).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.24.1] The society used civil disobedience (deliberately breaking rules in protest), e.g. painting over English-only road signs.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.24.2] The campaign that secured S4C, described under media that protect minority languages, was part of the same activism.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.b] Activism was followed by a series of laws that gradually gave Welsh equal standing with English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.0] The Welsh Language Act 1967, a first step, gave Welsh limited equal validity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.1] The Welsh Language Act 1993 gave Welsh and English equal treatment in the public sector and created the Welsh Language Board.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.2] The Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave Welsh official status and led to a Welsh Language Commissioner from 2012.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.3] The National Assembly for Wales (1999), renamed the Senedd in 2020, makes laws through Welsh and English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.4] The Cymraeg 2050 strategy (2017) sets a target of 1 million Welsh speakers by 2050.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.26.b] As with Irish, schooling and youth organisations are how Welsh is passed to the next generation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.26.0] Welsh is compulsory in all schools in Wales up to age 16, and Welsh-medium schools are found across Wales.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.26.1] Urdd Gobaith Cymru (a youth movement founded in 1922) and the National Eisteddfod (a Welsh festival of culture) promote Welsh among the young.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.b] The Census 2021 figures show a decline in Welsh speakers, and the heartland faces pressures such as second homes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.0] Census 2021: about 538,000 people aged 3 and over (17.8%) could speak Welsh, down from 19.0% in 2011. Wales has a population of about 3.1 million.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.1] The highest shares are in Gwynedd (about 64%) and the Isle of Anglesey (over half), in the north-west heartland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.2] Second homes and English in-migration push up house prices in Welsh-speaking rural areas, so local Welsh speakers struggle to afford homes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.3] From April 2023 Welsh councils may charge up to a 300% council-tax premium on second homes, as a response to this pressure.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.4] Evaluation: Welsh is often cited as the most successful Celtic-language revival, but its decline in the heartland shows the limits of revival despite statutory support (support set out in law).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.5] There is more on Welsh political history in A European regional case study of identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.b] Basque, spoken in northern Spain and south-west France, was suppressed under Franco and has been strongly supported since. It is the main comparison with Irish and Welsh.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.0] The Statute of Gernika (1979) created the Basque Autonomous Community, a self-governing region of Spain, where Basque is co-official with Spanish.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.1] The school models are A (Spanish-medium, with Basque as a subject), B (both languages) and D (all through Basque, with Spanish as a subject). Model D is now chosen for most pupils.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.2] Ikastolak are Basque-medium schools that began as a clandestine (secret) movement under Franco.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.3] Euskaltzaindia, the Royal Academy of the Basque Language, founded in 1919, created standard Basque, Batua, from 1968, so that speakers of different dialects share one written form.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.4] Basque television is provided by EITB, described under media that protect minority languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.5] Evaluation: use of Basque is rising among the young, which suggests the school models are working.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.b] Breton, the Celtic language of Brittany in France, shows what happens when the state gives little support. It contrasts with Wales and the Basque Country.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.0] Brittany was united with France in 1532, so it has long been part of a state where French dominates.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.1] Article 2 of the French Constitution, added in 1992, states that the language of the Republic is French.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.2] Since 2008 Article 75-1 states that regional languages belong to France's heritage.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.3] France signed the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 1999 but has not ratified it (formally approved it so that it becomes binding).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.4] Diwan: Breton-medium immersion schools (where pupils are taught entirely in Breton), the first opened in 1977, run as a voluntary association, so they depend on volunteers rather than the state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.5] In 2021 France's Constitutional Council struck down parts of a new law, the Molac Law, that allowed immersion teaching in public schools.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.6] Breton has declined sharply, and most of its speakers are elderly, so few young people are learning it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.30.b] Māori shows that a language can be revived with state backing and early-years schooling. It is a brief non-European example.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.30.0] Te reo Māori became an official language of New Zealand in 1987 under the Māori Language Act.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.30.1] Kōhanga reo (language nests), preschools run entirely in Māori, began in 1982, so children learn the language from the earliest age.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.31.b] Beyond individual states, Europe-wide bodies also recognise minority languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.31.0] The Council of Europe adopted the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 1992, which gives minority languages recognition across Europe.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.31.1] Irish's EU status is set out in Irish and the EU, and English as Europe's second language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.b] This is a data skill from the Ordinary-level examination: a bar chart of languages spoken in Ireland by age group. If you practise it, invent clearly labelled illustrative data.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.0] Find the tallest bar to identify the age group that speaks the language most.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.1] Add the percentages for one language across the groups to find its combined share.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.2] A promotion idea for Irish should target the group where the chart shows the lowest use, and link the language to jobs, travel or culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.3] An advantage of speaking another language is access to jobs with multinational companies, to travel, and to understanding another culture.
