TARGET geo-region-ireland-s1 (part 1 of 2): 1. A Core Irish Region
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.b] The Greater Dublin Area (GDA) is the core region of Ireland's east coast, facing Britain and Europe across the Irish Sea. Knowing exactly which counties it includes is the starting point for every question on it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.0] The GDA is Dublin City and County Dublin, plus Counties Meath, Kildare and Wicklow. Louth, including Drogheda, is not part of it, so do not count it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.1] Dublin City, Fingal, South Dublin and Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown are the four local authorities (the councils that run local services) of Dublin; the single Co. Dublin county council was split into the last three in 1994.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.b] A core region is one that concentrates people, wealth and services, in contrast to peripheral regions (remote areas with fewer of these). The GDA fits this description in several ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.0] Its large, dense and growing population is the largest in the State, which gives firms customers and workers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.1] It holds most of the State's wealth, jobs, multinational investment, government and services, which means firms and workers want to be there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.2] It has the best infrastructure in the State, including the main port, airport and motorway network, so goods and people move easily.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.3] It attracts in-migrants (people moving in) from the rest of Ireland and from abroad.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.4] Cumulative causation (growth that feeds more growth): growth attracts firms and workers, which attracts further investment and widens the gap with peripheral regions such as the West.

[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.b] The GDA has a cool temperate oceanic climate (mild and wet because of the nearby ocean), with prevailing south-westerly winds bringing moist Atlantic air. Because of where the mountains sit, the east is drier than the west.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.0] Rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of high ground): the Wicklow Mountains and the uplands further west force moist air to drop its rain on the western slopes, so the east is the driest part of Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.1] Rainfall in the east is about 750–1,000 mm a year, so it is much drier than the west; Dublin Airport records about 750 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.2] By contrast, the west receives 1,000–1,250 mm a year and some mountains exceed 2,000 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.3] The south-east is the sunniest part of Ireland, which adds to the east's value for crops.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.4] Mean January temperatures in Ireland are about 4–7 °C and mean July temperatures about 14–16 °C, so winters are mild and summers cool.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.5] Being farther from the Atlantic than the west gives a slightly greater temperature range: warmer summers, colder winters and more frost.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.6] Heavier rain and snow fall on the Wicklow uplands than on the lowlands, because rising air cools and drops its moisture.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.b] Climate decides what the land can be used for. The dry east and the wet uplands are used in different ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.0] Effect on farming: the dry, relatively sunny lowlands suit tillage (growing crops such as cereals and vegetables) and horticulture (growing vegetables, fruit and flowers).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.1] Effect on land use: heavy upland rain supports conifer forestry and reservoirs, because the rain feeds fast-growing trees and fills water stores.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.b] Relief means the height and shape of the land. The GDA has a wide lowland to the north and west and a mountain block to the south, and each is used differently.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.0] A low-lying, gently undulating lowland covers most of Meath, Kildare and north Co. Dublin, part of the Central Lowlands.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.1] The Central Lowlands are underlain by Carboniferous limestone (a sedimentary rock), covered by glacial deposits.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.2] The Wicklow and Dublin Mountains lie to the south, with a core of the Leinster granite batholith (a huge mass of granite that cooled underground), about 400 million years old (Caledonian, the mountain-building episode that made it).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.3] Lugnaquilla, 925 m, in Co. Wicklow, is the highest peak in Leinster, so it is a good example of the region's upland relief.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.4] Kippure, about 757 m, stands on the Dublin–Wicklow border, so the mountains are close enough to the city to be used for recreation.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.5] The Great Sugar Loaf, Co. Wicklow, is a quartzite peak (quartzite is a very hard rock) that stands out from the granite.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.6] Effect: the lowland is easy to farm, build on and cross with roads and rail; the uplands suit forestry, water supply and recreation.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.b] Ice sheets and the sea have both shaped the region. These named landforms are the examples to use.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.0] The U-shaped valleys (steep-sided, flat-floored valleys) of Glendalough and Glenmalure were carved by glacial erosion, which is why their sides are steep and their floors flat.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.1] Esker Riada is a long ridge of sand and gravel deposited by glacial meltwater, which is why it is made of loose, rounded material.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.2] Howth Head is joined to the mainland by the Sutton tombolo (a bar of sand or shingle linking an island or headland to the land).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.3] North Bull Island lies in Dublin Bay, so it is an example of a coastal feature there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.b] Soil follows the rock and drainage beneath it. The lowlands have deep, fertile soils, while the granite uplands and bogs have poor ones, so soil sets how land is used.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.0] Deep, fertile brown earths (a well-developed soil rich in nutrients) on limestone boulder clay (stony clay left by ice sheets) cover Meath, Kildare and north Co. Dublin, among the best farmland in Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.1] Brown earths are well drained, which suits both tillage and grassland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.2] The Curragh, Co. Kildare, has free-draining sand and gravel from glacial outwash (material washed out from the ice by meltwater), with well-drained grassland used for horse training.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.3] The Bog of Allen, Co. Kildare, is a raised bog (a dome of peat built up from dead plants) of deep peat, which is unsuitable for farming unless drained.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.4] Blanket bog (peat covering the ground like a blanket) on granite at the Sally Gap, Co. Wicklow, is waterlogged and acidic, so it is of little use for farming.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.5] Acidic, leached podzols (grey soils with nutrients washed out of the top layer) cover the granite uplands of Wicklow, poor for farming but suitable for conifers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.6] Effect on land use: fertile lowland soils support farming; thin, acidic upland soils and bog are used for forestry, recreation and water catchment (land that collects rain for a river or reservoir).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.b] Drainage covers the rivers, canals and reservoirs of the region. In the GDA they matter because a large city needs a reliable water supply.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.0] The River Liffey rises in the Wicklow Mountains, flows through Kildare and enters the sea at Dublin Bay, so it links upland to city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.1] The Poulaphouca dam on the Liffey created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, used for hydro-electricity and for Dublin's drinking water.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.2] The Vartry Reservoir at Roundwood, Co. Wicklow, supplies Dublin with water, so the uplands serve the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.3] The River Boyne flows through Trim and Navan, Co. Meath, and reaches the sea near Drogheda, outside the GDA, so its mouth is not in the region.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.4] The smaller Dublin rivers, the Dodder and the Tolka, flow through the city to Dublin Bay, so they drain urban areas.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.5] The Royal and Grand Canals link Dublin to the River Shannon, so they connect the east coast to the interior.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.6] Uisce Éireann (Irish Water) has proposed a pipeline to bring water from the River Shannon to the Dublin region, because demand on local supplies is growing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.7] Paved surfaces in the city let rain run off quickly into rivers, raising urban flood risk.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.b] Farming does well in the GDA because physical and human advantages come together. Each factor below can be developed as a separate point in an answer.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.0] Climate: the dry, relatively sunny east suits tillage (growing crops) and horticulture (growing vegetables and similar crops).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.1] Relief: flat lowland allows large fields and mechanised farming, which cuts the cost of each crop.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.2] Soils: deep, fertile brown earths in Meath and Kildare give high-quality land for crops and grass.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.3] Market: Dublin, the largest urban area in the State, lies on the farms' doorstep, so produce has a short journey to many buyers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.4] Transport: Dublin Port and Dublin Airport export farm produce and livestock, so farmers can sell abroad.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.5] Policy and technology: EU and Irish agricultural policy and modern machinery support large, productive farms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.b] Farming here is large-scale and commercial, but each county specialises in something different, shaped by its soil, relief and nearness to Dublin.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.0] Large, commercial and highly productive farms dominate the lowlands, because the flat, fertile land suits them.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.1] Meath, for example: beef cattle fattening (raising cattle to slaughter weight) and tillage on large farms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.2] Kildare: bloodstock (breeding of racehorses) and racehorse training, centred on the Curragh racecourse and the Irish National Stud at Tully, Kildare town.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.3] Fingal, for example: horticulture, with field vegetables and glasshouse crops centred on Rush and Lusk.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.4] Wicklow: sheep grazing on the upland pasture of the Wicklow Mountains, because the steep, poor land suits sheep better than crops.

[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.b] Heavy use of transport brings problems, and the authorities have responded with new public transport and a long-term plan.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.0] Traffic congestion on radial roads and the M50 slows journeys, because so many commuters use the same routes.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.1] Long commutes from Meath, Kildare and Wicklow into the city add to congestion.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.2] Low-density sprawl makes households car-dependent, because homes are too spread out for frequent public transport.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.3] Responses include investment in public transport, such as DART, Luas and planned MetroLink.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.4] Cycle lanes encourage travel by bicycle, so fewer people need to drive.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.5] The National Transport Authority (NTA) sets out the Greater Dublin Area Transport Strategy 2022–2042.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.b] Besides tourism and transport, the GDA holds the State's main financial, technology and public services, concentrated in the capital.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.0] The International Financial Services Centre (IFSC), a district for banking and finance firms, in Custom House Docks, was set up in 1987.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.1] Google's European headquarters, on Barrow Street in Grand Canal Dock, is part of 'Silicon Docks'.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.2] The Dublin Docklands Development Authority, set up in 1997, regenerated the docklands for offices and tech firms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.3] Government, health and education services are concentrated in the capital, because Dublin is the seat of national government.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.32.b] Census 2022 shows how large the GDA's population is and how it is shared between the counties. The State total was about 5.1 million.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.32.0] Census 2022 counted about 2.1 million people in the GDA, about 40% of the State's population of about 5.1 million.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.32.1] Co. Dublin had about 1.45 million people; Kildare about 250,000; Meath about 220,000; Wicklow about 156,000.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s1: 1. Physical regions
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.b] Geographers divide the earth into regions so that places can be studied as groups instead of one by one. A region is an area of the earth's surface that shares one or more characteristics, called criteria, which make it different from the areas around it.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.0] Physical criteria: climate (the usual weather of a place), relief (the height and shape of the land) and rock type, such as limestone.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.1] Administrative criteria: the boundaries set by government, such as county lines.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.2] Cultural criteria: language and religion, which separate groups of people.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.3] Socio-economic criteria: wealth, employment and development (how advanced a place's economy and living standards are).
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.4] Urban criteria: a city and its hinterland (the surrounding area that depends on the city for services and jobs).
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.5] These five types of region are the five subsections of this chapter, and this subsection deals with physical regions.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.b] Regions can be big or small, and they can be defined by one criterion or by several. Their edges also differ: some are fixed by law, others fade gradually.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.0] Regions exist at different scales. A local region is a county or a city's hinterland, e.g. Co. Clare; a national region is the West of Ireland; a continental region is North West Europe; a global region is the Islamic world.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.1] A single index (one measurable criterion) defines a region by that one thing: rock type alone gives a karst region (a limestone landscape shaped by water dissolving the rock).
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.2] Multiple indices combine several criteria. For example, a peripheral region (one on the edge of a country's economic activity, far from its centre) can be defined by income, unemployment and out-migration (people leaving the area) together.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.3] Physical and cultural regions usually merge into their neighbours through transition zones (a gradual belt of change) rather than ending at a sharp line.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.4] Administrative regions have exact boundaries, fixed by law, so you can say precisely which side of the line a place lies on.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.5] Regions also change over time as their criteria change.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.b] Climate is the usual pattern of weather in a place over many years. A climatic region is a large area with similar temperature, precipitation (rain, snow and other moisture falling from the sky) and seasonal pattern, usually with similar natural vegetation and soils.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.0] The world can be divided into climatic regions. This subsection studies the cool temperate oceanic type in detail.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.1] Cool temperate oceanic: Ireland and North West Europe, which is why Ireland is the case studied here.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.2] Warm temperate oceanic: the Mediterranean type, with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, so it differs from Ireland.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.3] Hot desert: e.g. the Sahara.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.b] The cool temperate oceanic climate is mild and wet, with no extremes of heat or cold. These are the features to describe, with the Irish figures (Met Éireann long-term averages).
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.0] Mild winters, because of the warm sea: mean January (coldest month) temperatures are about 4-7°C.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.1] Cool summers, so it is never hot: mean July (warmest month) temperatures are about 14-16°C.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.2] The annual range of temperature (the gap between the warmest and coldest month) is small, only about 10°C, and snow is rare on low ground.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.3] Rain falls in every month, so there is no dry season.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.4] The weather is changeable, with prevailing (most frequent) south-westerly winds.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.b] Ireland's climate comes from several causes working together: where it sits on the globe, the ocean beside it and the winds that cross it. Each cause explains one feature of the climate.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.0] Latitude: Ireland lies between about 51.5°N and 55.5°N, in the mid-latitudes, where the sun's angle is moderate, so it is neither very hot nor very cold.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.1] North Atlantic Drift: a warm ocean current, the extension of the Gulf Stream, keeps winters mild and coasts free of ice.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.2] Maritime influence (the effect of being close to the sea): the sea heats and cools slowly, so the annual temperature range is small.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.3] Prevailing south-westerly winds blow off the Atlantic, carrying its mild, moist air over Ireland.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.4] Frequent Atlantic depressions (areas of low pressure that bring wind and rain) and their fronts (the boundaries between warm and cold air) bring rain. These weather systems are taught in the Weather and Climate chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.5] Relief rainfall: moist air is forced up the western mountains, cools and releases its rain on their western slopes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.b] The climate is not the same everywhere in Ireland. Because the wet south-westerly winds arrive from the Atlantic, the west gets the most rain and the east the least.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.0] The west is wetter than the east: about 1,000-1,250 mm of rain a year across much of the west, against about 750 mm in the driest parts of the east, around Dublin.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.1] Over 2,000 mm a year falls on many western mountains, because moist air is forced to rise over them.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.2] The east lies in a rain shadow (a drier area on the sheltered side of high ground), protected from the south-westerly winds by the western uplands.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.3] Wet days (days with measurable rain): about 150 a year on the east and south-east coasts, rising to about 225 a year in parts of the west.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.4] The south-east is the sunniest region, because it is sheltered from the wet south-westerlies.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.5] The north is slightly cooler than the south, and coasts are milder in winter than the inland Midlands, because the sea warms them.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.b] North West Europe is the international setting for this climate, because the same conditions that affect Ireland affect its neighbours. Further east the climate changes step by step.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.0] The same climate covers Ireland, Britain, north-west France (Brittany), Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark and coastal Norway.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.1] All of these face the Atlantic, share the North Atlantic Drift and lie in the path of westerly depressions, which is why they have the same climate.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.2] Moving east, away from the Atlantic, winters become colder, summers warmer, the annual range larger and rainfall lower, with a summer maximum (most rain falls in summer). This is the continental influence (the effect of being far from the sea).
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.3] The change is gradual, so the boundary of a climatic region is a transition zone, not a line.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.b] Climate shapes more than the weather. In a cool temperate oceanic region it also influences what grows naturally and how the land is farmed.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.0] Natural vegetation is deciduous forest (trees that lose their leaves in winter), mainly oak and ash.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.1] Soils are brown earth, the typical soil under deciduous forest.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.2] Grass grows for most of the year, because winters are mild and rain falls in every month. This favours pastoral farming (rearing animals on grass) in Ireland, mainly cattle and dairying.
[geo-region-concept-s1.8.b] Geomorphology is the study of landforms (natural features of the land) and how they formed. A geomorphological region is an area with a distinctive landscape produced by the same rock type, geological structure (how rocks are arranged and folded) or landforming processes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.8.0] Three settings are studied here: the Burren (a karst region), Munster ridge and valley (South Munster) and the Northern European Plain.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.b] The Burren in north-west Co. Clare is a karst region on Carboniferous limestone, a rock that water slowly dissolves. Formation of its landforms is taught in the notes on Landforms associated with particular rock types; here each landform is only named.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.0] The name comes from the Irish Boireann, meaning rocky place.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.1] Limestone pavement (a bare, flat expanse of rock): blocks called clints, separated by fissures (cracks) called grikes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.2] Turloughs: seasonal lakes in hollows, which fill in winter and drain in summer.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.3] Caves: Aillwee Cave and Doolin Cave, which holds the Great Stalactite, about 7.3 m long.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.4] Mullaghmore: a hill of folded, terraced limestone, an example of how the rock forms stepped slopes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.5] Very few surface rivers, because drainage is underground: water sinks through the cracks in the limestone.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.6] Glaciation: granite erratics (boulders carried by ice and dropped far from their source) from Connemara rest on the limestone, showing that ice scraped off the soil and exposed the pavement.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.b] The limestone shapes life on the Burren as well as its landforms: it affects which plants grow, how farmers use the land and why visitors come.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.0] Plants from Arctic-alpine and Mediterranean environments grow side by side, for example the spring gentian and mountain avens.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.1] Winterage: cattle graze the upland limestone in winter, because the rock stores heat and the ground is dry.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.2] Poulnabrone, a Neolithic (New Stone Age) portal tomb (dolmen, a stone tomb of upright stones with a large capstone), stands on the limestone pavement.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.3] Burren National Park protects part of the area near Mullaghmore.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.4] The Burren and Cliffs of Moher UNESCO Global Geopark (an area recognised internationally for its geological heritage): tourism is a major activity.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.b] South Munster is a region of Old Red Sandstone ridges alternating with Carboniferous limestone valleys. Its parallel pattern comes from folding of the rocks and then erosion.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.0] Armorican folding (a period of crustal folding) about 300 million years ago, on an east-west trend, produced anticlines (upfolds) and synclines (downfolds).
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.1] Differential erosion (different rocks wearing away at different rates) left the harder sandstone standing as ridges and wore the softer limestone down into valleys.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.2] Ridges (anticlines of sandstone): Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains, the Knockmealdown Mountains and the Caha Mountains.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.3] Valleys (synclines of limestone): the Blackwater valley and the Lee valley.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.4] Trellised drainage (a pattern of rivers following parallel valleys, with short links across them): rivers flow east along the valleys. The River Blackwater then turns sharply south at Cappoquin, Co. Waterford, and reaches the sea at Youghal. The Lee flows east to Cork Harbour.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.5] Drowned valleys at the coast, flooded by the sea, form rias (long, narrow inlets): Bantry Bay, Dingle Bay and the Kenmare River.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.6] Human consequences: towns, routes and fertile farmland lie in the valleys, while the ridges carry rough grazing, forestry and bog.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.b] The Northern European Plain is a vast lowland, mostly low, flat or gently rolling, stretching from western France into Russia. Ice and wind shaped its surface, and its flat, fertile land supports many people.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.0] Countries crossed: France (north), Belgium, the Netherlands, northern Germany, Denmark and Poland, and into Russia.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.1] Glacial deposits (material left by ice sheets), including moraines (ridges of rock debris), outwash sands (sand washed out from melting ice) and lakes, shape the north of the plain.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.2] A belt of loess, a wind-blown silt, forms fertile soils along the southern edge.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.3] Loess soils are fertile, so they support crops such as wheat and sugar beet.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.4] The Rhine and Elbe flow north to the North Sea; the Oder and Vistula flow north to the Baltic, which shows the plain slopes gently north.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.5] Polders: land reclaimed from the sea in the Netherlands. The Afsluitdijk, completed in 1932, closed off the Zuiderzee.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.6] Human consequences: fertile farmland, easy movement for transport because the land is flat, dense population and major cities, including Berlin, Warsaw, Hamburg and Amsterdam.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.b] Each of the three regions is defined by a different criterion, which shows how geomorphology can be used to define regions.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.0] The Burren is defined by its rock type, Carboniferous limestone, because that rock produces its karst landforms.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.1] Munster ridge and valley is defined by its geological structure, folded sandstone and limestone, because the folds gave alternating ridges and valleys.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.2] The Northern European Plain is defined by its relief and its surface deposits, glacial and loess, because it is flat and its surface is made of these materials.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.3] The Wicklow Mountains and the Leinster batholith (a huge mass of granite) are granite uplands, also classed as geomorphological regions; their landforms are taught in the notes on Landforms associated with particular rock types.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s5: 5. Nodal, city and urban regions
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.b] A nodal region is a city together with the surrounding area linked to it by flows of people, goods, services and information. Unlike the other regions in this chapter, it is defined by connections, not by uniform features.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.0] A node is a central point where routes meet by road, rail, sea or air, usually a town or city. Think of it as the hub that the surrounding area is connected to.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.1] A nodal region is also called a functional, city or urban region. It is functional because it is defined by what the links to the centre do, not by everything in it being the same.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.2] A hinterland is the area around a town or city that it serves and depends on for jobs, shopping, services and markets. It is also called its sphere of influence or urban field.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.3] The urban area is the continuously built-up part of the city, with houses and streets running on without a break. The hinterland extends far beyond it, into countryside and smaller towns.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.4] The commuter belt is the part of the hinterland from which people travel daily to work in the city (commuting means making that regular journey).
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.b] A city and its hinterland depend on each other. Four kinds of flow tie them together: jobs, services, goods and information.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.0] Jobs: commuters travel in from the hinterland every day, because the city has the workplaces and the hinterland has many of the workers.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.1] Services: the city provides shops, hospitals, third-level colleges (universities and similar colleges), entertainment and offices that smaller places cannot support, and people from the hinterland travel in to use them.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.2] Goods: the hinterland supplies food, water, raw materials and labour (workers); the city supplies manufactured goods and services in return.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.3] A port's hinterland is the area whose trade passes through it, so goods from that area are exported, and goods for it imported, through that port.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.4] Information: local newspapers, radio and TV stations based in the city serve the hinterland around it.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.b] A hinterland has no marked border, so geographers work out where it ends by tracing where a city's services and links reach. Each method below gives a picture of the area.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.0] Commuting data from the census (the official count of the population, which records where people travel to work) show how far workers come from.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.1] The catchment area of a hospital, college or secondary school is the area from which it draws its patients or students.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.2] The circulation area of a local newspaper (where it is sold) or the reach of a local radio station (where it can be heard) shows where the city's information flows.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.3] Bus and rail routes and timetables show which places are linked to the city and how often.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.4] The delivery areas of businesses, and the home addresses of shoppers or of members of a sports club, show who uses the city's shops and clubs and where they live.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.5] The edge of a hinterland is not a sharp line: neighbouring hinterlands overlap, and different services give different boundaries, since a hospital and a newspaper do not reach the same distance.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.6] Larger cities have larger hinterlands. A capital city's hinterland for some services is the whole country.
[geo-region-concept-s5.3.b] Hinterlands are not fixed. Changes in transport, work and shopping can stretch them or squeeze them.
[geo-region-concept-s5.3.0] Motorways, car ownership, faster rail and remote working (working from home, so fewer daily trips) extend commuter belts outwards, because people can live further from the city and still reach it.
[geo-region-concept-s5.3.1] New shopping centres or services in smaller towns can shrink a city's hinterland, because people no longer need to travel to the city to use them.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.b] A primate city is the largest city in a country, far larger than any other. Dublin is Ireland's primate city and capital: County Dublin has about 1.45 million people out of a State total of about 5.1 million (Census 2022).
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.0] It is the focus of the national motorway network: radial routes (roads spreading out from the centre like spokes) meet at the M50 ring road, which circles the city. The main rail lines also meet there.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.1] Dublin Port and Dublin Airport, Ireland's busiest airport, make the city the main national gateway (entry and exit point) for trade and travel.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.2] The commuter belt extends into Meath, Kildare, Wicklow and Louth, with fast-growing commuter towns (towns where many residents work in the city): Navan (Co. Meath), Naas and Newbridge (Co. Kildare), Bray (Co. Wicklow) and Drogheda (Co. Louth).
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.3] For government, national media, specialist hospitals and the main airport, Dublin's hinterland is the whole country, because these functions exist in Dublin and nowhere else on the same scale.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.4] Problems of its urban region: urban sprawl (the built-up area spreading outwards into the countryside), long commutes and congestion, and high house prices.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.5] There is more on the growth of city regions and Dublin's council structures in the European Regions chapter, and on land use and planning in the Urban Geography chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.b] Outside Dublin, Ireland's other cities act as smaller nodes, each serving its own hinterland.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.0] Cork: Ireland's second city, at the head of Cork Harbour on the River Lee.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.1] Cork Harbour has a port and a pharmaceutical industry (making medicines), with plants at Ringaskiddy. Cork's hinterland covers much of Munster, with commuter towns Carrigaline, Midleton and Cobh.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.2] Galway: the main city of the West, serving a large rural hinterland that includes Connemara.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.3] Galway is a centre of the medical devices industry (making equipment used in healthcare) and of higher education (study after secondary school), home to the University of Galway.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.4] Limerick and Waterford are regional cities serving the Mid-West and the South-East.
[geo-region-concept-s5.6.b] Dublin is so much larger than every other Irish city that national planning tries to even things out.
[geo-region-concept-s5.6.0] The National Planning Framework, part of Project Ireland 2040 and published in 2018, aims to direct more growth to Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford to counterbalance Dublin (to offset its dominance by building up the other cities).
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.b] Cities across Europe are nodes with hinterlands of different sizes. Some reach across a whole country, and one crosses national borders.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.0] Paris is the primate city of France, at the centre of the Paris Basin. Radial motorways and TGV (high-speed train) lines meet there, so its hinterland reaches across France.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.1] The first TGV line, Paris to Lyon, opened in 1981. Charles de Gaulle Airport serves the city, and its region, Ile-de-France, has about 12 million people.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.2] London is the capital of the United Kingdom and a global financial centre, through the City of London. Greater London has about 9 million people (Census 2021), and its commuter belt covers much of south-east England.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.3] Brussels is the capital of Belgium and seat of the European Commission and the Council of the EU, so its influence extends across the EU. NATO headquarters is also there.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.4] The European Parliament meets both in Brussels and in Strasbourg, its official seat, so not all EU institutions are in Brussels.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.5] Rotterdam is Europe's largest port, at the mouth of the Rhine (Europoort). Its hinterland is the whole Rhine basin, linked by river barges, rail and pipelines, so it crosses national borders.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.6] Milan is the main city of Lombardy, the core of northern Italy, and Italy's financial centre, home to the Borsa Italiana stock exchange (where company shares are traded).
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.7] Other urban regions named in exam matching include Munich (capital of Bavaria), Berlin (capital of Germany) and Rome (capital of Italy).
[geo-region-concept-s5.8.b] Most city regions have one dominant centre, but the Netherlands has a region built around several cities of similar importance.
[geo-region-concept-s5.8.0] The Randstad in the Netherlands is a ring of cities (Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague and Utrecht) forming one large urban region around a rural area called the Green Heart (Groene Hart).
[geo-region-concept-s5.8.1] It is polycentric, meaning it has many centres, in contrast to the single-centre city regions of Paris and Dublin.

CANDIDATES geo-glaciation-s1: 1. Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.b] Ireland was glaciated (covered by moving ice) during the Pleistocene, the geological period of repeated ice ages. The landforms in this subsection were shaped by ice in its two main cold stages, and the type of ice involved decides what it can shape.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.0] The older cold stage is the Munsterian; the more recent is the Midlandian. Their names are the ones to use when you are asked to date Irish glaciation.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.1] Munsterian ice covered almost all of the island, so it was more extensive than the Midlandian ice, which left the far south free.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.2] Midlandian glaciation ended about 10,000 years ago, and its ice did not cover the far south of Ireland, so the south kept land the Midlandian ice never reached.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.3] An ice sheet is a continental mass of ice covering whole landscapes, buried hills and all, as in Antarctica and Greenland today.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.4] A valley glacier is confined to a valley, so its path is set by the landscape. It is fed by snow and ice from corries (armchair-shaped hollows high on the mountainside).
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.b] Glacier ice is not frozen water poured into a valley. It is built up slowly from snow that never melts away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.0] Snow that survives the summer is compacted into firn (granular snow, also called nevé), because each year's new snow presses down on the older snow beneath.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.1] Firn becomes glacier ice as the weight of overlying snow squeezes out the air and the ice crystals grow larger and interlock, producing dense solid ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.b] A glacier gains ice at its head and loses it at its snout (its lower end), and the balance between these determines its behaviour. Geographers compare this to a budget of income against spending.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.0] Accumulation zone: the upper part of the glacier, where snow and ice are gained, because more snow falls there than melts.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.1] Ablation zone: the lower part, including the snout, where ice is lost by melting. Ablation simply means loss of ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.2] Equilibrium line: the boundary between the two zones, where gain and loss are equal over a year, so above it the glacier gains and below it the glacier loses.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.b] A glacier is not frozen in place. Gravity and the weight of the ice make it creep downhill by three different mechanisms, and friction makes some parts move faster than others.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.0] Basal slip: meltwater at the base lubricates the bed (the rock floor beneath the ice), so the whole glacier slides over the rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.1] Internal deformation (plastic flow): ice crystals slip past one another under the weight of the ice, so the glacier flows downhill.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.2] Rotational movement: ice in a corrie (the hollow where a glacier begins) rotates within the hollow instead of moving straight down the valley.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.3] Ice moves fastest at the centre of the surface and slowest at the sides and base, where friction with the valley walls and bed is greatest.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.b] The snout's position depends on whether more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts at the snout. This balance decides whether the glacier is getting longer or shorter.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.0] Advance: when more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts, the snout moves forward.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.1] Retreat: when melting exceeds the ice arriving, the snout moves back up the valley, although the ice is still flowing forward. The snout retreats because it melts back faster than the ice feeds it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.2] Heavier snowfall or cooler summers, which reduce melting, cause the glacier to advance.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.3] Less snowfall or warmer summers, which increase melting, cause the glacier to retreat.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.b] Average rate of movement is found by dividing the distance moved by the number of years. The figures below are illustrative, invented for practice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.0] Average rate = distance moved ÷ number of years, given in metres per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.1] Illustrative: a snout that retreats 600 m over 20 years has moved an average of 600 ÷ 20 = 30 m per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.2] Illustrative: a snout that advances 450 m over 9 years has moved an average of 450 ÷ 9 = 50 m per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.b] Glaciers erode mainly by plucking and abrasion, while freeze-thaw supplies rock debris at the edges of the ice. Together these processes carve the corries, valleys and lakes of glaciated uplands.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.0] Plucking (quarrying): meltwater enters joints (cracks) in the bedrock and freezes, bonding the ice to the rock; as the glacier moves on, it tears out blocks, mainly on the downhill side of rock obstacles.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.1] Abrasion: rock fragments frozen into the base of the ice scrape and polish the bedrock, like sandpaper on wood. It produces striations (scratches), polished rock and rock flour.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.2] Rock flour: fine powder ground from the bedrock by abrasion, which meltwater carries away, giving glacial lakes a milky colour.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.3] Freeze-thaw on the rock above and beside the ice: water freezing in cracks expands and breaks off fragments, which fall onto the ice and are carried away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.b] A corrie (also called a cirque) is the armchair-shaped hollow where a mountain glacier begins. Corrie ice feeds valley glaciers, so corries are linked to the valleys below them.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.0] 1. Snow collects in a hollow on the mountainside; nivation (erosion by snow patches) and freeze-thaw enlarge the hollow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.1] 2. The snow turns to ice, and the rotating glacier plucks and abrades the back wall and floor, deepening the hollow and steepening the back wall.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.2] 3. The result is a bowl with a steep back wall and a rock lip, called the threshold, at its lower edge, because the ice erodes the floor more deeply than the rim.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.3] 4. When the ice melts, water collects behind the lip, because the lip dams the hollow, and forms a lake called a tarn.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.4] Examples: Coumshingaun, Comeragh Mountains, Co. Waterford; the Devil's Punchbowl, Mangerton, Co. Kerry.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.5] Diagram: cross-section of a corrie with steep back wall, rock lip and tarn; plan view of the bowl-shaped hollow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.b] Corries do not only deepen hollows; they also eat into the mountain around them. Where corries meet, they leave sharp ridges and peaks standing between them.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.0] 1. Two corries cut back into the mountainside from opposite sides of a ridge.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.1] 2. Their back walls retreat, so the ridge between them is sharpened into a thin, knife-edged arête.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.2] 3. Three or more corries erode back around one summit from different directions.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.3] 4. The summit is left as a sharp, pointed pyramidal peak, also called a horn, because erosion has cut away every side of it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.4] Arête example: Striding Edge, Helvellyn, Lake District, England.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.5] Pyramidal peak examples: Carrauntoohil, Macgillycuddy's Reeks, Co. Kerry, Ireland's highest mountain; the Matterhorn, Swiss-Italian Alps.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.6] Diagram: plan view of two corries backing onto an arête; plan view of a horn ringed by three corries.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.b] A glacier reshapes the river valley it occupies. It turns a narrow V-shaped valley into a wide trough, which is why glaciated valleys look so different from river valleys.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.0] 1. Before glaciation the valley is V-shaped, cut by a river.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.1] 2. A glacier fills the valley and deepens and widens its floor by plucking and abrasion.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.2] 3. The glacier cuts off the river's interlocking spurs (ridges that overlap from alternate sides of a river valley), leaving blunt cliff faces called truncated spurs.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.3] 4. The result is a broad, flat-floored trough with steep sides, which gives the valley its U-shape because the ice cuts down and sideways at once. It is also called a glacial trough.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.4] Examples: Glendalough and Glenmalure, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.5] Diagram: cross-sections of a V-shaped valley beside a U-shaped valley, with truncated spurs labelled.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.b] When a small glacier joins a large one, the two do not cut to the same depth. This leaves the smaller valley stranded high above the main one.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.0] 1. A smaller tributary glacier joins the larger glacier that is filling the main valley.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.1] 2. Because the tributary glacier is smaller, it erodes less deeply, so its valley is left hanging above the main valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.2] 3. When the ice melts, the tributary stream falls over the edge of the hanging valley as a waterfall.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.3] Example: Poulanass Waterfall, falling from a hanging valley into the Glendalough valley, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.4] Diagram: long profile (side view along the valley) of a hanging valley and its waterfall above the main valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.b] A glacier does not erode its valley floor evenly. Where it cuts deeper, it leaves a rock basin (a hollow in the bedrock) that fills with water as a lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.0] 1. Plucking and abrasion overdeepen a section of the valley floor more than the rest, leaving a rock basin.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.1] 2. When the ice melts, the basin fills with water, forming a long, narrow ribbon lake, because the lake takes the shape of the valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.2] Example: Upper Lake, Glendalough, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.3] Paternoster lakes: a chain of rock-basin lakes along a valley floor, separated by bars of harder rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.4] Tarns, ribbon lakes and kettle lakes are all glacial lakes.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.5] Diagram: long profile of an overdeepened valley with a ribbon lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.b] A fjord is a glaciated valley on the coast that the sea has flooded after the ice melted. It forms only where a glacier cut the valley floor below sea level.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.0] 1. A glacier overdeepens a coastal valley so that its floor lies well below sea level.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.1] 2. When the ice melts, the sea floods the valley, leaving a fjord with steep sides.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.2] Examples: Killary Harbour, on the Co. Galway-Co. Mayo border; Sognefjord, Norway.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.3] Diagram: plan view of a fjord with steep sides and the sea at its mouth.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.b] Where ice flows over a bump of hard bedrock, it treats the two sides differently. Abrasion smooths the side it climbs and plucking roughens the side it leaves.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.0] 1. Ice flows over a bedrock outcrop (rock exposed at the surface), and abrasion smooths and polishes its upstream (stoss) side.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.1] 2. On the downstream (lee) side, plucking tears blocks from the rock, leaving a steep, rough face.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.2] 3. The result is a rounded hill with a smooth stoss side and a rough lee side.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.3] Example: Lembert Dome, Yosemite, California, USA.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.4] Striations: fine parallel scratches cut into bedrock by rock fragments held in the base of the ice; they are carved by the ice and show the direction of ice flow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.5] Diagram: cross-section of a roche moutonnee showing the stoss side, lee side and direction of ice flow; plan view of striations on bedrock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.b] A glacier carries rock debris, called its load, in three positions. Where the load sits decides how much work it does and what it looks like when dropped.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.0] Supraglacial load: debris resting on the surface of the glacier, such as rock falling from the valley sides.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.1] Englacial load: debris trapped within the ice, so it is carried inside the glacier, away from the surface and base.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.2] Subglacial load: debris dragged along the base; it is the most abrasive part of the load and does most of the erosion, because it is pressed against the bedrock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.3] Glacial load is angular and unsorted: ice does not sort it by size, and it is not rounded by water as river load is, so sharp edges and mixed sizes survive.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.b] Deposition means dropping the load. Ice that deposits material directly leaves a jumble, and this jumble also affects how water drains across the land afterwards.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.0] Till (boulder clay) is unsorted and unstratified (not laid down in layers), angular material ranging from clay to boulders, deposited directly by ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.1] Boulder clay plains of the Irish Midlands are formed where till is left across the land, for example when ice melts and drops its load over a wide area.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.2] Deranged drainage: no clear pattern, with many lakes and marshes, forms on poorly drained glacial deposits such as till.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.b] A moraine is a ridge or sheet of till. Its name depends on where in or around the glacier it was left.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.0] Lateral moraine: a ridge of till along the side of a glacier, built from debris falling from the valley walls, such as frost-shattered rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.1] Medial moraine: formed where two glaciers join, as their lateral moraines merge and run down the middle of the combined ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.2] Terminal (end) moraine: an arc-shaped ridge of till at the snout, marking the furthest advance or a long pause in retreat, because the snout dumps its load where it stays still.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.3] Recessional moraine: a ridge of till built at the snout during a pause while the glacier retreats.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.4] Ground moraine: a sheet of till left on the bed as the glacier melts back.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.5] Southern Irish End Moraine: marks the southern limit of the Midlandian ice sheet.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.6] Diagram: cross-section of a valley glacier with lateral, medial and terminal moraines labelled; plan view of an arc-shaped terminal moraine.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.b] Drumlins are smooth, oval hills of till, moulded by the ice that flowed over them. Their shape records which way the ice was moving.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.0] 1. Ice deposits or overrides an accumulation of till on the bed, building an irregular mass of material.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.1] 2. Ice flowing over the mass moulds it into an elongated, streamlined hill lying parallel to the direction of ice flow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.2] 3. The blunt, steep end faces up-ice, and the tapered end points down-ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.3] 4. Drumlins occur in swarms, all aligned in one direction, forming a basket of eggs landscape.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.4] 5. Where the sea floods low ground, drumlins become islands, forming drowned drumlins, as in Clew Bay, Co. Mayo, and Strangford Lough, Co. Down.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.5] Example: the drumlin belt across Co. Cavan, Co. Monaghan and Co. Leitrim.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.6] Diagram: plan view of a drumlin swarm with an ice-flow arrow; side profile showing the blunt up-ice end and tapered down-ice end.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.b] Ice can carry very large rocks over long distances. When it melts it drops them, so they end up in places where they do not belong geologically.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.0] Erratic: a boulder carried by ice and dropped far from its source, with a rock type unlike the bedrock beneath it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.1] Example: granite erratics from Connemara resting on the limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.b] Fluvio-glacial material is laid down by meltwater rather than directly by ice, so it differs from till. Exams ask you to sort landforms into three groups, listed here.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.0] Meltwater sorts material by size, so fluvio-glacial deposits are sorted, and layers are stratified (laid down in layers); till is neither.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.1] Exam classification, glacial erosion: corrie, arete, pyramidal peak, U-shaped valley, hanging valley, ribbon lake, fjord, roche moutonnee, striations.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.2] Exam classification, glacial deposition: till, moraines, drumlins, erratics.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.3] Exam classification, fluvio-glacial: eskers, outwash plains, kames, kettle holes.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.b] An esker is a long ridge built by a meltwater stream that once flowed through a tunnel in the ice. The ridge is what is left once the ice around it has gone.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.0] 1. Meltwater flows in a tunnel within or beneath the glacier.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.1] 2. The stream deposits sorted sand and gravel on the tunnel floor as its velocity falls, because slower water can no longer carry its load.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.2] 3. When the ice melts, the deposit is left as a long, winding ridge called an esker.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.3] Example: Esker Riada, a series of eskers across the Midlands from roughly Dublin to Galway, used as an ancient routeway (the Slí Mhór); Clonmacnoise, Co. Offaly, is built beside it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.4] Diagram: plan view of a winding esker ridge; cross-section of a ridge of sorted gravel.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.b] Meltwater leaves the glacier carrying sand and gravel and drops it beyond the ice limit. It builds a broad, flat plain, also called a sandur.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.0] 1. Meltwater spreads sand and gravel beyond the terminal moraine (the ridge of till marking the furthest ice advance) onto flat ground.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.1] 2. The deposit forms a flat, sorted outwash plain.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.2] Example: The Curragh, Co. Kildare, a plain of sand and gravel; it is well-drained grassland used for horse training and an army camp.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.3] Diagram: plan view of an outwash plain lying beyond a terminal moraine.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.b] Meltwater also dumps sand and gravel on and around the ice itself. When the ice is gone, mounds and hollows are left behind.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.0] 1. Meltwater deposits sand and gravel in a hollow on the ice surface or at its margin.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.1] 2. When the ice melts, the deposit is left as a mound called a kame; where it lies against a valley side it forms a kame terrace.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.2] 3. A block of ice buried in outwash melts, leaving a hollow called a kettle hole. When water fills the hollow it becomes a kettle lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.3] Diagram: cross-section of a kame beside a kettle hole.
