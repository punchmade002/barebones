TARGET geo-region-ireland-s1 (part 1 of 2): 1. A Core Irish Region
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.b] The Greater Dublin Area (GDA) is the core region of Ireland's east coast, facing Britain and Europe across the Irish Sea. Knowing exactly which counties it includes is the starting point for every question on it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.0] The GDA is Dublin City and County Dublin, plus Counties Meath, Kildare and Wicklow. Louth, including Drogheda, is not part of it, so do not count it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.1] Dublin City, Fingal, South Dublin and Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown are the four local authorities (the councils that run local services) of Dublin; the single Co. Dublin county council was split into the last three in 1994.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.b] A core region is one that concentrates people, wealth and services, in contrast to peripheral regions (remote areas with fewer of these). The GDA fits this description in several ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.0] Its large, dense and growing population is the largest in the State, which gives firms customers and workers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.1] It holds most of the State's wealth, jobs, multinational investment, government and services, which means firms and workers want to be there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.2] It has the best infrastructure in the State, including the main port, airport and motorway network, so goods and people move easily.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.3] It attracts in-migrants (people moving in) from the rest of Ireland and from abroad.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.4] Cumulative causation (growth that feeds more growth): growth attracts firms and workers, which attracts further investment and widens the gap with peripheral regions such as the West.

[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.b] The GDA has a cool temperate oceanic climate (mild and wet because of the nearby ocean), with prevailing south-westerly winds bringing moist Atlantic air. Because of where the mountains sit, the east is drier than the west.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.0] Rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of high ground): the Wicklow Mountains and the uplands further west force moist air to drop its rain on the western slopes, so the east is the driest part of Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.1] Rainfall in the east is about 750–1,000 mm a year, so it is much drier than the west; Dublin Airport records about 750 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.2] By contrast, the west receives 1,000–1,250 mm a year and some mountains exceed 2,000 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.3] The south-east is the sunniest part of Ireland, which adds to the east's value for crops.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.4] Mean January temperatures in Ireland are about 4–7 °C and mean July temperatures about 14–16 °C, so winters are mild and summers cool.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.5] Being farther from the Atlantic than the west gives a slightly greater temperature range: warmer summers, colder winters and more frost.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.6] Heavier rain and snow fall on the Wicklow uplands than on the lowlands, because rising air cools and drops its moisture.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.b] Climate decides what the land can be used for. The dry east and the wet uplands are used in different ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.0] Effect on farming: the dry, relatively sunny lowlands suit tillage (growing crops such as cereals and vegetables) and horticulture (growing vegetables, fruit and flowers).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.1] Effect on land use: heavy upland rain supports conifer forestry and reservoirs, because the rain feeds fast-growing trees and fills water stores.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.b] Relief means the height and shape of the land. The GDA has a wide lowland to the north and west and a mountain block to the south, and each is used differently.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.0] A low-lying, gently undulating lowland covers most of Meath, Kildare and north Co. Dublin, part of the Central Lowlands.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.1] The Central Lowlands are underlain by Carboniferous limestone (a sedimentary rock), covered by glacial deposits.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.2] The Wicklow and Dublin Mountains lie to the south, with a core of the Leinster granite batholith (a huge mass of granite that cooled underground), about 400 million years old (Caledonian, the mountain-building episode that made it).

[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.6] Effect: the lowland is easy to farm, build on and cross with roads and rail; the uplands suit forestry, water supply and recreation.

[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.b] Soil follows the rock and drainage beneath it. The lowlands have deep, fertile soils, while the granite uplands and bogs have poor ones, so soil sets how land is used.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.0] Deep, fertile brown earths (a well-developed soil rich in nutrients) on limestone boulder clay (stony clay left by ice sheets) cover Meath, Kildare and north Co. Dublin, among the best farmland in Ireland.

CANDIDATES geo-glaciation-s1: 1. Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.b] Ireland was glaciated (covered by moving ice) during the Pleistocene, the geological period of repeated ice ages. The landforms in this subsection were shaped by ice in its two main cold stages, and the type of ice involved decides what it can shape.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.0] The older cold stage is the Munsterian; the more recent is the Midlandian. Their names are the ones to use when you are asked to date Irish glaciation.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.1] Munsterian ice covered almost all of the island, so it was more extensive than the Midlandian ice, which left the far south free.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.2] Midlandian glaciation ended about 10,000 years ago, and its ice did not cover the far south of Ireland, so the south kept land the Midlandian ice never reached.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.3] An ice sheet is a continental mass of ice covering whole landscapes, buried hills and all, as in Antarctica and Greenland today.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.4] A valley glacier is confined to a valley, so its path is set by the landscape. It is fed by snow and ice from corries (armchair-shaped hollows high on the mountainside).
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.b] Glacier ice is not frozen water poured into a valley. It is built up slowly from snow that never melts away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.0] Snow that survives the summer is compacted into firn (granular snow, also called nevé), because each year's new snow presses down on the older snow beneath.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.1] Firn becomes glacier ice as the weight of overlying snow squeezes out the air and the ice crystals grow larger and interlock, producing dense solid ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.b] A glacier gains ice at its head and loses it at its snout (its lower end), and the balance between these determines its behaviour. Geographers compare this to a budget of income against spending.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.0] Accumulation zone: the upper part of the glacier, where snow and ice are gained, because more snow falls there than melts.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.1] Ablation zone: the lower part, including the snout, where ice is lost by melting. Ablation simply means loss of ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.2] Equilibrium line: the boundary between the two zones, where gain and loss are equal over a year, so above it the glacier gains and below it the glacier loses.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.b] A glacier is not frozen in place. Gravity and the weight of the ice make it creep downhill by three different mechanisms, and friction makes some parts move faster than others.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.0] Basal slip: meltwater at the base lubricates the bed (the rock floor beneath the ice), so the whole glacier slides over the rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.1] Internal deformation (plastic flow): ice crystals slip past one another under the weight of the ice, so the glacier flows downhill.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.2] Rotational movement: ice in a corrie (the hollow where a glacier begins) rotates within the hollow instead of moving straight down the valley.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.3] Ice moves fastest at the centre of the surface and slowest at the sides and base, where friction with the valley walls and bed is greatest.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.b] The snout's position depends on whether more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts at the snout. This balance decides whether the glacier is getting longer or shorter.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.0] Advance: when more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts, the snout moves forward.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.1] Retreat: when melting exceeds the ice arriving, the snout moves back up the valley, although the ice is still flowing forward. The snout retreats because it melts back faster than the ice feeds it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.2] Heavier snowfall or cooler summers, which reduce melting, cause the glacier to advance.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.3] Less snowfall or warmer summers, which increase melting, cause the glacier to retreat.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.b] Average rate of movement is found by dividing the distance moved by the number of years. The figures below are illustrative, invented for practice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.0] Average rate = distance moved ÷ number of years, given in metres per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.1] Illustrative: a snout that retreats 600 m over 20 years has moved an average of 600 ÷ 20 = 30 m per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.2] Illustrative: a snout that advances 450 m over 9 years has moved an average of 450 ÷ 9 = 50 m per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.b] Glaciers erode mainly by plucking and abrasion, while freeze-thaw supplies rock debris at the edges of the ice. Together these processes carve the corries, valleys and lakes of glaciated uplands.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.0] Plucking (quarrying): meltwater enters joints (cracks) in the bedrock and freezes, bonding the ice to the rock; as the glacier moves on, it tears out blocks, mainly on the downhill side of rock obstacles.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.1] Abrasion: rock fragments frozen into the base of the ice scrape and polish the bedrock, like sandpaper on wood. It produces striations (scratches), polished rock and rock flour.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.2] Rock flour: fine powder ground from the bedrock by abrasion, which meltwater carries away, giving glacial lakes a milky colour.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.3] Freeze-thaw on the rock above and beside the ice: water freezing in cracks expands and breaks off fragments, which fall onto the ice and are carried away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.b] A corrie (also called a cirque) is the armchair-shaped hollow where a mountain glacier begins. Corrie ice feeds valley glaciers, so corries are linked to the valleys below them.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.0] 1. Snow collects in a hollow on the mountainside; nivation (erosion by snow patches) and freeze-thaw enlarge the hollow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.1] 2. The snow turns to ice, and the rotating glacier plucks and abrades the back wall and floor, deepening the hollow and steepening the back wall.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.2] 3. The result is a bowl with a steep back wall and a rock lip, called the threshold, at its lower edge, because the ice erodes the floor more deeply than the rim.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.3] 4. When the ice melts, water collects behind the lip, because the lip dams the hollow, and forms a lake called a tarn.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.4] Examples: Coumshingaun, Comeragh Mountains, Co. Waterford; the Devil's Punchbowl, Mangerton, Co. Kerry.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.5] Diagram: cross-section of a corrie with steep back wall, rock lip and tarn; plan view of the bowl-shaped hollow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.b] Corries do not only deepen hollows; they also eat into the mountain around them. Where corries meet, they leave sharp ridges and peaks standing between them.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.0] 1. Two corries cut back into the mountainside from opposite sides of a ridge.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.1] 2. Their back walls retreat, so the ridge between them is sharpened into a thin, knife-edged arête.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.2] 3. Three or more corries erode back around one summit from different directions.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.3] 4. The summit is left as a sharp, pointed pyramidal peak, also called a horn, because erosion has cut away every side of it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.4] Arête example: Striding Edge, Helvellyn, Lake District, England.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.5] Pyramidal peak examples: Carrauntoohil, Macgillycuddy's Reeks, Co. Kerry, Ireland's highest mountain; the Matterhorn, Swiss-Italian Alps.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.6] Diagram: plan view of two corries backing onto an arête; plan view of a horn ringed by three corries.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.b] A glacier reshapes the river valley it occupies. It turns a narrow V-shaped valley into a wide trough, which is why glaciated valleys look so different from river valleys.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.0] 1. Before glaciation the valley is V-shaped, cut by a river.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.1] 2. A glacier fills the valley and deepens and widens its floor by plucking and abrasion.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.2] 3. The glacier cuts off the river's interlocking spurs (ridges that overlap from alternate sides of a river valley), leaving blunt cliff faces called truncated spurs.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.3] 4. The result is a broad, flat-floored trough with steep sides, which gives the valley its U-shape because the ice cuts down and sideways at once. It is also called a glacial trough.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.4] Examples: Glendalough and Glenmalure, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.5] Diagram: cross-sections of a V-shaped valley beside a U-shaped valley, with truncated spurs labelled.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.b] When a small glacier joins a large one, the two do not cut to the same depth. This leaves the smaller valley stranded high above the main one.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.0] 1. A smaller tributary glacier joins the larger glacier that is filling the main valley.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.1] 2. Because the tributary glacier is smaller, it erodes less deeply, so its valley is left hanging above the main valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.2] 3. When the ice melts, the tributary stream falls over the edge of the hanging valley as a waterfall.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.3] Example: Poulanass Waterfall, falling from a hanging valley into the Glendalough valley, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.4] Diagram: long profile (side view along the valley) of a hanging valley and its waterfall above the main valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.b] A glacier does not erode its valley floor evenly. Where it cuts deeper, it leaves a rock basin (a hollow in the bedrock) that fills with water as a lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.0] 1. Plucking and abrasion overdeepen a section of the valley floor more than the rest, leaving a rock basin.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.1] 2. When the ice melts, the basin fills with water, forming a long, narrow ribbon lake, because the lake takes the shape of the valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.2] Example: Upper Lake, Glendalough, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.3] Paternoster lakes: a chain of rock-basin lakes along a valley floor, separated by bars of harder rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.4] Tarns, ribbon lakes and kettle lakes are all glacial lakes.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.5] Diagram: long profile of an overdeepened valley with a ribbon lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.b] A fjord is a glaciated valley on the coast that the sea has flooded after the ice melted. It forms only where a glacier cut the valley floor below sea level.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.0] 1. A glacier overdeepens a coastal valley so that its floor lies well below sea level.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.1] 2. When the ice melts, the sea floods the valley, leaving a fjord with steep sides.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.2] Examples: Killary Harbour, on the Co. Galway-Co. Mayo border; Sognefjord, Norway.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.3] Diagram: plan view of a fjord with steep sides and the sea at its mouth.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.b] Where ice flows over a bump of hard bedrock, it treats the two sides differently. Abrasion smooths the side it climbs and plucking roughens the side it leaves.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.0] 1. Ice flows over a bedrock outcrop (rock exposed at the surface), and abrasion smooths and polishes its upstream (stoss) side.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.1] 2. On the downstream (lee) side, plucking tears blocks from the rock, leaving a steep, rough face.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.2] 3. The result is a rounded hill with a smooth stoss side and a rough lee side.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.3] Example: Lembert Dome, Yosemite, California, USA.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.4] Striations: fine parallel scratches cut into bedrock by rock fragments held in the base of the ice; they are carved by the ice and show the direction of ice flow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.5] Diagram: cross-section of a roche moutonnee showing the stoss side, lee side and direction of ice flow; plan view of striations on bedrock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.b] A glacier carries rock debris, called its load, in three positions. Where the load sits decides how much work it does and what it looks like when dropped.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.0] Supraglacial load: debris resting on the surface of the glacier, such as rock falling from the valley sides.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.1] Englacial load: debris trapped within the ice, so it is carried inside the glacier, away from the surface and base.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.2] Subglacial load: debris dragged along the base; it is the most abrasive part of the load and does most of the erosion, because it is pressed against the bedrock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.3] Glacial load is angular and unsorted: ice does not sort it by size, and it is not rounded by water as river load is, so sharp edges and mixed sizes survive.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.b] Deposition means dropping the load. Ice that deposits material directly leaves a jumble, and this jumble also affects how water drains across the land afterwards.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.0] Till (boulder clay) is unsorted and unstratified (not laid down in layers), angular material ranging from clay to boulders, deposited directly by ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.1] Boulder clay plains of the Irish Midlands are formed where till is left across the land, for example when ice melts and drops its load over a wide area.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.2] Deranged drainage: no clear pattern, with many lakes and marshes, forms on poorly drained glacial deposits such as till.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.b] A moraine is a ridge or sheet of till. Its name depends on where in or around the glacier it was left.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.0] Lateral moraine: a ridge of till along the side of a glacier, built from debris falling from the valley walls, such as frost-shattered rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.1] Medial moraine: formed where two glaciers join, as their lateral moraines merge and run down the middle of the combined ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.2] Terminal (end) moraine: an arc-shaped ridge of till at the snout, marking the furthest advance or a long pause in retreat, because the snout dumps its load where it stays still.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.3] Recessional moraine: a ridge of till built at the snout during a pause while the glacier retreats.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.4] Ground moraine: a sheet of till left on the bed as the glacier melts back.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.5] Southern Irish End Moraine: marks the southern limit of the Midlandian ice sheet.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.6] Diagram: cross-section of a valley glacier with lateral, medial and terminal moraines labelled; plan view of an arc-shaped terminal moraine.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.b] Drumlins are smooth, oval hills of till, moulded by the ice that flowed over them. Their shape records which way the ice was moving.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.0] 1. Ice deposits or overrides an accumulation of till on the bed, building an irregular mass of material.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.1] 2. Ice flowing over the mass moulds it into an elongated, streamlined hill lying parallel to the direction of ice flow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.2] 3. The blunt, steep end faces up-ice, and the tapered end points down-ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.3] 4. Drumlins occur in swarms, all aligned in one direction, forming a basket of eggs landscape.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.4] 5. Where the sea floods low ground, drumlins become islands, forming drowned drumlins, as in Clew Bay, Co. Mayo, and Strangford Lough, Co. Down.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.5] Example: the drumlin belt across Co. Cavan, Co. Monaghan and Co. Leitrim.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.6] Diagram: plan view of a drumlin swarm with an ice-flow arrow; side profile showing the blunt up-ice end and tapered down-ice end.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.b] Ice can carry very large rocks over long distances. When it melts it drops them, so they end up in places where they do not belong geologically.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.0] Erratic: a boulder carried by ice and dropped far from its source, with a rock type unlike the bedrock beneath it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.1] Example: granite erratics from Connemara resting on the limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.b] Fluvio-glacial material is laid down by meltwater rather than directly by ice, so it differs from till. Exams ask you to sort landforms into three groups, listed here.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.0] Meltwater sorts material by size, so fluvio-glacial deposits are sorted, and layers are stratified (laid down in layers); till is neither.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.1] Exam classification, glacial erosion: corrie, arete, pyramidal peak, U-shaped valley, hanging valley, ribbon lake, fjord, roche moutonnee, striations.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.2] Exam classification, glacial deposition: till, moraines, drumlins, erratics.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.3] Exam classification, fluvio-glacial: eskers, outwash plains, kames, kettle holes.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.b] An esker is a long ridge built by a meltwater stream that once flowed through a tunnel in the ice. The ridge is what is left once the ice around it has gone.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.0] 1. Meltwater flows in a tunnel within or beneath the glacier.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.1] 2. The stream deposits sorted sand and gravel on the tunnel floor as its velocity falls, because slower water can no longer carry its load.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.2] 3. When the ice melts, the deposit is left as a long, winding ridge called an esker.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.3] Example: Esker Riada, a series of eskers across the Midlands from roughly Dublin to Galway, used as an ancient routeway (the Slí Mhór); Clonmacnoise, Co. Offaly, is built beside it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.4] Diagram: plan view of a winding esker ridge; cross-section of a ridge of sorted gravel.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.b] Meltwater leaves the glacier carrying sand and gravel and drops it beyond the ice limit. It builds a broad, flat plain, also called a sandur.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.0] 1. Meltwater spreads sand and gravel beyond the terminal moraine (the ridge of till marking the furthest ice advance) onto flat ground.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.1] 2. The deposit forms a flat, sorted outwash plain.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.2] Example: The Curragh, Co. Kildare, a plain of sand and gravel; it is well-drained grassland used for horse training and an army camp.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.3] Diagram: plan view of an outwash plain lying beyond a terminal moraine.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.b] Meltwater also dumps sand and gravel on and around the ice itself. When the ice is gone, mounds and hollows are left behind.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.0] 1. Meltwater deposits sand and gravel in a hollow on the ice surface or at its margin.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.1] 2. When the ice melts, the deposit is left as a mound called a kame; where it lies against a valley side it forms a kame terrace.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.2] 3. A block of ice buried in outwash melts, leaving a hollow called a kettle hole. When water fills the hollow it becomes a kettle lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.3] Diagram: cross-section of a kame beside a kettle hole.

CANDIDATES geo-urban-s5: 5. Urban problems in developed-world cities
[geo-urban-s5.0.b] Congestion occurs when the volume of traffic on a road network exceeds the space and capacity available, so vehicles move slowly or stop. Most causes come from how cities grew and how people travel in them.
[geo-urban-s5.0.0] Rising car ownership puts more vehicles onto streets that were laid out for far fewer, so the same road space has to carry more traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.0.1] Low-density suburbs (spread-out housing with few homes per hectare) and commuter towns (towns whose residents travel to work elsewhere) are poorly served by public transport, so most journeys are made by car.
[geo-urban-s5.0.2] Radial roads (roads that run out from the centre like spokes) converge on a historic centre with narrow streets and few river crossings, funnelling traffic into one place.
[geo-urban-s5.0.3] Journeys to work and school peak at the same times of day, so the network is filled all at once.
[geo-urban-s5.0.4] Delivery vehicles and heavy goods vehicles (HGVs, large lorries) share the same streets as commuters and shoppers.
[geo-urban-s5.0.5] Jobs and shopping have dispersed (spread out) to suburbs that can be reached only by car, so even short trips add to road traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.0.6] Historically weak investment in rail has left cities without a fast alternative to the car.
[geo-urban-s5.1.b] Congestion costs a city time, money and health. Several of its effects feed back into more congestion.
[geo-urban-s5.1.0] Workers lose time and productivity, and businesses face higher costs because deliveries and staff are delayed.
[geo-urban-s5.1.1] Commuters suffer stress and a reduced quality of life.
[geo-urban-s5.1.2] Stop-start traffic causes air and noise pollution, with carbon emissions from idling engines (engines left running while the vehicle is stationary).
[geo-urban-s5.1.3] Crowded routes lead to road accidents.
[geo-urban-s5.1.4] Buses are slowed by the same traffic, which makes public transport less attractive and so adds to car use.
[geo-urban-s5.1.5] Businesses and tourism are harmed by poor access to the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.1.6] There is less space for pedestrians and cyclists, because road space is given over to traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.2.b] Dublin is the Irish city the exams use for traffic congestion. Its congestion comes from the way it spread out and the late arrival of alternatives to the car.
[geo-urban-s5.2.0] Sprawl (the spread of low-density development at the city edge) and car dependence bring large numbers of commuters into the city every day by car.
[geo-urban-s5.2.1] Radial routes converge on the centre, and there are few crossings of the River Liffey, so traffic is squeezed onto a handful of bridges.
[geo-urban-s5.2.2] The light rail network (electric trams) arrived late: the Luas opened in 2004, after the city's roads had already become congested.
[geo-urban-s5.2.3] Port traffic formerly ran through the city centre until the Port Tunnel opened in 2006.
[geo-urban-s5.3.b] Cities tackle congestion in two ways: by giving people a better alternative to the car and by managing where and how vehicles travel. These measures are mostly drawn from Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.3.0] Public transport: DART (the electric commuter rail along the coast, opened 1984), Luas (opened 2004), commuter rail and BusConnects (a redesign of the bus network), plus the planned MetroLink, which has no date or cost yet.
[geo-urban-s5.3.1] Luas Cross City linked the Red and Green lines through the city centre in December 2017, so passengers could travel between the two lines.
[geo-urban-s5.3.2] Bus lanes and quality bus corridors give buses priority over cars, so buses are not slowed by general traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.3.3] Park and ride: car parks at the edge of the city where drivers can switch to public transport, so fewer cars enter the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.3.4] Cycle lanes and Dublin Bikes, the public bicycle scheme launched in 2009.
[geo-urban-s5.3.5] Integrated ticketing: the Leap card, introduced in 2011, allows one card to be used across different transport services.
[geo-urban-s5.3.6] Orbital roads and bypasses: the M50 orbital motorway, built in stages from 1990, takes traffic around the city instead of through it.
[geo-urban-s5.3.7] The Port Tunnel, opened in 2006, took port traffic out of the city centre; a 2007 restriction banned most five-axle heavy goods vehicles from the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.4.b] The second group of measures aims to cut the number of car journeys, by making driving dearer or by making journeys unnecessary.
[geo-urban-s5.4.0] Pedestrianisation: the busiest shopping streets are closed to traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.4.1] Parking charges and limits discourage commuters from driving into the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.4.2] Congestion charging: the London Congestion Charge, introduced in February 2003, is a daily charge to drive into central London on weekdays, and traffic in the zone fell in its early years.
[geo-urban-s5.4.3] Car sharing, remote working (working from home) and staggered working hours (different start times) spread traffic over time.
[geo-urban-s5.4.4] Land-use planning that puts homes close to jobs and transport reduces the need to travel by car.
[geo-urban-s5.5.b] Urban decay is the physical, economic and social decline of part of a city, usually the inner city (the older area close to the centre). It is decline inside the built-up area, not growth at the edge.
[geo-urban-s5.5.0] Old industries and port jobs close, removing the economic base of the inner city.
[geo-urban-s5.5.1] Better-off residents move to the suburbs, leaving a smaller and poorer population behind.
[geo-urban-s5.5.2] Buildings are left derelict (empty and falling into disrepair) and are poorly maintained, particularly social housing (homes provided by the local authority).
[geo-urban-s5.5.3] Reduced rates income (the local tax paid by businesses and property) means the local authority has less money to maintain buildings and services.
[geo-urban-s5.5.4] Dereliction attracts crime and vandalism, which deter investment and residents.
[geo-urban-s5.5.5] Road schemes and planning blight: the threat of future road building leaves property uncertain, so owners stop investing in repairs.
[geo-urban-s5.6.b] Once decay begins, its effects reinforce each other, so a run-down area is hard to recover without outside help.
[geo-urban-s5.6.0] Unemployment and poverty among residents of the affected area, because local jobs have gone.
[geo-urban-s5.6.1] Derelict sites stand empty for long periods, because owners and investors see little reason to rebuild.
[geo-urban-s5.6.2] Population decline as families leave, so fewer people remain to support local shops and services.
[geo-urban-s5.6.3] Drug and crime problems, which grow where there are empty buildings and few jobs.
[geo-urban-s5.6.4] Poor services, because fewer people and less money remain to support them.
[geo-urban-s5.6.5] Stigma: the reputation of the area discourages investment and visitors.
[geo-urban-s5.6.6] A cycle of decline, in which each problem makes the others worse.
[geo-urban-s5.7.b] Dublin's inner city shows how the loss of port jobs and the clearing of old housing left an area empty and run down.
[geo-urban-s5.7.0] Dublin's docks lost most of their jobs with containerisation (the shift to shipping goods in standard metal containers handled by machinery, needing far fewer workers) from the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-urban-s5.7.1] Inner-city tenements (old, overcrowded rented housing) were cleared, and their residents moved to the suburbs.
[geo-urban-s5.7.2] Dereliction of inner-city streets and buildings followed through the 1970s and 1980s. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal, which covers the renewal of these areas.
[geo-urban-s5.8.b] Limerick is an Irish example of decay concentrated in social-housing estates rather than in the old centre.
[geo-urban-s5.8.0] Disadvantage (poverty and lack of opportunity) was concentrated in social-housing estates: Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston.
[geo-urban-s5.8.1] After an arson attack on two children in Moyross in 2006, the Government commissioned John Fitzgerald's report, published in 2007.
[geo-urban-s5.8.2] The report led to the Limerick Regeneration programme for Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston. There is more on its results in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s5.9.b] Detroit, Michigan, USA, is known as 'Motor City', the home of Ford, General Motors and Chrysler. Its decline shows decay on a city-wide scale.
[geo-urban-s5.9.0] Population peaked at about 1.85 million at the 1950 US census and fell to about 640,000 at the 2020 US census.
[geo-urban-s5.9.1] Middle-class, largely white families moved to the suburbs from the 1950s, helped by freeways (high-speed urban motorways), which left the city with fewer taxpayers.
[geo-urban-s5.9.2] Car-industry jobs moved out of the city or were automated (done by machines), so the city lost its main employer base.
[geo-urban-s5.9.3] Tens of thousands of houses were abandoned, because families had left and there was no demand for them.
[geo-urban-s5.9.4] Detroit filed for bankruptcy in July 2013, the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history, and exited it in December 2014.
[geo-urban-s5.9.5] Downtown and Midtown have since seen new investment, although many areas remain derelict.
[geo-urban-s5.10.b] Urban sprawl is the uncontrolled outward spread of low-density housing and other development over rural land at the edge of a city. It is growth at the edge, unlike urban decay, which is decline inside the city.
[geo-urban-s5.10.0] Population growth, high prices in the inner city, and demand for houses with gardens push development outwards.
[geo-urban-s5.10.1] Car ownership and motorways make distant sites accessible, so people can live far from where they work.
[geo-urban-s5.10.2] Planning that zoned greenfield land (land never built on before) for development, and developers' preference for cheaper greenfield sites, drive building at the edge.
[geo-urban-s5.10.3] Tax incentives during the Celtic Tiger boom (the period of rapid Irish economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s) encouraged building.
[geo-urban-s5.11.b] Sprawl spreads people thinly over a large area, which is costly for the people who live there and for the city they left behind.
[geo-urban-s5.11.0] Long commutes and congestion, and car dependence, because distances are too long to walk or cycle and public transport is poor.
[geo-urban-s5.11.1] Loss of farmland and habitats, because rural land is built on. There is more on this in Urban land use and planning.
[geo-urban-s5.11.2] Costly services, such as roads, water and schools, spread thinly over a large area, so each household costs more to serve.
[geo-urban-s5.11.3] Dormitory towns, whose residents sleep there and work elsewhere, leaving little local activity.
[geo-urban-s5.11.4] Decline of the city centre as people and spending move out, and higher carbon emissions from longer car journeys.
[geo-urban-s5.12.b] Dublin is the Irish example of sprawl, and its sprawl left a visible legacy when the property market collapsed.
[geo-urban-s5.12.0] Dublin's sprawl has extended into the commuter counties beyond the city boundary.
[geo-urban-s5.12.1] A Government survey in 2010 found almost 3,000 unfinished housing estates, known as ghost estates, after the 2008 property crash (the sudden collapse in house prices and building).
[geo-urban-s5.13.b] Solutions either stop the city spreading outwards or make better use of land already built on.
[geo-urban-s5.13.0] Green belts: a protected ring of open land around the city to stop its spread.
[geo-urban-s5.13.1] Compact growth and brownfield development (reusing land that was built on before), set out in the National Planning Framework, to build on land within the city.
[geo-urban-s5.13.2] Higher densities of housing near public transport, so that fewer people need cars.
[geo-urban-s5.13.3] New towns planned as self-contained settlements with their own services, so residents do not have to travel far.
[geo-urban-s5.13.4] Regeneration of inner-city sites that are vacant or derelict, so growth is directed inwards.
[geo-urban-s5.14.b] A community is a group of neighbours who know each other and share local life. In some urban areas the way housing was planned or built stops this from forming.
[geo-urban-s5.14.0] New estates are built quickly without schools, shops, community centres or public transport, so residents have nowhere to meet.
[geo-urban-s5.14.1] High-rise flats have no shared space where neighbours can meet.
[geo-urban-s5.14.2] Long commutes leave little time for local life.
[geo-urban-s5.14.3] Transient renting populations (tenants who move often) do not put down roots.
[geo-urban-s5.14.4] Old inner-city communities were demolished when tenements were cleared, breaking up established networks of family and neighbours.
[geo-urban-s5.14.5] Social segregation separates people by income and class into different areas.
[geo-urban-s5.15.b] When a place has no shared life, the people in it feel the lack, and the area itself suffers.
[geo-urban-s5.15.0] Isolation and loneliness among residents who do not know their neighbours.
[geo-urban-s5.15.1] Anti-social behaviour, which is harder to deal with where neighbours do not know each other.
[geo-urban-s5.15.2] Little sense of belonging or local organisation, so there are few residents' groups or clubs to take action on local problems.
[geo-urban-s5.16.b] Examples come from Dublin and from Paris. The responses aim to build the facilities and involvement that were missing.
[geo-urban-s5.16.0] Ballymun's tower blocks in Dublin: seven 15-storey towers built 1966 to 1969, demolished 2004 to 2015.
[geo-urban-s5.16.1] Commuter estates and the ghost estates of Dublin's commuter counties.
[geo-urban-s5.16.2] Paris banlieues (the suburbs on the edge of Paris): large grands ensembles (estates built in the 1950s to 1970s) such as Sarcelles, with high unemployment and the riots that began in Clichy-sous-Bois in October 2005.
[geo-urban-s5.16.3] Responses: community facilities built alongside housing, with phasing conditions (planning rules) so that schools and shops come before the homes are occupied.
[geo-urban-s5.16.4] Responses: regeneration with resident participation, mixed tenure (a mix of owned, rented and social homes in one area), and sports clubs and family resource centres.
[geo-urban-s5.17.b] Urban heritage includes historic buildings, streetscapes (the look of a street as a whole), archaeological sites and monuments, together with their setting. It is valuable in three ways.
[geo-urban-s5.17.0] Identity: heritage gives a city and its residents a sense of their history and place.
[geo-urban-s5.17.1] Tourism: historic buildings and monuments attract visitors and the income they bring.
[geo-urban-s5.17.2] Economic value: restored heritage buildings can support shops, hotels and cultural venues.
[geo-urban-s5.18.b] Heritage is easily lost because the pressures of a growing city work against old buildings.
[geo-urban-s5.18.0] Demolition for offices and roads, as when historic buildings are cleared for new development.
[geo-urban-s5.18.1] Neglect and dereliction of old buildings, so that without repair they decay and may have to be demolished.
[geo-urban-s5.18.2] Inappropriate new buildings, with heights that are out of scale with the historic streetscape.
[geo-urban-s5.18.3] Traffic vibration and pollution, which damage old buildings and monuments.
[geo-urban-s5.18.4] Tourist pressure, which can wear down historic sites and crowd out local residents.
[geo-urban-s5.19.b] Dublin has both losses and successes. The losses show the threats above, and the reuse examples show heritage put to work.
[geo-urban-s5.19.0] Lower Fitzwilliam Street: the ESB (Ireland's electricity company) demolished a row of 16 Georgian houses in 1965 to build its head office. That office block has itself since been demolished and replaced.
[geo-urban-s5.19.1] Wood Quay: one of the largest Viking-age sites in Europe. Despite protests and court cases in the late 1970s, Dublin Corporation (the city council) built its civic offices on the site.
[geo-urban-s5.19.2] 14 to 17 Moore Street, where the 1916 leaders spent their last hours before surrender, was declared a national monument in 2007.
[geo-urban-s5.19.3] Positive reuse: Temple Bar was regenerated in the 1990s, and Docklands warehouses have been reused for new purposes.
[geo-urban-s5.20.b] Heritage is protected by law, by voluntary groups and by international listing.
[geo-urban-s5.20.0] The Planning and Development Act 2000 created the Record of Protected Structures (a list of buildings the law protects) and Architectural Conservation Areas (whole areas protected for their character).
[geo-urban-s5.20.1] National monuments are protected by the State.
[geo-urban-s5.20.2] An Taisce (the National Trust for Ireland), founded in 1948, campaigns for the protection of heritage.
[geo-urban-s5.20.3] UNESCO World Heritage listing (international recognition of sites of outstanding value): Ireland's two World Heritage Sites are Brú na Bóinne, listed in 1993, and Skellig Michael, listed in 1996. Neither is urban.
[geo-urban-s5.20.4] International urban examples: the Old and New Towns of Edinburgh, listed by UNESCO in 1995, and the Historic Centre of Rome, listed in 1980.
[geo-urban-s5.21.b] Heritage attracts visitors in smaller cities as well as in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.21.0] Kilkenny's Medieval Mile attracts heritage tourism to a smaller Irish city. There is more on its medieval history in Pre-historic and historic settlement.
[geo-urban-s5.22.b] Environmental quality is how clean, quiet and pleasant an urban area is. Air quality is the first aspect, and Dublin's ban on smoky coal is the Irish example of a response to air pollution.
[geo-urban-s5.22.0] Sources of urban air pollution: smoke from coal fires, and traffic emissions of nitrogen dioxide and particulates (tiny solid particles in the air).
[geo-urban-s5.22.1] Dublin's severe winter smog (smoke and fog mixed) in the 1980s led to the ban on smoky coal in Dublin from 1 September 1990.
[geo-urban-s5.22.2] Research after the ban found that black smoke levels and winter death rates fell.
[geo-urban-s5.22.3] The ban on smoky coal was extended nationwide in October 2022.
[geo-urban-s5.23.b] Cities produce large amounts of sewage and polluted runoff, which must be treated before it reaches rivers and the sea.
[geo-urban-s5.23.0] The Ringsend wastewater treatment plant treats most of Dublin's sewage and discharges into Dublin Bay.
[geo-urban-s5.23.1] The Ringsend plant is being upgraded, so that it can keep treating the sewage of a growing city.
[geo-urban-s5.23.2] Storm overflows: heavy rain can send mixed sewage and surface water into urban rivers and the bay, because the drains cannot carry the extra volume.
[geo-urban-s5.23.3] Urban rivers carry pollution from paved surfaces, roads and drains.
[geo-urban-s5.24.b] Waste that is not recycled or treated becomes litter or goes to landfill, which harms the look and quality of an urban area. Ireland has used charges, recycling and energy recovery to cut it.
[geo-urban-s5.24.0] Litter and landfill: waste that is not recycled or treated goes to landfill (buried in the ground).
[geo-urban-s5.24.1] Plastic bag levy: introduced in March 2002 at 15 cent, after which plastic bag use fell sharply, showing a charge can change behaviour.
[geo-urban-s5.24.2] Recycling schemes reduce the amount of waste sent to landfill, because materials are reused instead of buried.
[geo-urban-s5.24.3] Poolbeg (Dublin Waste-to-Energy) incinerator, operating since 2017, burns residual waste (what is left after recycling) to generate energy.
[geo-urban-s5.25.b] Not all pollution is chemical. Noise and an ugly environment also lower the quality of life in a city.
[geo-urban-s5.25.0] Noise from traffic, construction and night-time activity affects residents, for example by disturbing sleep.
[geo-urban-s5.25.1] Visual pollution: graffiti, poor signage and unsightly derelict buildings reduce the quality of the streetscape.
[geo-urban-s5.25.2] Derelict sites: the Derelict Sites levy is a charge on owners of derelict property to encourage its reuse.
[geo-urban-s5.26.b] Green space gives wildlife a home and gives residents somewhere to relax. Dublin has several examples.
[geo-urban-s5.26.0] Parks provide habitats and recreation: Phoenix Park, about 700 hectares, established 1662.
[geo-urban-s5.26.1] Canals provide green corridors (strips of greenery that wildlife can move along) through the city: the Royal Canal and the Grand Canal.
[geo-urban-s5.26.2] North Bull Island, in Dublin Bay, is a green space beside the city.
[geo-urban-s5.26.3] Loss of green space to infill: small gaps between buildings are built on, reducing open space for wildlife and residents.
[geo-urban-s5.27.b] Building over land changes how water and heat behave in a city, which causes two problems.
[geo-urban-s5.27.0] Urban flooding: paved surfaces stop rain soaking into the ground, so runoff (water flowing over the surface) rises quickly into rivers and drains.
[geo-urban-s5.27.1] Hurricane Charley, in August 1986, caused severe flooding of the River Dodder in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.27.2] Urban heat island: built-up areas are warmer than the surrounding countryside because buildings and paving absorb and hold heat.
