MODE: cost and links
TARGET geo-economic-globalisation-s2: 2. The MNC and change
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.b] Modern manufacturing and services can move between regions and countries far more easily than older heavy industry. They are described as footloose, which means they are not tied to a raw material, a coalfield or a port. A multinational company (MNC, a firm that operates in several countries) can therefore choose where to locate.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.0] Light, high-value products such as chips, medicines and software are cheap to move by air, so the factory does not need to be near its customers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.1] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard sealed boxes) and cheaper transport lower the cost of moving goods between plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.2] Information and communications technology lets headquarters, the central office that controls the company, direct branches on any continent.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.3] Free movement of capital (money for investment) and lower trade barriers, through the World Trade Organization (WTO) and the EU single market, let companies serve markets across borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.4] Governments compete with grants and low taxes to attract investment, which gives companies a choice of locations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.5] MNCs can switch production between their own plants, so a plant that becomes less attractive can lose work to another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.b] Companies move production when another location offers a better return. The reasons are mostly about cost, markets and risk, and the moves go in both directions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.0] Lower labour and land costs, because wages and rents are a large part of what it costs to make a product.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.1] Tax advantages and grants, which lower the cost of setting up and running a plant.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.2] Being inside a trade bloc (a group of countries with shared trade rules), which avoids tariffs (taxes on imported goods).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.3] Being close to growing markets, so goods reach customers more cheaply.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.4] Access to skills, and political and economic stability, which make an investment safer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.5] Investment moved into Ireland, for example Intel in Leixlip in 1989 and Dell in Limerick in 1990, from the 1970s to the 1990s.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.6] Investment also moved out of Ireland when costs rose, for example Dell's manufacturing in 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.b] Mobility is not limited to the case study. These examples show the same forces at work for other companies and places, and they can support an answer on mobility.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.0] Nissan opened a car plant at Sunderland, north-east England, in 1986. It is a Japanese company locating inside the EU (then EEC) market, so its cars avoid tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.1] Bengaluru (Bangalore), India, is an IT and business-process outsourcing hub (a centre for work contracted out to another firm). Tata Consultancy Services, Infosys and Wipro are Indian IT firms, and call centres and IT services have moved there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.2] Apple is growing its iPhone assembly in India, with Foxconn and Tata near Chennai and Bengaluru, to reduce dependence on China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.3] Intel opened Fab 34 (a fab is a chip-making factory) at Leixlip in 2023. It cancelled a planned fab at Magdeburg, Germany, which had been announced in 2022 and was cancelled in 2025.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.3.b] When a plant moves, one region gains what another loses. The effects on the region matter because many plants are controlled from outside it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.3.0] The receiving region gains jobs, skills and exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.3.1] The losing region suffers unemployment, the loss of spin-off jobs (jobs in local suppliers and services that depended on the plant) and emigration of workers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.3.2] Branch-plant economies (regions whose factories are owned by companies based elsewhere) are vulnerable because the decisions about their plants are taken abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.b] A branch plant is a factory or office owned and controlled by a company headquartered elsewhere. Headquarters decides whether it opens or closes, to suit the whole company rather than the host region.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.0] Strategies that open branch plants: expansion into new markets; locating inside a trade bloc; following tax incentives and grants; acquiring other companies through mergers and takeovers; joint ventures that share the cost of huge investments; and government subsidy schemes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.1] The US CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 is a subsidy scheme: it provided about 50 billion US dollars of US government support for chip making and research in the USA. The EU Chips Act followed in 2023.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.2] Strategies that close branch plants: cost-cutting and rationalisation, which means concentrating production in fewer, larger plants; relocation to cheaper countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.3] Other closing strategies: outsourcing to contract manufacturers (other firms paid to make the product); falling demand for a mature product; and mergers that leave duplicate plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.4] Reshoring, which means bringing production back home for security of supply, so that the company is not dependent on distant plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.b] Dell is the clearest Irish example of an MNC moving production. Its story begins with a company built on a new way of selling computers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.0] Dell was founded in 1984 by Michael Dell in Austin, Texas. Its headquarters are in Round Rock, Texas.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.1] Dell sold direct: it built computers to order and sold them by phone and, from the mid-1990s, online, with no shops in between.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.2] Dell set up in Limerick (Raheen) in 1990. Limerick became its European manufacturing base, and Dell was one of Ireland's largest exporters.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.3] Dell went private in 2013 and bought EMC in 2016, which gave it EMC's large operation at Ovens, Co. Cork.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.b] In 2009 Dell closed its Limerick manufacturing and moved it to Poland. The move shows headquarters choosing what suits the whole company, with serious effects on the host region.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.0] In January 2009 Dell announced that it would move its Limerick manufacturing to Łódź, Poland, with the loss of about 1,900 jobs, plus job losses at local suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.1] Reasons: lower wages and costs in Poland; Polish government aid, which the EU approved; nearness to growing markets in Central and Eastern Europe.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.2] More reasons: falling PC prices, which squeezed profits, and the 2008-09 recession, which cut demand and made cost savings urgent.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.3] Effects on Limerick: the loss of about 1,900 direct jobs and of jobs at local suppliers, and the loss of one of Ireland's largest exporters.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.4] The EU Globalisation Adjustment Fund paid for retraining and support for the former Dell workers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.5] Dell kept its sales, support and other offices in Ireland. Manufacturing closed; the company did not leave Ireland.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.b] Intel, the chip maker studied in the multinational company note, shows corporate strategy at work: heavy investment, raising money by selling a share, cutting jobs when profits fall, and relying on government support.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.0] Intel invested billions of euro in its Leixlip campus, with over €30 billion invested by 2023.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.1] In 2024 Intel sold 49% of the Fab 34 joint venture to Apollo Global Management, an American investment firm, for about 11 billion US dollars, to raise money for its other projects.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.2] Intel announced about 15,000 global job cuts in August 2024, when its profits fell, with further cuts in 2025.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.3] Government support includes the US CHIPS and Science Act and a US government stake of about 10% in Intel in 2025.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.b] Product life cycle (PLC) theory, put forward by Raymond Vernon in 1966, says a product passes through introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Its best location changes at each stage, which is why MNCs move production over time.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.0] Stages: introduction (development), growth, maturity and decline. Sales are drawn as a curve against time.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.1] Introduction and growth happen in the core (the rich, developed countries), close to headquarters, research and development, skilled workers and wealthy early customers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.2] At maturity the product is standardised and competition cuts prices, so production moves to cheaper locations to keep costs down: first semi-peripheral countries (middle-income countries) such as Ireland in the past, then Eastern Europe and Asia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.3] In decline the plant closes, or it is upgraded to make a new product.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.9.b] Dell's move to Poland is a textbook mature-stage relocation.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.9.0] The personal computer became a mature, standardised product with falling prices, so competing on cost became essential.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.9.1] Dell therefore moved its assembly from Limerick to Łódź in Poland, a cheaper location, in 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.9.2] The Dell case shows the mature-stage pattern: production is moved to cut costs while the company keeps sales and support in Ireland.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.b] Intel's products have short lives, so its fabs are always being renewed. The cycle shapes where new technology is made and which plants survive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.0] Each chip generation has a short life of a few years.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.1] New processes are developed at Hillsboro, Oregon, then copied exactly into fabs such as Leixlip. This is Intel's 'Copy Exactly!' policy, which copies equipment and procedures exactly from the development fab to the others.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.2] Older fabs are upgraded or closed as the cycle moves on.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.3] Ireland keeps its place by winning each new generation, as Fab 34 does for the Intel 4 and Intel 3 generations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.4] Intel missed the start of the smartphone and AI chip cycles, which helped rivals. Nvidia leads in chips for artificial intelligence, and Apple dropped Intel chips from its Macs from 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.b] Information technology (IT) changes how an MNC makes, controls and sells its products, and it changes what customers want. Exams pair this with the product life cycle.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.0] Computer-aided design (designing on computer) and automation (machines doing tasks) are used in the fab.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.1] Global communication lets one headquarters run plants on several continents.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.2] Real-time stock control and just-in-time supply (parts arrive just when needed) reduce stock held at each plant.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.3] Online sales: Dell's direct model moved online from the mid-1990s.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.4] New technology changes demand, moving from PCs to smartphones and then to cloud computing (using remote servers to store and process data) and AI. This creates new markets and destroys older ones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.5] Moore's Law, from Gordon Moore in 1965, states that the number of transistors (tiny switches that make up a chip) on a chip doubles about every two years.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.b] Teleservices are services delivered at a distance using telecommunications, such as call centres, customer support, technical help desks, and shared services such as payroll and accounts. They can be located almost anywhere, so countries compete for them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.0] Why Ireland attracted them: an English-speaking and multilingual workforce, a time zone between the USA and Europe, good broadband, and IDA support (help from the State agency that attracts foreign investment).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.1] Apple's Cork campus provides AppleCare customer support in many European languages.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.2] Microsoft has been in Ireland since 1985.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.3] Threat: routine teleservices are moving to cheaper destinations such as India (Bengaluru) and the Philippines (Manila), and automation and AI chatbots are reducing routine jobs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.13.b] Ireland hosts the European or international headquarters of major internet companies, which makes the IT sector an important part of the economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.13.0] Google's European headquarters are at Barrow Street, Grand Canal Dock, Dublin, in the area known as Silicon Docks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.13.1] Meta (Facebook) has its international headquarters in Dublin.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.13.2] Ireland is one of the world's largest exporters of computer services.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.b] Data centres are large buildings filled with servers (powerful computers) that run cloud computing. Ireland has attracted many, but they bring a pressure on the electricity grid.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.0] Ireland attracts them because of its cool climate, which reduces cooling costs, its connectivity, and the technology companies already here.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.1] Data centres used about 21% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO), up from 5% in 2015. Most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.2] Operators include Amazon Web Services, Microsoft and Google.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.3] EirGrid (the company that runs the electricity grid) restricted new data-centre connections in the Dublin area from 2022, because they use such a large share of the electricity.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.b] E-commerce is buying and selling goods and services online. It changes where goods are stored, how they are delivered and which shops survive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.0] Amazon was founded in 1994 by Jeff Bezos in Seattle, USA. Alibaba was founded in 1999 in Hangzhou, China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.1] Effects: online-only companies appear, some high-street shops decline, and warehouses and delivery services grow.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.2] Cross-border parcels from Asia increase.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.3] Dell was an early large online seller.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.4] COVID-19 lockdowns, which began in Ireland in March 2020, accelerated online buying.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.b] The future of economic activity is being shaped by new ways of working, new technology and changing politics. Each trend brings opportunities and risks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.0] Remote and hybrid working (partly at home, partly in the office) has continued after COVID-19.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.1] Artificial intelligence and automation are reducing routine jobs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.2] Reshoring and friend-shoring (producing in allied countries): a move to secure supply of strategic industries such as chips.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.3] Risks for Ireland come from the global minimum tax, under which the rate is 15% for groups with turnover over €750 million, which reduces the tax advantage, and from US tariffs.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-globalisation-s1: 1. One multinational company (MNC)
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.b] A multinational company (MNC) is a company based in one home country that runs production or service branches in several other countries. The syllabus asks you to study one MNC to understand how it is structured and organised, so the general pattern comes first.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.0] Definition: a company with its headquarters (the main control centre) in its home country and production or service branches in several host countries (the countries that receive the branches). It is also called a transnational corporation (TNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.1] Typical structure: headquarters, research and development (R&D, the work of inventing and designing new products) and top management stay in the home country as the core. Branch plants, regional headquarters, and sales and service offices are placed abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.2] Decisions are made at headquarters, and branch plants compete with each other inside the company for investment, so a plant that is less efficient or more costly risks losing out to another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.3] Production is split into stages: design and R&D; basic processing of raw materials and components; assembly and testing; distribution and sales. Each stage is placed where it is cheapest or most efficient, which spreads sourcing, basic processing and markets across the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.4] The largest MNCs have sales or market values (the total worth of the company's shares) greater than the gross domestic product (GDP, the value of all goods and services a country produces in a year) of many countries, and they control much of world trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.5] The largest companies by market value in the mid-2020s are American technology companies: Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Alphabet (Google's parent) and Amazon.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.b] Foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment by a company in another country, made by building or buying a plant there. Ireland has a State agency whose job is to win this investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.0] IDA Ireland (Industrial Development Authority), set up in 1949, is the State agency responsible for attracting and keeping FDI, so it is the body that supports foreign companies both before and after they arrive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.1] IDA client companies employ about 300,000 people in about 1,800 companies (2023-24), concentrated in the Dublin region, which has the largest labour pool, universities, Dublin Port and Airport, services and existing clusters of firms.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.2] Other Irish-based MNCs: Pfizer (Ringaskiddy, Co. Cork, and Grange Castle, Dublin), Google (European headquarters, Barrow Street, Dublin), Meta (international headquarters, Dublin), Microsoft (in Ireland since 1985) and Dell (Limerick).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.b] Ireland has no shortage of competition for foreign investment, so a company needs reasons to build here. These are the MNC-specific advantages of Ireland, and each one can be developed into an explained point.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.0] Low corporation tax (the tax a company pays on its profits) of 12.5%, rising to 15% for very large multinational groups (turnover over 750 million euro) from 2024 under the OECD global minimum tax agreement.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.1] A young, educated, English-speaking workforce, which suits high-skill and customer-facing work.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.2] EU membership, which Ireland joined in 1973 as the EEC (now the EU), gives access to the single market (one trading area with no tariffs between members).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.3] Since the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020, Ireland is the only euro-area country where English is the main language (Malta also has English as an official language).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.4] IDA grants and support for companies that invest here, which lower the cost of setting up.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.5] Good international links, including ports, airports and broadband, so goods, staff and data can move easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.6] Clusters of similar firms and suppliers already in the country, so a new plant can find skilled workers and local suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.7] Universities and research links with industry, which supply graduates and shared research.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.8] A stable democracy with a pro-business government, which gives a company confidence its investment is safe.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.b] Intel is the main case study. It is a clear example of an MNC whose headquarters and design work stay in the home country while manufacturing is spread across several continents. Intel was founded in 1968, which is earlier than Apple (1976).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.0] Intel Corporation was founded in 1968 in California by Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore. Its headquarters are in Santa Clara, California, in Silicon Valley (the area of California where many technology companies are based).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.1] Intel made the first commercial microprocessor (a computer's processing chip), the Intel 4004, in 1971.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.2] Intel is best known for the processors inside most personal computers: the x86 family, sold as Pentium and Core, and Xeon for servers (powerful computers that serve other computers).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.3] Wafer fabrication plants ('fabs', factories that make chips on silicon wafers) are at Hillsboro, Oregon (Intel's main research and development fabs); Chandler, Arizona; Rio Rancho, New Mexico; Leixlip, Ireland; and Kiryat Gat, Israel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.4] Assembly and test plants are at Penang and Kulim in Malaysia, Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam, and Chengdu in China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.5] Sales offices are located around the world, close to the customers they serve.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.b] Ireland's part of Intel is a manufacturing branch plant at Leixlip, Co. Kildare. It is the country's largest private-sector investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.0] Intel came to Leixlip, Co. Kildare, in 1989.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.1] Its campus is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, with over 30 billion euro invested by 2023. It has several fabs and employs several thousand people.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.2] Fab 34 opened in 2023 and was the first fab in Europe to make chips in high volume using extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography (printing the tiny circuit patterns onto the wafer with a very short wavelength of light).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.3] Fab 34 makes the Intel 4 and Intel 3 generations, such as Core Ultra processors.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.4] In 2024 Intel sold a 49% share of the Fab 34 joint venture (a business owned jointly by two companies) to Apollo Global Management, an American investment firm, for about 11 billion US dollars, to raise money for its other projects.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.b] A fab has very particular needs: easy transport, a big site, skilled people and huge supplies of water and electricity. Leixlip met them, which is the answer to the Ordinary question on the advantages of Ireland for Intel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.0] Leixlip is on the M4 motorway west of Dublin, giving road access to the capital, so staff and goods can travel easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.1] A large, flat greenfield site, which is undeveloped land, so there was room to build very large plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.2] Maynooth University is nearby in Co. Kildare, and Trinity College Dublin and University College Dublin are in the city, giving access to graduates and research links.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.3] A large labour pool in the Dublin area, so Intel can recruit many workers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.4] A water and electricity supply; fabs use huge amounts of both, so a plant cannot work without a dependable supply.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.b] For the syllabus you must study one product of the MNC. Intel's product is the microprocessor, and knowing how it is made explains why Intel needs the inputs and locations described next.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.0] A microprocessor, or chip, is the 'brain' of a computer, because it carries out the computer's instructions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.1] It is made by building billions of tiny transistors (tiny electronic switches) in layers on a thin disc of pure silicon called a wafer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.2] Wafers are processed in a dust-free clean room, because dust would ruin the tiny circuits.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.b] Sourcing means where a company gets the materials and parts it needs. A fab depends on a few specialist inputs, and some come from a single supplier.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.0] Silicon, refined from quartz sand, is bought as silicon wafers from specialist wafer makers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.1] Chemicals, specialty gases and metals, which are used up in processing the wafers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.2] Huge volumes of ultra-pure water and electricity, because the fab uses them continuously.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.3] Manufacturing machines, especially lithography machines (which print the circuit patterns onto wafers) from ASML, based at Veldhoven in the Netherlands. ASML is the only maker of EUV lithography machines, which cost hundreds of millions of euro each.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.b] Intel splits chip making into stages and puts each where it suits best. Following the chain from wafer to computer shows how the stages are spread across several continents.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.0] 1. Stage 1: wafers are processed in fabs at Leixlip, Hillsboro, Chandler and Kiryat Gat.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.1] 2. Stage 2: finished wafers are flown to assembly and test plants in Penang and Kulim (Malaysia), Ho Chi Minh City (Vietnam) and Chengdu (China), where they are cut into individual chips, packaged and tested.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.2] 3. Stage 3: the chips go to computer makers' assembly plants, mostly in Asia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.3] Chips are small, light and very valuable, so they are moved by air.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.4] The Leixlip plant makes processed wafers only. Cutting, packaging and testing of the chips are not done in Ireland.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.b] A market is where a product is sold, and a market can be a country, a region or a type of customer. Intel's main customers are computer makers, server makers, governments and businesses.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.0] Computer manufacturers are the main customers: Lenovo (China), HP and Dell (USA).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.1] Makers of servers for data centres and cloud computing (storing and running data and programs on remote computers).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.2] Governments and businesses worldwide, because they also buy computers and servers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.3] Markets are in North America, Europe and Asia, with China one of the largest.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.4] Two markets outside Ireland can be named as countries, for example the USA and China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.b] Intel faces pressures from rivals, costs and supply chains. The exam asks for one difficulty, so learn several to choose from and give each in one explained sentence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.0] Competition from AMD (USA); TSMC (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company), the world's largest contract chip maker; Samsung (South Korea); and Nvidia (USA), the leader in chips for artificial intelligence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.1] Loss of Apple as a customer: Apple began replacing Intel chips in its Mac computers with its own M1 chip in November 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.2] Very high cost of building new fabs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.3] Falling profits: about 15,000 global job cuts were announced in August 2024, with further cuts in 2025 under the new chief executive, Lip-Bu Tan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.4] In 2025 the US government took a stake of about 10% in Intel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.5] Risk that production moves abroad if Ireland's tax advantage narrows (the 15% minimum rate for very large groups) or if costs rise.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.6] High energy costs and pressure on Ireland's electricity grid, since fabs need large amounts of power.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.7] Dependence on Asian supply chains: the global chip shortage of 2021, caused by COVID-19 disruption, halted car and electronics production worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.b] Intel's presence helps Ireland in several ways. These benefits are described without numbers because no checked figures exist for them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.0] Direct jobs on the Leixlip campus, and indirect (spin-off) jobs with local suppliers and services, which grow because Intel buys goods and services locally.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.1] Corporation tax paid to the State, and exports from the plant.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.2] Skills development and research links with universities.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.b] Apple is the alternative case study to Intel. Its product is the iPhone, and it is a good example of a company that designs in one country and has others make its products. Apple was founded in 1976, later than Intel (1968).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.0] Apple was founded in 1976 by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak. Its headquarters are at the Apple Park campus in Cupertino, California, which opened in 2017.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.1] Products: the iPhone (2007), iPad (2010), Mac computers and Apple Watch (2015), plus services such as the App Store, iCloud and Apple Music.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.2] Design, software and management are based in California, which makes California the core of the company.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.b] Apple's Irish base is in Cork. It is mainly a European headquarters and operations centre, not a phone factory.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.0] Apple opened at Hollyhill, Cork, in 1980. It was Apple's first manufacturing plant in Europe.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.1] It employs about 6,000 people in Cork and is Apple's European headquarters.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.2] The Cork campus runs operations and supply chain, finance, and customer support (AppleCare) in many European languages. It also assembles some built-to-order iMac computers.

[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.14.b] Unlike a company that builds everything itself, Apple concentrates on design and management and pays other firms to make its products.

[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.17.b] Apple sells its iPhones in almost every region, through its own outlets and through phone companies.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-activities-s2: The mobility of modern economic activities and factors in their location
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.b] Firms try to locate where total transport costs are lowest. This least-cost principle of industrial location was set out by Alfred Weber in 1909, and it depends on whether processing makes the materials lighter or heavier.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.0] Bulk-reducing industries are those where the raw material loses weight in processing, so the plant locates near the raw material because carrying the heavy input a long way would waste money. Examples: alumina refining, dairy processing, fish processing, mining.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.1] Bulk-gaining industries are those where the product gains weight or bulk in production, so the plant locates near the market because the finished product is the costly thing to move. Examples: brewing, soft drinks bottling, bakeries.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.2] Test for an exam location question: if the inputs lose weight in processing, the plant is pulled towards the raw material; if the finished product is heavier or bulkier, it is pulled towards the market.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.b] Location questions use a set of working terms. Knowing them makes the location factors easier to follow.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.0] Footloose industry: one that is not tied to a raw material or a market, so it is free to choose its location. The factors below explain where footloose activities choose to locate.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.1] Branch plant: a factory or office owned by a company headquartered elsewhere, usually carrying out routine production.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.2] Headquarters (HQ): the head office where a company's main decisions are made. Research and development (R&D): the work of designing new products and processes.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.3] Greenfield site: undeveloped land on which a new plant is built. Brownfield site: land already built on, often a former industrial site that may need clearing before reuse.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.4] Industrial estate or business park: a serviced area of land set aside for firms, with roads and utilities already in place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.5] Cluster, or agglomeration: firms in the same or related industries grouped together, sharing skills, suppliers and research.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.6] Backward linkages are links to a firm's suppliers; forward linkages are links to its customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.7] Infrastructure: the transport, energy, water, communications and waste systems that firms depend on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.b] A location factor is any condition that makes one place more suitable than another for an activity. The first three are the inputs a firm needs to make its product.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.0] Raw materials: weight-losing raw materials pull processing towards their source or towards a port where they arrive, which keeps the cost of moving heavy loads down.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.1] Energy: industry once clustered on coalfields, where the fuel was. National electricity grids freed most industry from this, but energy-hungry activities such as alumina refining and data centres still need large, reliable and affordable power.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.2] Labour: the quantity, skills and education, cost, productivity and flexibility of the workforce.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.3] High-technology and service firms seek graduates because their work needs advanced skills; standardised assembly (repeating the same simple steps) seeks low wages.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.4] Irish examples for labour: the Intel plant at Leixlip and the Galway cluster, both described below.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.b] Once a product is made it has to reach its customers, and firms need to receive inputs. Markets, transport links and communications decide how cheaply and quickly that happens.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.0] Markets: the size and wealth of the market, and access to the EU single market (the EU area with no trade barriers between members). Bulk-gaining and perishable goods locate near the market; services locate near their customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.1] Ports: deep water lets bulk carriers (large ships carrying loose cargo) berth. Dublin Port is Ireland's busiest port. Port of Cork and Shannon Foynes Port are also major ports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.2] Rosslare Europort: direct ferry routes to France grew after Brexit (2021) to avoid the UK land bridge, the route across Britain to continental Europe.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.3] Whitegate, Cork Harbour: Ireland's only oil refinery, opened 1959.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.4] Airports: air freight suits light, high-value, time-sensitive goods such as pharmaceuticals, medical devices and microchips, because the high price of the goods can bear the high cost of air transport.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.5] Dublin Airport handled over 30 million passengers in 2023; its North Runway opened in 2022.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.6] Freight modes: road offers door-to-door flexibility over short and medium distances; rail carries heavy loads on fixed routes; sea is the cheapest way to move bulk over long distances; air is the fastest but the most expensive.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.7] Motorways, rail, broadband and fibre (fibre-optic cable) connect firms to markets and suppliers; for services, broadband and fibre matter most. One environmental challenge of air transport is its CO2 emissions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.b] A firm also needs money and a welcoming policy environment. Governments, and the EU above them, can make a location more or less attractive through money, tax and rules.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.0] Capital: investment money comes from banks, shareholders, government grants and venture capital (money from investors who back new, risky businesses).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.1] IDA Ireland was set up in 1949 and became an independent agency in 1969. It attracts firms by offering grants, serviced sites (land with utilities already supplied) and advance factories (buildings built ready for a firm to move into).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.2] Corporation tax (the tax on company profits) was 12.5% from 2003. Very large groups pay 15% from 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.3] Enterprise Ireland (1998) supports Irish-owned firms. Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980) supports enterprise in Gaeltacht areas.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.4] Regional policy steers firms towards less-developed areas through grants and incentives.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.5] Planning permission (official approval from the local authority) is needed before a site can be built on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.6] EU state-aid rules limit the subsidies governments may give firms, while EU funds support regional development.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.7] Trade agreements reduce the barriers that firms face when selling across borders, which makes a location inside the agreement more attractive.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.b] After a firm picks a general area it must find a suitable piece of land, and workers and the plant both need services. The site and its services decide whether the plant can actually be built and run.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.0] Site: flat, well-drained land, large enough for expansion, at a cheap price, so that building is easy and the plant can grow later.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.1] Site: a water supply, and facilities for effluent (liquid waste) and waste disposal.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.2] Site: hazardous industry needs distance from homes, so that accidents or pollution do not harm residents.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.3] Services: water, electricity, gas, broadband, waste treatment, housing and schools. Workers must be able to live near the plant, which is why housing and schools count as location factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.b] Some location factors are about people and history rather than land and transport. Firms gather together, stay put out of habit, or follow the pull of a pleasant place to live.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.0] Agglomeration: firms in the same industry gather to share skilled workers, suppliers, research and universities, so each firm gains from the others being nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.1] Cork Harbour (pharmaceuticals) and Galway (medical technology) grew into clusters in this way.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.2] Personal and historical factors: the choices of founders and the history of a site can decide a location, whatever the economics of the place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.3] Industrial inertia: an industry stays where it is after the original reason for locating there has gone.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.4] Quality of life: attractive places to live help firms recruit skilled staff, because graduates can choose where to work.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.b] Local opposition and environmental regulation can stop or delay a project. Corrib gas shows how long a delay can be.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.0] Corrib gas was discovered in 1996. Its terminal (the plant that receives and processes the gas) is at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.1] The Shell to Sea campaign opposed the project, and the Rossport Five were jailed in 2005.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.2] Gas first flowed in December 2015, after a long delay caused by local opposition and changes to planning.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.b] The weight of each factor has changed over time as transport, energy supply and technology have changed. The pattern runs from resources, to customers, to skills and quality of life.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.0] In the 19th century, raw materials and power were the main location factors, so industry sat on or near coalfields and sources of materials.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.1] In the 20th century, markets and labour became the main factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.2] Today, footloose activities (those free to choose a location) chase skills, tax, connectivity and quality of life.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.b] A bulk-reducing heavy industry located on a deep-water estuary, where imported raw material arrives by sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.0] Aughinish Alumina opened in 1983 on Aughinish Island near Askeaton, Co. Limerick, on the south shore of the Shannon Estuary. It is Europe's largest alumina refinery.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.1] Bulk-reducing: about two to three tonnes of bauxite (the ore, or raw rock, that alumina is made from) make one tonne of alumina, so the refinery sits at the raw material's port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.2] Bauxite is imported by sea, mainly from Guinea and Brazil. The alumina (aluminium oxide) is shipped to aluminium smelters elsewhere; the refinery does not make aluminium.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.3] Deep, sheltered water in the Shannon Estuary, Ireland's deepest natural sheltered water, allows large bulk carriers to berth.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.4] The site is a flat island location away from towns, which suits a large heavy industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.5] Government support for heavy industry is a further factor in its siting.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.6] Problems: bauxite residue, known as red mud, is stored in large disposal areas on site.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.7] The company has been owned by the Russian company Rusal since 2007.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.b] The same estuary that serves Aughinish has also been used for energy, because deep water lets fuel arrive by ship.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.0] Moneypoint power station, Co. Clare, is on the Shannon Estuary and imported coal by sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.1] Coal burning at Moneypoint ended in 2025, so the estuary's energy role has changed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.b] Intel is footloose in the sense that its products are not tied to a raw material or a market, so it chose Leixlip from many possible sites. Its choice shows which factors matter to a high-technology firm.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.0] Intel's Leixlip plant opened in 1989. It is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, worth billions of euro.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.1] Skilled graduates are available, so with Maynooth University nearby, which meets the plant's need for a skilled workforce.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.2] IDA support and low corporation tax attracted the investment. There is more on this in Capital, government and EU policy.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.3] Access to the EU single market from an English-speaking base.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.4] The plant lies beside the M4 motorway and close to Dublin Airport, so goods and staff can move easily.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.5] It occupies a large greenfield site (undeveloped land) with a supply of electricity and water.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.6] Footloose does not mean likely to leave: the large fixed investment makes relocation unlikely.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.7] Global job cuts announced in 2024 and 2025 affected Leixlip, showing that even a large plant depends on parent-company decisions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.b] Cork Harbour combines deep water, industrial land and skilled graduates, so pharmaceutical firms have gathered there. Their closeness to each other reinforces the pattern.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.0] Pfizer, Janssen (Johnson & Johnson) and Novartis are at Ringaskiddy; Eli Lilly is at Kinsale, Co. Cork.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.1] The Port of Cork has deep-water berths at Ringaskiddy, which suits the import and export of pharmaceutical goods.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.2] Large flat sites are zoned for industry (set aside for it in the local plan), with water supply and waste facilities.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.3] Skilled labour comes from University College Cork (UCC) and Munster Technological University (MTU). MTU was formed in 2021 from Cork IT and IT Tralee.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.4] IDA support, tax and clustering reinforce the pattern.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.5] Pharmaceuticals and chemicals are Ireland's largest goods exports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.6] Older heavy industry in the same harbour: Irish Steel at Haulbowline closed in 2001.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.b] Galway shows how the closure of one firm can feed the growth of another industry. Skilled people left behind became the base for a cluster making medical devices.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.0] Boston Scientific opened in Galway in 1994. Medtronic, Parkmore, Galway followed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.1] The cluster draws on skilled engineers left by the closure of DEC's plant. The Dell and DEC block below gives the date.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.2] University of Galway research (renamed in 2022) supports the cluster, and a network of suppliers has grown around the firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.3] There is more on this in Irish Regions — Core and Peripheral, in the peripheral Irish region subsection.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.b] Food and drink show both sides of Weber's idea. Processing that makes the product lighter stays at the source, while brewing, which makes the product heavier, stays near its customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.0] Kerry Group grew from the North Kerry Milk Products co-operative at Listowel, founded in 1972. Its headquarters are in Tralee, Co. Kerry.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.1] Dairy processing is bulk-reducing: plants at Listowel and Tralee sit near the supply of milk.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.2] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland's largest fishing port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.3] Fish processing plants at Killybegs handle pelagic fish (fish that live near the surface) such as mackerel and herring, a bulk-reducing process that locates at the port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.4] Guinness at St James's Gate, Dublin, was founded in 1759 by Arthur Guinness on a 9,000-year lease.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.5] Brewing is bulk-gaining because water is added to the product, so the brewery sits near its market.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.6] Industrial inertia: Guinness still brews at St James's Gate today, long after the original reasons for the site have gone.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.b] Data centres (buildings full of computers that store and process online data) are a modern footloose activity, but they use huge amounts of electricity. For them the power grid has become a limit on location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.0] Data centres used about 21% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.1] EirGrid (the electricity grid operator) stated in 2022 that it would not connect new data centres in the Dublin region until about 2028, so the electricity grid now limits their location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.2] Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, Google and Meta operate data centres in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.3] Why Ireland: a cool, mild climate reduces cooling costs; fibre links to the USA and Europe; existing clusters of technology firms; tax.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.b] Modern economic activities can move more easily than older ones. Five changes have loosened the link between an activity and a fixed place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.0] Lower transport costs, through containerisation (goods packed in standard boxes that move easily between ship, rail and lorry) and air freight, let goods move cheaply over long distances.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.1] Instant communications, through the internet, fibre and video calls, let firms manage plants at a distance.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.2] Standardised production can be done by low-skilled workers, so the work can go anywhere with a cheap labour supply.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.3] The removal of trade barriers, through the EU single market and the World Trade Organization, makes it easier to sell across borders from any member location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.4] Multinationals (firms operating in many countries) can compare countries and move capital quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.b] A country that hosts a branch plant gains quickly, but its gains are not secure. The same mobility that brings the plant can take it away.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.0] Investment brings rapid job creation, but jobs can leave just as quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.1] Branch plants are vulnerable because the decisions are made at a distant headquarters, not in the host country.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.2] Countries compete for investment by offering tax and grants.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.b] A company opens a branch plant in another country when the move helps its own plans. These are the strategies behind such decisions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.0] Expansion into new markets, for example, so that customers are served from nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.1] Getting inside a trade bloc (a group of countries trading freely together), which gives tariff-free access to the EU. A tariff is a tax on imports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.2] Lower costs, because cheaper land, labour or tax raises profit.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.3] Access to skilled labour, because some work cannot be done without trained staff.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.4] Following competitors into a cluster, so as to share the skills and suppliers already there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.5] Tax planning, because a low-tax country leaves the firm more profit.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.b] The strategies that open plants have opposites that close them. A parent company closes a branch plant when the plant no longer suits its plans.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.0] Rationalisation: cutting surplus capacity after a fall in demand or a merger.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.1] Relocation to lower-cost countries, because production costs are lower there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.2] Outsourcing: contract manufacturers make the goods instead of the company's own plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.3] Automation (machines replacing workers) reduces the number of workers needed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.4] Mergers and takeovers, after which a parent firm may close a duplicate plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.5] A product reaching the end of its life cycle, because falling demand leaves the plant with no future output.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.b] A branch plant does not have to close when its routine work moves away. It can survive by changing what it does.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.0] A plant can survive by moving up the value chain (the steps that add more value to a product) into more R&D, advanced products and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.1] Intel's continuing investment at Leixlip is the example: Fab 34, opened in 2023, makes advanced chips using extreme ultraviolet lithography, a chip-making technique.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.b] Dell and DEC are two short examples of plants that closed or moved, and of how a closure can affect a region.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.0] Dell opened in Limerick (Raheen) in 1990.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.1] In January 2009 Dell announced that manufacturing would move to Łódź, Poland, with about 1,900 jobs lost in Limerick. It kept sales and service functions in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.2] Poland joined the EU on 1 May 2004, with nine other states, so its plants lay inside the enlarged EU with lower costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.3] DEC closed its Galway plant in 1993, and its skilled workforce later helped attract the medtech cluster.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.4] There is more on Dell in The MNC and change subsection of The Global Economy — Multinationals and Globalisation.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.b] A product passes through stages from launch to decline, and its best production location changes at each stage. This is how the product life cycle shapes where plants are found.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.0] Introduction: the product is developed near headquarters and R&D, in a core region with highly skilled labour.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.1] Growth: branch plants are opened in other developed countries near large markets; Ireland gained many.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.2] Maturity: the standardised product is mass-produced in low-wage locations, because it no longer needs high skills.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.3] Decline: the plant closes or production is concentrated in the cheapest plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.b] Apple's Cork site shows a plant changing its role as the company's production methods change, rather than closing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.0] Apple opened at Hollyhill, Cork, in 1980 and first made computers there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.1] As Apple's manufacturing moved to contract manufacturers in Asia, the Cork site shifted mainly to operations, support and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.2] The Cork site is now Apple's European headquarters, with about 6,000 staff (2020s).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.b] A host country is not helpless against mobile firms. It can aim to make its jobs harder to move.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.0] Attract firms early in new life cycles, for example in pharmaceuticals, medical technology and software, so the country has the work while it is new and growing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.1] Invest in education and research so that jobs are harder to move: Science Foundation Ireland, set up in 2000 and made a statutory body (one created by law) in 2003, funds research; it merged into Research Ireland in 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.2] Universities supply the graduates that keep high-technology firms in place.
