TARGET geo-urban-s6: 6. Urban planning and renewal
[geo-urban-s6.0.b] Cities try to solve their problems by controlling how land is used and by fixing areas that have decayed. Three terms describe the different approaches, and examiners expect you to keep them apart.
[geo-urban-s6.0.0] Urban planning is controlling the use and development of land through development plans (official documents setting out what may be built where), zoning (assigning each area a permitted use), planning permission and public investment.
[geo-urban-s6.0.1] Urban renewal is improving and rehabilitating existing run-down areas, keeping the community in place, so the buildings are upgraded but the people stay.
[geo-urban-s6.0.2] Urban redevelopment is clearing an area completely and rebuilding it, often with new uses and new people, so the old community is usually not the one that returns.
[geo-urban-s6.0.3] Renewal improves what is already there, while redevelopment clears and rebuilds. Ballymun and Limerick combined both, while the Docklands was mainly redevelopment because it was cleared and rebuilt.
[geo-urban-s6.0.4] Gentrification can be a side-effect of renewal or redevelopment: better-off people move in, prices rise and original residents are displaced, meaning they are forced out because they can no longer afford to live there.
[geo-urban-s6.1.b] Planners have a toolkit of legal controls, financial incentives and special bodies. Each one is used to steer where and how a city grows or is renewed.
[geo-urban-s6.1.0] Development plans and zoning decide the uses permitted in each area, such as housing, industry or open space, so that incompatible uses are kept apart.
[geo-urban-s6.1.1] Tax incentives in designated areas (areas officially chosen for support) reduce the cost of restoring or building there, which makes private developers willing to invest in run-down places.
[geo-urban-s6.1.2] Special development agencies with planning powers, such as the Dublin Docklands Development Authority (DDDA) and the London Docklands Development Corporation (LDDC), take charge of one area and drive its renewal.
[geo-urban-s6.1.3] Strategic Development Zones are fast-track planning for areas of national importance, so large projects avoid the usual delays.
[geo-urban-s6.1.4] Part V of the Planning and Development Act 2000 requires that part of new housing development be social (State-provided for low-income households) and affordable housing. Up to 20% of land zoned for housing could be required for this purpose.
[geo-urban-s6.1.5] The Part V share was reduced to 10% in 2015 and restored to 20% by the Affordable Housing Act 2021. Strategic Development Zones are created under the same Act.
[geo-urban-s6.2.b] Other tools aim at reusing unused land, connecting areas and involving the people who live there.
[geo-urban-s6.2.0] Derelict and vacant sites levies charge owners of unused property, to encourage its reuse instead of leaving it empty.
[geo-urban-s6.2.1] Public transport investment links new and renewed areas to the rest of the city, so residents can reach jobs and services.
[geo-urban-s6.2.2] New towns are planned as new settlements away from the existing city, taking growth from the older built-up area.
[geo-urban-s6.2.3] Regeneration companies with community participation bring residents into decisions about their area, so renewal is done with local people and not just to them.
[geo-urban-s6.3.b] The Advisory Regional Plan for Dublin, by Myles Wright in 1967, proposed the western new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown. It is the first national-scale plan to judge for effectiveness.
[geo-urban-s6.3.0] What it did: it housed rapid growth in new towns on the western edge of Dublin, rather than letting the existing city spread unplanned.
[geo-urban-s6.3.1] Effectiveness: the towns were built before services, jobs and transport, so their residents depended on distant jobs and facilities.
[geo-urban-s6.3.2] Tallaght's shopping centre, The Square, opened only in 1990, and the Luas Red Line (Dublin's light rail tram line) to Tallaght opened in 2004, decades after the first homes.
[geo-urban-s6.3.3] Criticism: the plan produced sprawl (unplanned spreading of the built-up area into the countryside) and the absence of community, because the towns lacked the facilities that would have made them places in their own right.
[geo-urban-s6.4.b] The National Spatial Strategy tried to balance growth across the country so that Dublin did not take everything. It did this by building up other centres.
[geo-urban-s6.4.0] What it did: it named nine gateways (large centres to drive regional growth: Dublin, Cork, Limerick/Shannon, Galway, Waterford, Dundalk, Sligo, Letterkenny with Derry, and Athlone-Tullamore-Mullingar) and nine hubs (smaller supporting centres), to balance growth away from Dublin.
[geo-urban-s6.4.1] The 2003 Budget announced a decentralisation programme (moving government jobs out of Dublin) that moved civil servants to dozens of towns.
[geo-urban-s6.4.2] Effectiveness: the plan was weakened by too many designated centres, which spread investment thinly, by decentralisation to towns not named in the plan, and by the property boom.
[geo-urban-s6.4.3] Dublin continued to sprawl, so the plan did not achieve its aim of balancing growth.
[geo-urban-s6.4.4] The strategy was replaced in 2018 by the National Planning Framework.
[geo-urban-s6.5.b] The National Planning Framework, known as Project Ireland 2040, was published in 2018 to replace the National Spatial Strategy. It plans where a growing population will live.
[geo-urban-s6.5.0] It plans for about 1 million more people in Ireland by 2040.
[geo-urban-s6.5.1] Half of future population growth is to be in Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford, the five cities.
[geo-urban-s6.5.2] Galway, Cork, Limerick and Waterford are each to grow by at least 50%, so that the regional cities act as a counterweight to Dublin.
[geo-urban-s6.5.3] Compact growth: 40% of new homes nationally are to be within the built-up footprint of existing settlements, on brownfield (previously used) and infill (gaps between existing buildings) land. This limits sprawl.
[geo-urban-s6.5.4] Regional Spatial and Economic Strategies are prepared by the three regional assemblies (regional bodies of local councillors), to apply the national plan at regional level.
[geo-urban-s6.5.5] The Urban Regeneration and Development Fund was set up in 2018 so as to fund renewal projects.
[geo-urban-s6.5.6] Effectiveness: too early to judge fully, but housing supply has lagged behind the targets.
[geo-urban-s6.6.b] Housing is one of three urban problems (with transport and pollution) on which planning effectiveness is judged. The strategies are the Part V requirement, Rent Pressure Zones and national housing targets.
[geo-urban-s6.6.0] Housing for All (2021) set a target of an average of about 33,000 new homes a year to 2030.
[geo-urban-s6.6.1] Rent Pressure Zones (areas where rent rises are capped) were introduced in December 2016, first in Dublin and Cork, to limit rent increases.
[geo-urban-s6.6.2] Part V social and affordable housing, described under Tools available to planners, adds homes within private developments, so new estates are not only for those who can pay market prices.
[geo-urban-s6.6.3] Effectiveness: completions (finished new homes) have risen, but they have stayed below the level of need, so shortages continue.
[geo-urban-s6.6.4] Over 15,000 people were in State-funded emergency accommodation in 2025, a record level of homelessness.
[geo-urban-s6.6.5] High rents remain a problem, which shows that the strategies have not yet matched demand.
[geo-urban-s6.7.b] The transport strategy covers public transport investment and cycling, with the aim of reducing congestion (traffic jams caused by too many vehicles on the roads).
[geo-urban-s6.7.0] DART (the electric coastal rail line) opened in 1984, and the Luas Cross City linked the Red and Green lines through the city centre in December 2017.
[geo-urban-s6.7.1] The Port Tunnel opened in 2006, and the 2007 restriction on heavy goods vehicles kept most five-axle lorries out of the city centre.
[geo-urban-s6.7.2] Dublin Bikes (a public bike-hire scheme) was launched in 2009 to encourage cycling, and BusConnects is the bus network redesign.
[geo-urban-s6.7.3] The NTA (National Transport Authority) Greater Dublin Area Transport Strategy 2022 to 2042 sets out the long-term plan.
[geo-urban-s6.7.4] Effectiveness: the Luas and the Port Tunnel succeeded in their aims, because they added rail capacity and took heavy lorries out of the centre.
[geo-urban-s6.7.5] Metro North, a planned rail line to Dublin Airport and Swords, was shelved by the Government in 2011. Its successor, MetroLink, is planned but has been repeatedly delayed, and no date or cost is yet set.
[geo-urban-s6.7.6] Congestion persists because land use remains dispersed (spread out), so people still need cars to reach jobs and shops.
[geo-urban-s6.8.b] Pollution policy mixes bans, charges and infrastructure upgrades. Some measures have clearly worked, while others have left problems behind.
[geo-urban-s6.8.0] Smoky coal ban: introduced in Dublin on 1 September 1990 and extended nationwide in October 2022.
[geo-urban-s6.8.1] Plastic bag levy, introduced in 2002 to make bags cost money, which cut plastic bag use sharply.
[geo-urban-s6.8.2] Ringsend wastewater treatment plant upgrade, so as to reduce discharges into Dublin Bay.
[geo-urban-s6.8.3] Clean Air Strategy, published in 2023 to tackle air pollution from traffic and solid fuel.
[geo-urban-s6.8.4] Effectiveness: smoke and smog were largely removed after the coal ban, because the main source of the smoke was banned.
[geo-urban-s6.8.5] Limits: traffic nitrogen dioxide (a gas from vehicle exhausts) and particulates (tiny airborne particles) from solid fuel remain problems.
[geo-urban-s6.9.b] The Docklands were derelict after the docks lost their trade to containerisation (goods moving in standard steel containers, which needed deep-water ports with more space). Renewal there was mainly redevelopment, clearing and rebuilding the area.
[geo-urban-s6.9.0] Actions: the Urban Renewal Act 1986 gave tax incentives in the designated area.
[geo-urban-s6.9.1] The Custom House Docks Development Authority was set up in 1986 to lead the renewal, and the International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) was set up in 1987. These are two different dates from the DDDA.
[geo-urban-s6.9.2] The Dublin Docklands Development Authority was set up in 1997, and was wound up in 2016 with its functions passing to Dublin City Council.
[geo-urban-s6.9.3] Flagship projects (high-profile showpiece developments): the Samuel Beckett Bridge (2009), the Convention Centre Dublin (2010), the Bord Gais Energy Theatre (2010) and the Luas Red Line extension to The Point (2009).
[geo-urban-s6.9.4] A Strategic Development Zone covering North Lotts and Grand Canal Dock was designated in 2014.
[geo-urban-s6.9.5] Results: new offices, homes and culture venues, and the Docklands became a centre for tech firms, including Google's European headquarters at Grand Canal Dock, known as 'Silicon Docks'.
[geo-urban-s6.9.6] Criticism: early schemes favoured offices and expensive apartments, while local residents got few of the jobs created. Social housing was slow to be built, and gentrification pushed up prices, so locals could not afford the area.
[geo-urban-s6.9.7] The DDDA was damaged by its role in the purchase of the Irish Glass Bottle site, Ringsend, in 2006 for about €412 million, at the peak of the property bubble. The land value collapsed after 2008.
[geo-urban-s6.10.b] Temple Bar is a renewal case: an existing run-down area was improved and turned into a cultural quarter rather than cleared.
[geo-urban-s6.10.0] Problem: the CIÉ (the State transport company) planned a central bus station in Temple Bar in the 1980s, and bought up property in the area.
[geo-urban-s6.10.1] Artists and small businesses moved in on short leases because rents were cheap in the run-down area, bringing new life to the area.
[geo-urban-s6.10.2] The bus station plan was dropped, and Temple Bar Properties was set up in 1991 under the Temple Bar Area Renewal and Development Act 1991.
[geo-urban-s6.10.3] Dublin was European City of Culture in 1991, which gave the area national attention.
[geo-urban-s6.10.4] Results: cultural venues, including the Irish Film Institute and the Project Arts Centre; restored buildings; and tourism.
[geo-urban-s6.10.5] Criticism: the area became dominated by pubs and night-time tourism, and few people live there permanently.
[geo-urban-s6.11.b] Ballymun combined renewal and redevelopment. Its 1960s high-rise flats were replaced by new low-rise housing and a new town centre.
[geo-urban-s6.11.0] Problems: seven 15-storey towers built in 1966 to 1969 suffered isolation, poor maintenance, unemployment and drugs.
[geo-urban-s6.11.1] Ballymun Regeneration Ltd was set up by Dublin Corporation in 1997.
[geo-urban-s6.11.2] The towers were demolished between 2004 and 2015, and new low-rise homes were built.
[geo-urban-s6.11.3] New facilities included Main Street, the Axis arts and community centre, and an IKEA store, which opened in 2009 as IKEA's first store in the Republic of Ireland.
[geo-urban-s6.11.4] Effectiveness: housing quality improved, and residents were involved in the regeneration, so people had a say.
[geo-urban-s6.11.5] Limits: the recession stalled the town centre, so it stayed unfinished; the promised Metro North rail link was never built (it was shelved in 2011), and unemployment stayed high.
[geo-urban-s6.12.b] Limerick Regeneration addresses estates of concentrated disadvantage (where poverty and social problems are clustered together), after the 2006 arson attack in Moyross and the 2007 report by John Fitzgerald.
[geo-urban-s6.12.0] Limerick Regeneration agencies were set up in 2007, and were later integrated into Limerick City and County Council, formed in 2014.
[geo-urban-s6.12.1] The programme covered Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston.
[geo-urban-s6.12.2] Actions: demolition of the worst estates, building of new homes, and social and economic programmes.
[geo-urban-s6.12.3] Criticism: the regeneration was slow, and demolition outpaced rebuilding, so families were left waiting for new homes.
[geo-urban-s6.12.4] Criticism: the populations of the estates fell during the process, because residents moved away.
[geo-urban-s6.13.b] Adamstown, near Lucan in South Dublin, is a planned new suburb built as a Strategic Development Zone. It tests whether planners can deliver services alongside homes, the failing of earlier new towns.
[geo-urban-s6.13.0] Designated a Strategic Development Zone in 2001, with fast-track planning.
[geo-urban-s6.13.1] Planned as a new suburb with its own railway station on the Kildare line, and with schools and services built in phases alongside the housing.
[geo-urban-s6.13.2] Effectiveness: a model of planned phasing, in which services arrive with the homes, so residents are not left without schools or transport.
[geo-urban-s6.13.3] Development stalled after the 2008 property crash and resumed in the late 2010s, showing how much the scheme depended on the market.
[geo-urban-s6.14.b] Renewal is not only a Dublin matter. Tax relief and strategic plans were also used in the smaller cities, with Cork the main example.
[geo-urban-s6.14.0] Living City Initiative, introduced in 2015: tax relief for refurbishing older buildings in special regeneration areas of Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway, Waterford and Kilkenny, later extended.
[geo-urban-s6.14.1] Cork Area Strategic Plan, 2001, a plan for the city and its surroundings, guided planning in the city.
[geo-urban-s6.14.2] The Cork city boundary was extended in 2019, so the city could plan for its growing suburbs.
[geo-urban-s6.14.3] Luas Cork: a light rail line proposed in the Cork Metropolitan Area Transport Strategy of 2020. It is proposed only, and no cost, route length or opening date has been set.
[geo-urban-s6.14.4] Cork Docklands is the next large regeneration project in the city.
[geo-urban-s6.15.b] The London Docklands were redeveloped after the docks closed in the 1960s to 1980s. The scheme was top-down (decided and run from above rather than by local people), led by an agency with its own planning powers.
[geo-urban-s6.15.0] London Docklands Development Corporation, 1981 to 1998, was an unelected body with planning powers.
[geo-urban-s6.15.1] An Enterprise Zone on the Isle of Dogs gave tax breaks and simplified planning, to attract investors.
[geo-urban-s6.15.2] Canary Wharf was developed, with One Canada Square completed in 1991.
[geo-urban-s6.15.3] The Docklands Light Railway opened in 1987, and London City Airport opened in 1987.
[geo-urban-s6.15.4] The Jubilee Line extension opened in 1999, which is why critics say transport came late.
[geo-urban-s6.15.5] Results: a second financial centre (after the City of London), so jobs and housing followed.
[geo-urban-s6.15.6] Criticism: the corporation was top-down and bypassed local councils, because it was unelected and held the planning powers.
[geo-urban-s6.15.7] Criticism: jobs went to commuters from outside the area, while local residents were priced out, and transport came late.
[geo-urban-s6.16.b] Paris gives a second developed-world example of planning by new towns and large estates.
[geo-urban-s6.16.0] The five new towns from the 1965 regional plan, so as to relieve pressure, took growth away from the city of Paris.
[geo-urban-s6.16.1] The grands ensembles, large housing estates in the banlieues (the suburbs around the city), became problem estates.
[geo-urban-s6.16.2] The Grand Paris Express, about 200 km of new automatic metro lines with 68 stations, aims to link the banlieues to the rest of the region.
[geo-urban-s6.17.b] Putting the strategies and case studies together gives three conclusions that you can use to finish a long answer.
[geo-urban-s6.17.0] Planning works best, for example in Adamstown, when housing, jobs, services and transport are delivered together, and when residents are involved in decisions.
[geo-urban-s6.17.1] Flagship property-led schemes create jobs and buildings, but do not always bring benefits to local people, as in the Docklands.
[geo-urban-s6.17.2] National plans are undermined when other policies pull in the opposite direction, such as tax incentives for development and decentralisation to towns outside the plan.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-activities-s1: 1. Case study of a developed economy: Ireland
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.b] Economic activities (the ways people earn a living) are grouped into three sectors by what they do with raw materials or with people's needs. Knowing which sector a job belongs to is the first step in describing any economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.0] Primary activity is extracting raw materials from nature, such as farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying and energy extraction. Nothing is made yet, so the resource is simply taken from the land or sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.1] Secondary activity is processing raw materials or assembling components (parts) into goods, such as manufacturing, food processing and construction.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.2] Tertiary activity is services, which provide help or access rather than goods, such as retail, banking, tourism, teaching, health, transport and IT (information technology).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.3] Classifying jobs: a miner or forester is primary; a builder is secondary, because construction is secondary; a teacher, banker or tour guide is tertiary.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.4] Some writers add a quaternary sector for research and information, but the three-sector model is used here.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.b] The share of workers in each sector changes as a country becomes richer. Ireland shows the end result of that shift.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.0] As an economy develops, employment moves from primary to secondary and then to tertiary activity. This is why primary employment falls as countries develop.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.1] Services employ about three-quarters of Irish workers (2020s), which means Ireland is a tertiary economy; agriculture, forestry and fishing employ about 4–5%.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.2] Census 2022 counted a State population of about 5.1 million; the CSO (Central Statistics Office) Labour Force Survey (2024) recorded over 2.7 million people in employment.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.3] In a developing economy, by contrast, primary employment is high. There is more on this in the Economic Development notes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.b] For its first decades as an independent State, Ireland tried to shelter its own firms from foreign competition. This is the starting point of the timeline of how Ireland became a developed economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.0] The Control of Manufactures Acts, 1932 and 1934, restricted foreign ownership of Irish firms, so industry stayed in Irish hands.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.1] High tariffs (taxes on imported goods) protected small Irish firms serving the home market, so they had little pressure to become efficient or to export.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.2] The 1950s brought high unemployment and heavy emigration (people leaving the country to live abroad), which showed that protection had not created enough jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.b] From the late 1950s Ireland reversed its policy and opened up to trade and foreign companies. Each step below was meant to bring in jobs and exports.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.0] The Industrial Development Authority (IDA) was set up in 1949 to attract industry, so Ireland had an agency for winning new firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.1] Export Profits Tax Relief, 1956, removed tax on profits from exports, so firms had a reason to sell abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.2] T.K. Whitaker's Economic Development and the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958) set the policy of free trade, foreign investment and export-led growth (growth driven by selling abroad), carried out under Seán Lemass as Taoiseach from 1959.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.3] The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement (1965) reduced trade barriers with Britain, Ireland's nearest and biggest market.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.4] Ireland joined the EEC (European Economic Community, the forerunner of the EU) on 1 January 1973, together with the UK and Denmark.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.b] Attracting foreign firms also needed people with the right skills. Free schooling was the policy that built them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.0] Free second-level education was announced in 1966 by Donogh O'Malley and introduced in 1967.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.1] A better-educated workforce made Ireland attractive to firms that needed skilled labour in the decades that followed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.2] The IDA became an independent agency under the Industrial Development Act 1969, so it could pursue foreign investment in its own right.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.b] Later steps tied Ireland more closely to Europe and set the tax rates that attract multinational companies. Corporation tax is the tax a company pays on its profits.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.0] The EU Single Market began in 1993, giving Irish firms and foreign investors access to the wider EU market without trade barriers.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.1] The euro was adopted in 1999, with notes and coins introduced in 2002, which means Irish firms trade with the eurozone (the countries using the euro) without exchange-rate costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.2] Corporation tax on trading profits was 12.5% from 2003. This low rate is a main reason multinationals locate in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.3] From 2024 very large multinational groups (global turnover over €750 million) pay a 15% minimum rate under the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) agreement.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.b] The Celtic Tiger is the name for Ireland's period of very rapid growth. It followed from the earlier policies of free trade, investment and education.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.0] Rapid growth from the mid-1990s to 2007 was driven first by exports and foreign direct investment (FDI, money invested by foreign firms in Irish plants), especially from US technology and pharmaceutical firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.1] A property and construction boom added to growth later in the period, because rising prices encouraged more building.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.2] Unemployment fell to about 4–5% by the mid-2000s, because so many new jobs were created.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.3] Net immigration (more people arriving than leaving) replaced emigration, with large inflows from the new EU states after the 2004 enlargement.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.b] The property boom ended in a crash that put Irish banks in danger. The State stepped in to protect them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.0] The property bubble (a rise in prices that could not last) burst in 2007–2008, leaving banks with large bad loans (loans unlikely to be repaid) secured on property that had lost value.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.1] On 30 September 2008 the government guaranteed Irish bank liabilities, promising that the banks' debts would be covered.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.2] Anglo Irish Bank was nationalised (taken into State ownership) in January 2009.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.3] The National Asset Management Agency (NAMA), set up in 2009, took over property loans from the banks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.b] The cost of rescuing the banks left the State unable to borrow normally, so it needed outside help. The crash also hit jobs and population.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.0] An EU–IMF (International Monetary Fund) programme was agreed in November 2010, providing €67.5 billion in external loans.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.1] Ireland left the programme in December 2013, which means the State could borrow on normal markets again.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.2] Unemployment peaked at about 16% in early 2012, so net emigration resumed from 2009 to 2014.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.3] Government debt peaked at about 120% of GDP (the value of everything produced in the country in a year) in 2012: the state borrowed to rescue the banks while its tax base (the income and spending that can be taxed) collapsed with the economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.b] After 2013 the economy recovered strongly. Jobs returned and public finances improved.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.0] Unemployment was about 4–5% in 2023–2024, near full employment (almost everyone who wants work has it).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.1] Budget surpluses (tax income greater than spending) in the 2020s came largely from corporation tax paid by a small number of large multinationals, so public finances depend on them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.2] COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020–2021 hit tourism, hospitality and retail hardest.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.b] When most people who want work have it, the whole economy gains, not only the workers. Each benefit below follows from people earning and spending.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.0] Employed people pay income tax, and their spending pays VAT (value-added tax, a tax on goods and services), so tax revenue rises.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.1] Welfare spending falls because fewer people need unemployment payments, so the State has more to spend elsewhere.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.2] Consumer spending by employed people supports jobs in other sectors, such as shops and cafes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.3] Less emigration keeps skilled people in the economy, so firms can find the workers they need.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.4] Government revenue can pay for investment in infrastructure (roads, rail, utilities) and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.b] Because so many multinationals are based in Ireland, their profits can distort the national figures. This makes GDP a poor guide to how well Irish people are doing.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.0] In 2015 Irish GDP grew by about 26% after multinationals moved intellectual property assets (such as patents and software rights) to Ireland; the effect was nicknamed 'leprechaun economics'.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.1] Because GDP is inflated by these profits, the CSO introduced modified gross national income (GNI*) in 2017 to strip out their effects.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.b] Farming is Ireland's main primary activity, and its pattern follows the climate. Mild, wet weather suits grass, so animals matter more than crops.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.0] The Census of Agriculture 2020 counted about 135,000 farms, averaging about 33 hectares.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.1] Grass grows for most of the year because winters are mild and rain is reliable, so grass-based livestock farming dominates.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.2] Pastoral farming is livestock farming: beef and dairy cattle, sheep and pigs. The national cattle herd is about 7 million (2020s), which is why beef and dairy cattle are central to farming.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.3] Arable (tillage) farming grows crops: barley is the largest tillage crop, with wheat, oats and potatoes also grown.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.4] Tillage is concentrated in the south and east, in Counties Wexford, Carlow, Kildare, Meath and Cork.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.b] Livestock farming earns money abroad, but a large herd also damages the environment. Exams ask for a benefit and a problem.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.0] EU milk quotas (limits on how much milk each country could produce) ended on 1 April 2015, and dairy cow numbers and milk output rose strongly afterwards.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.1] About 90% of Irish beef and dairy output is exported (Bord Bia, 2020s).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.2] A large cattle herd brings exports, food-processing jobs and incomes to rural areas.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.3] Agriculture is the largest single source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions (gases that warm the atmosphere), about 38% (EPA, 2022 data), with methane from livestock a main part of this.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.b] Ireland's geography gives it good conditions for fishing. Several harbours are officially designated as fishing centres.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.0] Ireland has a long, indented coastline with sheltered harbours, so boats can land fish safely, and a wide continental shelf (shallow seabed around the coast where fish feed).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.1] Plankton-rich waters and the North Atlantic Drift (a warm ocean current) support productive stocks, especially pelagic (surface-swimming) fish such as mackerel.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.4] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM) is the Irish sea fisheries board, so it is the State body for the industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.b] Irish fishing is limited by EU rules, but it still matters to the coastal places that depend on it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.0] The EU Common Fisheries Policy (1983) sets fishing quotas (limits on the catch) for member states, so Irish boats cannot catch as much as they wish.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.1] The 2020 EU–UK Brexit trade agreement reduced Irish quotas, which means Irish fishers may catch less.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.2] Mackerel is the most valuable species landed in Ireland, which is why pelagic waters matter so much.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.3] Fishing earns export income, provides jobs in remote coastal communities and supports fish processing.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.b] Ireland has little forest, which limits how much wood can be processed. This also explains why the wood industry employs few people.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.0] Enterprise Ireland, set up in 1998, supports Irish-owned firms, so they can grow as well as the foreign ones.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.2] Ryanair, an Irish-founded airline, is a tertiary firm because it provides a transport service.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.3] Ireland has a dual economy: a highly productive foreign-owned sector sits beside a smaller, lower-productivity Irish-owned sector.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.b] Ireland's industrial success rests on a few foreign firms and favourable conditions. Each challenge below puts one of those at risk.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.0] Dependence on a few large US multinationals means that a single firm's decisions can change output and jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.1] The 15% global minimum tax for very large groups reduces the advantage of Ireland's low rate. There is more on this in the block on the Single Market, euro and corporation tax.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.4] Brexit: the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020 and the transition ended on 31 December 2020, changing trade, supply chains and access to the UK market.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.b] A footloose industry is not tied to a location by raw materials or power, so it can locate almost anywhere. This freedom is why governments compete to attract it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.0] Footloose firms choose places with skilled labour, good communications, quality of life and government incentives, because none of these is fixed by nature.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.1] Services such as software, internet and data processing are the most footloose, since their product is information that needs no bulky raw materials.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.b] The Dublin region is Ireland's main example of an area dominated by services and footloose industry. Its best-known cluster is Silicon Docks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.0] Silicon Docks is the Grand Canal Dock area, south of the River Liffey, where digital firms cluster.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.1] Microsoft has been in Ireland since 1985; Google has had its European headquarters in Dublin since 2003, at Barrow Street on Grand Canal Dock.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.2] Meta (Facebook), LinkedIn and Salesforce also have large Dublin offices.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.0] Skilled graduates come from Trinity College Dublin, UCD, DCU, TU Dublin and Maynooth University.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.1] Firms recruit staff from across the EU for offices serving Europe, so language and skills are not limited to Ireland.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.3] A low tax rate and government incentives attract firms, because they cut costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.4] Agglomeration: firms gain from being close to each other, sharing skills and suppliers, so one firm attracts others.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.1] Demand for offices and housing has pushed rents up, because supply cannot keep pace.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.0] Data centres used about a fifth (about 21%) of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO), and most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.1] A cool climate reduces cooling costs, so running the machines is cheaper; fibre links connect them to global networks; firms cluster near each other; and tax is favourable.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.50.b] Industries do not last forever. Industrial decline is a downward trend, and it leaves visible signs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.50.0] Industrial decline is the long-term fall in the number of firms and jobs in a traditional industry or region.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.b] Irish industry declined for several reasons. Each reason is illustrated by the dated examples that follow.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.b] Cork lost several large employers in the 1980s, during a recession.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.0] The Dunlop tyre factory in Cork closed in 1983.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.1] Ford's Marina plant in Cork closed in 1984; Ford's Cork company was founded in 1917.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.2] Verolme Cork Dockyard at Rushbrooke closed in 1984.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.b] Further closures followed in the 2000s, each with a different cause. Dates matter here.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.0] Irish Steel (later Ispat) closed on Haulbowline Island, Cork Harbour, in 2001; the site needed a costly clean-up of contaminated waste.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.1] Irish Sugar (Greencore) closed its Carlow beet factory in 2005 and its Mallow factory in 2006, ending Irish sugar production after EU sugar-regime reform.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.2] Waterford Crystal's Kilbarry factory closed in January 2009 when the company went into receivership (when an outside manager takes control of a company that cannot pay its debts).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.3] Dell opened at Raheen, Limerick, in 1990; in January 2009 it announced that manufacturing would move to Łódź, Poland, with the loss of about 1,900 jobs, while sales and service stayed in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.b] Energy industries also declined as Ireland moved away from fossil fuels (fuels such as coal and peat, formed from ancient plants).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.0] Moneypoint, Co. Clare, a coal-fired power station built in the 1980s, was Ireland's last coal-burning power station.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.1] Coal burning at Moneypoint ended in 2025, so Ireland no longer burns coal for power, part of the move away from fossil fuels that also closed the peat stations.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.b] When industries close, the damage spreads from the workers to the whole town.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.0] Unemployment reached about 17% in the mid-1980s.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.1] Heavy emigration in the 1980s drew skilled people away from the affected towns, because there were no jobs for them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.2] Derelict docklands and empty factories were left in city and town centres.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.3] Local towns lost skills and spending as jobs disappeared.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.b] Governments and agencies tried to replace lost jobs and reuse empty sites.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.0] Replacement investment: the IDA and Enterprise Ireland sought new firms to replace lost jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.1] Regeneration (rebuilding and renewing run-down areas) of the Dublin Docklands and the IFSC. There is more on this in the IFSC blocks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.2] Regeneration of Cork Docklands, through a city plan to redevelop the old port quays.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.3] Heritage reuse: a smaller House of Waterford Crystal factory and visitor centre opened in Waterford city centre (the Mall) in 2010.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.4] Retraining for displaced workers, so they can move into new jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.5] The EU Just Transition Fund supports the Midlands peat region, because peat jobs have ended.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.57.b] Ireland is not alone in losing traditional industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.57.0] There is more on coalfield and steel regions of decline elsewhere in Europe in the Socio-economic regions notes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.b] Economic activity is unevenly spread across Ireland. Some regions have far more jobs and income than others.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.0] The Greater Dublin Area had about 2.1 million people in Census 2022, about 40% of the State's population, and holds most multinational and service jobs, along with Cork, Galway and Limerick.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.1] The Border, Midlands and West have more small farms, fewer FDI jobs and lower incomes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.2] The Border, Midland and Western (BMW) region was designated for EU funding in 1999 (Objective 1 for 2000–2006); the rest of the State was the Southern and Eastern region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.3] The National Planning Framework (Project Ireland 2040, 2018) aims for Galway, Cork, Limerick and Waterford to each grow by at least 50% by 2040, to balance growth away from Dublin.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s5: 5. Nodal, city and urban regions
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.b] A nodal region is a city together with the surrounding area linked to it by flows of people, goods, services and information. Unlike the other regions in this chapter, it is defined by connections, not by uniform features.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.0] A node is a central point where routes meet by road, rail, sea or air, usually a town or city. Think of it as the hub that the surrounding area is connected to.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.1] A nodal region is also called a functional, city or urban region. It is functional because it is defined by what the links to the centre do, not by everything in it being the same.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.2] A hinterland is the area around a town or city that it serves and depends on for jobs, shopping, services and markets. It is also called its sphere of influence or urban field.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.3] The urban area is the continuously built-up part of the city, with houses and streets running on without a break. The hinterland extends far beyond it, into countryside and smaller towns.
[geo-region-concept-s5.0.4] The commuter belt is the part of the hinterland from which people travel daily to work in the city (commuting means making that regular journey).
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.b] A city and its hinterland depend on each other. Four kinds of flow tie them together: jobs, services, goods and information.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.0] Jobs: commuters travel in from the hinterland every day, because the city has the workplaces and the hinterland has many of the workers.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.1] Services: the city provides shops, hospitals, third-level colleges (universities and similar colleges), entertainment and offices that smaller places cannot support, and people from the hinterland travel in to use them.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.2] Goods: the hinterland supplies food, water, raw materials and labour (workers); the city supplies manufactured goods and services in return.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.3] A port's hinterland is the area whose trade passes through it, so goods from that area are exported, and goods for it imported, through that port.
[geo-region-concept-s5.1.4] Information: local newspapers, radio and TV stations based in the city serve the hinterland around it.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.b] A hinterland has no marked border, so geographers work out where it ends by tracing where a city's services and links reach. Each method below gives a picture of the area.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.0] Commuting data from the census (the official count of the population, which records where people travel to work) show how far workers come from.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.1] The catchment area of a hospital, college or secondary school is the area from which it draws its patients or students.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.2] The circulation area of a local newspaper (where it is sold) or the reach of a local radio station (where it can be heard) shows where the city's information flows.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.3] Bus and rail routes and timetables show which places are linked to the city and how often.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.4] The delivery areas of businesses, and the home addresses of shoppers or of members of a sports club, show who uses the city's shops and clubs and where they live.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.5] The edge of a hinterland is not a sharp line: neighbouring hinterlands overlap, and different services give different boundaries, since a hospital and a newspaper do not reach the same distance.
[geo-region-concept-s5.2.6] Larger cities have larger hinterlands. A capital city's hinterland for some services is the whole country.
[geo-region-concept-s5.3.b] Hinterlands are not fixed. Changes in transport, work and shopping can stretch them or squeeze them.
[geo-region-concept-s5.3.0] Motorways, car ownership, faster rail and remote working (working from home, so fewer daily trips) extend commuter belts outwards, because people can live further from the city and still reach it.
[geo-region-concept-s5.3.1] New shopping centres or services in smaller towns can shrink a city's hinterland, because people no longer need to travel to the city to use them.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.b] A primate city is the largest city in a country, far larger than any other. Dublin is Ireland's primate city and capital: County Dublin has about 1.45 million people out of a State total of about 5.1 million (Census 2022).
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.0] It is the focus of the national motorway network: radial routes (roads spreading out from the centre like spokes) meet at the M50 ring road, which circles the city. The main rail lines also meet there.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.1] Dublin Port and Dublin Airport, Ireland's busiest airport, make the city the main national gateway (entry and exit point) for trade and travel.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.2] The commuter belt extends into Meath, Kildare, Wicklow and Louth, with fast-growing commuter towns (towns where many residents work in the city): Navan (Co. Meath), Naas and Newbridge (Co. Kildare), Bray (Co. Wicklow) and Drogheda (Co. Louth).
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.3] For government, national media, specialist hospitals and the main airport, Dublin's hinterland is the whole country, because these functions exist in Dublin and nowhere else on the same scale.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.4] Problems of its urban region: urban sprawl (the built-up area spreading outwards into the countryside), long commutes and congestion, and high house prices.
[geo-region-concept-s5.4.5] There is more on the growth of city regions and Dublin's council structures in the European Regions chapter, and on land use and planning in the Urban Geography chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.b] Outside Dublin, Ireland's other cities act as smaller nodes, each serving its own hinterland.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.0] Cork: Ireland's second city, at the head of Cork Harbour on the River Lee.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.1] Cork Harbour has a port and a pharmaceutical industry (making medicines), with plants at Ringaskiddy. Cork's hinterland covers much of Munster, with commuter towns Carrigaline, Midleton and Cobh.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.2] Galway: the main city of the West, serving a large rural hinterland that includes Connemara.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.3] Galway is a centre of the medical devices industry (making equipment used in healthcare) and of higher education (study after secondary school), home to the University of Galway.
[geo-region-concept-s5.5.4] Limerick and Waterford are regional cities serving the Mid-West and the South-East.
[geo-region-concept-s5.6.b] Dublin is so much larger than every other Irish city that national planning tries to even things out.
[geo-region-concept-s5.6.0] The National Planning Framework, part of Project Ireland 2040 and published in 2018, aims to direct more growth to Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford to counterbalance Dublin (to offset its dominance by building up the other cities).
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.b] Cities across Europe are nodes with hinterlands of different sizes. Some reach across a whole country, and one crosses national borders.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.0] Paris is the primate city of France, at the centre of the Paris Basin. Radial motorways and TGV (high-speed train) lines meet there, so its hinterland reaches across France.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.1] The first TGV line, Paris to Lyon, opened in 1981. Charles de Gaulle Airport serves the city, and its region, Ile-de-France, has about 12 million people.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.2] London is the capital of the United Kingdom and a global financial centre, through the City of London. Greater London has about 9 million people (Census 2021), and its commuter belt covers much of south-east England.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.3] Brussels is the capital of Belgium and seat of the European Commission and the Council of the EU, so its influence extends across the EU. NATO headquarters is also there.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.4] The European Parliament meets both in Brussels and in Strasbourg, its official seat, so not all EU institutions are in Brussels.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.5] Rotterdam is Europe's largest port, at the mouth of the Rhine (Europoort). Its hinterland is the whole Rhine basin, linked by river barges, rail and pipelines, so it crosses national borders.
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.6] Milan is the main city of Lombardy, the core of northern Italy, and Italy's financial centre, home to the Borsa Italiana stock exchange (where company shares are traded).
[geo-region-concept-s5.7.7] Other urban regions named in exam matching include Munich (capital of Bavaria), Berlin (capital of Germany) and Rome (capital of Italy).
[geo-region-concept-s5.8.b] Most city regions have one dominant centre, but the Netherlands has a region built around several cities of similar importance.
[geo-region-concept-s5.8.0] The Randstad in the Netherlands is a ring of cities (Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague and Utrecht) forming one large urban region around a rural area called the Green Heart (Groene Hart).
[geo-region-concept-s5.8.1] It is polycentric, meaning it has many centres, in contrast to the single-centre city regions of Paris and Dublin.
