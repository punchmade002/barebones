TARGET geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4 (part 1 of 2): Atmospheric and oceanic circulation
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.0.b] Air moves because pressure differences push it. The Earth's rotation then bends its path, so winds away from the equator do not blow straight from high to low pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.0.0] Pressure gradient force: air moves from high to low pressure. The closer the isobars (lines on a weather map joining places of equal pressure), the steeper the gradient and the stronger the force and wind.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.0.1] Pressure differences arise from unequal heating (thermal causes) and from air converging or diverging aloft (dynamic causes), which means air piles up or is drawn away high in the atmosphere.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.0.2] Coriolis force: because the Earth rotates, moving air is deflected to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.0.3] The Coriolis effect is zero at the equator and strongest at the poles, and it is greater for faster-moving air. It changes the direction of the wind, not its speed, because it acts sideways on moving air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.0.4] Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, a French scientist, described the effect mathematically in 1835.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.0.5] Geostrophic wind: above about 1 km, where there is little friction, the pressure gradient force and the Coriolis force balance, so the wind blows parallel to the isobars.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.0.6] Diagram: a globe with arrows showing the Coriolis deflection to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.1.b] Near the ground, friction changes the balance of forces. Together with the Coriolis force it makes surface air spiral round pressure systems instead of blowing straight into or out of them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.1.0] Friction: near the surface, friction with the ground slows the wind and weakens the Coriolis effect, so surface winds cross the isobars at an angle, blowing into lows and out of highs.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.1.1] Friction is greater over land (rough) than over sea (smooth), so surface winds are stronger over the sea and on coasts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.1.2] Result: in the northern hemisphere air spirals anticlockwise into a depression (a low-pressure system) and clockwise out of an anticyclone (a high-pressure system). The directions reverse in the southern hemisphere.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.1.3] Buys Ballot's law, from Weather and Climate — Weather Maps and Satellite Imagery, links the direction of the wind to the position of low pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.2.b] Ocean currents are driven mainly by wind. The Coriolis effect, the continents and density then shape them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.2.0] Wind drag: prevailing winds (the winds that blow most often from one direction) blowing over the sea surface drive the surface currents. The trade winds push the equatorial currents westward, and the westerlies push the mid-latitude currents eastward.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.2.1] The surface current is deflected about 45° from the wind direction, because the Coriolis effect acts on moving water as it does on air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.2.2] The net movement of the whole wind-driven layer is about 90° to the right of the wind in the northern hemisphere. This is Ekman transport.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.2.3] Vagn Walfrid Ekman, a Swedish oceanographer, described the Ekman spiral and Ekman transport in the early 20th century.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.2.4] Density differences: colder and saltier water is denser and sinks, while warmer and fresher water rises or spreads at the surface. This drives the deep thermohaline circulation (the slow ocean circulation driven by temperature and salt differences).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.2.5] Continents and the shape of the ocean floor: land masses block and deflect currents north or south, turning them into closed gyres (circular current systems).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.2.6] Gravity and tides also move water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.3.b] Air rises near the equator and sinks at around 30° and at the poles. This creates a regular pattern of pressure belts in each hemisphere, and each belt has its own weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.3.0] Equatorial low, also called the ITCZ (the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, where the trade winds meet) or doldrums: calm, rising air and heavy rain, because strong heating makes moist air rise.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.3.1] Subtropical highs at about 30° north and south, also called the horse latitudes: sinking, dry air, which is why deserts lie in these belts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.3.2] Subpolar lows at about 60° north and south: the polar front (where cold polar air meets warm tropical air), where depressions form.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.3.3] Polar highs: cold, sinking air, because cold air is dense.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.3.4] Diagram: a tri-cellular model (a model with three circulation cells in each hemisphere) showing the pressure belts, cells and surface winds for both hemispheres.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.4.b] Surface air flows from the high-pressure belts towards the low-pressure belts. The Coriolis force bends each flow, which gives the three great wind systems.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.4.0] North-east and south-east trade winds blow from the subtropical highs towards the ITCZ.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.4.1] Westerlies blow from the subtropical highs towards the subpolar lows: south-westerly in the northern hemisphere and north-westerly in the southern hemisphere.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.4.2] Polar easterlies blow from the polar highs towards the subpolar lows, because air always flows from high to low pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.4.3] Each of these winds is deflected by the Coriolis force, which is why none blows due north or south.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.5.b] The pressure belts and winds belong to three circulation cells (closed loops of rising and sinking air) in each hemisphere.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.5.0] Hadley cell: a thermal cell (driven by heating) powered by equatorial heating. George Hadley described it in 1735.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.5.1] Ferrel cell: an indirect cell, which means it is driven by the Hadley and polar cells on either side rather than by heating of its own. William Ferrel, an American meteorologist of the 19th century, is associated with it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.5.2] Polar cell: a thermal cell in which cold, dense air sinks at the poles.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.6.b] The pressure belts follow the overhead sun, so the whole system shifts with the seasons. This explains why some climates have a dry season and a wet season.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.6.0] The pressure belts and the ITCZ move north in the northern summer and south in the northern winter.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.6.1] The shift is greater over continents than over oceans, because land heats and cools faster than sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.6.2] This shift produces the dry summer of the Mediterranean, which lies under the subtropical high in summer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.6.3] It also brings the summer rain of the monsoon (a seasonal wind reversal that brings heavy rain) and the single wet season of the Sahel (the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara). There is more on this in A Continental / Subcontinental Region.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.7.b] High above the surface, narrow rivers of fast air steer weather systems. Their path decides what kind of weather Ireland gets.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.7.0] Jet streams are narrow, fast bands of westerly wind in the upper troposphere (the lowest layer of the atmosphere, where weather happens), near the tropopause (its upper limit), at about 9–12 km up.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.7.1] They form where temperature contrasts are sharpest, because big contrasts mean steep pressure gradients, and often blow at over 150 km/h. They are faster in winter.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.7.2] The polar front jet stream meanders in large waves, called Rossby waves, and steers depressions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.7.3] A strong, straight jet over the Atlantic brings stormy winters to Ireland. Its position decides whether Ireland gets a run of wet, windy depressions or settled weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.7.4] A slow, looping jet can block weather systems in place for weeks, so one kind of weather persists.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.7.5] The subtropical jet also has a role in the Indian monsoon. There is more on this in A Continental / Subcontinental Region.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.8.b] The North Atlantic pressure systems that dominate Ireland's weather are linked by a seesaw of pressure. When one is strong the other changes with it, and this decides the winter.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.8.0] The Icelandic Low and the Azores High are semi-permanent pressure systems (always present, though they shift in strength) that dominate Ireland's weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.8.1] The North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) is the seesaw of pressure between these two systems. It controls the strength of the westerlies.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.8.2] In the positive phase, with a large pressure difference, the westerlies are strong and Irish winters are mild, wet and stormy.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.8.3] In the negative phase, with a small pressure difference, the westerlies are weaker, blocking highs (highs that stay in place and block the westerly flow) are more common, and Irish winters are colder and drier.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.9.b] An air mass is a large body of air that takes on the temperature and moisture of the region it comes from. Ireland receives five, and each brings its own weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.9.0] Tropical maritime: comes from the Atlantic near the Azores. It is warm, moist and mild, with cloud and drizzle.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.9.1] Polar maritime: comes from the North Atlantic near Greenland and Iceland. It is cool, moist and showery, because it crosses the sea, and is Ireland's most frequent air mass.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.9.2] Arctic maritime: comes from the Arctic. It is cold, with snow showers in the north and west in winter.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.9.3] Polar continental: comes from Russia and Scandinavia in winter. It is very cold and dry, and it brings snow to east coasts after crossing the Irish Sea. The 2018 case is described under Blocking anticyclones.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.9.4] Tropical continental: comes from North Africa and southern Europe in summer. It is hot, dry and hazy because it has crossed warm land, and it brings heatwaves.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.10.b] Surface currents do not run in straight lines across an ocean. Wind, the Coriolis effect and the continents bend them into great circles.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.10.0] Gyres are large, circular systems of surface currents, driven by wind, the Coriolis effect and the continents.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.10.1] Gyres turn clockwise in northern hemisphere oceans and anticlockwise in southern hemisphere oceans, because the Coriolis effect deflects water right in the north and left in the south.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.10.2] The five main gyres are the North Atlantic, South Atlantic, North Pacific, South Pacific and Indian Ocean gyres.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.10.3] Diagram: a world map of the main surface currents, with warm currents in red, cold currents in blue, and the five gyres marked.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.11.b] The North Atlantic gyre carries warm water to Ireland and returns cold water to the tropics. It is the circulation pattern the syllabus names as its setting.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.11.0] The North Equatorial Current flows west with the trade winds.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.11.1] The current turns north as the Gulf Stream along the North American coast, because the land blocks its westward path.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.11.2] The Gulf Stream starts in the Gulf of Mexico and the Straits of Florida, and it carries more water than all the world's rivers combined.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.11.3] The westerlies carry the Gulf Stream north-east across the Atlantic as the North Atlantic Drift, towards Ireland, Britain and Norway.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.11.4] Part of the current turns south as the cold Canary Current along north-west Africa and rejoins the North Equatorial Current.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.11.5] The calm Sargasso Sea sits in the centre of the gyre, inside the circling currents. It is named after its floating sargassum seaweed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.11.6] The cold Labrador Current flows south from the Arctic between Greenland and Canada.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.12.b] Where a current goes decides the climate of the coast it passes. Warm and cold currents in the North Atlantic give very different results.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.12.0] The North Atlantic Drift gives Ireland mild winters. It also keeps ports ice-free, and the Norwegian coast stays ice-free at Hammerfest and Murmansk, while Labrador's coast is frozen.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.12.1] Warm sea adds moisture and energy to Atlantic depressions (low-pressure systems that bring rain and wind).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.12.2] Fog forms where the Gulf Stream meets the Labrador Current, and advection fog (fog formed when warm, moist air moves over a cold surface) forms on the Grand Banks off Newfoundland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.12.3] Icebergs are carried south in the Labrador Current. The Titanic sank on 15 April 1912 after hitting an iceberg south of Newfoundland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.12.4] The Saharan coast of north-west Africa is arid, because it lies beside the cold Canary Current.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.13.b] The same pattern repeats round the world. Warm currents flow along the western edges of oceans and cold currents along the eastern edges.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.13.0] Warm western-boundary currents carry warm water away from the tropics along the western sides of ocean basins, such as the Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian and Agulhas currents.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.13.1] Cold eastern-boundary currents bring cold water towards the equator along the eastern sides of ocean basins, such as the California, Humboldt and Benguela currents.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.13.2] The Antarctic Circumpolar Current flows west to east round Antarctica. It is the largest ocean current by volume, because no land blocks it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.13.3] The strong westerlies of the Southern Ocean (the ocean around Antarctica) are called the Roaring Forties and the Furious Fifties.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.14.b] Where winds move surface water away from a coast, deep water must rise to fill the gap. This brings food and cold to the surface.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.14.0] Winds push surface water away from a coast (Ekman transport), and cold, nutrient-rich deep water rises to replace it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.14.1] Plankton (tiny floating organisms) thrive in the nutrient-rich water, supporting rich fisheries off Peru, south-west Africa, California and north-west Africa.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.14.2] The Peruvian anchoveta fishery, fed by upwelling off Peru in the Humboldt Current, is the largest single-species fishery in the world.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.14.3] The cold upwelled water chills the air above it, which gives the coast fog and aridity.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.15.b] The thermohaline circulation (thermo means temperature, haline means salt) is the global ocean conveyor belt. It is driven by differences in temperature and salinity, and it moves water through the deep ocean.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.15.0] Surface water is cooled and made saltier by evaporation and sea-ice formation in the Greenland, Norwegian and Labrador Seas and around Antarctica. It becomes dense and sinks to the ocean floor.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.15.1] The deep water flows slowly south through the Atlantic, round Antarctica and into the Indian and Pacific Oceans.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.15.2] There the water gradually rises and returns to the surface as warm currents, so the loop is complete.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.15.3] One circuit of the conveyor belt takes about 1,000 years, because the deep water moves so slowly.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.15.4] The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is the Atlantic part of this system. It pulls the Gulf Stream's warm water north, because sinking water in the north draws surface water towards it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.15.5] Diagram: a world map showing the conveyor belt of deep and surface currents.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.16.b] The AMOC depends on cold, salty water sinking in the North Atlantic. Anything that changes that water could slow the whole system.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.16.0] Melting Greenland ice and more rain make North Atlantic water fresher and less dense. It may then sink less, and the AMOC may weaken.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.16.1] The IPCC (AR6, 2021) judged that the AMOC is very likely to weaken over the 21st century, because of the fresher water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.16.2] A weaker AMOC could cool north-west Europe relative to the rest of the world and change rainfall patterns.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.17.b] A depression is a low-pressure system in the mid-latitudes (the zones between the tropics and the poles). It is the main source of Ireland's rain and wind.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.17.0] A depression is roughly 1,000–3,000 km across, with closed isobars, winds blowing anticlockwise inwards in the northern hemisphere, and warm and cold fronts (the boundaries between warm and cold air).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.17.1] Depressions move from west to east, steered by the jet stream, so they reach Ireland first from the Atlantic, and bring most of Ireland's rain and wind.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.17.2] Polar front theory, which explains how depressions form along the polar front, was developed by the Bergen School of Norwegian meteorologists, led by Vilhelm Bjerknes and his son Jacob, around the end of the First World War.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.17.3] Diagram: a plan view of a mature depression with isobars, fronts and the warm sector.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.18.b] A depression grows in stages along the polar front, where two air masses meet and move in opposite directions. Learn the stages in order.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.18.0] 1. At the polar front, cold polar maritime air from the north meets warm, moist tropical maritime air from the south-west, and the two move in opposite directions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.18.1] 2. A small wave or kink develops on the front, which starts the depression, often where the jet stream above is diverging and removing air aloft.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.18.2] 3. Warm air pushes into the cold air and begins to rise over it. Surface pressure falls because air is rising, so air is drawn in, and it turns anticlockwise under the Coriolis force.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.18.3] 4. A warm front forms ahead of the warm air and a cold front forms behind it, with the warm sector between them. The system deepens (pressure falls) as more warm air rises and latent heat (heat released when water vapour condenses) is released.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.18.4] 5. The cold front moves faster than the warm front and catches up with it, lifting the warm sector off the ground to form an occluded front. This is occlusion.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.18.5] 6. Once the warm air is cut off from the surface, the depression fills as pressure rises, and it dies. This usually takes a few days to about a week, because the fronts have lost their supply of warm air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.18.6] Diagram: a cross-section along a line through both fronts, showing cloud types and rain belts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.19.b] As a depression moves from west to east, the front, the warm sector and the cold front pass over a place one after another. Each brings its own cloud, rain, wind and pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.19.0] Approach: pressure starts to fall. High cirrus (wispy ice cloud) is followed by cirrostratus (a thin high sheet), which gives a halo round the sun. The wind backs (changes anticlockwise), for example from westerly to southerly or south-easterly, and strengthens.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.19.1] Warm front: cloud thickens and lowers, first to altostratus (a mid-level grey sheet) and then to nimbostratus (thick rain cloud). A long period of steady, moderate rain follows, sometimes for several hours. Pressure falls steadily and temperature rises slightly.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.19.2] Warm sector: rain eases to drizzle or stops. Low stratus (a low grey layer of cloud) and mist form on hills. The air is mild and humid, the wind veers (changes clockwise) to south-westerly, and pressure is steady.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.19.3] Cold front: cumulonimbus clouds (towering storm clouds) form and bring short, heavy showers, sometimes with hail, thunder and gusty squalls. Temperature falls sharply, the wind veers to westerly or north-westerly, and pressure begins to rise.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.19.4] Behind the cold front: the air is clearer, colder and brighter, with scattered cumulus (heaped clouds) and showers and good visibility. Pressure rises.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.19.5] Occluded front: the cloud and rain of both fronts combine, often with lighter rain as the system ages.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.20.b] Because Ireland lies in the path of the depressions that cross the Atlantic, their passing shapes the whole climate.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.20.0] Ireland's weather is changeable, with rain in every month, because depressions cross the country regularly.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.20.1] The west is wetter than the east, because depressions arrive there first.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.20.2] Strong winds and storms are most common in autumn and winter, when the temperature contrast between warm and cold air and the jet stream are strongest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.20.3] Coastal storm surges, which flood low coasts (a rise in sea level driven by wind and low pressure) accompany the strongest depressions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.20.4] Flooding happens when depressions follow each other in quick succession, because the ground has no time to drain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.20.5] Storms named by Met Éireann include ex-hurricane Ophelia (16 October 2017) and Storm Éowyn (24 January 2025, when Red wind warnings were issued).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.b] An anticyclone is the opposite of a depression: a large area of sinking air and high pressure. Its sinking air explains its settled weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.0] An anticyclone is an area of high pressure with widely spaced, closed isobars. It has light winds blowing clockwise outwards in the northern hemisphere, and no fronts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.1] Anticyclones are large and slow-moving, so they can last for days or weeks.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.2] Air converging high in the atmosphere sinks, a process called subsidence. As it sinks it is compressed and warms adiabatically (by compression alone, without taking in heat), so its relative humidity falls and clouds evaporate.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.3] Dynamic (warm) highs form from sinking air in the general circulation. The Azores High is an example, and it often extends a ridge (a long narrow extension of high pressure) over Ireland in summer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.4] Thermal (cold) highs form when the ground cools intensely in winter. The Siberian High, a cold anticyclone over Central Asia, is an example.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.21.5] A temperature inversion (a layer where temperature rises with height instead of falling) often forms in the sinking air, trapping moisture and pollution beneath it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.b] Sinking, drying air means few clouds. In summer this gives hot, bright weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.0] Clear skies, strong sunshine, high daytime temperatures and light winds.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.1] Cool nights with dew and sometimes morning mist, because clear skies let heat escape.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.2] Occasional thunderstorms, triggered by the heat, at the edges of the high.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.3] Sea breezes on coasts by day, because the land heats faster than the sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.22.4] Prolonged summer highs cause heatwaves and drought, because there is little cloud or rain for days.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.23.b] In winter the same clear skies let the ground lose heat, so an anticyclone can bring cold rather than warmth.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.23.0] Clear nights let the ground lose heat rapidly. This causes hard frost and freezing fog in valleys (radiation fog, fog formed when the ground cools the air above it), which may persist all day.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.23.1] Cold, crisp, sunny days are common, because the sky is clear.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.23.2] Anticyclonic gloom: a layer of stratocumulus (low, lumpy layered cloud) is trapped under the inversion, keeping the sky grey and dull.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.23.3] Smog (smoke and fog) forms in towns where pollution is trapped beneath the inversion. There is more on this in Economic Activity and Environmental Impact.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.24.b] A high that stays put can steer weather away from a region for weeks. The 2018 cold spell in Ireland is the example.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.24.0] A high that stays in place, often over Scandinavia or west of Ireland, blocks the westerly flow and diverts depressions north or south.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.24.1] The result is a long spell of dry weather, with a heatwave in summer or a cold spell in winter, while other regions get prolonged rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.24.2] A block to the east can draw very cold polar continental air to Ireland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.24.3] In late February and early March 2018, a block of high pressure over Scandinavia drew bitterly cold polar continental air from Russia over Ireland. This was the Beast from the East.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.24.4] Storm Emma then arrived from the south on 1–2 March 2018, bringing heavy snow.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.24.5] Met Éireann issued a Red snow warning for the whole country. It was the first nationwide Red warning.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.b] The two systems are opposites in almost every respect. Set them side by side, point for point.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.0] Pressure: a depression has low pressure at its centre and an anticyclone has high pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.1] Isobars: closely spaced in a depression, widely spaced in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.2] Wind: strong in a depression and light in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.3] Air movement: air rises in a depression and sinks in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.4] Cloud and rain: cloud and rain in a depression, clear skies in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.5] Typical weather: changeable, windy and wet in a depression, and settled, dry and calm in an anticyclone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.25.6] Movement: depressions travel from west to east, while anticyclones are slow-moving.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.b] Land and sea heat up at different rates. By day this difference sets up a small circulation that blows from the sea onto the land.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.0] Land heats faster than the sea under sunshine. Air over the land warms, expands and rises, creating lower pressure over the land, while the cooler sea has relatively higher pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.1] Cool air flows onshore from the sea to the land, because air moves towards the lower pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.2] The air that rose returns seaward at height and sinks over the sea, completing a circulation cell.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.26.3] Sea breezes are strongest on hot, calm, sunny summer afternoons, often under an anticyclone, and in the tropics they may blow most days.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.b] After dark the heating difference reverses, so the breeze reverses with it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.0] After sunset, land loses heat faster than the sea. Air over the land cools and becomes denser, creating higher pressure over the land and lower pressure over the warmer sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.1] Air flows offshore, from the land to the sea, because air moves towards the lower pressure over the sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.2] The land breeze is weaker than the sea breeze, because the night-time temperature difference is smaller.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.27.3] Diagram: two cross-sections, one for day and one for night, with arrows showing the direction of the air and the relative pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.b] These local winds change the temperature, humidity and cloud along a coast, and people have used them for centuries.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.0] Sea breezes lower coastal temperatures in the afternoon and raise humidity.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.1] A sea-breeze front (the line where the cool sea air meets the warmer land air) can trigger a line of cumulus or showers inland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.2] Sea breezes can carry sea fog onshore and trap urban pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.3] Land breezes help clear coastal fog, because they blow the fog out to sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.4] Sailing fishermen traditionally went out at dawn with the land breeze and came back in the afternoon with the sea breeze.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.28.5] Lake breezes form in the same way on large lakes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.29.b] Slopes in the mountains heat and cool faster than the air above the valley. By day this makes air flow up the slopes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.29.0] Sunshine heats the valley sides. Air in contact with them warms and rises upslope.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.29.1] Cumulus may form over the peaks in the afternoon, because the rising air cools and its moisture condenses.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.b] At night the slopes cool and the process runs in reverse. Cold air drains downhill and collects in the valley.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.0] Slopes lose heat by radiation. Air in contact with them cools, becomes denser and drains downslope under gravity, collecting on the valley floor.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.1] Cold air pooling on the valley floor creates frost hollows and temperature inversions, so valley floors are colder than the slopes above.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.2] Orchards and vineyards are planted on slopes above the frost line to avoid frost hollows.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.3] Radiation fog forms in the valleys, because the cold pooled air chills moisture to saturation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.4] In Antarctica and Greenland, where the ice sheets are very cold, katabatic winds flowing off the ice sheets can reach gale force.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.30.5] Diagram: a day and night cross-section of a valley showing the anabatic and katabatic flows.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.b] A föhn is a warm, dry wind on the lee side of a mountain range (the side sheltered from the prevailing wind). Its temperature change comes from the difference between saturated and dry cooling and warming.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.0] The name föhn comes from the Alps, the original area where the wind was described, in the German-speaking Alpine valleys.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.1] Air rising on the windward side cools at the saturated rate (the slower rate) after condensation begins, because condensation releases latent heat.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.2] Air descending on the lee side warms at the faster dry rate, so it arrives warmer and drier than it started.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.3] The föhn is warm because of this difference in rates, not because the air comes from a warm place.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.4] Effects: rapid snowmelt, avalanche and fire risk, and sudden temperature rises.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.31.5] The Chinook is a föhn on the eastern (lee) side of the Rocky Mountains in Canada and the USA. It is called the snow eater because it melts snow rapidly.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.32.b] Where high ground meets the Mediterranean coast, cold air can pour through gaps in the mountains. Two such winds have local names.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.32.0] The mistral is a cold, dry northerly wind funnelled down the Rhône valley in southern France to the Mediterranean.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.32.1] The bora is a cold, dry north-easterly wind descending to the Adriatic coast, in Croatia and at Trieste in Italy.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.32.2] Both winds pour from high ground towards the sea, usually when high pressure lies inland and low pressure lies over the sea, because air flows from high to low pressure.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.33.b] A thunderstorm is a violent storm of heavy rain, lightning and thunder, which grows in a tall cumulonimbus cloud. It needs three things to start.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.33.0] Thunderstorms need plentiful moisture, which fuels the cloud, and unstable air (air that keeps rising once it is lifted).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4.33.1] They also need a trigger to lift the air: strong surface heating (convection), a cold front, mountains, or converging air.

CANDIDATES geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1: 1. The atmosphere and oceans as linked systems
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.0.b] The atmosphere (the envelope of gases around the earth) and the oceans are not separate. They swap heat, water, gases and momentum (movement), so a change in one alters the other.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.0.0] Heat: the ocean warms or cools the air above it, depending on whether the sea surface is warmer or colder than the air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.0.1] Water: evaporation (liquid water turning to vapour) from the sea supplies the moisture for precipitation (rain, snow and so on) over both sea and land.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.0.2] Gases: the ocean absorbs and releases carbon dioxide and oxygen, so it takes part in the air's chemistry as well as its weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.0.3] Momentum: wind drives surface ocean currents, and the currents in turn shift the pressure and wind patterns above them, so the push goes both ways.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.0.4] El Niño shows the link at work: a change in sea surface temperature across the Pacific alters atmospheric circulation (the large-scale pattern of air movement) there. There is more on this in Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.1.b] The ocean's effect on climate depends on how water handles heat. Specific heat capacity is the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of a material, and water's is high.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.1.0] Water has a high specific heat capacity, so the ocean heats and cools far more slowly than land and stores very large amounts of heat.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.1.1] Because the ocean responds slowly, maritime climates (those near the sea) have small temperature ranges, the gap between the warmest and coldest readings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.1.2] The ocean has absorbed over 90% of the extra heat trapped by global warming, according to the IPCC (the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, the international body that assesses climate science). This shows how large its heat store is.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.2.b] Air is a mixture of gases. The permanent gases are those whose share stays the same everywhere, and these figures are percentages by volume of dry air (air with the water vapour left out).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.2.0] By volume, dry air is about 78% nitrogen and about 21% oxygen, so these two gases make up nearly all of it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.2.1] Argon makes up about 0.9% and carbon dioxide about 0.04%, so even the fourth gas is a tiny fraction.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.2.2] Traces of neon, helium, methane, ozone and other gases make up the rest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.3.b] Some parts of the air change from place to place and time to time, unlike the permanent gases. These variable components matter far more than their small amounts suggest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.3.0] Water vapour (water as an invisible gas) ranges from almost 0% of the air over deserts and the poles to about 4% in hot, humid tropical air, because warm air can hold more of it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.3.1] Aerosols (tiny solid or liquid particles floating in the air), including dust, sea salt, smoke, volcanic ash and pollen, act as condensation nuclei, the surfaces on which water vapour condenses into droplets, and they scatter sunlight.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.3.2] Ozone, a form of oxygen with three atoms per molecule, is concentrated in the stratosphere, the layer of the atmosphere above the weather layer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.4.b] Gases that make up only a tiny share of the air still control how much heat the earth keeps and how much harmful radiation reaches the ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.4.0] Water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide are greenhouse gases: they absorb long-wave radiation (heat given off by the warm earth), which keeps the lower atmosphere warmer. There is more on this in Solar energy and its redistribution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.4.1] Ozone absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation, the high-energy part of sunlight that damages living tissue.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.4.2] Atmospheric carbon dioxide was about 280 ppm (parts per million) before the Industrial Revolution and was over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s, because of human activity.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.4.3] Carbon dioxide has been measured continuously at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958. The record, known as the Keeling Curve, was begun by Charles David Keeling.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.5.b] The air has weight, and gravity (the pull of the earth) keeps it from drifting off into space. This explains why the air gets thinner as you go up.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.5.0] Gravity holds the atmosphere, so it is densest (most mass packed into a given space) at the surface and thins rapidly with height.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.5.1] Pressure (the weight of the air pressing down) therefore falls with altitude, because less air lies above; about half the mass of the atmosphere lies below about 5.5 km.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.6.b] The atmosphere has four layers, separated by three pauses (boundaries between layers). Each layer is defined by how its temperature changes with height.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.6.0] Diagram: draw a vertical profile with height in km on the vertical axis, label the four layers and the three pauses, and add the temperature line, which falls, rises, falls and rises again.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.7.b] The troposphere is the lowest layer and the one we live in. It is where weather happens.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.7.0] It extends from the surface to about 8 km over the poles and about 16-18 km over the equator, being thicker where warm air expands and convection (warm air rising) is strongest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.7.1] Temperature falls with height at an average of about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m (the environmental lapse rate), because the air is heated from below by the ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.7.2] It contains most of the atmosphere's mass and nearly all its water vapour, so nearly all weather and clouds occur here.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.7.3] Its top is the tropopause, where the temperature stops falling; jet streams (fast, narrow bands of high-altitude wind) blow near it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.8.b] Above the tropopause the temperature trend reverses, because of the ozone layer, the stratosphere's special feature.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.8.0] It extends from the tropopause to about 50 km.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.8.1] Temperature rises with height because ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation and the absorbed energy warms the air; the ozone layer is concentrated at about 15-35 km.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.8.2] It is very dry and stable, so few clouds form, but the anvils (flat, spread-out tops) of cumulonimbus clouds (tall storm clouds) spread out at its base.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.8.3] Long-haul jets cruise near the tropopause to avoid turbulence (rough, bumpy air).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.8.4] Its top is the stratopause.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.9.b] In the mesosphere, above the ozone layer's warming effect, temperature falls with height again.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.9.0] It extends from about 50 km to about 80-85 km.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.9.1] Temperature falls again with height, reaching about -90 °C at its top, the coldest point in the atmosphere.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.9.2] Most meteors (rock fragments from space) burn up here. Its top is the mesopause.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.10.b] The outer layers are so thin that they behave very differently from the air near the ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.10.0] Above about 85 km the air is very thin and its temperature rises sharply, because the few molecules absorb high-energy solar radiation; there are too few molecules for it to feel hot.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.10.1] Auroras (northern lights, glowing displays in the sky) occur here, and the International Space Station orbits within it at about 400 km.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.10.2] The exosphere is the outermost fringe, merging into space.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.11.b] Oceans dominate the planet's surface and its water, so they are the other half of the linked system.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.11.0] The oceans cover about 71% of the earth's surface and hold about 97% of its water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.11.1] The five oceans are the Pacific (the largest and deepest), Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic; the Pacific covers about a third of the earth's surface.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.11.2] Average ocean depth is about 3.7 km. The deepest point is the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench (a very deep, narrow trough in the sea floor), in the western Pacific, at about 11,000 m.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.12.b] Seawater is salty, and salinity is the measure of how salty. It is one of the main physical and chemical characteristics of the oceans.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.12.0] Salinity is the dissolved salt content of seawater: about 35 parts per thousand on average, or 35 g of salt per kg.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.12.1] Sodium chloride is the main salt, with magnesium, sulphate, calcium and potassium also present.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.12.2] The salts come from rocks weathered (broken down) and carried by rivers and from volcanic gases and vents.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.13.b] Salinity is not the same everywhere. It rises where water is lost to the air and falls where fresh water is added.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.13.0] Salinity is higher where evaporation exceeds precipitation, because water leaves but the salt stays behind, as in the subtropical oceans and in nearly enclosed seas in hot, dry areas.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.13.1] The Red Sea, at about 40 parts per thousand, is among the saltiest open seas, and the Mediterranean is saltier than the Atlantic.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.13.2] Salinity is lower where rainfall is heavy (the equatorial belt), where large rivers enter the sea and where ice melts (polar seas), because fresh water dilutes the salt.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.13.3] The Baltic Sea is brackish (slightly salty), well under 10 parts per thousand in much of it, because of heavy river inflow and little evaporation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.13.4] When sea ice forms it leaves its salt behind, so the water beneath becomes saltier and denser; this feeds the thermohaline circulation (the deep ocean circulation driven by differences in temperature and salt). There is more on this in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.14.b] The ocean is layered by temperature, just as the atmosphere is. The sun heats only the top, so the layers run from warm at the surface to cold below.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.14.0] A warm, well-mixed surface layer, heated by the sun and stirred by wind, lies at the top of the tropical ocean, about the top 100-200 m.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.14.1] The thermocline is the layer where temperature falls rapidly with depth.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.14.2] The deep ocean is cold (about 0-4 °C) and dark, and is the largest part of the ocean by volume.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.14.3] In polar waters there is little difference between the surface and the deep, so there is no strong thermocline.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.14.4] Diagram: temperature against depth for a tropical ocean, showing the three layers.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.15.b] How dense seawater is decides whether it sinks or floats, and density depends on temperature and salinity.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.15.0] Seawater is denser when it is colder and saltier; differences in density make water sink or rise and drive deep ocean circulation. There is more on this in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.15.1] Surface temperature varies with latitude, from about 28-30 °C in the tropics to about -2 °C at the poles, which is the freezing point of seawater; ocean currents shift these patterns.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.16.b] The ocean also holds dissolved gases. Its uptake of carbon dioxide has a useful and a harmful effect.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.16.0] The ocean dissolves oxygen, from the air and from phytoplankton (tiny floating plant-like organisms that give off oxygen), and carbon dioxide.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.16.1] It has absorbed roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide released by human activity. This slows global warming but makes the water more acidic (ocean acidification), which harms corals and shellfish.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.16.2] Average surface ocean pH (a scale measuring acidity, where lower means more acidic) has fallen from about 8.2 to about 8.1 since pre-industrial times.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.17.b] To observe the atmosphere and oceans you need readings from many places at the same moment. Observing networks provide them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.17.0] Synoptic weather stations (stations that record the weather at the same moment as others), staffed or automatic, record at fixed times in UTC (a single world time standard) to World Meteorological Organization (WMO) standards, so that readings can be compared worldwide.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.17.1] Other sources include ships, buoys, aircraft, weather balloons, radar and satellites.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.17.2] Met Éireann (the Irish national weather service) collects the Irish data. There is more on this in Weather maps and weather data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.b] Temperature is measured by several instruments, each suited to a different job, and a screen keeps the readings honest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.0] A liquid-in-glass thermometer uses mercury or alcohol, which expands and rises up a narrow tube as it warms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.1] A maximum-minimum thermometer, Six's thermometer, uses small metal indexes (markers) that are pushed up to the highest and lowest points reached and stay there until reset with a magnet; it is read once a day and then reset. James Six invented it in 1782.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.2] Electronic thermometers in automatic stations use sensors whose electrical resistance changes with temperature; a thermograph gives a continuous trace.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.3] Thermometers sit in a Stevenson screen (a ventilated white box) about 1.25 m above the ground. It is white to reflect sunlight, louvred (slatted) to let air circulate, and mounted on legs above grass.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.4] The Stevenson screen's door faces away from direct sun (north in Ireland), so readings are true shade air temperatures.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.5] A grass minimum thermometer, laid just above short grass, measures ground frost.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.6] Daily mean, daily range and annual range are derived from these readings. There is more on the calculation method in Weather maps and weather data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.b] Air pressure is the weight of the air above, and two types of barometer measure it in different ways.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.0] A mercury barometer: the weight of the atmosphere supports a column of mercury in a glass tube, and the column rises as pressure rises. Evangelista Torricelli invented it in 1643.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.1] An aneroid barometer (aneroid means without liquid) uses a sealed, partly emptied metal capsule that is squeezed in as pressure rises and expands as it falls; levers move a needle on a dial. It is portable, so it is used in homes and on ships.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.2] A barograph links an aneroid to a pen that draws a continuous record on a rotating drum; the trend of rising or falling pressure matters most in forecasting.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.3] Electronic pressure sensors are used in automatic stations.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.4] Readings are corrected to mean sea level so that stations at different heights can be compared and isobars (lines joining places of equal pressure) drawn. Pressure is measured in hPa (hectopascals, equal to mb), with an average of about 1013 hPa.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.b] Wind speed is measured by an anemometer, standing at a standard height so that readings can be compared.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.0] A cup anemometer has three or four cups on arms that spin faster as wind speed rises; the turns are counted electrically and converted to speed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.1] Sonic anemometers, which have no moving parts, are used on some modern stations.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.2] The WMO standard height for surface wind is 10 m above open ground, away from buildings and trees, which would shelter the instrument and lower its readings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.3] Speed is given in km/h, knots or m/s, and gusts (short bursts of stronger wind) are recorded separately from mean speed. Without instruments, speed is estimated using the Beaufort scale.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.21.b] Wind direction is the way the wind blows from, and it is shown by a vane or a windsock.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.21.0] A wind vane has a large tail that is pushed downwind, so its pointer faces the direction the wind blows from; direction is read against compass points or degrees.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.21.1] Windsocks show wind direction and strength at airports.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.21.2] Wind is always named from the direction it comes from, so a westerly wind blows from the west.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.b] Humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air. The wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer measures it by using evaporative cooling.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.0] A wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer (psychrometer) has two thermometers side by side. The wet bulb is wrapped in muslin (thin cloth) kept wet from a water reservoir; evaporation from the muslin cools it, so it reads lower than the dry bulb.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.1] The drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the bigger the difference, called the wet bulb depression. If both thermometers read the same, the air is saturated (holding all the vapour it can).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.2] Relative humidity, given as a percentage, is read from tables using the dry bulb reading and the difference between the two readings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.3] A hair hygrometer uses human hair, which lengthens in humid air and shortens in dry air, to move a pointer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.4] Automatic stations use electronic (capacitance) sensors.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.23.b] Rain, sunshine, evaporation and cloud each have their own instrument.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.23.0] Rain gauge: a funnel of standard size, set in open ground with its rim raised above the surface to avoid splash, collects rain into a measuring cylinder that is read in mm each day; tipping-bucket gauges record automatically.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.23.1] Campbell-Stokes sunshine recorder: a glass sphere focuses the sun's rays and burns a trace on a card; the length of the burn gives hours of bright sunshine.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.23.2] Evaporation pan: measures the water lost from an open water surface by evaporation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.23.3] Cloud cover is estimated in oktas (eighths of the sky covered) by observers or measured by ceilometers (instruments that find the height of cloud bases).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.24.b] Weather also has to be measured high above the ground, where ground instruments cannot reach.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.24.0] Weather balloons carry radiosondes, small instrument packs that measure temperature, humidity and pressure and radio them back; tracking the balloon gives the upper winds.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.24.1] Balloons are launched twice a day, at fixed times, from stations worldwide, including Valentia Observatory, Co. Kerry, which has one of the longest weather records in Ireland, dating from the 19th century.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.24.2] Aircraft also report temperature and wind automatically.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.25.b] Remote sensing means measuring from a distance, without touching the thing measured.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.25.0] Geostationary (fixed over one spot) and polar-orbiting (passing over the poles) weather satellites measure cloud, sea-surface temperature, water vapour and ice. There is more on this in Satellite imagery.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.25.1] Rainfall radar detects precipitation from the echo returned by raindrops.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.b] Ocean measurements come from instruments floating on, drifting in and orbiting above the sea. Together they supply the data on the ocean side of the linked system.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.0] Moored weather buoys (anchored floating stations) record air pressure, air and sea temperature, wind, wave height and humidity, and transmit the readings, hourly. The Irish Marine Data Buoy Observation Network, run by the Marine Institute with Met Éireann, moors such buoys off the Irish coast.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.1] Drifting buoys follow ocean currents, and voluntary observing ships report weather at sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.2] Argo floats drift at depth, rise to the surface every 10 days or so, and measure temperature and salinity down to about 2,000 m before transmitting by satellite. The Argo programme began in 2000, and about 4,000 floats now drift in the world's oceans.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.3] Tide gauges and satellite altimeters (instruments that measure height from space) measure sea level; satellites also measure sea-surface temperature and sea ice.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.4] Research ships lower CTD instruments (conductivity, temperature, depth) to sample salinity and temperature at depth.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.5] The TAO array, a line of moored buoys across the equatorial Pacific, monitors the ocean to give warning of El Niño.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.27.b] A reading is only useful if it is trustworthy, so how and where instruments are set up matters as much as the instrument.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.27.0] Instruments must be correctly sited: in an open place, at the standard height, shaded for thermometers and away from buildings and heated surfaces, which would distort the readings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.27.1] Instruments must be regularly calibrated (checked against a standard), read at the same times each day, and long records kept at the same site, so the record stays consistent.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.27.2] Urban growth around a station (the urban heat island, the extra warmth of built-up areas) can bias its records upward.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.b] Measurements are collected because they are used. Each use below is a separate reason, from forecasting and safety to farming, energy and climate science.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.0] Forecasting: observations are the starting point (initial conditions) for computer forecast models, and more and better observations give better forecasts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.1] Warnings: storm, rain, snow and heat warnings, including Met Éireann's colour codes, save lives and property. There is more on this in Weather maps and weather data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.2] Storm surge (sea level raised by a storm) and flood warnings are issued, and tropical cyclones are tracked; a falling barograph trace warns of an approaching depression (a low-pressure weather system).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.3] Aviation needs wind, visibility, icing and pressure for altimeter settings (so a plane's height reading is correct); shipping and fishing need sea area forecasts and wave heights from buoys; offshore work depends on the same data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.4] Farming: the timing of silage and hay making, spraying, sowing and harvesting depends on forecasts; frost warnings protect crops; soil temperature and moisture show when grass starts to grow; drought monitoring is also part of this use. There is more on drought in Climate and economic development.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.5] Energy and the economy: wind measurements are used to site wind farms and predict their output; temperature forecasts predict electricity and gas demand; construction, retail, insurance, sport and tourism planning all use weather data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.6] Climate records: long, consistent records, such as the Mauna Loa carbon dioxide record and Valentia's long weather record, show trends including global warming, sea-level rise and ocean warming; they are the evidence used by the IPCC and by governments.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.7] The IPCC was set up in 1988 by the WMO and the UN Environment Programme.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.8] Ocean-atmosphere link: buoy and Argo data on sea temperature and salinity track El Niño, ocean heat content and the Atlantic circulation, and they improve seasonal forecasts.
