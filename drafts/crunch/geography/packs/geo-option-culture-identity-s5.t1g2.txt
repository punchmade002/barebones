TARGET geo-option-culture-identity-s5: 5. Nationality and the nation state
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.b] Nationality and the nation state are political entities placed on the physical and cultural landscape. Three words that sound alike, state, nation and nationality, each have a precise meaning, and the rest of this option depends on keeping them apart.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.0] A state is a sovereign political unit (one that governs itself and answers to no outside ruler) with territory, a permanent population, a government and recognition by other states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.1] The Montevideo Convention (1933) set out the criteria for a state: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.2] The United Nations has 193 member states, which shows how many sovereign units the world map is divided into.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.3] A nation is a people who share a culture, history and often a language or religion, and who see themselves as one community. A nation is defined by identity, not by a government.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.4] Nationality is membership of a nation or, legally, citizenship of a state, so the word can mean either belonging or a legal status.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.5] A nation state is a state whose people largely form one nation, for example Iceland, Portugal or Japan. Here the nation and the state match.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.6] A multinational state contains several nations, for example the UK, Belgium, Switzerland or Spain.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.7] A stateless nation is a nation without its own state, for example the Kurds.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.0.8] Ireland is close to a nation state but has minorities, such as Irish Travellers; the UK is a multinational state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.1.b] The idea that every nation should have its own state is fairly recent, and it spread in two main waves. Even so, it has never been fully achieved.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.1.0] In Europe, nationalism (the belief that a people sharing a culture should govern itself) held from the 19th century that each nation should have its own state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.1.1] The break-up of empires after the First World War and after 1989–91 created new nation states in Europe.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.1.2] Elsewhere, decolonisation (colonies winning independence from the European powers that ruled them) after 1945 created most of the new states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.1.3] Most states are not pure nation states, because they contain more than one cultural group (a people sharing a language, religion or way of life).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.2.b] A boundary is the line that marks where one state ends and another begins. Boundaries can be classified by what they follow on the ground and by when they were drawn relative to the people living there.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.2.0] Physical boundaries follow natural features, such as mountains, rivers, lakes or seas.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.2.1] Geometric boundaries are straight lines, often lines of latitude (lines running east-west around the globe), so they ignore the landscape.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.2.2] Cultural boundaries follow a language or religion, so they separate peoples rather than landscapes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.2.3] Antecedent boundaries are drawn before settlement; subsequent boundaries are drawn after settlement and follow cultural divisions, so they fit the people already living there.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.2.4] Superimposed boundaries are imposed by outside powers regardless of culture, as in colonial Africa (Africa when European powers ruled it).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.2.5] Relict boundaries no longer function but remain visible, for example the line of the Berlin Wall.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.3.b] Mountains, rivers and seas are the most obvious places to draw a border, because they are easy to see and hard to cross. Whether they match the cultural map is another matter.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.3.0] The Pyrenees, the Alps, the Rhine, the Rio Grande and the Himalayas are major physical boundaries.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.3.1] The Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659) used the Pyrenees as much of the France–Spain border.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.3.2] The Rhine forms part of the border between France and Germany; the Rio Grande forms part of the USA–Mexico border.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.3.3] Physical boundaries often fit cultural divides but can also cut through a cultural group: the Basques live on both sides of the Pyrenees, so a mountain border still splits one people between two states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.4.b] Where boundaries are drawn as straight lines on a map, they take no account of the people on the ground. This matters most where outside powers drew them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.4.0] The 49th parallel forms most of the western USA–Canada border, agreed in 1818 and extended in 1846.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.4.1] The Berlin Conference (1884–85) set rules for the European division of Africa, so European powers carved up the continent among themselves.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.4.2] Many African borders are straight lines that ignore ethnic territories (the lands of different peoples), splitting some groups and joining rival ones.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.4.3] These colonial borders are a cause of later conflict and secession (a region breaking away to form its own state), because rival groups were forced into one state and single peoples were divided.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.5.b] A boundary does more than mark territory. It shapes who people feel they are, and it can unite, divide, harden or soften depending on how it is managed.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.5.0] Boundaries create a shared national identity within them, through citizenship, schools, media, sport and symbols (such as flags), which all treat everyone inside the border as one people.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.5.1] Boundaries split cultural groups and create minorities (smaller groups living within a state dominated by another group): partition (dividing a territory between two states) in Ireland, the Kurds and the Basques.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.5.2] Boundaries can harden into barriers: the Berlin Wall stood from 1961 to 1989, and the Cyprus Green Line, the UN buffer zone (a neutral strip separating two sides) through Nicosia, has divided the island since 1974.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.5.3] Boundaries can also soften: the Schengen Agreement, first signed in 1985, removed passport checks between most EU states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.5.4] Ireland is outside Schengen because of the Common Travel Area with the UK, in place since 1923; the border has been open since 1998.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.5.5] Boundary disputes can cause war, and changing boundaries create new identities and new minorities.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.6.b] The island of Ireland is a clear physical unit, but its political border divides it. This is the national example of a boundary that does not follow the landscape.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.6.0] The Government of Ireland Act 1920 created two administrations on the island.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.6.1] The Anglo-Irish Treaty was signed in December 1921; Northern Ireland was established in 1921.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.6.2] Northern Ireland consists of six of Ulster’s nine counties: Antrim, Armagh, Down, Fermanagh, Londonderry (Derry), and Tyrone.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.6.3] Cavan, Donegal and Monaghan, the other three Ulster counties, went to the Irish Free State in 1922.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.6.4] The Boundary Commission of 1925 (a body set up to review the border) was not published and the border was left unchanged.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.6.5] The border is about 499 km long and follows old county boundaries rather than physical features, so it cuts through communities and farms, and through the Erne and Foyle catchments (the areas of land drained by those rivers).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.6.6] There is more on the partition boundary history in The complexity of regions (ii).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.7.b] Partition did not just draw a line. It created two states, each with its own majority and its own minority, and Ireland’s constitution had to respond.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.7.0] Partition created two states, two majorities and two minorities, so each state contained people who identified with the other.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.7.1] Ireland became a republic and left the Commonwealth (the association of states once part of the British Empire) in 1949.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.7.2] Articles 2 and 3 of the Constitution originally claimed the whole island as national territory.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.7.3] After the Good Friday Agreement referendum of May 1998, the Articles were changed to an aspiration to unity by consent, in force from December 1999.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.7.4] Everyone born on the island of Ireland is entitled to Irish citizenship; under the Agreement, people in Northern Ireland may be Irish, British or both.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.8.b] Since 1998 the border has been open, so most people cross it without noticing. Brexit, the UK’s departure from the EU, made that openness an issue again.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.8.0] The border has been open since 1998, and its openness became an issue after Brexit because leaving the EU could have meant checks on the line.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.8.1] In the UK referendum on EU membership on 23 June 2016, Northern Ireland voted about 56% Remain.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.8.2] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020, which is why the status of the border mattered.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.8.3] The Windsor Framework (2023) keeps the land border free of checks, so the border stays open.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.9.b] Few states are made of one people. Three historical processes explain why almost every state holds more than one cultural group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.9.0] Historical conquest and union brought different peoples into one state, as one people took over or joined with another.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.9.1] Boundaries drawn without regard to culture placed groups in the wrong state, leaving them as minorities under a government of another group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.9.2] Migration added new cultural groups to existing states, as people moved in from elsewhere.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.10.b] States respond to cultural diversity in very different ways, from sharing power to denying a group’s existence. The responses form a range.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.10.0] Federalism (power divided between a national and regional governments) and autonomy (a region controlling its own affairs) give regions control of their own affairs, as in Belgium, Switzerland and Spain’s autonomous communities.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.10.1] Devolution gives regions their own parliament or assembly while the state keeps overall power, as in the UK in 1999.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.10.2] Power-sharing gives each main group a role in government, so no group is shut out, as in Northern Ireland and Lebanon.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.10.3] Multiculturalism recognises several cultures within one state, as in Canada.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.10.4] Assimilation requires minorities to adopt the dominant culture, as in France.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.10.5] Suppression denies a group its identity: historically the Kurds in Turkey, and today the Uyghurs in China.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.11.b] Spain is a multinational state whose Basque and Catalan regions each have a distinct identity. It has answered with autonomy rather than a single national model.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.11.0] Basques and Catalans have their own languages, autonomy statutes (the laws setting out each region’s powers) and strong regional identities.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.11.1] Spain’s 1978 constitution created 17 autonomous communities.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.11.2] The Basque Autonomous Community (Statute of Gernika, 1979) and Navarre collect their own taxes, a sign of how much power they hold.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.11.3] Catalonia’s autonomy statute dates from 1979 and was revised in 2006; Barcelona is its capital.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.11.4] Basque and Catalan were suppressed under Franco (1939–1975), the period of dictatorship before the 1978 constitution.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.11.5] Independence movements arose in both regions: the ETA campaign in the Basque Country and the 2017 referendum in Catalonia. There is more on these in the conflicts blocks below.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.12.b] The UK is a multinational state of four nations. Devolution since 1999 gave three of them their own elected bodies.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.12.0] The UK is made up of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.12.1] Devolution in 1999 created the Scottish Parliament, the National Assembly for Wales (renamed the Senedd in 2020) and the Northern Ireland Assembly.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.12.2] The Scottish independence referendum of 18 September 2014 voted about 55% No, so Scotland stayed in the UK.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.12.3] The 2016 EU referendum was a different vote: the UK voted about 52% Leave, but Scotland voted about 62% Remain. There is more on Northern Ireland’s result in The island of Ireland and partition.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.13.b] Quebec is the clearest example of a distinct cultural group inside a state that has stayed together, though narrowly.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.13.0] Quebec is French-speaking, so its identity is distinct within Canada.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.13.1] Quebec’s Charter of the French Language (Bill 101) was passed in 1977, to protect the French language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.13.2] Quebec held sovereignty referendums (votes on whether to become independent) in 1980, when about 60% voted No, and in 1995, when about 50.6% voted No, a very narrow result.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.13.3] In 2006 Canada’s House of Commons recognised that “the Québécois form a nation within a united Canada”.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.14.b] Belgium and Switzerland are two multinational states that are full case studies elsewhere in this option, so only a pointer is needed here.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.14.0] Belgium has several language groups and federal regions, so it is a multinational state. There is more on this in the Belgium option of A European regional case study of identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.14.1] Switzerland has cantons (the member regions of the Swiss federation) and four national languages. There is more on this in the Switzerland option of A European regional case study of identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.15.b] Ireland is close to a nation state, but it has a recognised ethnic minority and new communities formed by recent immigration.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.15.0] Irish Travellers were recognised as an ethnic minority on 1 March 2017 and number about 33,000 (Census 2022).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.15.1] Census 2022 recorded about 20% of residents born outside Ireland, creating new immigrant communities.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.16.b] Indigenous peoples (the original inhabitants of a region) often now live as minorities inside states. The Sámi show how one such people can span several states and still have its own elected bodies.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.16.0] The Sámi are the indigenous people of the far north of Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia’s Kola Peninsula (Sápmi), traditionally reindeer herders.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.16.1] The Sámi have their own elected parliaments, for example in Norway (1989), Sweden (1993) and Finland (1996).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.16.2] Aboriginal Australians and Māori are also indigenous peoples within states. There is more on them in Racial groupings.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.17.b] China is a state with large minority peoples who have no state of their own. Two of them are treated as stateless groups below.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.17.0] China’s minorities include the Tibetans and the Uyghurs, both dealt with under cultural groups without a nation state below.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.18.b] The Kurds are the main case study of a stateless nation: a large people with a shared language and homeland, split between four states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.18.0] The Kurds are commonly estimated at 30 million or more, the largest ethnic group without its own state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.18.1] Most Kurds live in a mountainous region, Kurdistan, divided between Turkey (the largest number, in the south-east), Iran, Iraq and Syria.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.18.2] Kurdish is an Indo-European (Iranian) language, so it is one marker that sets Kurds apart as a people.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.19.b] A Kurdish state was promised after the First World War and then withdrawn. Turkey, home to the largest Kurdish population, then suppressed Kurdish identity, and that led to armed conflict.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.19.0] The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) provided for a possible Kurdish state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.19.1] The Treaty of Lausanne (1923) replaced it and drew the borders of modern Turkey with no Kurdish state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.19.2] Turkey long denied a separate Kurdish identity and banned Kurdish in public life and broadcasting until the early 1990s.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.19.3] The PKK (Kurdistan Workers’ Party), founded in 1978, began an armed campaign in 1984; the conflict cost about 40,000 lives.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.19.4] The PKK announced its dissolution in May 2025.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.20.b] In Iraq and Syria the Kurds have fared differently from Turkey: Iraqi Kurds suffered an attack but won autonomy, and Syrian Kurds fought the Islamic State group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.20.0] On 16 March 1988 Saddam Hussein’s forces attacked the Kurdish town of Halabja with chemical weapons, killing about 5,000 people.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.20.1] After the 1991 Gulf War a no-fly zone (airspace closed to Iraqi aircraft) protected Iraqi Kurds.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.20.2] Iraq’s 2005 constitution recognised the Kurdistan Region as autonomous, with its own government and parliament in Erbil.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.20.3] On 25 September 2017 about 93% voted for independence in a referendum that Iraq’s government rejected; Iraqi forces then retook Kirkuk.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.20.4] In Syria, Kurdish-led forces fought the Islamic State group, including the defence of Kobani (2014–15), and ran an autonomous area in the north-east.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.21.b] A people without a state keeps itself together through shared culture. For the Kurds, language and a national festival do this work.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.21.0] The Kurdish language and Newroz, the Kurdish New Year on 21 March, are major expressions of Kurdish identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.22.b] The Palestinians are a second example of a people without a fully sovereign state, with many living as refugees (people forced to flee their homes) in neighbouring countries.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.22.0] About 700,000 Palestinians fled or were expelled in 1948. There is more on the events of 1948 and 1967 in Religion as a cultural indicator.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.22.1] Palestinian refugees and their descendants live in Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, the West Bank and Gaza.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.22.2] The Oslo Accords of 1993 created the Palestinian Authority, with limited self-rule, so Palestinians gained some government but not a sovereign state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.22.3] Ireland, Spain and Norway recognised the State of Palestine on 28 May 2024; the UK, Canada and Australia did so on 21 September 2025, and France on 22 September 2025.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.23.b] The Roma are a people spread across Europe with no territory of their own. Their history shows both migration from India and long-running persecution and discrimination.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.23.0] The Roma number about 10–12 million in Europe (European Commission estimate) and have no territory of their own.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.23.1] Their Romani language is related to languages of northern India, showing that their ancestors migrated from India; they reached south-eastern Europe by the 14th century.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.23.2] Large Roma populations live in Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, Slovakia, Spain and Turkey.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.23.3] Roma were targeted for extermination in the Holocaust, the Nazi genocide of the Second World War.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.23.4] In D.H. and Others v Czech Republic (2007) the European Court of Human Rights ruled that placing Roma children in special schools was discrimination.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.24.b] Tibetans are a people whose homeland has been under Chinese control since 1950, with their leader in exile.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.24.0] China’s army entered Tibet in 1950, which began Chinese control of the region.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.24.1] After a failed uprising in 1959, the Dalai Lama (Tibet’s spiritual and political leader) fled to India; Tibet’s government-in-exile is based in Dharamsala.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.24.2] The Tibet Autonomous Region was set up in 1965.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.24.3] Tibetans see Han Chinese migration (settlement by China’s majority ethnic group), controls on monasteries and Mandarin-medium schooling (teaching in China’s official language) as threats to their culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.25.b] The Uyghurs, a Muslim people of north-west China, are a recent example of a state acting against a cultural group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.25.0] The Uyghurs are a Turkic-speaking, mainly Muslim people of Xinjiang in north-west China.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.25.1] Since 2017 an estimated one million or more Uyghurs and other Muslims have been held in detention, or “re-education”, camps.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.25.2] A UN human rights office report of August 2022 found “serious human rights violations” that may amount to crimes against humanity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.26.b] Not every stateless nation is in the same position. Basques and Catalans show a nation without a state that nonetheless has real self-government.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.26.0] Basques and Catalans are nations without independent states that have strong autonomy, which contrasts sharply with the position of the Kurds.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.27.b] Groups without a state have a limited set of ways to respond, and the results vary widely.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.27.0] Groups without a state keep their identity through language, festivals and diaspora organisations (groups of people living abroad who keep ties to their homeland).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.27.1] They pursue peaceful political campaigns, demand autonomy, or in some cases use armed struggle.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.27.2] Outcomes range from autonomy, as in Iraqi Kurdistan and Catalonia, to repression, as in Tibet and Xinjiang.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.28.b] Conflicts between a state and a cultural group tend to have the same underlying causes, whichever country they happen in.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.28.0] Boundaries cut across cultural groups, so one people divided between states or minorities inside another group’s state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.28.1] A dominant group controls the state, so other groups feel excluded from power.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.28.2] The language and religion of a group are suppressed.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.28.3] Economic inequality between regions or groups creates grievances.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.28.4] Two groups make competing claims to the same territory.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.29.b] Northern Ireland is the national case study of a state built around one cultural group while another lived within it. This led to civil rights protest and then the Troubles.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.29.0] The state of Northern Ireland was created around a unionist majority (people wanting to stay in the UK) with a large nationalist minority (people wanting a united Ireland).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.29.1] Discrimination against nationalists fuelled the civil rights movement from 1968.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.29.2] The Troubles (late 1960s–1998) killed over 3,500 people.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.29.3] The 2021 census recorded Catholics at about 46% and Protestants and other Christians at about 43% (religion or religion brought up in).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.30.b] The Good Friday Agreement redesigned Northern Ireland’s political structure so that both cultural groups had a place in government.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.30.0] The Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement was signed on 10 April 1998; referendums on 22 May 1998 returned about 71% Yes in Northern Ireland and about 94% Yes in the Republic.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.30.1] The Agreement redesigned the political structure for two cultural groups: a power-sharing Assembly and Executive, a North/South Ministerial Council (a body linking both parts of the island), and the consent principle (no change in status without majority agreement).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.30.2] The Agreement did not move the border.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.30.3] The Assembly was restored in February 2024, with Michelle O’Neill as the first nationalist First Minister.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.30.4] Brexit challenged the Agreement’s arrangements. There is more on this in The island of Ireland and partition.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.31.b] In the Basque Country, one wing of the independence movement turned to violence. Spain responded mainly through autonomy.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.31.0] ETA (Euskadi Ta Askatasuna, “Basque Homeland and Freedom”) was founded in 1959.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.31.1] ETA killed over 800 people in its campaign for an independent Basque state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.31.2] ETA declared a definitive end to violence in 2011 and dissolved in 2018.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.31.3] Spain responded through the autonomy granted under its 1978 constitution. There is more on this in Spain under cultural groups within nation states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.32.b] Catalonia’s conflict with Spain was fought mostly through courts and votes rather than violence, and it escalated after a court ruling in 2010.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.32.0] In 2010 Spain’s Constitutional Court struck down parts of Catalonia’s 2006 autonomy statute, including its description of Catalonia as a nation, fuelling the independence movement.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.32.1] On 1 October 2017 an independence referendum was declared illegal by Spain’s courts and police tried to stop voting; about 90% of those voting backed independence, on a turnout of about 43%.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.32.2] Spain imposed direct rule (central government taking over the region’s powers) using Article 155 of its constitution, and the regional president Carles Puigdemont fled to Belgium.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.32.3] Nine leaders were jailed in 2019 and pardoned in 2021; an amnesty law (a law forgiving those convicted) was passed in 2024.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.33.b] Yugoslavia was a multinational state that broke apart, and it is a full case study elsewhere in this option.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.33.0] Yugoslavia was a multinational state that broke up into separate states. There is more on this in the Balkans option of A European regional case study of identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.34.b] Other examples show the same causes at work in different parts of the world.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.34.0] Sri Lanka: civil war from 1983 to 2009 between the government, led by the Sinhalese majority (mainly Buddhist), and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE), who sought a Tamil state in the north and east; the LTTE was defeated in May 2009.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.34.1] Chechnya, a mainly Muslim republic in Russia’s North Caucasus, fought Russia in wars in 1994–96 and 1999–2009; Grozny was largely destroyed.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.34.2] Cyprus has been divided since 1974, so one island holds two separate communities. There is more on this in The complexity of regions (ii).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s5.34.3] Rwanda’s ethnic conflict is another example. There is more on this in Racial groupings.

CANDIDATES geo-option-culture-identity-s3: 3. Religion as a cultural indicator
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.b] A cultural indicator is a feature that helps identify and map a cultural group. Religion is one of the strongest, because it affects how people think, live and look, and it leaves visible marks on the landscape.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.0] Religion shapes beliefs, values and moral codes (the rules about right and wrong), and so it often marks group identity more strongly than nationality does.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.1] It shows in the landscape, so a place's religion can be read from what is built there: churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, holy wells, wayside shrines and cemeteries.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.2] It shapes diet, such as halal (permitted under Islam), kosher (permitted under Judaism) and Hindu vegetarianism, and dress, such as the headscarf, the turban and the kippah (the Jewish skullcap).

[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.b] A hearth is the place where a religion began, before it spread outwards. Judaism, Christianity and Islam began in the Middle East, while Hinduism and Buddhism began in the Indian subcontinent (the region of India and its neighbours).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.0] Judaism is the oldest of the three Abrahamic faiths (Judaism, Christianity and Islam), so the other two grew after it, and Jerusalem is its holiest city.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.1] Christianity began with the life of Jesus in Roman Palestine, so its hearth is in the Middle East, for example Jerusalem.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.2] Islam was founded by the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, Arabia, in the early 7th century. The Hijra (the move of the early Muslims) to Medina in 622 begins the Islamic calendar.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.3] Hinduism has no single founder; it developed gradually in the Indian subcontinent.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.4] Buddhism was founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha) in north-east India around the 5th century BC. Bodh Gaya is where he is said to have gained enlightenment (spiritual awakening).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.5] Sikhism was founded by Guru Nanak in Punjab in the 15th century. The Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib) at Amritsar is its holiest site.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.b] Religions moved out of their hearths through five main routes: empire, missionaries, trade, colonialism and migration. Each route explains why a religion is found where it is today.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.0] Empire: Christianity spread through the Roman Empire, which made it the official religion in the 4th century. Islam spread with the Arab conquests from the 7th century, across the Middle East and North Africa and into Spain within about a century.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.1] Missionaries (people sent to spread their faith): Irish monks, such as St Columba on Iona and St Columbanus in continental Europe, spread Christianity in the early medieval period.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.2] Trade: Islam spread to Indonesia and West Africa, for example along trade routes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.3] Colonialism (rule over overseas territories by a foreign power): Christianity spread to the Americas, Africa and the Philippines.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.4] Migration: Islam, Hinduism and Sikhism spread into Europe as people moved there.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.b] Christianity split into three main branches, and each is strong in different regions. Two events, the Great Schism and the Reformation, created these divisions.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.0] Roman Catholic: southern Europe, Ireland, Poland, Latin America and the Philippines.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.1] Protestant, which began with the Reformation: northern Europe, the UK and the USA.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.2] Orthodox, which grew from the Great Schism: eastern Europe, Russia, Greece and Serbia.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.3] The Great Schism (the split) of 1054 divided the Catholic West (Rome) from the Orthodox East (Constantinople).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.4] The Reformation (the break from Catholicism that created Protestantism) began in 1517 with Martin Luther in Germany, and Calvinism spread from Geneva.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.5] Christianity is growing in sub-Saharan Africa (Africa south of the Sahara).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.7.b] Islam is spread across a broad belt of the world, and the largest Muslim population is not in the Arab world. It has two main branches, Sunni and Shia.

[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.7.3] Mecca and Medina, in Saudi Arabia, are Islam's holiest cities, because Islam was founded in Mecca and the Hijra went to Medina. The Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca is a duty for every Muslim able to make it.

[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.0] Hinduism is found in India and Nepal and in the Indian diaspora. About four in five Indians are Hindu, and Varanasi on the Ganges is a major pilgrimage city.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.1] Buddhism, which began in India, is now found in Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Tibet, Japan and Vietnam.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.2] Judaism is found mainly in Israel and the USA, because most of the world's Jews live there.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.3] Sikhism is found in Punjab, with a diaspora in the UK and Canada.

[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.b] Countries differ in how far religion and government are joined. The four types run from religious rule to strict separation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.0] Theocracy: religious leaders rule the state, so religious authority is also political authority.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.1] An established or state church (an official church recognised by the state): the Church of England is the established church in England.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.2] Strict separation of church and state: France's laïcité (the French principle of secularism) and the USA's First Amendment.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.3] States in which one church had a dominant influence without being the official church: Ireland in the 20th century and Poland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.12.b] In a theocracy, or where religion and state are fused, religious law and religious leaders hold political power. Iran, Vatican City and Saudi Arabia show this in different ways.
