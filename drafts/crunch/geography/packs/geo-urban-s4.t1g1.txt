MODE: cost and links
TARGET geo-urban-s4: 4. Urban land use and planning
[geo-urban-s4.0.b] A large city is divided into zones of land use (areas where one kind of activity dominates). Each zone has its own pattern of buildings and activity, and its own land value. The zones below are typical of a large European city and can all be identified in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s4.0.0] CBD (central business district): the commercial heart of the city, with the highest land values, tall buildings and the most intensive use of land, meaning the most activity packed onto each site.
[geo-urban-s4.0.1] Zone in transition: the inner-city area of mixed old housing, warehouses and small industry, where decay and renewal exist side by side. It is called a zone of transition because it is changing from one use to another.
[geo-urban-s4.0.2] Inner residential zone: older terraced housing (houses joined in rows) close to the centre, so it lies near the CBD and jobs.
[geo-urban-s4.0.3] Suburbs: lower-density housing, mainly semi-detached houses (pairs of houses sharing one wall), built later and further from the centre, so there is more space for each house.
[geo-urban-s4.0.4] Industrial zones: the port, inner-city industry, suburban industrial estates and business parks (landscaped sites of modern offices and light industry).
[geo-urban-s4.0.5] Open space and recreation: parks, playing fields and other green areas within the built-up city, such as Phoenix Park in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s4.0.6] Transport land: roads, railway lines, the port and the airport, such as Dublin Port and Dublin Airport.
[geo-urban-s4.0.7] Rural-urban fringe: the transitional land at the edge of the built-up area, where urban and rural land uses meet and compete for the same sites.
[geo-urban-s4.1.b] The CBD is the most intensively used part of any city. Almost everything about it follows from one fact: it is the most accessible place, so everyone wants a site there.
[geo-urban-s4.1.0] Land values are highest here because accessibility is greatest, so the most valuable sites are used for the most profitable activities.
[geo-urban-s4.1.1] Tall buildings maximise the use of expensive land, with several floors of offices or shops on a small site.
[geo-urban-s4.1.2] Vertical zoning: shops occupy the ground floor, with offices or other uses above, so each floor of a costly site earns money.
[geo-urban-s4.1.3] Department stores, chain stores and specialist shops occupy the main shopping streets, because these sites bring the most customers.
[geo-urban-s4.1.4] Offices, banks and the head offices of companies cluster together in the centre, because they can pay the high land values and need to be close to each other and to clients.
[geo-urban-s4.1.5] Government and cultural buildings are located in the centre, where they are most accessible to the whole population.
[geo-urban-s4.1.6] Few people live in the CBD, so it is crowded during the working day and quiet at night, when workers and shoppers go home.
[geo-urban-s4.1.7] Pedestrian streets are used in the busiest shopping areas, where traffic and crowds would otherwise congest the streets.
[geo-urban-s4.2.b] Dublin's CBD shows these features on a real map, and it is the Irish example to use for the CBD.
[geo-urban-s4.2.0] Dublin's CBD lies on both sides of the River Liffey.
[geo-urban-s4.2.1] Its main shopping streets are Grafton Street, south of the Liffey, and Henry Street, north of it. Both are pedestrianised, meaning closed to traffic.
[geo-urban-s4.2.2] O'Connell Street is the city's main street.
[geo-urban-s4.3.b] Three models describe how land uses arrange themselves around a city centre. Each is a simplified generalisation, so it is judged by how well it fits the real city. The first is the oldest, and it draws the city as a set of rings.
[geo-urban-s4.3.0] Ernest Burgess proposed the concentric zone model in 1925, based on Chicago. Land uses form rings around the CBD, with land values and densities (the number of people per area) falling with distance from the centre.
[geo-urban-s4.3.1] Strength: it shows the regular fall in land value and density outwards from the CBD. Limitation: it ignores transport routes, so it does not fit cities whose growth follows roads and rail lines.
[geo-urban-s4.3.2] Diagram: concentric rings drawn around a central point labelled CBD, with the zones of transition, residential and commuter land shown in turn.
[geo-urban-s4.4.b] The second model answers the weakness of the rings: cities do not grow evenly in all directions. Roads, railways and rivers pull growth outwards in lines.
[geo-urban-s4.4.0] Homer Hoyt proposed the sector model in 1939. Land uses extend outwards in wedge-shaped sectors along transport routes, and a high-class residential sector lies away from industry.
[geo-urban-s4.4.1] Strength: it explains why sectors of the city are elongated along roads, rail lines and rivers, because transport routes make the land beside them more accessible. Limitation: it does not show the separate suburban centres that grow in large cities.
[geo-urban-s4.4.2] Diagram: a circle divided into wedge-shaped sectors, with radial transport routes (routes running out from the centre) marked and the high-class residential sector shown away from the industrial sector.
[geo-urban-s4.5.b] The third model drops the idea of a single centre. In a large modern city, activities gather around several centres (nuclei) at once.
[geo-urban-s4.5.0] Chauncy Harris and Edward Ullman proposed the multiple nuclei model in 1945. A city grows around several separate centres, such as the CBD, suburban shopping centres, an airport and business parks.
[geo-urban-s4.5.1] Strength: it explains the growth of suburban centres. Limitation: it gives no simple pattern of rings or sectors, so it is less clear as a general description.
[geo-urban-s4.5.2] Diagram: a city outline with several separate centres marked, each surrounded by its own cluster of land use.
[geo-urban-s4.5.3] Dublin fits the multiple nuclei and sector models better than the concentric model: the CBD is one nucleus, while suburban shopping centres and business parks are other nuclei, and growth follows the main roads out of the city.
[geo-urban-s4.6.b] Dublin shows the zones of a modern European city, with the inner city in transition and the outer city growing along the M50 (the orbital motorway circling the city) and the western new towns.
[geo-urban-s4.6.0] Georgian core: the Georgian squares (planned 18th-century squares), such as Merrion Square and Fitzwilliam Square, are now largely offices.
[geo-urban-s4.6.1] The Wide Streets Commissioners, set up in 1757, widened and improved the city's streets, shaping the centre's wide thoroughfares.
[geo-urban-s4.6.2] Inner-city docklands and the north inner city are in transition, with older industrial and port land being renewed.
[geo-urban-s4.6.3] Older inner suburbs lie between the centre and the outer suburbs, so their older housing forms an inner residential zone.
[geo-urban-s4.6.4] Outer suburbs and the western new towns: Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown, planned in the Wright Report of 1967 (a planning report for the growth of the city).
[geo-urban-s4.6.5] Business and industrial parks lie near the M50 and the N7 (the national road running south-west from Dublin): Grange Castle, Citywest, Park West and Sandyford. Roads give them access for lorries and workers.
[geo-urban-s4.6.6] The port and airport are major transport and industrial land uses: Dublin Port and Dublin Airport.
[geo-urban-s4.6.7] Open space includes Phoenix Park, about 700 hectares and one of Europe's largest enclosed city parks, established in 1662; the Dublin Mountains and Dublin Bay are further open spaces at the edge of the city.
[geo-urban-s4.7.b] Paris is the world city studied for comparison with Dublin. Its land uses are concentrated in the inner city and organised around a ring road and a set of outer business and housing areas.
[geo-urban-s4.7.0] The City of Paris has about 2.1 million people on about 105 km2, divided into 20 arrondissements (administrative districts). The wider Ile-de-France region has about 12 million people.
[geo-urban-s4.7.1] The CBD and government quarter lie in the inner arrondissements, the most central districts.
[geo-urban-s4.7.2] Haussmann's boulevards: Haussmann rebuilt central Paris between 1853 and 1870, creating wide boulevards that give the inner city its formal street pattern.
[geo-urban-s4.7.3] La Défense, west of the city, is one of Europe's largest purpose-built business districts, an office district outside the historic inner city.
[geo-urban-s4.7.4] The Boulevard Périphérique, completed in 1973, is the ring road that forms the boundary of the inner city.
[geo-urban-s4.7.5] The banlieues are the suburbs beyond the Périphérique, where grands ensembles (large public housing estates built in the 1950s to 1970s) are found, for example Sarcelles.
[geo-urban-s4.7.6] The 1965 regional plan created five new towns (planned towns built to take growth away from the city): Cergy-Pontoise, Évry, Marne-la-Vallée, Melun-Sénart and Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines.
[geo-urban-s4.8.b] Industry has moved out of the inner city to the edge, where land is cheaper and road access is better. This is the first of three movements (industrial, residential, commercial) that change a city's land-use pattern.
[geo-urban-s4.8.0] Old inner-city industry beside the port, canals and railways (the transport of the time) included brewing, biscuit-making and distilling.
[geo-urban-s4.8.1] Many of these sites closed or moved to suburban industrial estates and business parks near motorways, so that lorries can reach them easily.
[geo-urban-s4.8.2] Reasons for the move: lack of space for expansion in the inner city, cheaper land on the edge, road access for lorries, less congestion, planning policy, and new industries needing clean, modern sites.
[geo-urban-s4.8.3] The vacated inner-city sites are brownfield land (land previously developed, now available for reuse), which can then be redeveloped for other uses.
[geo-urban-s4.8.4] The Guinness brewery is an exception, having stayed at St James's Gate since Arthur Guinness's lease of 1759.
[geo-urban-s4.8.5] Jacob's biscuit factory moved from Bishop Street in the inner city to Tallaght in the 1970s. The old factory now houses the National Archives, an example of a brownfield site reused.
[geo-urban-s4.8.6] Paris: Renault's Boulogne-Billancourt plant closed in 1992, an example of industry leaving the inner city.
[geo-urban-s4.9.b] Housing has also moved outwards, but in recent years some people have moved back in. Each change raises planning issues.
[geo-urban-s4.9.0] Inner-city tenements (old multi-occupied houses) were cleared in the 20th century, and their residents were moved to new suburbs and new towns.
[geo-urban-s4.9.1] Growth of suburban housing spread the city outwards, with sprawl (uncontrolled spread of building) into commuter counties, the counties around the city whose residents travel in to work.
[geo-urban-s4.9.2] The trend has been reversed in places, with a return to inner-city apartment living in areas such as the Docklands, through regeneration (investment to renew a run-down area).
[geo-urban-s4.9.3] Issues raised by residential change: the density of housing, the height of buildings and the social mix of the area.
[geo-urban-s4.10.b] Shops and offices have followed the roads and the car-owning population to the edge of the city. This takes trade and activity away from the centre.
[geo-urban-s4.10.0] Suburban shopping centres and retail parks have been built beside motorway junctions, where there is car access, free parking and cheaper land.
[geo-urban-s4.10.1] The Square, Tallaght, opened in 1990; Blanchardstown Centre in 1996; Liffey Valley in 1998; and Dundrum Town Centre in 2005. All are beside major roads or the M50.
[geo-urban-s4.10.2] Out-of-town office parks have been built in the same way, on sites with good road access, so that workers can drive to them.
[geo-urban-s4.10.3] Effects on the city centre: vacant units and lost trade, because shoppers move to the edge.
[geo-urban-s4.10.4] Responses: pedestrianisation of the centre, regeneration of central streets and limits on the size of retail parks, so that the centre can keep its shoppers.
[geo-urban-s4.10.5] Paris: La Défense takes offices out of the inner city, while the suburban business districts of the region take other commercial uses.
[geo-urban-s4.11.b] Industry, housing and shops all moving at once creates problems that planners have to manage. These are the main issues.
[geo-urban-s4.11.0] Zoning: planning authorities divide land into use zones through development plans (official plans for an area) to control where each use may be located.
[geo-urban-s4.11.1] Brownfield and greenfield development: reuse of old urban land must be balanced against building on undeveloped edge land.
[geo-urban-s4.11.2] Height and density: taller and denser buildings in the centre raise questions of design, light and services.
[geo-urban-s4.11.3] Transport: new zones must be linked to public transport, or they generate car journeys and congestion.
[geo-urban-s4.11.4] Mixed land use: combining housing, offices and shops in one area reduces travel but can cause conflict between uses.
[geo-urban-s4.11.5] Conflict between business, residents and heritage: new offices or roads can threaten housing or historic buildings.
[geo-urban-s4.12.b] Bid-rent theory, put forward by William Alonso in 1964, explains why land values and land uses change with distance from the centre. Bid-rent is the most a user can afford to pay for a plot of land.
[geo-urban-s4.12.0] Accessibility (how easily a site can be reached by people) is greatest at the centre, so competition for sites is fiercest there and land is dearest at the peak land value intersection (PLVI), the point of highest value.
[geo-urban-s4.12.1] Land values fall with distance from the centre, because each step outwards means less accessibility.
[geo-urban-s4.12.2] Commerce outbids industry for central sites, and industry outbids housing, because each use can pay a different amount for the same land.
[geo-urban-s4.12.3] Secondary peaks of land value form at suburban centres, ring-road junctions and along main roads, where accessibility is also high.
[geo-urban-s4.12.4] Effect on building: tall buildings and intensive use occur at the centre, while large, low-density uses such as warehouses and car parks occur at the edge.
[geo-urban-s4.13.b] Social stratification is the division of a city's population into groups by income, class, ethnicity, age and family stage, and segregation is the separation of these groups into different areas.
[geo-urban-s4.13.0] Causes: land and house prices, the allocation of social housing (housing provided by the State for those who cannot afford to buy or rent privately), planning decisions, transport access, household choice and discrimination.
[geo-urban-s4.13.1] Results: affluent areas are set against areas of concentrated disadvantage, and gated developments (housing estates closed off by gates) create closed residential areas.
[geo-urban-s4.13.2] Gentrification: better-off people move into and renovate a poorer inner-city area, raising prices and displacing lower-income residents. It is a different process from urban renewal and redevelopment. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s4.14.b] Both of the case-study cities show segregation on the map. Use these named examples, and keep descriptions of places factual.
[geo-urban-s4.14.0] Dublin: affluent south-east suburbs, including Ballsbridge, Donnybrook, Dalkey and Foxrock, contrast with lower-income areas in parts of the north inner city and in some outer social-housing estates.
[geo-urban-s4.14.1] Ballymun is the case of concentrated social housing in Dublin, with seven 15-storey tower blocks built between 1966 and 1969.
[geo-urban-s4.14.2] Gentrification around the Docklands: the inner-city area has attracted new residents and businesses, including Google's European headquarters at Grand Canal Dock, known as 'Silicon Docks'.
[geo-urban-s4.14.3] Paris: the wealthy western districts contrast with the poorer north-eastern banlieues of Seine-Saint-Denis, the poorest département (administrative area) in mainland France.
[geo-urban-s4.14.4] Immigrant residents are concentrated in grands ensembles, and the riots that began in Clichy-sous-Bois in October 2005 showed the tensions in these areas.
[geo-urban-s4.15.b] As cities grow, they take in rural land at the rural-urban fringe. The pressures on rural land are caused by population growth and by the choices made by households and firms.
[geo-urban-s4.15.0] Causes: population growth and in-migration (people moving in from elsewhere); demand for cheaper, larger houses; car ownership and motorways; high house prices in the city; and jobs moving to the suburbs.
[geo-urban-s4.15.1] Loss of farmland: housing, roads and business parks replace fields, and rising land prices encourage farmers to sell, because the land is worth more built on than farmed.
[geo-urban-s4.15.2] Hobby farms and horse paddocks spread across the fringe, which takes land out of commercial farming.
[geo-urban-s4.15.3] Commuter villages gain new estates, and their character and community can be lost as their population changes.
[geo-urban-s4.15.4] Other uses on the fringe: retail parks, business parks, data centres (buildings full of computer servers), landfill sites, quarries and golf courses.
[geo-urban-s4.15.5] Environmental effects: loss of habitats and hedgerows, and flooding as paved surfaces replace soil that would absorb rainwater.
[geo-urban-s4.15.6] Conflicts between farmers, residents and developers over the future of the fringe.
[geo-urban-s4.16.b] Dublin's growth shows these pressures at work in the counties around it.
[geo-urban-s4.16.0] Sprawl into Meath, Kildare and Wicklow. The Greater Dublin Area had about 2.1 million people at the Census of 2022, about 40% of the State.
[geo-urban-s4.16.1] Commuter towns: Navan, Ashbourne, Naas, Newbridge, Maynooth, Bray and Greystones, with one-off housing (single houses built in the countryside) across the commuter belt.
[geo-urban-s4.16.2] Data centres on the city edge used about a fifth of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO), most of them in the Dublin region.
[geo-urban-s4.17.b] Planners have tried to control expansion by protecting land, by building new settlements and by deciding in advance where growth may go.
[geo-urban-s4.17.0] Green belts (protected rings of open land around a city): London's Metropolitan Green Belt was protected from the 1940s and 1950s to stop the outward sprawl of London.
[geo-urban-s4.17.1] New towns (planned towns built to take growth away from a city): Britain's New Towns Act 1946 led to Stevenage, the first new town designated, in 1946, and Milton Keynes, designated in 1967.
[geo-urban-s4.17.2] Paris's five new towns, from the 1965 plan, took population growth away from the city, so that it did not all go into the existing built-up area.
[geo-urban-s4.17.3] Dublin's western new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown were planned after the Wright Report of 1967, so that growth went into planned towns.
[geo-urban-s4.17.4] Compact growth and brownfield targets, set out in the National Planning Framework, aim to build on land within existing settlements. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s4.17.5] Development plans zone land for particular uses, so that the authorities decide where expansion may take place.

CANDIDATES geo-urban-s3: 3. Urban settlement: site, hierarchy, hinterland and central place theory
[geo-urban-s3.0.b] A town's site is the exact ground it is built on. Towns grew where the land gave an advantage for crossing rivers, trading, defence or getting resources, and Irish towns show each type.
[geo-urban-s3.0.0] Bridging point or ford (a shallow place where a river can be crossed): Dublin, on the Liffey, is the lowest bridging point on the river, so traffic had to cross there.
[geo-urban-s3.0.1] Route focus, where several roads meet: Athlone, at a crossing of the River Shannon in the centre of Ireland, gathers traffic from many directions.
[geo-urban-s3.0.2] Island site: Limerick, on King's Island in the Shannon, which gave natural protection from water on all sides.
[geo-urban-s3.0.3] Sheltered harbour or estuary head (the inner end of a river mouth where it widens to the sea): Waterford, on the Suir estuary, and Galway, at the mouth of the River Corrib, both suit ships.
[geo-urban-s3.0.4] Defensive sites on a hill, river loop or island, because attackers find them hard to approach, and dry points above wet ground, which are safe to build on.
[geo-urban-s3.0.5] Resources close to the site, such as water, fish, timber and stone, which settlers need for living and building.
[geo-urban-s3.0.6] A river confluence (where two rivers join) is another site characteristic.
[geo-urban-s3.1.b] Site is the spot itself. Situation is the wider picture: where the town lies in relation to the land and trade around it.
[geo-urban-s3.1.0] Centre of a fertile farming area, so the town can supply goods to farmers and take in their produce.
[geo-urban-s3.1.1] On a coast facing trade partners, which gives access to sea routes.
[geo-urban-s3.1.2] At a gap through the uplands (high ground), where routes crossing the high ground are forced to pass through the town.
[geo-urban-s3.2.b] A function is the job a town does for its own people and for the area around it. Knowing the functions lets you describe what a town is for and, later, how that has changed.
[geo-urban-s3.2.0] Residential (people live there), market and retail (buying and selling), and services such as health, education and administration.
[geo-urban-s3.2.1] Industrial (making goods), port (handling ships and cargo) and transport (road and rail links).
[geo-urban-s3.2.2] Tourism and recreation, ecclesiastical (to do with the Church) and defence or garrison (soldiers and fortifications).
[geo-urban-s3.2.3] Commuter (dormitory) towns, where most residents work elsewhere and the town is mainly a place to sleep.
[geo-urban-s3.2.4] Most towns are multi-functional, which means they carry out several of these functions at once.
[geo-urban-s3.3.b] Settlements are not all alike. A hierarchy ranks them by size and by the number and range of their functions and services.
[geo-urban-s3.3.0] The ranking runs from hamlet to village, small town, large town, city and capital (primate) city.
[geo-urban-s3.3.1] There are few large centres and many small ones, so the hierarchy takes the shape of a pyramid.
[geo-urban-s3.3.2] A place higher in the hierarchy has more functions and services, so it draws people from a wider area.
[geo-urban-s3.4.b] Ireland's settlements fit this ranking from one very large capital down to many small villages. These are Census 2022 figures for cities and suburbs, and for towns.
[geo-urban-s3.4.0] Dublin: about 1.26 million people, far above any other city, so it tops the hierarchy.
[geo-urban-s3.4.1] Regional cities: Cork, about 222,000; Limerick, about 102,000; Galway, about 86,000; Waterford, about 60,000.
[geo-urban-s3.4.2] Largest towns: Drogheda, about 44,000, which is Ireland's largest town; Dundalk, about 43,000; Swords, about 41,000. Swords is a town in Fingal, not a separate city.
[geo-urban-s3.4.3] Many smaller county and market towns, and villages. The State's population is about 5.1 million.
[geo-urban-s3.5.b] In some countries one city dwarfs all the others. A primate city is much larger than the second city in the country and dominates its functions.
[geo-urban-s3.5.0] Dublin is a primate city: about six times the size of Cork, the second city.
[geo-urban-s3.5.1] Causes of Dublin's primacy: its role as capital, its port, its routeway focus (where routes meet), colonial administration (rule by Britain), multinational firms and services.
[geo-urban-s3.5.2] The rank-size rule, in which the nth city is one nth the size of the largest, does not fit Ireland, because Dublin is far bigger than that rule predicts.
[geo-urban-s3.5.3] The National Spatial Strategy and the National Planning Framework (government plans) try to balance Dublin's growth with other centres. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s3.6.b] Goods and services differ in how often people buy them and how far they will travel for them. This explains why some are in every village and others only in cities.
[geo-urban-s3.6.0] Low-order goods are for convenience, such as bread and newspapers. They are found in every village, are bought often and have a low threshold (few customers are needed to keep the shop open).
[geo-urban-s3.6.1] High-order goods are comparison goods (shoppers compare before buying) and specialised services, such as jewellers, department stores, hospitals and universities. They have a high threshold, needing many customers, so they are found only in large centres.
[geo-urban-s3.7.b] No town serves only its own residents. The hinterland, also called the sphere of influence, catchment area or umland, is the surrounding area a town serves and draws people from.
[geo-urban-s3.7.0] The size of a hinterland depends on the town's size and functions, on transport, and on competition from nearby centres.
[geo-urban-s3.8.b] A hinterland has no fixed edge, so geographers find it by collecting evidence of where people come from to use a town.
[geo-urban-s3.8.0] Newspaper circulation areas (where the town's paper is sold).
[geo-urban-s3.8.1] Bus routes and timetables, which show where services run to and from the town.
[geo-urban-s3.8.2] Catchments of schools and hospitals, meaning the areas they draw pupils and patients from.
[geo-urban-s3.8.3] Shop customer surveys and delivery areas.
[geo-urban-s3.8.4] Commuter flows, the movements of people travelling to work in the town.
[geo-urban-s3.8.5] Sports club catchments, such as the areas their members come from.
[geo-urban-s3.9.b] Hinterlands are not fixed. They shift as travel and shopping habits change.
[geo-urban-s3.9.0] Hinterlands overlap, because nearby towns compete for the same customers, and they change over time.
[geo-urban-s3.9.1] Cars, motorways and online shopping widen hinterlands, because people can reach a town from further away.
[geo-urban-s3.9.2] Bypasses (roads taking traffic around a town) and out-of-town centres change them.
[geo-urban-s3.9.3] Example: the large hinterland of Galway city across the West.
[geo-urban-s3.10.b] Central place theory tries to explain why settlements of different sizes are spaced the way they are. Walter Christaller, a German geographer, published it in 1933, based on a study of southern Germany.
[geo-urban-s3.10.0] A central place is a settlement providing goods and services to people in its surrounding area.
[geo-urban-s3.10.1] Threshold is the minimum number of customers needed to keep a service in business. It is a number of people.
[geo-urban-s3.10.2] Range is the maximum distance people will travel to obtain a service. It is a distance.
[geo-urban-s3.10.3] A service locates where its threshold falls within its range, so it has enough customers and they are prepared to travel to it.
[geo-urban-s3.11.b] To make the theory work, Christaller imagined an ideal landscape with no differences between places.
[geo-urban-s3.11.0] A flat, uniform (isotropic, the same in every direction) plain, so nothing blocks movement.
[geo-urban-s3.11.1] An evenly spread population with equal purchasing power, so demand is the same everywhere.
[geo-urban-s3.11.2] Equal ease of transport in all directions.
[geo-urban-s3.11.3] Consumers buy at the nearest centre.
[geo-urban-s3.11.4] No excess profits, so no centre earns more than the others.
[geo-urban-s3.12.b] On such an ideal plain, the theory predicts a regular pattern of centres and market areas.
[geo-urban-s3.12.0] Market areas are hexagonal, because circles overlap or leave gaps and hexagons fit together.
[geo-urban-s3.12.1] A hierarchy of centres forms: many small, closely spaced low-order centres, and fewer, more widely spaced high-order centres.
[geo-urban-s3.12.2] Each higher-order centre also supplies all the lower-order goods, so a city has everything a village has.
[geo-urban-s3.12.3] Nesting (smaller areas fitting inside larger ones): k = 3 is the marketing principle, k = 4 the transport principle and k = 7 the administrative principle.
[geo-urban-s3.13.b] Some real places roughly match the theory, especially where farming land is fairly even and settlements were planned.
[geo-urban-s3.13.0] In parts of the Irish Midlands, market towns serving farming areas are regularly spaced.
[geo-urban-s3.13.1] The Dutch polder (land reclaimed from water) Noordoostpolder, in the Netherlands, was drained in 1942. Emmeloord was planned as the central town, with ten villages around it, including Marknesse, Ens, Kraggenburg and Bant.
[geo-urban-s3.13.2] The villages were spaced so that farmers could reach one by bicycle. This gives a planned two-level hierarchy, an almost perfect example of the theory.
[geo-urban-s3.14.b] Real landscapes are not the ideal plain, so the theory has many weaknesses. Each criticism below breaks one of the assumptions or ignores something real.
[geo-urban-s3.14.0] Relief (the shape of the land), rivers and coasts are not uniform, and population is unevenly spread.
[geo-urban-s3.14.1] Transport routes are linear, not equal in all directions, so some places are easier to reach.
[geo-urban-s3.14.2] People shop on the way to work or on multi-purpose trips, not always at the nearest centre.
[geo-urban-s3.14.3] Car ownership, motorways, out-of-town shopping centres and online shopping change ranges and thresholds.
[geo-urban-s3.14.4] Government decisions, such as county towns and new towns, and history, such as Viking and Norman towns, fix locations.
[geo-urban-s3.14.5] Dublin's primacy breaks the regular hierarchy.
[geo-urban-s3.14.6] The theory was designed for market (service) towns, not for industrial, port or tourist towns.
[geo-urban-s3.15.b] Irish settlements can be set against the theory to see how well it fits.
[geo-urban-s3.15.0] Dublin is at the top, the regional cities come next, then county and market towns, then villages.
[geo-urban-s3.15.1] The Irish hierarchy is broadly hierarchical but distorted by Dublin's primacy and by the coastline.
[geo-urban-s3.16.b] Towns do not keep the same jobs forever. Across Ireland, some functions have faded and others have grown.
[geo-urban-s3.16.0] Defence functions were lost, so walls and garrisons no longer shape towns.
[geo-urban-s3.16.1] Market and fair functions declined, as marts (livestock markets) and supermarkets took over.
[geo-urban-s3.16.2] Industrial functions moved from the inner city to the suburbs or closed, so those areas lost jobs.
[geo-urban-s3.16.3] Services grew, such as offices, tourism, education and health.
[geo-urban-s3.16.4] Many towns near cities became commuter towns, for example Navan and Naas.
[geo-urban-s3.16.5] Village services such as the post office, Garda station, pub, shop and school have closed.
[geo-urban-s3.17.b] Galway is the main case study for changing functions. It began as a walled trading port and grew rich on merchant trade before decline set in.
[geo-urban-s3.17.0] Galway was a Norman town founded by the de Burgo family in the 13th century.
[geo-urban-s3.17.1] It was governed by 14 merchant families, the Tribes of Galway. A royal charter (a grant of rights from the Crown) of 1484 was granted.
[geo-urban-s3.17.2] Galway traded wine with Spain and France, which made it a merchant town and port.
[geo-urban-s3.17.3] The Spanish Arch, built in 1584, is a surviving part of the town walls.
[geo-urban-s3.17.4] Galway surrendered to Cromwellian forces in 1652, and a long decline followed.
[geo-urban-s3.18.b] After its decline, Galway gained new functions in education, high-tech industry, culture and tourism, and became the regional capital of the West.
[geo-urban-s3.18.0] Queen's College Galway was founded in 1845. It is now the University of Galway, renamed in 2022.
[geo-urban-s3.18.1] Digital Equipment Corporation opened a plant in Galway in 1971 and closed its manufacturing there in 1993.
[geo-urban-s3.18.2] Galway is now a centre of the medical devices industry, with multinationals such as Medtronic and Boston Scientific.
[geo-urban-s3.18.3] The National Spatial Strategy of 2002 named Galway a gateway (a centre chosen for growth). The National Planning Framework of 2018 aims for at least 50% growth in Galway by 2040.
[geo-urban-s3.18.4] Galway was European Capital of Culture in 2020, jointly with Rijeka, Croatia.
[geo-urban-s3.18.5] The Galway Arts Festival and the Galway Races at Ballybrit attract visitors, which supports its tourism function.
[geo-urban-s3.18.6] Galway is the regional capital of the West.
[geo-urban-s3.19.b] Limerick shows a different path: a river-island settlement that became a fortified city and then a trading port.
[geo-urban-s3.19.0] Limerick began as a Viking settlement, from about 922.
[geo-urban-s3.19.1] King John's Castle was built in the early 13th century on King's Island, the island site in the Shannon.
[geo-urban-s3.19.2] The Treaty of Limerick was signed in 1691.
[geo-urban-s3.19.3] Limerick developed as a Georgian commercial port (Georgian means the 18th-century style of planning and building). Its Georgian Newtown Pery dates from the late 18th century.
[geo-urban-s3.20.b] Limerick moved from port and milling to multinational manufacturing, then lost some of that industry and has rebuilt through education, culture and new governance.
[geo-urban-s3.20.0] Limerick became an industrial city based on its port and flour milling.
[geo-urban-s3.20.1] Later industry linked to the Shannon area brought multinational investment, including the Shannon Free Zone of 1959 and Shannon Airport nearby.
[geo-urban-s3.20.2] Dell opened in Limerick in 1990. In January 2009 it announced that it would move manufacturing to Łódź, Poland, with the loss of about 1,900 jobs.
[geo-urban-s3.20.3] The University of Limerick was founded in 1972 as the National Institute for Higher Education and became a university in 1989.
[geo-urban-s3.20.4] Limerick was Ireland's first National City of Culture, in 2014.
[geo-urban-s3.20.5] Limerick City and County Councils merged in 2014.
[geo-urban-s3.20.6] In 2024 Limerick elected Ireland's first directly elected mayor.
[geo-urban-s3.21.b] Dublin's functions changed in stages as new roles were added to the old ones.
[geo-urban-s3.21.0] Viking trading port, from 841.
[geo-urban-s3.21.1] Administrative capital.
[geo-urban-s3.21.2] Georgian city, with the Wide Streets Commissioners of 1757 (the body that planned wide new streets).
[geo-urban-s3.21.3] Port and industry.
[geo-urban-s3.21.4] Services and technology in the Docklands: the International Financial Services Centre (1987), and Google at Grand Canal Dock.
[geo-urban-s3.22.b] Smaller towns change too. A market town near a big city can lose its old role and take on a new one as a home for people who work elsewhere.
[geo-urban-s3.22.0] Market functions, such as the mart and shops serving farmers, decline as the town's population changes.
[geo-urban-s3.22.1] Services, housing and shops shift to serve commuters who travel to work in a larger city.
[geo-urban-s3.22.2] Commuter towns of the Greater Dublin Area include Navan, Ashbourne, Naas, Newbridge, Maynooth, Bray and Greystones.
[geo-urban-s3.23.b] When asked why an urban area grew, use this checklist and back each reason with a case study such as Galway or Limerick.
[geo-urban-s3.23.0] Site and situation: a bridging point, island, estuary or harbour.
[geo-urban-s3.23.1] Route focus and port: the town gathers traffic from roads, rivers or the sea.
[geo-urban-s3.23.2] Administrative role: a national capital, a regional capital or a merged council.
[geo-urban-s3.23.3] Industry and multinationals: manufacturing and foreign investment.
[geo-urban-s3.23.4] Services and education: universities and hospitals.
[geo-urban-s3.23.5] Tourism and culture.
[geo-urban-s3.23.6] Government policy: national gateways and growth targets, as in the National Spatial Strategy and National Planning Framework.
[geo-urban-s3.23.7] In-migration: people moving in for work and study.

CANDIDATES geo-aerial-photographs-s1: Aerial photograph interpretation
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.0.b] An aerial photograph is taken from the air, and its type depends on the angle of the camera. A vertical photograph is the first type: the camera points straight down.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.0.0] A vertical photograph is taken with the camera pointing straight down, so it shows only roofs and the tops of features, with no horizon (the line where land meets sky).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.0.1] The scale of a vertical photograph (how much real distance is shrunk onto the picture) is roughly constant across the picture, so it looks like a map and can be used for measuring and mapping.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.0.2] Features are harder to recognise on a vertical photograph because their sides and heights are hidden, so you must identify them from the shape of the roof.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.b] An oblique photograph is taken with the camera tilted at an angle to the ground, like a photograph taken from a hilltop or aircraft window. This gives depth but changes how the picture can be used.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.0] An oblique photograph is taken with the camera at an angle to the ground, so the sides as well as the roofs of buildings are visible.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.1] The scale of an oblique photograph is not constant: foreground features (those nearest the camera) look larger than identical features in the background, and the area covered widens towards the background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.2] Features are easier to recognise on an oblique photograph because you can see their sides, but distance and area cannot be measured directly from it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.3] A low oblique photograph does not show the horizon; a high oblique photograph shows the horizon or skyline.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.2.b] To say which type you have, look for the clues below and quote them as your reason.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.2.0] Oblique clues: the sides of buildings are visible, foreground features look larger than background features, and depth or a horizon is shown.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.2.1] Vertical clues: only roofs are visible, the scale looks even, and the picture looks like a map.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.b] To say where a feature is on an oblique photograph, imagine the picture divided into nine areas and name the area it lies in. This is called relative location, because it gives the position within the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.0] Three strips run across the picture: left, centre and right, so every feature falls in one of three columns.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.1] Three strips run into the picture: foreground at the bottom, nearest the camera; middleground in the centre; background at the top, furthest from the camera.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.2] Accepted notation gives one term from each set, across-term first: left foreground, centre middleground, right background. Both parts must be correct to score.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.3] Use the exam spelling foreground, middleground and background, and use centre, not middle, for the across strip.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.4] Method: draw the grid lightly on the photo in pencil (or fold the picture into thirds in your head), find the feature, and state its zone.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.5] The same method answers true or false statements about what lies in a zone, and matches cropped sections of the photo to zones.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.4.b] The nine-area grid applies to oblique photographs. A vertical photograph looks straight down, so there is no near or far.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.4.0] On a vertical photograph foreground and background have no meaning, because the camera looks straight down and nothing is nearer to it than anything else.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.4.1] Use top, centre or bottom with left, centre or right instead, or compass directions such as north-east and south-west if the orientation is known.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.b] Rivertown is an invented market town on an invented oblique photograph. Every worked example here uses it and is labelled illustrative.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.0] Illustrative: a river flows from the left foreground to the right background, and a stone bridge crosses it in the centre middleground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.1] Illustrative: a church with a spire stands in the left middleground, with a graveyard beside it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.2] Illustrative: a main street and a square lie in the centre middleground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.3] Illustrative: an industrial estate lies in the right foreground, and a housing estate of semi-detached houses lies in the left background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.4] Illustrative: fields with hedgerows lie in the right background, and a GAA pitch lies in the centre background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.5] Illustrative: waste ground lies in the centre foreground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.6] Illustrative true or false: there is waste ground in the centre foreground. This is true, because the waste ground is in the centre foreground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.b] Each feature has a clue that identifies it on the photograph. Use the clue to name the feature, then give its zone.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.0] Church: a spire or tower, a cross-shaped plan, and a graveyard beside it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.1] Graveyard: rows of small headstones.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.2] School: a large building with a playground, pitches and a car park.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.3] Shopping centre: a very large flat roof and an extensive car park, because many shoppers arrive by car.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.4] Factory or warehouse: a large flat or sawtooth roof (a row of angled ridges like saw teeth), with yards and lorries but no gardens.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.5] Apartments: a large multi-storey block with communal parking, meaning parking shared by all residents.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.b] Houses are told apart by how they are joined, and open land by its markings or lack of them.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.0] Housing estate: rows of similar houses with gardens, often arranged in cul-de-sacs (dead-end streets).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.1] Terraced houses share side walls; semi-detached houses share one wall; detached houses stand alone. This is how you tell the three apart.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.2] Car park: marked bays with vehicles in them, so you can tell it from waste ground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.3] Sports pitch: a rectangle with lines and goalposts. Running track: an oval with lanes.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.4] Waste or derelict ground: rough, unused land with no clear use.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.b] Transport routes and water have their own shapes and surfaces, which make them easy to pick out.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.0] Railway: gentle curves, no cars, and crossings of roads by bridges.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.1] River and lake: smooth, dark water with a natural outline.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.2] Bridge: a structure carrying a road or railway across water or another route; its parapet (the low wall along its edge) is a clue.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.3] Harbour, pier or quay: boats moored alongside a structure that projects into the water or lines it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.4] Roundabout: a circular junction with roads leading off it, such as the one at the end of a bridge.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.9.b] A street map, satellite image or street-view images (ground-level photographs) may show the same features, labelled A to E, in a different orientation from the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.9.0] Find two features common to both, because they give you fixed reference points; such as the river, a bridge, the railway or a large church, and turn the second source mentally until they match. This is needed because it may be oriented differently from the photo.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.9.1] Identify each feature by a distinctive clue: a spire, a cruciform (cross-shaped) roof, pitch markings, a large flat roof beside a car park, a bridge parapet, or shopfronts.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.9.2] Give each feature's zone on the photograph in accepted notation.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.b] There are two ways to say where something is. Absolute location uses the map's grid, and relative location uses the photograph itself or neighbouring features.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.0] Absolute location is a grid reference read from the OS (Ordnance Survey) map. Relative location is a zone in the nine-area notation, or a position relative to other features, such as beside the bridge, north of the river.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.1] To give a grid reference for a feature seen on the photograph, find it on the photograph, locate it on the map from its position relative to roads, the river and junctions, then give a four- or six-figure grid reference as taught in Map interpretation.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.2] On a 1:50 000 map individual buildings are often not drawn, so locate the feature from the surrounding road pattern; churches are shown by map symbols.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.3] To name a road or river seen on the photograph, trace it on the photograph, find the same route on the map, and read its number (N, R or M) or its name from the map.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.b] The map can tell you which way the camera was facing when the photograph was taken. This is also what lets you turn left and right on the photo into compass directions.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.0] The direction from a foreground feature to a background feature on the map is the direction the camera was pointing, because the background is the part furthest in front of the camera.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.1] Identify one foreground feature and one background feature on the photograph, find both on the map, and state the direction in an eight-point compass term such as north-east.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.2] Check the camera direction with a third feature, so that one wrongly identified feature does not mislead you.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.3] Illustrative: on the map the bridge (centre middleground) lies due south of the pitch (centre background), and the waste ground (centre foreground) lies south of the bridge.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.4] Illustrative: foreground to background runs south to north, so the camera was pointing north.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.b] The photograph and the map record different things, so each answers questions the other cannot.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.0] The photograph shows what the map does not: individual buildings, land-use type, crops and field use, building height and age, car parks, traffic, temporary features and recent developments.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.1] The map shows what the photograph does not: names, road numbers, height and contours (lines joining places of equal height), the grid, labelled antiquities (ancient monuments), boundaries, and features hidden by trees or lying beyond the frame.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.2] The map covers the whole area at a constant scale; an oblique photograph gives a partial view with changing scale.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.3] The photograph and map were usually made on different dates, so differences between them can reveal change.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.b] Whether you can work out a scale depends on the type of photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.0] An oblique photograph has no single scale, so measure distances on the map instead.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.1] The scale of a vertical photograph is found by comparing a distance on the photograph with the real ground distance, which is read from the map. The map-scale method is taught in Map interpretation.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.2] Illustrative: two road junctions are 5 cm apart on a vertical photograph and 2 cm apart on the 1:50 000 map.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.3] Illustrative: 2 cm on the 1:50 000 map equals 1 km, which is 100 000 cm on the ground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.4] Illustrative: the photograph scale is 5 cm to 100 000 cm, which is 1:20 000.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.b] A sketch map is a drawn copy of the photograph's main features, not a tracing. Following the rules for size, shapes and labels is where the marks are won.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.0] Draw a frame with four ruled sides, the same shape as the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.1] The exam usually asks for half the length and half the breadth: each side is halved, so the sketch has a quarter of the photograph's area.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.2] Measure the photograph and calculate the sketch size before drawing.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.3] Divide the photograph and the frame into the nine-area grid lightly in pencil, so that you can place features accurately.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.4] Show only the features asked for, in approximately the correct position, shape and proportion, because a sketch is a simplified copy and not a tracing.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.b] Different kinds of feature are drawn in different ways, and every one must be labelled.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.0] Draw rivers, roads and railways as lines, with the river as a broad double line; draw areas such as housing, industry, farmland, car parks and lakes as closed outlined shapes; draw single buildings as small blocks or symbols.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.1] Label every feature on or beside it, or in a key (a list explaining the symbols), and give the sketch a title. An unlabelled feature scores nothing.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.2] A feature specified with a position, such as a car park in the centre foreground, must be drawn in that zone.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.3] Common requests to practise: river, coastline, lake, bridge, railway, two connecting streets (two streets drawn meeting at a junction), car park, church, ecclesiastical land use, housing estate, residential land use, industrial building or estate, warehouse, agricultural land, recreational land use, sports ground, waste ground, a historic ruin, and the CBD.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.b] This shows the rules applied to the invented Rivertown photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.0] Illustrative: a photograph 22 cm by 16 cm gives a sketch frame of 11 cm by 8 cm, because each side is halved.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.1] Illustrative request: the river, a bridge, a church, an industrial area in the foreground, and agricultural land in the background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.2] Illustrative: draw the river as a broad double line from the left foreground to the right background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.3] Illustrative: draw the bridge as a short line crossing the river in the centre middleground, and label it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.4] Illustrative: draw the church as a small cross-shaped symbol in the left middleground, and label it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.5] Illustrative: draw the industrial area as a closed outlined shape in the right foreground, because the request places it there, and label it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.6] Illustrative: draw the agricultural land as field outlines with hedgerows in the background of the right side, and label it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.17.b] Land use means what an area of land is used for. Sorting what you see into categories is the first step in describing a town.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.17.0] Land-use categories: residential, commercial or retail, industrial, ecclesiastical, educational, recreational, transport, agricultural, open space, and waste or derelict ground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.17.1] Ecclesiastical land use is religious land use: churches, their grounds and graveyards. Schools are educational land use.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.17.2] Agricultural land around the town shows field patterns with hedgerows, and is either pasture (grass, often with livestock) or tillage (ploughed or cropped fields).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.18.b] Industry needs space and good transport, so it sits in particular places in a town.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.18.0] Industrial land is usually on the edge of town: large flat-roofed buildings in an industrial estate, near main roads, a bypass (a road built round a town to take traffic away from its centre), a railway or a port, with space for parking and lorries.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.18.1] Older industry, such as mills, may be found beside the river or quays.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.b] Density means how many homes fit into an area. It changes from the centre of a town outwards, and the centre itself has a distinctive look.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.0] Older terraced housing near the centre has high density, with small or no gardens, because the houses share walls and are packed together.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.1] Suburban estates of semi-detached and detached houses lie further out, at lower density, with gardens and cul-de-sacs.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.2] Apartments are high density, because many homes are stacked in one multi-storey block.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.3] Residential density usually falls outward from the centre.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.4] The CBD (central business district) is the commercial core of the town, found at the centre where roads converge.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.5] CBD clues: the tallest and most densely packed buildings, no gardens, shops fronting onto main streets, car parks and heavy traffic, and often a square or main street.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.20.b] A function is a purpose a town serves, such as trade, worship or transport. Each function can be seen on a photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.20.0] Functions visible on photographs: residential; commercial or retail; industrial or manufacturing; ecclesiastical or religious; educational; recreational or leisure; medical, such as a hospital.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.20.1] Further functions: transport (bridge, railway station, converging roads, harbour); port or fishing (piers, boats); tourism (hotels, caravan parks, marinas, beaches, historic sites).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.20.2] Further functions: market (a square, a market house); administrative (courthouse, council offices, Garda station, which is often hard to confirm from a photograph alone); defensive (a historic castle or town walls).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.b] A function question wants evidence, a location and a reason. Following the same four steps each time gives a full answer.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.0] Answer formula for describing and explaining a function: name the function, give the photograph evidence, give its location in accepted notation, and explain why the town performs it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.1] Explain the reason using its site (the land it stands on), situation (its position relative to surroundings), size or hinterland (the area around it that it serves).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.2] Give several pieces of evidence for each function, so that one doubtful clue does not weaken the answer.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.3] Illustrative ecclesiastical function: a church with a spire and graveyard in the left middleground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.4] Illustrative industrial function: large flat-roofed units and lorry yards in the right foreground, beside the main road.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.5] Illustrative market or commercial function: a square and a main street of shops in the centre middleground, where roads converge at the bridge.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.b] A town's history is visible in its buildings and layout, so the photograph can show which parts are old and which are new.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.0] Older features show early growth: a castle, tower house or other ruin; an old church or abbey ruin with an old graveyard; town walls or gates.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.1] A market square, fair green or market house shows the town's role as a market centre.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.2] Old stone bridges, quays and warehouses on the river, and mills and weirs (low dams across a river) show the town's use of the river.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.3] A railway line and station show growth in the 19th century.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.4] Modern housing estates and industrial estates on the edge of town show recent growth.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.23.b] Towns grow where a site offers an advantage, and many of those advantages can be seen on the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.23.0] Reasons for the town's location seen on the photograph: a bridging point, a river or coastal site, converging routes, flat land, a harbour, surrounding farmland for a market town, or a defensive site.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.b] The layout of the streets shows whether a town was designed or grew bit by bit, and the pattern can be named.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.0] A planned town has wide, straight streets, a regular grid, a central square or diamond, and uniform terraces.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.1] An unplanned town has narrow, winding, irregular streets, often of medieval origin.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.2] Wide streets on their own do not show that a town is unplanned, because planned towns also have wide, straight streets.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.3] Linear growth: development along a main road, for example houses strung out beside it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.4] Nucleated growth: development clustered around a centre or junction.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.5] Radial pattern: streets radiating from a bridge or square.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.6] Grid pattern: streets laid out at right angles in regular blocks.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.7] Name the pattern on the photograph and use it as evidence for the type of town.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.b] Congestion means roads so full that traffic slows or stops. Several causes can be spotted on a photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.0] Narrow streets in the old core of the town, which cannot carry many vehicles.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.1] On-street parking that narrows the road.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.2] Routes converging on one bridge or junction, so all traffic must squeeze through one point.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.3] Through-traffic passing the town centre, meaning vehicles that are only passing through and do not stop there.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.4] Delivery vehicles, and limited off-street parking.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.5] Schools and shopping centres that generate peak traffic.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.b] Traffic management means the measures used to keep traffic moving and safe. Many can be spotted on the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.0] Measures visible on photographs: roundabouts, traffic lights, pedestrian crossings, one-way streets (all parked cars face one way, with arrows on the road), double yellow lines (no parking), bus lanes and cycle lanes.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.1] Further measures: off-street and multi-storey car parks, pedestrianised streets (closed to vehicles), bypasses, relief roads and ring roads, bollards (short posts that block vehicles) and traffic-calming ramps.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.2] Answer formula for a measure: name it, give its location in accepted notation, and explain how it reduces congestion or improves safety.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.3] Answer formula for a problem: name it, locate it, and explain why it causes congestion.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.4] A suggested solution, such as a bypass to remove through-traffic, a car park on waste ground, a one-way system or pedestrianisation, must be justified with photograph evidence.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.27.b] This applies the formulas to the invented Rivertown photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.27.0] Illustrative problem: all routes converge on the single bridge in the centre middleground, and on-street parking on the main street narrows the road.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.27.1] Illustrative measures: a roundabout at the end of the bridge, and a car park beside the square.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.27.2] Illustrative solution: a relief road crossing the river further downstream, using the open fields.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.b] Land use changes as a town grows. The photograph can be compared with the map to show what a place used to be.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.0] Land-use change is found by comparing the map with the photograph, which are dated differently, or by comparing a location's present use with a listed change.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.1] Listed changes include agricultural to housing, industrial to residential, green space to recreation, open space to residential, farmland to relief road, and green space to waste ground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.2] Method: find the location, identify its present use, then infer its earlier use from the map or from surrounding clues.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.3] Clues to earlier use: new estates on the edge with fields beyond, cleared sites, and new roads cutting across field patterns.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.4] Illustrative: the map shows fields where the photograph shows the left background housing estate, so the change is agricultural to residential.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.5] Second pair, same method: a site shown as industrial on the map and as housing on the photograph is recorded as industrial to residential.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.b] A growing town leaves signs on its edges, which the photograph can show.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.0] New estates on the edge of the town.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.1] Ribbon development (houses built in a line along a road) along approach roads.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.2] Industrial estates and retail parks on the outskirts.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.3] New roundabouts and bypasses.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.4] Sprawl is the spreading of a town into the countryside. There is more on this wider urban issue in the Urban Geography chapter.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.b] A site is chosen or rejected on evidence from the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.0] Choose a site on the photograph for a proposed development, such as a shopping centre, factory, car park or playground, and give three reasons, each with photograph evidence.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.1] For example, reasons to look for: available flat land; access to main roads; nearness to the population served; space for parking; a position away from a flood-prone river bank.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.2] For industry, a site away from housing is suitable.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.3] To show a given site is unsuitable, name the reason it fails these tests and give the photograph evidence for it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.b] The season can be worked out from what plants, animals and people are doing. Reason from the evidence rather than guessing a month.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.0] Deciduous trees (trees that lose their leaves in autumn) in full leaf indicate summer; bare trees indicate winter.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.1] Crops growing or ripening, golden fields and harvested stubble (the short stalks left after harvest) indicate late summer to autumn.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.2] Ploughed fields indicate autumn or spring.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.3] Silage or hay bales in fields indicate summer.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.4] Cattle grazing outdoors indicate the grazing season, roughly spring to autumn, because Irish cattle are usually housed indoors over winter.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.5] People in light clothing, and outdoor activity on beaches or pitches, indicate warm weather.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.6] Give two or three pieces of evidence and reason from them, without guessing exact months.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.7] Illustrative: trees in full leaf and silage bales in the right background fields indicate summer.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.b] Shadows point away from the sun. Use the camera direction from the map to turn a shadow direction into a compass direction.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.0] Ireland is in the northern hemisphere, so the midday sun is due south and midday shadows point north, where they are at their shortest.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.1] The sun rises in the east and sets in the west, roughly. Morning shadows point west; evening shadows point east.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.2] Long shadows mean a low sun, which occurs early or late in the day, or in winter, so long shadows alone do not give the hour.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.3] Other clues: busy streets and full car parks in business hours, school car parks full or empty, rush-hour traffic, and street lights on.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.4] Illustrative: the camera points north, so left is west and right is east. Building shadows fall to the left, which is west, so the sun is in the east: morning.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.5] Illustrative: the shadows are long, so the sun is low and it is early morning.
