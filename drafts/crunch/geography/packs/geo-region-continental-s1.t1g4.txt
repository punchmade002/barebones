[geo-region-continental-s1.0.b] The region studied is the Republic of India. Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nepal are referred to only as neighbours, so keep them out of your answers as parts of the region.
[geo-region-continental-s1.0.0] India is part of South Asia, a subcontinent (a large landmass that is part of a continent but distinct from the rest of it), bounded by the Himalayas to the north, the Arabian Sea to the west and the Bay of Bengal to the east.
[geo-region-continental-s1.0.1] India is the world's seventh-largest country by area, about 3.3 million km².
[geo-region-continental-s1.0.2] The Tropic of Cancer (the line of latitude north of the equator, marking the northern edge of the tropics) crosses central India, so much of the country lies in the tropics.
[geo-region-continental-s1.0.3] Neighbours, which are not part of the region studied: Pakistan to the west, China and Nepal to the north, Bangladesh to the east.
[geo-region-continental-s1.0.4] The republic has 28 states and 8 union territories (areas ruled more directly by the central government); its capital is New Delhi.
[geo-region-continental-s1.0.5] India is treated as a subcontinental region because of its size, the Himalayan barrier that gives it physical unity, and its own cultural and economic identity.
[geo-region-continental-s1.1.b] A sketch map earns marks for an accurate outline and for showing and naming each feature asked for. Names may go on the map itself or in a key, so choose one named example of each feature and learn where it goes.
[geo-region-continental-s1.1.0] Outline: a triangular peninsula (land almost surrounded by sea) tapering to the south, with a north-eastern lobe around Bangladesh.
[geo-region-continental-s1.1.1] Relief (the shape of the land), one option: outline the Himalayas as a band along the northern border, or outline the Deccan Plateau as the peninsula south of the Narmada, between the Western and Eastern Ghats.
[geo-region-continental-s1.1.2] River: the Ganges, drawn from the Himalayas in Uttarakhand south-east across the northern plain, past Varanasi, to its delta (the fan of land where a river splits and meets the sea) on the Bay of Bengal. Show it within India, ending at the delta.
[geo-region-continental-s1.1.3] Urban centres as dots on the correct river or coast: Mumbai on the west coast; Kolkata on the Hooghly near the Bay of Bengal; Delhi on the Yamuna in the north; Chennai on the east coast; Bengaluru on the southern Deccan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.1.4] Agricultural region as an outlined area: Punjab in the north-west (wheat and the Green Revolution, the introduction of high-yielding crops and modern inputs), or Assam (tea, Brahmaputra valley).
[geo-region-continental-s1.1.5] Industrial region as an outlined area: the Chota Nagpur Plateau and Damodar valley, north-east of the Deccan around Jamshedpur, or Mumbai–Pune.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.b] India's climate is dominated by the monsoon, which divides the year into three seasons. Understanding the monsoon explains most of India's farming, rivers and hazards.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.0] India has a tropical monsoon climate. A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind direction, not simply a wet period, so the winds blow from a different direction in summer and in winter.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.1] The word monsoon comes from the Arabic word for season.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.2] Hot season: March to May, with the hottest conditions on the northern plains, because they are far inland and the land heats strongly before the rains.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.3] Wet south-west monsoon: June to September, bringing most of the year's rain, because moist winds from the ocean blow onto the land.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.4] Cool dry season: October to February, when most of India is dry because the winds blow off the land.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.5] The monsoon retreats in October and November, marking the change between the wet and dry seasons.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.b] The summer monsoon is driven by heating of the land and by the movement of global wind belts. Follow the steps in order, because each one causes the next.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.0] 1. Intense heating of the land in early summer creates low pressure (rising warm air) over north-west India and the Tibetan Plateau, which draws in air from the cooler ocean.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.1] 2. The Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ, the belt near the equator where trade winds from both hemispheres meet) shifts north over India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.2] 3. South-east trade winds (steady winds blowing towards the equator) cross the equator and are deflected by the Coriolis force (the effect of the Earth's spin, which bends winds) into south-west winds.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.3] 4. These winds pick up moisture over the Indian Ocean and reach the land as the monsoon.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.4] 5. The subtropical westerly jet stream (a fast, high-altitude river of air) moves north of the Himalayas in early summer, and the monsoon arrives suddenly, a 'burst'.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.5] 6. The jet stream returns south of the Himalayas in winter, when the monsoon withdraws.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.b] The monsoon does not arrive everywhere at once. It sweeps north in two branches, which hit different parts of India first.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.0] The south-west monsoon normally reaches Kerala around 1 June and covers all of India by mid-July.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.1] It brings about three-quarters of India's annual rainfall, falling mainly in June to September, so the other eight months share the remaining quarter.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.2] The Arabian Sea branch strikes the Western Ghats (the mountain range along the west coast) first, dropping heavy rain on the windward side, the side facing the wind.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.3] The Bay of Bengal branch strikes the north-east hills, then is turned west along the Ganges plain by the Himalayas, bringing rain eastwards and along the plain.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.b] Rainfall in India depends on where the monsoon winds meet high ground. Mountains force moist air up, making one side very wet and the other dry.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.0] Relief (orographic) rain: moist air is forced up the windward slopes of the Western Ghats, cools and drops its rain, giving heavy rain on the Konkan and Malabar coasts.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.1] The Deccan Plateau behind the Western Ghats lies in their rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of mountains, where the air has already lost its moisture) and is much drier.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.2] Mawsynram and Cherrapunji, in the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, are among the wettest places on earth, due to relief rain from the Bay of Bengal branch.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.3] Rainfall falls off westwards along the Ganges plain: Kolkata in the east is wetter than Delhi in the north-west, because the winds lose moisture as they travel inland.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.4] The Thar Desert in Rajasthan is the driest region, because the Arabian Sea branch passes parallel to the Aravalli Range and has no barrier to force it upwards.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.b] In winter the wind pattern reverses and India is mostly dry, but there are two regional exceptions. Both matter for farming.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.0] High pressure over Central Asia gives north-east winds that blow off the land, so most of India is dry in winter, because air from land carries little moisture.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.1] The Himalayas shelter India from cold air from Central Asia.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.2] Exception: the Tamil Nadu (Coromandel) coast receives most of its rain from the north-east monsoon, as these winds pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.3] Western disturbances (depressions from the west, areas of low pressure that move in) bring light winter rain to the north-west, which is important for wheat.
[geo-region-continental-s1.7.b] India lies mostly in the tropics, so heat is the norm. Only height above sea level and the season change it much.
[geo-region-continental-s1.7.0] The hot season brings high temperatures to the northern plains, often above 40°C, with hot, dry winds called loo.
[geo-region-continental-s1.7.1] The south stays hot all year.
[geo-region-continental-s1.7.2] The Himalayas are cold, so temperatures fall with altitude (height above sea level).
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.b] The monsoon is not reliable, and both too little and too much rain cause disasters. Climate change is adding to the risk.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.0] A late, weak or broken monsoon (one with long dry breaks) causes drought, because crops and reservoirs depend on its rain.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.1] A heavy monsoon causes floods, because rivers receive more water than their channels can carry.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.2] Tropical cyclones (violent rotating storms that form over warm seas) strike the east coast, mainly in the pre-monsoon and retreating-monsoon periods.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.3] Odisha super cyclone, October 1999: about 10,000 deaths, an example of how deadly a storm on the east coast can be.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.4] Climate change is making the monsoon more erratic (so drought and flood risks both rise) and heatwaves more severe.
[geo-region-continental-s1.9.b] Because most Indians depend on farming, the timing and strength of the monsoon shape the whole economy.
[geo-region-continental-s1.9.0] Its timing decides the farming calendar, so a late monsoon delays sowing, with kharif crops (sown with the monsoon) and rabi crops (sown after it), which are covered under the farming calendar below.
[geo-region-continental-s1.9.1] It refills reservoirs and groundwater, which supply irrigation and drinking water.
[geo-region-continental-s1.9.2] Because farming is a large part of the economy, a poor monsoon affects GDP (the total value of what the country produces).
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.b] Soil type follows the parent material (the rock or sediment the soil forms from) and the climate. Alluvial soil is India's most extensive soil type.
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.0] Alluvial soils (soils made of silt and sand laid down by rivers) are the most extensive and most fertile, found on the Indo-Gangetic Plain and the eastern deltas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.1] They are deposited by Himalayan rivers, which carry silt down from the mountains, forming newer khadar (floodplain) soils and older bhangar (higher terraces).
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.2] Desert soils in the Thar, Rajasthan, are sandy, so they hold little water, low in humus (decayed plant matter that feeds soil) and sometimes saline (salty).
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.3] Desert soils are productive only where canal irrigation reaches them: the Indira Gandhi Canal, Rajasthan, brings water from Punjab's rivers into the Thar Desert.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.b] Black soils form on volcanic rock and are among the best soils of the Deccan for one particular crop.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.0] Black soils (regur, or black cotton soil) are weathered (broken down) from the basalt lava (dark volcanic rock) of the Deccan Traps in north-west Deccan, Maharashtra.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.1] They are clay-rich, so they hold moisture well.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.2] They crack deeply when dry, a feature sometimes called self-ploughing, because the cracks let air and water into the soil.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.3] They are ideal for cotton, the main crop on the Deccan Traps, because cotton needs soil that keeps moisture.
[geo-region-continental-s1.12.b] Red soils form on old, hard rock and are poorer than black soils.
[geo-region-continental-s1.12.0] Red soils are weathered from ancient crystalline rocks (hard rocks made of interlocking crystals, here granite and gneiss) of the south and east Deccan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.12.1] They are red because of iron oxide (rust), and are low in nitrogen and humus.
[geo-region-continental-s1.12.2] They support millets (small-seeded drought-tolerant cereals), groundnuts and pulses (beans, peas and lentils), and give better yields with fertiliser and irrigation.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.b] Laterite forms where heat and heavy rain wash goodness out of the ground. It is a poor soil for farming, though it has a use in building.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.0] Laterite forms under high temperatures and heavy rainfall, which leach out (wash away) silica and nutrients, leaving iron and aluminium oxides behind.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.1] Laterite is found on the Western Ghats and in Kerala.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.2] It is infertile because the leaching removed the nutrients, but with fertiliser it supports tea, coffee and cashew.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.3] It hardens when exposed to air, so it is cut into building bricks.
[geo-region-continental-s1.14.b] On steep Himalayan slopes soil cannot build up, so farming depends on cutting flat steps into the hillside.
[geo-region-continental-s1.14.0] Mountain soils on the Himalayan slopes are thin and immature (not yet developed into deep, layered soil).
[geo-region-continental-s1.14.1] On the terraces (flat steps cut into the slopes, which give level ground on steep land) tea and fruit are grown.
[geo-region-continental-s1.15.b] Farming and the monsoon together damage India's soils in three main ways.
[geo-region-continental-s1.15.0] Erosion (the wearing away of soil): monsoon downpours and deforestation create gullies and ravines, such as the Chambal ravines of Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.15.1] Salinisation (salt build-up in the soil) and waterlogging (soil saturated with water) occur under canal irrigation in Punjab and Haryana.
[geo-region-continental-s1.15.2] Nutrient exhaustion results from intensive Green Revolution farming, which takes crop after crop from the same land.
[geo-region-continental-s1.16.b] Two Indian soils show how rock type and weathering link to soil.
[geo-region-continental-s1.16.0] Basalt weathers to black soil, so black soils are tied to soils to the Deccan Traps, the thick basalt layers covered under the relief notes below.
[geo-region-continental-s1.16.1] Laterite is the product of intense chemical weathering (rock broken down by chemical reactions) in hot, wet conditions.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.b] Relief means the shape and height of the land. India has three main relief divisions, with the Thar Desert, the coastal plains and the islands added to them.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.0] The Himalayas lie to the north, forming India's mountain wall.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.1] The Northern Plains, the Indo-Gangetic Plain, lie in front of the Himalayas, where the rivers spread their silt.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.2] The Peninsular Plateau, the Deccan, forms the southern peninsula.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.3] Coastal plains and islands lie along the coasts.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.b] The Himalayas form India's northern wall. They are the youngest and highest of its landforms.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.0] The Himalayas are young fold mountains (formed when rock layers are crumpled up by collision) formed by the collision of the Indian (Indo-Australian) plate with the Eurasian plate. The collision began about 50 million years ago and continues.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.1] Three parallel ranges run west to east: the Greater Himalaya (Himadri), the Lesser Himalaya and the Shiwaliks.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.2] Kangchenjunga, 8,586 m, on the India (Sikkim)–Nepal border, is India's highest peak and the world's third highest.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.3] Mount Everest (8,849 m) is on the Nepal–China border, not in India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.b] The Himalayas shape India's climate, rivers and settlement. Their effects are both helpful and harmful.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.0] The mountains are a barrier to cold air from Central Asia.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.1] They trigger monsoon rain, as the monsoon is forced upwards by the mountain wall.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.2] Perennial rivers (rivers that flow all year) fed by snow melt rise in the Himalayas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.3] The rivers have hydroelectric potential (power from falling water) because they drop steeply.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.4] The mountains are a barrier to transport and are prone to earthquakes and landslides.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.5] Settlement is sparse because of the steep, cold terrain, which makes farming and building hard.
[geo-region-continental-s1.20.b] South of the Himalayas lies the flattest and most useful land in India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.20.0] The Northern Plains are vast, flat and low.
[geo-region-continental-s1.20.1] They are built from alluvium (silt and sand laid down by rivers) deposited by the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra river systems in a trough (a long hollow) in front of the Himalayas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.20.2] They are the agricultural and population heartland of India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.b] The Deccan is an old, stable upland that covers most of southern India. Part of it is covered by lava from ancient eruptions.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.0] The Deccan Plateau is very old, made of hard crystalline rock, so it forms a stable shield (a block of ancient rock that is no longer folded or moved much).
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.1] It was once part of the ancient Gondwanaland landmass, the supercontinent that later broke apart.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.2] The plateau tilts from west to east, so most of its rivers flow east.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.3] The Chota Nagpur Plateau in the north-east of the plateau is rich in minerals, such as coal and iron ore.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.4] The Deccan Traps are thick layers of basalt from huge fissure eruptions (lava pouring from long cracks) about 66 million years ago, covering much of the north-west Deccan (the source of the black soils).
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.b] Two mountain ranges edge the Deccan Plateau, one on each coast. They differ in height and continuity.
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.0] The Western Ghats, also called the Sahyadri, form a steep, continuous escarpment (a long steep slope) along the west coast.
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.1] The Western Ghats are a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2012) for their biodiversity (the variety of living things).
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.2] Anamudi, 2,695 m, in Kerala, is the highest peak in India south of the Himalayas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.3] The Eastern Ghats are lower and broken into separate hills, because east-flowing rivers cut through them.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.b] Between the plains and the plateau, and in the north-west, are older ranges that also shape drainage and rainfall.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.0] The Vindhya and Satpura ranges separate the plains from the Deccan, forming a divide between north and south.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.1] The Narmada flows west between the Vindhya and Satpura ranges.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.2] The Aravalli Range, Rajasthan, is among the oldest fold mountains on earth.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.3] The Thar Desert lies west of the Aravalli Range, on the dry side where the monsoon winds are not forced upwards.
[geo-region-continental-s1.24.b] Narrow plains run along both coasts, but the west and east differ in width and in what the rivers have built.
[geo-region-continental-s1.24.0] The narrow western plain includes the Konkan coast (Maharashtra, Goa) in the north and the Malabar coast (Kerala) in the south, with backwaters and lagoons (shallow coastal waters partly cut off from the sea).
[geo-region-continental-s1.24.1] The wider eastern plain is the Coromandel coast (Tamil Nadu), with large river deltas, because the Deccan's rivers flow east and deposit silt there.
[geo-region-continental-s1.25.b] Each relief division supports a different way of life, which is why people and industry are spread unevenly.
[geo-region-continental-s1.25.0] The plains support dense population, intensive farming and easy transport, because the land is flat and fertile.
[geo-region-continental-s1.25.1] The plateau is rich in minerals, so it supports industry.
[geo-region-continental-s1.25.2] The mountains support tourism and hydroelectricity, with sparse settlement because the land is steep and cold.
[geo-region-continental-s1.26.b] India's rivers fall into two groups. The Himalayan and peninsular rivers differ in their source, flow and seasonal regime (how their flow changes through the year).
[geo-region-continental-s1.26.0] Himalayan rivers are perennial, because they are fed by snow and glacier melt plus monsoon rain, and are large, with high flows in summer.
[geo-region-continental-s1.26.1] Peninsular rivers are seasonal, because they are rain-fed, so they are low in the dry season, and shorter.
[geo-region-continental-s1.26.2] Peninsular rivers flow in older, harder rock, with fixed valleys, so they cannot easily shift course.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.b] The Ganges is India's most important river. It drains the largest basin in the country and feeds the Northern Plains.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.0] The Ganges rises as the Bhagirathi at Gaumukh, the snout of the Gangotri Glacier in Uttarakhand.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.1] It becomes the Ganga where the Bhagirathi meets the Alaknanda at Devprayag.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.2] It flows past Haridwar, Kanpur, Prayagraj (Allahabad), Varanasi and Patna.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.3] The Yamuna, a tributary (a river that joins a larger one) which flows past Delhi and Agra, joins it at Prayagraj.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.4] The river is about 2,500 km long and its basin (the area drained by it and its tributaries) covers about a quarter of India, the largest basin in India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.5] It enters Bangladesh and joins the Brahmaputra to form the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta, the world's largest delta; the delta is shared with Bangladesh.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.6] The Sundarbans mangrove forest (trees that grow in salty coastal mud), home of the Bengal tiger, lies on the delta across India and Bangladesh.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.7] The Hooghly, a distributary (a branch that leaves the main river), flows through Kolkata, which is why the city is a river port.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.b] The Brahmaputra drains the north-east and carries so much sediment that it floods the same area every year.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.0] The Brahmaputra rises in Tibet as the Yarlung Tsangpo.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.1] It enters India in Arunachal Pradesh and flows west through Assam.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.2] It joins the Ganges in Bangladesh.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.3] It is braided (split into many shifting channels around sandbanks) and carries huge loads of silt, so its channels clog and it floods Assam annually.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.b] The Indus is a Himalayan river that mostly flows outside India, yet its tributaries water India's wheat belt.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.0] The Indus rises in Tibet and flows through Ladakh into Pakistan, mostly through Pakistan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.1] Its five Punjab tributaries are the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.2] Its waters irrigate India's wheat belt in the Punjab, which is why the Indus matters to India even though it flows mostly through Pakistan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.3] The Indus Waters Treaty with Pakistan, 1960, brokered by the World Bank, divides its waters.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.b] Because the Deccan Plateau tilts eastwards, its main rivers cross it to reach the Bay of Bengal.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.0] The Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri flow east to the Bay of Bengal.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.1] The Godavari is the longest river of peninsular India, which is the name for the southern triangle of the country.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.2] They build deltas on the Bay of Bengal coast, which are fertile rice lands because of the silt they deposit.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.3] The Kaveri (Cauvery) water dispute is between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, two states that both want the river's water.
[geo-region-continental-s1.31.b] Two peninsular rivers run against the general eastward tilt and reach the Arabian Sea.
[geo-region-continental-s1.31.0] The Narmada and the Tapi flow west into the Arabian Sea.
[geo-region-continental-s1.31.1] They flow through rift valleys (long valleys formed where land has dropped between faults) and end in estuaries (wide river mouths affected by the tide), not deltas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.b] Rivers on the flat plains both create and destroy. Their floods bring fertile silt but also loss.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.0] Monsoon floods occur on the Ganges plain and in Bihar and Assam, because the rivers swell with monsoon rain and snow melt.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.1] Silt deposition renews soils, but floods destroy crops and homes.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.2] The Kosi, a Ganges tributary in Bihar, is called the 'Sorrow of Bihar' because of its floods and shifting course; it broke its embankment in 2008.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.3] Rivers shift course on the flat plains, because there is little slope to hold them in one channel.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.b] People try to control the rivers so that water is available all year and floods are reduced. This brings benefits and conflict.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.0] Multipurpose dams (dams built for several uses at once) provide irrigation, hydroelectricity and flood control.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.1] Bhakra Dam on the Sutlej, Himachal Pradesh, supplies irrigation water and power to Punjab and Haryana.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.2] Sardar Sarovar Dam on the Narmada, Gujarat, brings irrigation and drinking water to Gujarat but displaced many thousands of villagers.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.3] The displacement led to the Narmada Bachao Andolan ('Save the Narmada') protest movement.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.4] Canal irrigation spreads water beyond the river valleys, so dry land far from a river can be farmed.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.5] Water is disputed between states, as in the Kaveri dispute, and with neighbouring countries, as in the Indus Waters Treaty.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.b] The same river that millions depend on is heavily polluted, and governments have launched clean-up programmes.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.0] The Ganges is polluted by sewage, industrial waste and cremations.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.1] Ritual bathing and cremation take place along the river at Varanasi, which adds to the pollution because ashes and waste enter the water.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.2] The Ganga Action Plan was launched in 1985.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.3] The Namami Gange programme followed in 2014.
[geo-region-continental-s1.35.b] Rivers are central to religion and daily life as well as to the economy.
[geo-region-continental-s1.35.0] The Ganges is sacred to Hindus, so millions bathe in it and visit it.
[geo-region-continental-s1.35.1] Rivers provide water for drinking, irrigation and transport, such as boats on the Ganges.

[geo-region-continental-s1.36.b] Agriculture is the biggest employer in India but contributes far less to the economy than its workforce suggests.
[geo-region-continental-s1.36.0] Agriculture employs the largest share of workers: in Census 2011, about 55% of workers were cultivators or agricultural labourers.
[geo-region-continental-s1.36.1] It produces a much smaller share of GDP, under a fifth in the early 2020s, which shows low productivity (little output per worker).
[geo-region-continental-s1.36.2] Its share of employment is falling, as more people move into industry and services.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.b] Indian farming ranges from tiny family plots to large commercial farms. Each type suits a particular region and climate.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.0] Subsistence farming (growing food mainly for the farmer's own family) on small plots, so little is left to sell.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.1] Intensive wet-rice (paddy) farming in the wet east, the south and the deltas, where rain and flooded fields suit rice.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.2] Commercial irrigated wheat farming (growing for sale, using canal or well water) in the north-west, in Punjab and Haryana.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.3] Plantation farming (large estates growing one crop for sale): tea in Assam and Darjeeling (West Bengal), and in the Nilgiri Hills (Tamil Nadu); coffee in Karnataka.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.4] Cash crops (crops grown to sell): cotton on black soils in Maharashtra and Gujarat, sugar cane, and jute in West Bengal.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.5] Dairy farming, such as the co-operatives described under the White Revolution, and pastoralism (herding animals) in dry areas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.b] The monsoon splits the farming year into two main cropping seasons, kharif and rabi.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.0] Kharif crops are sown with the monsoon in June and July, so they use its rain, and harvested in autumn: rice, cotton, maize and millets.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.1] Rabi crops are sown in October and November and harvested in March and April: wheat, mustard and gram.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.2] Rabi crops depend on irrigation and on the rain from western disturbances, because the main monsoon has ended.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.3] Kharif means monsoon (summer) crops such as rice; rabi means winter crops such as wheat.
[geo-region-continental-s1.39.b] India's climate, soils and rivers give farmers several natural advantages.

CANDIDATES geo-plate-tectonics-s3: 3. Plate boundaries as zones of crustal construction and destruction
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.b] Plates meet in only three ways: they move apart, move together or slide past each other. Each way has a name, and exams use all the synonyms, so learn them together.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.0] Constructive (also called divergent or separating) boundaries: plates move apart and crust is created, because magma fills the gap.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.1] Destructive (also called convergent or colliding) boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, because one plate sinks into the mantle, or modified where two continental plates collide.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.2] Passive (also called conservative or transform) boundaries: plates slide past each other and crust is conserved, meaning it is neither created nor destroyed, so no magma is involved.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.b] At constructive boundaries plates move apart, and new crust is formed in the gap. The process runs in three steps, driven by the mantle (the hot layer beneath the crust).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.0] 1. Convection currents (slow circulating flows in the hot mantle) carry the plates apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.1] 2. Pressure drops as the plates separate, so the mantle partly melts, producing magma (molten rock).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.2] 3. Magma rises into the gap and cools to form new basaltic oceanic crust, basalt being a dark, runny volcanic rock.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.b] Where the plates separate under the ocean, the new crust builds up a long underwater mountain chain called a mid-ocean ridge. This is the main oceanic example of a constructive boundary.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.0] A rift valley (a long valley formed where the crust splits and drops) runs along the crest of the ridge, with frequent shallow earthquakes (earthquakes starting close to the surface) and volcanic activity.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.1] Transform faults (cracks where plates slide past each other) cut across the ridge, so it is offset in sections.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.2] Where a mid-ocean ridge rises above sea level it forms volcanic islands, such as Iceland.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.3] The Mid-Atlantic Ridge also separates the South American and African plates in the South Atlantic.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.4] Iceland is a volcanic island on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the rift is visible on land at Þingvellir (Thingvellir).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.5] The Azores are also on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the East Pacific Rise is another mid-ocean ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.6] Surtsey, a new volcanic island, formed off the south coast of Iceland in an eruption that began in 1963.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.7] Diagram: cross-section of a ridge showing convection currents, rising magma, the rift valley and arrows showing the plates moving apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.b] Constructive boundaries can also start under a continent. Here the land is pulled apart instead of the sea floor, and if the process continues a new ocean can open.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.0] Where a continent starts to split, the crust stretches and a block drops between parallel faults (cracks in the crust) to form a rift valley, with volcanoes nearby.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.1] The East African Rift Valley is where the African plate is splitting apart; Mount Kilimanjaro, Tanzania, is a volcano associated with it.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.2] If spreading continues, the sea floods in and a new ocean begins; the Red Sea, between Africa and the Arabian plate, is at this stage.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.b] These are the landforms and activity to name when asked what is found at a constructive boundary. These features are found where plates pull apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.0] Features of constructive boundaries, where plates pull apart, are the mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, volcanic islands, fissure eruptions (eruptions from long cracks rather than one vent) of runny basalt lava, and shallow earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.b] Where two plates converge, one plate is forced down into the mantle. Which one sinks depends on the density of the crust.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.0] Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so where the two meet the oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.1] This process is subduction, and the zone where the plate sinks is the subduction zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.2] Crust is destroyed as the subducting plate melts in the hot mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.3] The place where the two plates meet at the surface is an ocean trench, a deep, narrow valley in the sea floor.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.b] When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks. This one collision produces a trench, fold mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes, which is why such coasts are so active.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.0] An ocean trench forms where the oceanic plate bends down, because the plate is forced into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.1] Sediments (loose material laid down on the sea floor) are scraped off the subducting plate and crumpled against the continent into fold mountains.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.2] Melting of the sinking plate produces magma that rises to form a chain of volcanoes on the continent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.3] Earthquakes occur along the sinking plate because it grinds past the rock around it, shallow near the trench and deeper inland; this inclined zone is the Benioff zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.4] The Nazca plate subducts beneath the South American plate: the Peru-Chile Trench lies offshore and the Andes, with many volcanoes, rise on land.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.5] The Juan de Fuca plate subducts beneath the North American plate, producing the Cascade Range volcanoes, including Mount St Helens, Washington, USA.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.6] Diagram: cross-section with ocean trench, subducting plate, melting zone, rising magma, volcano, fold mountains and earthquake foci (the points underground where earthquakes start).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.b] When two oceanic plates meet, neither is light enough to resist sinking, so one is forced under the other. The result is a trench and a curved line of volcanic islands rather than a mountain chain on a continent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.0] The older, denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the other, because denser crust sinks more easily.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.1] An ocean trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands, called an island arc, form, and earthquakes are frequent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.2] The Mariana Trench, about 11 km deep, is the deepest place on earth; it lies where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the Philippine plate, and the Mariana Islands form an island arc.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.3] The Aleutian Islands, Alaska, are an island arc where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the North American plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.4] Japan lies where the Pacific, Philippine, Eurasian and North American plates meet.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.b] Continental crust is too light to sink into the mantle, so when two continents meet there is no subduction. The crust has nowhere to go but up.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.0] Both plates are too light to subduct, so the crust between them, including sediments from the ocean that once separated them, is crushed, folded and pushed up into high fold mountains.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.1] Earthquakes are common, but there is little or no volcanic activity, because no plate sinks and melts to make magma.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.2] Crust is modified, thickened and folded, rather than destroyed, because it is too light to sink.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.3] The Indo-Australian plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate, forming the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau; the collision began about 50 million years ago and continues.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.4] Mount Everest, 8,849 m, is the highest mountain on earth; for example, marine fossils in limestone near its summit show the rocks were once on the floor of the Tethys Sea.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.5] The Alps formed where the African plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.b] Destructive boundaries shape the land and also cause hazards such as powerful earthquakes, tsunamis and explosive volcanic eruptions. For a discussion of their impacts, give landforms and hazards, each with a named example.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.0] Landforms: ocean trenches, fold mountains, volcanic mountain chains and island arcs, such as the Andes and the Mariana Trench.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.1] Earthquakes: Nepal, 25 April 2015, magnitude 7.8 (a measure of earthquake size), killed about 9,000 people on the Himalayan collision boundary.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.2] Undersea earthquakes and tsunamis (huge sea waves caused by earthquakes under the sea): the Tōhoku, Japan, earthquake of 11 March 2011 had magnitude 9.0; its tsunami killed about 18,000 people (dead and missing) and caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.3] The Indian Ocean earthquake of 26 December 2004 had magnitude 9.1 off Sumatra, Indonesia, where the Indo-Australian plate subducts; its tsunami killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.4] Volcanic eruptions: Mount St Helens erupted on 18 May 1980, killing 57 people.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.b] At passive (transform) boundaries two plates slide past each other horizontally along a transform fault. Because nothing is pulled apart or pushed under, no crust is made or lost.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.0] Friction locks the plates until stress builds up, and the stress is released suddenly as an earthquake.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.1] No magma is produced, so there is no volcanic activity, and no crust is created or destroyed.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.2] The plates may move in the same direction at different speeds, or in opposite directions, so both count as sliding past.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.3] The San Andreas Fault, California, USA, is a transform boundary where the Pacific plate moves north-west relative to the North American plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.4] The San Francisco earthquake of 18 April 1906 had magnitude about 7.8 and was followed by great fires; the Loma Prieta earthquake of 1989, magnitude 6.9, struck near San Francisco.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.5] The North and East Anatolian Faults in Turkey are transform faults; the earthquake of 6 February 2023 on the East Anatolian Fault, magnitude 7.8, killed over 50,000 people in Turkey and Syria.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.6] Diagram: plan view (looking down from above) of two plates with arrows in opposite directions along a fault line.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.b] Putting the boundary types together shows that the ocean floor is constantly being made and used up. This is the tectonic cycle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.0] Crust is created at ridges, carried across the ocean floor, and destroyed at subduction zones, so the ocean floor is continually recycled.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.1] Continental crust is too light to be subducted, which is why it survives for so long.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.2] Oceans open and close over time: the Iapetus closed and the Atlantic opened.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.b] This summary pairs each boundary type with its plate movement and what happens to the crust. Exams ask for an example of each, so the examples are added here.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.0] Constructive boundaries: plates move apart and crust is created. Examples: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the East African Rift Valley.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.1] Destructive boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, or modified where two continental plates collide. Examples: the Andes, the Mariana Trench and the Himalayas.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.2] Transform (passive) boundaries: plates slide past and crust is conserved. Example: the San Andreas Fault.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.b] Earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains are concentrated in narrow belts along plate boundaries, with very few in plate interiors. This is how plate tectonics explains where they occur.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.0] The Pacific Ring of Fire is the belt of destructive and some transform boundaries around the edge of the Pacific plate and its neighbours.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.1] The Ring of Fire, because it follows destructive boundaries, contains about 75% of the world's active volcanoes and about 90% of its earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.2] Ridges, subduction zones, collision zones and transform faults are the boundaries where these belts form, because plates interact there.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.3] Fold mountains are found only at convergent boundaries, as long chains along the plate edge; the Andes, Himalayas and Alps are examples.

CANDIDATES geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3: 3. Landforms from volcanic and plutonic structures
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.b] Magma (molten rock) that rises into the crust cools in one of two ways, and the way it cools decides what landform results. Both ways are endogenic (internal) processes, driven from inside the Earth.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.0] Extrusive (volcanic) activity: magma reaches the surface and cools quickly as lava, ash and other material, building landforms on the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.1] Intrusive (plutonic) activity: magma cools slowly underground, forming bodies of rock that become landforms only when the rock above them is eroded away.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.2] Plutonic features are not formed by exogenic processes (surface processes such as weathering and erosion); erosion only reveals them, because the magma had already cooled into rock underground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.b] Rock is rarely one solid block. Cracks called joints run through it, and they decide where weathering and erosion do their work.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.0] A joint is a crack in rock along which no movement has taken place, which is what separates it from a fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.1] In igneous rock (rock formed from cooled magma), joints form by contraction as the rock cools.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.2] In granite, joints also form by expansion when the overlying rock is removed and pressure on the granite is released.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.3] Weathering and erosion work along joints, so the joint pattern shapes the landform.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.b] Not all eruptions come from a single volcano. When lava escapes along a long crack, it can build a wide, high, flat landform instead of a cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.0] 1. Basic lava (runny lava that flows easily) pours out of long cracks in the crust, called fissures, rather than from a single vent (an opening in the crust).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.1] 2. Each lava flow spreads widely before it cools, because the lava is runny.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.2] 3. Repeated flows build up, one on top of another, so the plateau grows higher with each eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.3] 4. The result is a high, flat-topped plateau with steep edges.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.4] Example: the Antrim plateau, Co. Antrim.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.5] Diagram: cross-section of stacked lava flows forming a flat-topped plateau with steep edges.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.b] The Antrim plateau is an Irish extrusive example, and the granite Mournes are its intrusive neighbour from the same period. Keep the two apart.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.0] The Antrim plateau is made of basalt (a dark volcanic rock) in layers from fissure eruptions about 60 million years ago, linked to the opening of the North Atlantic.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.1] Along the Antrim coast, dark basalt lies on top of white chalk, which the basalt protected from erosion.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.2] The Mourne Mountains, Co. Down, are granite intrusions (granite is a plutonic rock that cooled underground) of the same period as the Antrim basalts, much younger than the Leinster granite.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.3] Slieve Donard is the highest peak of the Mournes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.b] Basalt columns form in a lava flow as it cools and contracts (shrinks). They are a joint pattern, not a volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.0] Columnar jointing forms when a thick lava flow cools slowly and evenly, so the whole flow shrinks in a regular pattern.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.1] As the flow contracts, cracks form at about 120° to each other, which is why the pattern is regular.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.2] The cracks produce columns at right angles to the cooling surface, mainly six-sided, because cracks meeting at about 120° enclose six-sided shapes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.3] Sea erosion exposes the columns on the north Antrim coast, where about 40,000 interlocking basalt columns, most of them six-sided, form the Giant's Causeway, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.4] Fingal's Cave, Isle of Staffa, Scotland, has columns from the same volcanic period.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.5] The Giant's Causeway is a cooled lava flow, not a volcano, and its columns did not form from growing crystals.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.6] Diagram: labelled sketch of hexagonal columns standing at right angles to the cooling surface of the flow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.b] A shield volcano is built by runny lava, which is why it is broad and low rather than tall and steep. Viscosity is how thick a liquid is: low viscosity means it flows easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.0] 1. Low-viscosity (runny) basic lava flows a long way before it cools.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.1] 2. Mostly effusive eruptions (lava pours out steadily rather than exploding) build a broad cone with gentle slopes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.2] 3. Successive flows spread out from the central vent, so the volcano is wide rather than steep.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.3] Example: Mauna Loa and Kilauea, Hawaii.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.4] Diagram: cross-section of a broad, low cone with gentle slopes and a summit crater (the bowl-shaped hollow at the top).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.b] Hawaii is the setting to use for shield volcanoes, with its own record-sized examples and two named lava types.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.0] Hawaii's shield volcanoes sit over a hot spot (a hot area under the crust where magma rises), which is why volcanoes form there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.1] Mauna Loa is the largest active volcano on Earth by volume, for example, which shows how far runny shield-volcano lava can spread.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.2] Mauna Kea is more than 10,000 m high when measured from its base on the sea floor, so most of it lies under the sea.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.3] Pahoehoe lava has a smooth, ropy surface; aa lava has a rough, blocky surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.b] A composite volcano is the opposite of a shield. Its magma is too thick to flow far, so it piles up into a steep cone built of alternating layers.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.0] 1. Viscous (thick, sticky) acidic or intermediate magma is too thick to flow far.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.1] 2. Explosive eruptions alternate with lava flows, so the cone is built of layers of ash and lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.2] 3. Over time these layers build a steep cone with a crater at the summit.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.b] The smallest volcanic cone is built from loose fragments blown out in short bursts rather than from flowing lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.0] A cinder cone is a small, steep cone built mainly of pyroclastic fragments (rock blown out by explosions) during short, explosive eruptions.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.1] Example: Parícutin, Mexico, which began in a farmer's cornfield in 1943.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.2] Diagram: cross-section of a small, steep cone of loose fragments around a central crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.b] Some eruptions are so large that they destroy the volcano's own summit. The result is a huge basin called a caldera.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.0] A caldera is a large basin formed when the summit of a volcano collapses into the magma chamber (the underground reservoir of magma) emptied by a huge eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.1] A caldera is much larger than a crater and forms by collapse, not by explosion alone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.2] A caldera may fill with water to form a crater lake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.3] Crater Lake, Oregon, USA: Mount Mazama collapsed about 7,700 years ago, and the caldera now holds the lake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.4] Kilauea, Hawaii, has a summit caldera.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.5] Santorini, Greece, and Krakatoa, Indonesia, are further calderas; Anak Krakatau has grown inside the Krakatoa caldera.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.b] Erosion can leave a volcano's hardened core behind after the rest of the cone has gone, because the core is harder than the cone around it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.0] 1. Magma solidifies in the vent of a volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.1] 2. The softer cone around the vent is eroded away.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.2] 3. The resistant plug (rock that resists erosion well) is left standing as a steep-sided hill.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.3] Examples: Slemish, Co. Antrim; Castle Rock, Edinburgh, Scotland, a volcanic plug whose crag-and-tail shape was later shaped by ice.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.4] Diagram: cross-section of a plug standing above the eroded cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.b] Volcanic heat underground also produces smaller surface features, where hot water, steam and gas escape without any lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.0] A geyser erupts hot water and steam from the ground at intervals.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.1] A hot spring is hot water that rises to the surface and flows out without erupting, so it differs from a geyser.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.2] A fumarole is a vent that emits gases and steam.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.3] Example: Old Faithful, Yellowstone, USA, is a geyser.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.b] Iceland is the setting to use for fissure eruptions, geysers and new islands, and it shows how destructive a fissure eruption can be.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.0] The Laki fissure eruption of 1783 poured out huge lava flows; its gases caused crop failure and famine in Iceland, so the harm came from more than the lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.1] Geysir and Strokkur are geysers in Iceland, for example.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.2] Surtsey, a new island formed off southern Iceland from 1963.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.3] Hekla and Eyjafjallajökull are also Icelandic volcanoes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.13.b] The biggest plutonic landform is the batholith, a mass of rock that cooled deep underground and only becomes a landform once the rock above it has been stripped away.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.13.0] A batholith is a huge dome-shaped mass of magma that cooled slowly deep underground into granite.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.13.1] Heat from the magma altered the surrounding rock into a metamorphic aureole (metamorphic means changed by heat), a ring of altered rock around the batholith, for example mica schist.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.13.2] When the overlying rock is eroded, the granite is exposed and forms upland areas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.b] The Leinster batholith is Ireland's intrusive case study. It is the largest granite mass in Ireland and forms the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.0] The granite intruded about 400 million years ago, during the Caledonian mountain-building period.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.1] It is the largest granite mass in Ireland and runs from south Co. Dublin south-west through Wicklow and Carlow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.2] The overlying slate was eroded, exposing the granite as the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.3] A band of mica schist, the metamorphic aureole, surrounds the granite.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.4] The granite uplands have rounded summits, such as Lugnaquilla below.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.5] Lugnaquilla, at 925 m, is the highest mountain in Wicklow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.15.b] Magma that is forced between layers of rock, rather than rising to the surface, can bend those layers up or down. Which way the layers bend gives the two features their names.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.15.0] A laccolith forms when magma is forced between layers of rock, arching the layers above into a dome, so a laccolith bulges up.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.15.1] Henry Mountains, Utah, USA, are laccoliths.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.15.2] A lopolith is a saucer-shaped intrusion that sags downwards in the centre, so a lopolith sags down.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.b] Two sheet-shaped intrusions differ only in direction. A sill runs along the rock layers, while a dyke cuts across them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.0] A sill is a horizontal sheet of magma injected between the layers of rock (along a bedding plane, the boundary between layers), parallel to them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.1] When exposed, a resistant sill forms cliffs, ledges and waterfalls, because it resists erosion better than the layers around it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.2] Fair Head, Co. Antrim, is a dolerite sill with columnar jointing.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.3] Great Whin Sill, northern England, carries part of Hadrian's Wall along its crest.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.4] A dyke is a vertical or near-vertical sheet of magma cutting across the layers.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.5] A dyke harder than the surrounding rock stands up as a wall-like ridge because the softer rock erodes around it; a softer dyke is eroded into a trench.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.6] The Isle of Arran, Scotland, has a dyke swarm (a group of many dykes).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.17.b] Plutonic features end up as high ground because of differential erosion, meaning different rocks wear away at different speeds.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.17.0] Plutonic rocks such as granite are usually more resistant than the rocks around them, so they wear away more slowly.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.17.1] Once the surrounding cover is removed, the plutonic rock stands as upland while the weaker rock is lowered, because the weaker rock erodes faster.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.17.2] Diagram: one block of crust showing a batholith, laccolith, lopolith, sill, dyke and volcanic plug, each labelled.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.18.b] Devon and Cornwall in south-west England are the international example of granite uplands, parts of one exposed batholith.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.18.0] Dartmoor and Bodmin Moor are granite uplands, parts of one large batholith exposed by erosion, so they are upland for the same reason as the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.18.1] Haytor, Dartmoor, is a tor, a granite outcrop rising above the moor.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.18.2] St Austell, Cornwall, is a centre of china clay (kaolin) quarried from decomposed granite.
