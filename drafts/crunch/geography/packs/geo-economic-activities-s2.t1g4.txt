TARGET geo-economic-activities-s2 (part 1 of 2): The mobility of modern economic activities and factors in their location
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.b] Firms try to locate where total transport costs are lowest. This least-cost principle of industrial location was set out by Alfred Weber in 1909, and it depends on whether processing makes the materials lighter or heavier.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.0] Bulk-reducing industries are those where the raw material loses weight in processing, so the plant locates near the raw material because carrying the heavy input a long way would waste money. Examples: alumina refining, dairy processing, fish processing, mining.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.1] Bulk-gaining industries are those where the product gains weight or bulk in production, so the plant locates near the market because the finished product is the costly thing to move. Examples: brewing, soft drinks bottling, bakeries.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.2] Test for an exam location question: if the inputs lose weight in processing, the plant is pulled towards the raw material; if the finished product is heavier or bulkier, it is pulled towards the market.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.b] Location questions use a set of working terms. Knowing them makes the location factors easier to follow.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.0] Footloose industry: one that is not tied to a raw material or a market, so it is free to choose its location. The factors below explain where footloose activities choose to locate.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.1] Branch plant: a factory or office owned by a company headquartered elsewhere, usually carrying out routine production.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.2] Headquarters (HQ): the head office where a company's main decisions are made. Research and development (R&D): the work of designing new products and processes.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.3] Greenfield site: undeveloped land on which a new plant is built. Brownfield site: land already built on, often a former industrial site that may need clearing before reuse.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.4] Industrial estate or business park: a serviced area of land set aside for firms, with roads and utilities already in place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.5] Cluster, or agglomeration: firms in the same or related industries grouped together, sharing skills, suppliers and research.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.6] Backward linkages are links to a firm's suppliers; forward linkages are links to its customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.7] Infrastructure: the transport, energy, water, communications and waste systems that firms depend on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.b] A location factor is any condition that makes one place more suitable than another for an activity. The first three are the inputs a firm needs to make its product.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.0] Raw materials: weight-losing raw materials pull processing towards their source or towards a port where they arrive, which keeps the cost of moving heavy loads down.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.1] Energy: industry once clustered on coalfields, where the fuel was. National electricity grids freed most industry from this, but energy-hungry activities such as alumina refining and data centres still need large, reliable and affordable power.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.2] Labour: the quantity, skills and education, cost, productivity and flexibility of the workforce.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.3] High-technology and service firms seek graduates because their work needs advanced skills; standardised assembly (repeating the same simple steps) seeks low wages.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.4] Irish examples for labour: the Intel plant at Leixlip and the Galway cluster, both described below.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.b] Once a product is made it has to reach its customers, and firms need to receive inputs. Markets, transport links and communications decide how cheaply and quickly that happens.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.0] Markets: the size and wealth of the market, and access to the EU single market (the EU area with no trade barriers between members). Bulk-gaining and perishable goods locate near the market; services locate near their customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.1] Ports: deep water lets bulk carriers (large ships carrying loose cargo) berth. Dublin Port is Ireland's busiest port. Port of Cork and Shannon Foynes Port are also major ports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.2] Rosslare Europort: direct ferry routes to France grew after Brexit (2021) to avoid the UK land bridge, the route across Britain to continental Europe.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.3] Whitegate, Cork Harbour: Ireland's only oil refinery, opened 1959.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.4] Airports: air freight suits light, high-value, time-sensitive goods such as pharmaceuticals, medical devices and microchips, because the high price of the goods can bear the high cost of air transport.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.5] Dublin Airport handled over 30 million passengers in 2023; its North Runway opened in 2022.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.6] Freight modes: road offers door-to-door flexibility over short and medium distances; rail carries heavy loads on fixed routes; sea is the cheapest way to move bulk over long distances; air is the fastest but the most expensive.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.7] Motorways, rail, broadband and fibre (fibre-optic cable) connect firms to markets and suppliers; for services, broadband and fibre matter most. One environmental challenge of air transport is its CO2 emissions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.b] A firm also needs money and a welcoming policy environment. Governments, and the EU above them, can make a location more or less attractive through money, tax and rules.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.0] Capital: investment money comes from banks, shareholders, government grants and venture capital (money from investors who back new, risky businesses).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.1] IDA Ireland was set up in 1949 and became an independent agency in 1969. It attracts firms by offering grants, serviced sites (land with utilities already supplied) and advance factories (buildings built ready for a firm to move into).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.2] Corporation tax (the tax on company profits) was 12.5% from 2003. Very large groups pay 15% from 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.3] Enterprise Ireland (1998) supports Irish-owned firms. Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980) supports enterprise in Gaeltacht areas.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.4] Regional policy steers firms towards less-developed areas through grants and incentives.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.5] Planning permission (official approval from the local authority) is needed before a site can be built on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.6] EU state-aid rules limit the subsidies governments may give firms, while EU funds support regional development.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.7] Trade agreements reduce the barriers that firms face when selling across borders, which makes a location inside the agreement more attractive.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.b] After a firm picks a general area it must find a suitable piece of land, and workers and the plant both need services. The site and its services decide whether the plant can actually be built and run.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.0] Site: flat, well-drained land, large enough for expansion, at a cheap price, so that building is easy and the plant can grow later.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.1] Site: a water supply, and facilities for effluent (liquid waste) and waste disposal.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.2] Site: hazardous industry needs distance from homes, so that accidents or pollution do not harm residents.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.3] Services: water, electricity, gas, broadband, waste treatment, housing and schools. Workers must be able to live near the plant, which is why housing and schools count as location factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.b] Some location factors are about people and history rather than land and transport. Firms gather together, stay put out of habit, or follow the pull of a pleasant place to live.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.0] Agglomeration: firms in the same industry gather to share skilled workers, suppliers, research and universities, so each firm gains from the others being nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.1] Cork Harbour (pharmaceuticals) and Galway (medical technology) grew into clusters in this way.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.2] Personal and historical factors: the choices of founders and the history of a site can decide a location, whatever the economics of the place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.3] Industrial inertia: an industry stays where it is after the original reason for locating there has gone.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.4] Quality of life: attractive places to live help firms recruit skilled staff, because graduates can choose where to work.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.b] Local opposition and environmental regulation can stop or delay a project. Corrib gas shows how long a delay can be.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.0] Corrib gas was discovered in 1996. Its terminal (the plant that receives and processes the gas) is at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.1] The Shell to Sea campaign opposed the project, and the Rossport Five were jailed in 2005.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.2] Gas first flowed in December 2015, after a long delay caused by local opposition and changes to planning.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.b] The weight of each factor has changed over time as transport, energy supply and technology have changed. The pattern runs from resources, to customers, to skills and quality of life.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.0] In the 19th century, raw materials and power were the main location factors, so industry sat on or near coalfields and sources of materials.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.1] In the 20th century, markets and labour became the main factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.2] Today, footloose activities (those free to choose a location) chase skills, tax, connectivity and quality of life.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.b] A bulk-reducing heavy industry located on a deep-water estuary, where imported raw material arrives by sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.0] Aughinish Alumina opened in 1983 on Aughinish Island near Askeaton, Co. Limerick, on the south shore of the Shannon Estuary. It is Europe's largest alumina refinery.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.1] Bulk-reducing: about two to three tonnes of bauxite (the ore, or raw rock, that alumina is made from) make one tonne of alumina, so the refinery sits at the raw material's port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.2] Bauxite is imported by sea, mainly from Guinea and Brazil. The alumina (aluminium oxide) is shipped to aluminium smelters elsewhere; the refinery does not make aluminium.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.3] Deep, sheltered water in the Shannon Estuary, Ireland's deepest natural sheltered water, allows large bulk carriers to berth.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.4] The site is a flat island location away from towns, which suits a large heavy industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.5] Government support for heavy industry is a further factor in its siting.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.6] Problems: bauxite residue, known as red mud, is stored in large disposal areas on site.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.7] The company has been owned by the Russian company Rusal since 2007.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.b] The same estuary that serves Aughinish has also been used for energy, because deep water lets fuel arrive by ship.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.0] Moneypoint power station, Co. Clare, is on the Shannon Estuary and imported coal by sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.1] Coal burning at Moneypoint ended in 2025, so the estuary's energy role has changed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.b] Intel is footloose in the sense that its products are not tied to a raw material or a market, so it chose Leixlip from many possible sites. Its choice shows which factors matter to a high-technology firm.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.0] Intel's Leixlip plant opened in 1989. It is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, worth billions of euro.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.1] Skilled graduates are available, so with Maynooth University nearby, which meets the plant's need for a skilled workforce.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.2] IDA support and low corporation tax attracted the investment. There is more on this in Capital, government and EU policy.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.3] Access to the EU single market from an English-speaking base.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.4] The plant lies beside the M4 motorway and close to Dublin Airport, so goods and staff can move easily.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.5] It occupies a large greenfield site (undeveloped land) with a supply of electricity and water.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.6] Footloose does not mean likely to leave: the large fixed investment makes relocation unlikely.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.7] Global job cuts announced in 2024 and 2025 affected Leixlip, showing that even a large plant depends on parent-company decisions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.b] Cork Harbour combines deep water, industrial land and skilled graduates, so pharmaceutical firms have gathered there. Their closeness to each other reinforces the pattern.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.0] Pfizer, Janssen (Johnson & Johnson) and Novartis are at Ringaskiddy; Eli Lilly is at Kinsale, Co. Cork.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.1] The Port of Cork has deep-water berths at Ringaskiddy, which suits the import and export of pharmaceutical goods.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.2] Large flat sites are zoned for industry (set aside for it in the local plan), with water supply and waste facilities.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.3] Skilled labour comes from University College Cork (UCC) and Munster Technological University (MTU). MTU was formed in 2021 from Cork IT and IT Tralee.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.4] IDA support, tax and clustering reinforce the pattern.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.5] Pharmaceuticals and chemicals are Ireland's largest goods exports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.6] Older heavy industry in the same harbour: Irish Steel at Haulbowline closed in 2001.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.b] Galway shows how the closure of one firm can feed the growth of another industry. Skilled people left behind became the base for a cluster making medical devices.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.0] Boston Scientific opened in Galway in 1994. Medtronic, Parkmore, Galway followed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.1] The cluster draws on skilled engineers left by the closure of DEC's plant. The Dell and DEC block below gives the date.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.2] University of Galway research (renamed in 2022) supports the cluster, and a network of suppliers has grown around the firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.3] There is more on this in Irish Regions — Core and Peripheral, in the peripheral Irish region subsection.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.b] Food and drink show both sides of Weber's idea. Processing that makes the product lighter stays at the source, while brewing, which makes the product heavier, stays near its customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.0] Kerry Group grew from the North Kerry Milk Products co-operative at Listowel, founded in 1972. Its headquarters are in Tralee, Co. Kerry.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.1] Dairy processing is bulk-reducing: plants at Listowel and Tralee sit near the supply of milk.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.2] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland's largest fishing port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.3] Fish processing plants at Killybegs handle pelagic fish (fish that live near the surface) such as mackerel and herring, a bulk-reducing process that locates at the port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.4] Guinness at St James's Gate, Dublin, was founded in 1759 by Arthur Guinness on a 9,000-year lease.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.5] Brewing is bulk-gaining because water is added to the product, so the brewery sits near its market.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.6] Industrial inertia: Guinness still brews at St James's Gate today, long after the original reasons for the site have gone.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.b] Data centres (buildings full of computers that store and process online data) are a modern footloose activity, but they use huge amounts of electricity. For them the power grid has become a limit on location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.0] Data centres used about 21% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.1] EirGrid (the electricity grid operator) stated in 2022 that it would not connect new data centres in the Dublin region until about 2028, so the electricity grid now limits their location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.2] Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, Google and Meta operate data centres in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.3] Why Ireland: a cool, mild climate reduces cooling costs; fibre links to the USA and Europe; existing clusters of technology firms; tax.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.b] Modern economic activities can move more easily than older ones. Five changes have loosened the link between an activity and a fixed place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.0] Lower transport costs, through containerisation (goods packed in standard boxes that move easily between ship, rail and lorry) and air freight, let goods move cheaply over long distances.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.1] Instant communications, through the internet, fibre and video calls, let firms manage plants at a distance.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.2] Standardised production can be done by low-skilled workers, so the work can go anywhere with a cheap labour supply.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.3] The removal of trade barriers, through the EU single market and the World Trade Organization, makes it easier to sell across borders from any member location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.4] Multinationals (firms operating in many countries) can compare countries and move capital quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.b] A country that hosts a branch plant gains quickly, but its gains are not secure. The same mobility that brings the plant can take it away.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.0] Investment brings rapid job creation, but jobs can leave just as quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.1] Branch plants are vulnerable because the decisions are made at a distant headquarters, not in the host country.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.2] Countries compete for investment by offering tax and grants.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.b] A company opens a branch plant in another country when the move helps its own plans. These are the strategies behind such decisions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.0] Expansion into new markets, for example, so that customers are served from nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.1] Getting inside a trade bloc (a group of countries trading freely together), which gives tariff-free access to the EU. A tariff is a tax on imports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.2] Lower costs, because cheaper land, labour or tax raises profit.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.3] Access to skilled labour, because some work cannot be done without trained staff.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.4] Following competitors into a cluster, so as to share the skills and suppliers already there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.5] Tax planning, because a low-tax country leaves the firm more profit.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.b] The strategies that open plants have opposites that close them. A parent company closes a branch plant when the plant no longer suits its plans.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.0] Rationalisation: cutting surplus capacity after a fall in demand or a merger.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.1] Relocation to lower-cost countries, because production costs are lower there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.2] Outsourcing: contract manufacturers make the goods instead of the company's own plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.3] Automation (machines replacing workers) reduces the number of workers needed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.4] Mergers and takeovers, after which a parent firm may close a duplicate plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.5] A product reaching the end of its life cycle, because falling demand leaves the plant with no future output.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.b] A branch plant does not have to close when its routine work moves away. It can survive by changing what it does.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.0] A plant can survive by moving up the value chain (the steps that add more value to a product) into more R&D, advanced products and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.1] Intel's continuing investment at Leixlip is the example: Fab 34, opened in 2023, makes advanced chips using extreme ultraviolet lithography, a chip-making technique.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.b] Dell and DEC are two short examples of plants that closed or moved, and of how a closure can affect a region.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.0] Dell opened in Limerick (Raheen) in 1990.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.1] In January 2009 Dell announced that manufacturing would move to Łódź, Poland, with about 1,900 jobs lost in Limerick. It kept sales and service functions in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.2] Poland joined the EU on 1 May 2004, with nine other states, so its plants lay inside the enlarged EU with lower costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.3] DEC closed its Galway plant in 1993, and its skilled workforce later helped attract the medtech cluster.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.4] There is more on Dell in The MNC and change subsection of The Global Economy — Multinationals and Globalisation.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.b] A product passes through stages from launch to decline, and its best production location changes at each stage. This is how the product life cycle shapes where plants are found.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.0] Introduction: the product is developed near headquarters and R&D, in a core region with highly skilled labour.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.1] Growth: branch plants are opened in other developed countries near large markets; Ireland gained many.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.2] Maturity: the standardised product is mass-produced in low-wage locations, because it no longer needs high skills.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.3] Decline: the plant closes or production is concentrated in the cheapest plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.b] Apple's Cork site shows a plant changing its role as the company's production methods change, rather than closing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.0] Apple opened at Hollyhill, Cork, in 1980 and first made computers there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.1] As Apple's manufacturing moved to contract manufacturers in Asia, the Cork site shifted mainly to operations, support and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.2] The Cork site is now Apple's European headquarters, with about 6,000 staff (2020s).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.b] A host country is not helpless against mobile firms. It can aim to make its jobs harder to move.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.0] Attract firms early in new life cycles, for example in pharmaceuticals, medical technology and software, so the country has the work while it is new and growing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.1] Invest in education and research so that jobs are harder to move: Science Foundation Ireland, set up in 2000 and made a statutory body (one created by law) in 2003, funds research; it merged into Research Ireland in 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.2] Universities supply the graduates that keep high-technology firms in place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.b] Computing and the internet have freed many services from a fixed place. Delivering a service at a distance means it can be located almost anywhere.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.0] Computing and the internet have made many services footloose, and Ireland became a European base for software and internet firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.1] Teleservices are services delivered at a distance by phone or internet, such as customer support, technical support and remote data processing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.2] Teleservices can locate anywhere with broadband, including peripheral areas (those far from the main centres).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.3] Low-skilled teleservice work moves easily to cheaper countries or is automated.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.4] There is more on IT and teleservices in The MNC and change subsection of The Global Economy — Multinationals and Globalisation.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.b] E-commerce has three effects on where activities locate: on warehouses, on town-centre shops and on bank branches, because customers no longer need to travel.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.0] E-commerce is buying and selling goods and services over the internet.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.1] Warehouses and delivery depots locate near motorways and airports, so that parcels can be moved quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.2] Pressure on town-centre shops, and bank branch closures, follow the growth of online trade.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.3] There is more on e-commerce and an MNC's operations in The MNC and change subsection of The Global Economy — Multinationals and Globalisation.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.b] After COVID-19 many jobs could be done away from a city office. This gives rural areas a chance to attract work, but only if they have good broadband.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.0] Remote and hybrid working (part at home, part in the office) grew after COVID-19, so some jobs can be done from home or a local hub rather than a city office.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.1] National Broadband Plan: the contract was signed in 2019 with National Broadband Ireland to bring high-speed broadband to rural premises.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.2] Connected Hubs: a national network of remote-working hubs launched in 2021.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.3] Our Rural Future, rural development policy, 2021.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.4] Broadband availability differs from region to region, and a region's coverage affects whether it can attract remote-working jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.28.b] New technology continues to change where work is done and how many people are needed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.28.0] Artificial intelligence and automation are future drivers of location and job change.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.28.1] Automation can reduce the labour-cost advantage of low-wage locations, favouring places with skills and research, because machines make cheap labour less important.

[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.b] For a factory or multinational, look on the map for the features that supply land, transport, workers and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.0] Flat land and an edge-of-town site, where there is room and land is cheaper.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.1] A national primary road or motorway, a railway, and a port or airport, for moving goods.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.2] A town labour force and services: schools, hospital and housing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.3] Water and an existing industrial estate, because services and planning approval for industry are already in place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.b] A fish processing plant needs to be close to where fish are landed, because fish is a bulk-reducing, perishable raw material.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.0] A pier, harbour or fishing port, on the coast.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.1] Roads to markets, so that fresh fish reaches buyers quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.2] Water and a labour force, because processing needs both.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.3] Low-lying land, because it is flat enough to build on and close to the coast.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.32.b] A hotel depends on attractions that draw visitors, together with the services that let them reach and use the hotel.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.32.0] Coast, beach and scenery; lakes or mountains; antiquities (ancient sites and monuments).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.32.1] A golf course, and water sports, because these give guests activities.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.32.2] Nearby town services and road access, so guests and staff can reach the hotel.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.32.3] Tourist information, which helps visitors plan trips.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.33.b] The one reason against in your answer comes from the map features that make a site unsuitable.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.33.0] Steep or high land, which is hard and costly to build on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.33.1] A floodplain, which would flood.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.33.2] A protected or scenic area, where planning conflict is likely.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.33.3] Poor roads, remoteness from labour, distance from services, and impact on residents, because each makes the plant harder to run or provokes opposition.

CANDIDATES geo-aerial-photographs-s1: Aerial photograph interpretation
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.0.b] An aerial photograph is taken from the air, and its type depends on the angle of the camera. A vertical photograph is the first type: the camera points straight down.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.0.0] A vertical photograph is taken with the camera pointing straight down, so it shows only roofs and the tops of features, with no horizon (the line where land meets sky).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.0.1] The scale of a vertical photograph (how much real distance is shrunk onto the picture) is roughly constant across the picture, so it looks like a map and can be used for measuring and mapping.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.0.2] Features are harder to recognise on a vertical photograph because their sides and heights are hidden, so you must identify them from the shape of the roof.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.b] An oblique photograph is taken with the camera tilted at an angle to the ground, like a photograph taken from a hilltop or aircraft window. This gives depth but changes how the picture can be used.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.0] An oblique photograph is taken with the camera at an angle to the ground, so the sides as well as the roofs of buildings are visible.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.1] The scale of an oblique photograph is not constant: foreground features (those nearest the camera) look larger than identical features in the background, and the area covered widens towards the background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.2] Features are easier to recognise on an oblique photograph because you can see their sides, but distance and area cannot be measured directly from it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.1.3] A low oblique photograph does not show the horizon; a high oblique photograph shows the horizon or skyline.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.2.b] To say which type you have, look for the clues below and quote them as your reason.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.2.0] Oblique clues: the sides of buildings are visible, foreground features look larger than background features, and depth or a horizon is shown.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.2.1] Vertical clues: only roofs are visible, the scale looks even, and the picture looks like a map.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.b] To say where a feature is on an oblique photograph, imagine the picture divided into nine areas and name the area it lies in. This is called relative location, because it gives the position within the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.0] Three strips run across the picture: left, centre and right, so every feature falls in one of three columns.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.1] Three strips run into the picture: foreground at the bottom, nearest the camera; middleground in the centre; background at the top, furthest from the camera.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.2] Accepted notation gives one term from each set, across-term first: left foreground, centre middleground, right background. Both parts must be correct to score.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.3] Use the exam spelling foreground, middleground and background, and use centre, not middle, for the across strip.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.4] Method: draw the grid lightly on the photo in pencil (or fold the picture into thirds in your head), find the feature, and state its zone.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.3.5] The same method answers true or false statements about what lies in a zone, and matches cropped sections of the photo to zones.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.4.b] The nine-area grid applies to oblique photographs. A vertical photograph looks straight down, so there is no near or far.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.4.0] On a vertical photograph foreground and background have no meaning, because the camera looks straight down and nothing is nearer to it than anything else.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.4.1] Use top, centre or bottom with left, centre or right instead, or compass directions such as north-east and south-west if the orientation is known.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.b] Rivertown is an invented market town on an invented oblique photograph. Every worked example here uses it and is labelled illustrative.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.0] Illustrative: a river flows from the left foreground to the right background, and a stone bridge crosses it in the centre middleground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.1] Illustrative: a church with a spire stands in the left middleground, with a graveyard beside it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.2] Illustrative: a main street and a square lie in the centre middleground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.3] Illustrative: an industrial estate lies in the right foreground, and a housing estate of semi-detached houses lies in the left background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.4] Illustrative: fields with hedgerows lie in the right background, and a GAA pitch lies in the centre background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.5] Illustrative: waste ground lies in the centre foreground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.5.6] Illustrative true or false: there is waste ground in the centre foreground. This is true, because the waste ground is in the centre foreground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.b] Each feature has a clue that identifies it on the photograph. Use the clue to name the feature, then give its zone.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.0] Church: a spire or tower, a cross-shaped plan, and a graveyard beside it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.1] Graveyard: rows of small headstones.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.2] School: a large building with a playground, pitches and a car park.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.3] Shopping centre: a very large flat roof and an extensive car park, because many shoppers arrive by car.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.4] Factory or warehouse: a large flat or sawtooth roof (a row of angled ridges like saw teeth), with yards and lorries but no gardens.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.6.5] Apartments: a large multi-storey block with communal parking, meaning parking shared by all residents.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.b] Houses are told apart by how they are joined, and open land by its markings or lack of them.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.0] Housing estate: rows of similar houses with gardens, often arranged in cul-de-sacs (dead-end streets).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.1] Terraced houses share side walls; semi-detached houses share one wall; detached houses stand alone. This is how you tell the three apart.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.2] Car park: marked bays with vehicles in them, so you can tell it from waste ground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.3] Sports pitch: a rectangle with lines and goalposts. Running track: an oval with lanes.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.7.4] Waste or derelict ground: rough, unused land with no clear use.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.b] Transport routes and water have their own shapes and surfaces, which make them easy to pick out.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.0] Railway: gentle curves, no cars, and crossings of roads by bridges.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.1] River and lake: smooth, dark water with a natural outline.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.2] Bridge: a structure carrying a road or railway across water or another route; its parapet (the low wall along its edge) is a clue.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.3] Harbour, pier or quay: boats moored alongside a structure that projects into the water or lines it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.8.4] Roundabout: a circular junction with roads leading off it, such as the one at the end of a bridge.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.9.b] A street map, satellite image or street-view images (ground-level photographs) may show the same features, labelled A to E, in a different orientation from the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.9.0] Find two features common to both, because they give you fixed reference points; such as the river, a bridge, the railway or a large church, and turn the second source mentally until they match. This is needed because it may be oriented differently from the photo.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.9.1] Identify each feature by a distinctive clue: a spire, a cruciform (cross-shaped) roof, pitch markings, a large flat roof beside a car park, a bridge parapet, or shopfronts.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.9.2] Give each feature's zone on the photograph in accepted notation.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.b] There are two ways to say where something is. Absolute location uses the map's grid, and relative location uses the photograph itself or neighbouring features.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.0] Absolute location is a grid reference read from the OS (Ordnance Survey) map. Relative location is a zone in the nine-area notation, or a position relative to other features, such as beside the bridge, north of the river.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.1] To give a grid reference for a feature seen on the photograph, find it on the photograph, locate it on the map from its position relative to roads, the river and junctions, then give a four- or six-figure grid reference as taught in Map interpretation.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.2] On a 1:50 000 map individual buildings are often not drawn, so locate the feature from the surrounding road pattern; churches are shown by map symbols.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.10.3] To name a road or river seen on the photograph, trace it on the photograph, find the same route on the map, and read its number (N, R or M) or its name from the map.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.b] The map can tell you which way the camera was facing when the photograph was taken. This is also what lets you turn left and right on the photo into compass directions.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.0] The direction from a foreground feature to a background feature on the map is the direction the camera was pointing, because the background is the part furthest in front of the camera.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.1] Identify one foreground feature and one background feature on the photograph, find both on the map, and state the direction in an eight-point compass term such as north-east.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.2] Check the camera direction with a third feature, so that one wrongly identified feature does not mislead you.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.3] Illustrative: on the map the bridge (centre middleground) lies due south of the pitch (centre background), and the waste ground (centre foreground) lies south of the bridge.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.11.4] Illustrative: foreground to background runs south to north, so the camera was pointing north.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.b] The photograph and the map record different things, so each answers questions the other cannot.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.0] The photograph shows what the map does not: individual buildings, land-use type, crops and field use, building height and age, car parks, traffic, temporary features and recent developments.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.1] The map shows what the photograph does not: names, road numbers, height and contours (lines joining places of equal height), the grid, labelled antiquities (ancient monuments), boundaries, and features hidden by trees or lying beyond the frame.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.2] The map covers the whole area at a constant scale; an oblique photograph gives a partial view with changing scale.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.12.3] The photograph and map were usually made on different dates, so differences between them can reveal change.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.b] Whether you can work out a scale depends on the type of photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.0] An oblique photograph has no single scale, so measure distances on the map instead.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.1] The scale of a vertical photograph is found by comparing a distance on the photograph with the real ground distance, which is read from the map. The map-scale method is taught in Map interpretation.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.2] Illustrative: two road junctions are 5 cm apart on a vertical photograph and 2 cm apart on the 1:50 000 map.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.3] Illustrative: 2 cm on the 1:50 000 map equals 1 km, which is 100 000 cm on the ground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.13.4] Illustrative: the photograph scale is 5 cm to 100 000 cm, which is 1:20 000.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.b] A sketch map is a drawn copy of the photograph's main features, not a tracing. Following the rules for size, shapes and labels is where the marks are won.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.0] Draw a frame with four ruled sides, the same shape as the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.1] The exam usually asks for half the length and half the breadth: each side is halved, so the sketch has a quarter of the photograph's area.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.2] Measure the photograph and calculate the sketch size before drawing.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.3] Divide the photograph and the frame into the nine-area grid lightly in pencil, so that you can place features accurately.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.14.4] Show only the features asked for, in approximately the correct position, shape and proportion, because a sketch is a simplified copy and not a tracing.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.b] Different kinds of feature are drawn in different ways, and every one must be labelled.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.0] Draw rivers, roads and railways as lines, with the river as a broad double line; draw areas such as housing, industry, farmland, car parks and lakes as closed outlined shapes; draw single buildings as small blocks or symbols.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.1] Label every feature on or beside it, or in a key (a list explaining the symbols), and give the sketch a title. An unlabelled feature scores nothing.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.2] A feature specified with a position, such as a car park in the centre foreground, must be drawn in that zone.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.15.3] Common requests to practise: river, coastline, lake, bridge, railway, two connecting streets (two streets drawn meeting at a junction), car park, church, ecclesiastical land use, housing estate, residential land use, industrial building or estate, warehouse, agricultural land, recreational land use, sports ground, waste ground, a historic ruin, and the CBD.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.b] This shows the rules applied to the invented Rivertown photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.0] Illustrative: a photograph 22 cm by 16 cm gives a sketch frame of 11 cm by 8 cm, because each side is halved.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.1] Illustrative request: the river, a bridge, a church, an industrial area in the foreground, and agricultural land in the background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.2] Illustrative: draw the river as a broad double line from the left foreground to the right background.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.3] Illustrative: draw the bridge as a short line crossing the river in the centre middleground, and label it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.4] Illustrative: draw the church as a small cross-shaped symbol in the left middleground, and label it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.5] Illustrative: draw the industrial area as a closed outlined shape in the right foreground, because the request places it there, and label it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.16.6] Illustrative: draw the agricultural land as field outlines with hedgerows in the background of the right side, and label it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.17.b] Land use means what an area of land is used for. Sorting what you see into categories is the first step in describing a town.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.17.0] Land-use categories: residential, commercial or retail, industrial, ecclesiastical, educational, recreational, transport, agricultural, open space, and waste or derelict ground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.17.1] Ecclesiastical land use is religious land use: churches, their grounds and graveyards. Schools are educational land use.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.17.2] Agricultural land around the town shows field patterns with hedgerows, and is either pasture (grass, often with livestock) or tillage (ploughed or cropped fields).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.18.b] Industry needs space and good transport, so it sits in particular places in a town.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.18.0] Industrial land is usually on the edge of town: large flat-roofed buildings in an industrial estate, near main roads, a bypass (a road built round a town to take traffic away from its centre), a railway or a port, with space for parking and lorries.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.18.1] Older industry, such as mills, may be found beside the river or quays.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.b] Density means how many homes fit into an area. It changes from the centre of a town outwards, and the centre itself has a distinctive look.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.0] Older terraced housing near the centre has high density, with small or no gardens, because the houses share walls and are packed together.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.1] Suburban estates of semi-detached and detached houses lie further out, at lower density, with gardens and cul-de-sacs.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.2] Apartments are high density, because many homes are stacked in one multi-storey block.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.3] Residential density usually falls outward from the centre.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.4] The CBD (central business district) is the commercial core of the town, found at the centre where roads converge.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.19.5] CBD clues: the tallest and most densely packed buildings, no gardens, shops fronting onto main streets, car parks and heavy traffic, and often a square or main street.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.20.b] A function is a purpose a town serves, such as trade, worship or transport. Each function can be seen on a photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.20.0] Functions visible on photographs: residential; commercial or retail; industrial or manufacturing; ecclesiastical or religious; educational; recreational or leisure; medical, such as a hospital.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.20.1] Further functions: transport (bridge, railway station, converging roads, harbour); port or fishing (piers, boats); tourism (hotels, caravan parks, marinas, beaches, historic sites).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.20.2] Further functions: market (a square, a market house); administrative (courthouse, council offices, Garda station, which is often hard to confirm from a photograph alone); defensive (a historic castle or town walls).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.b] A function question wants evidence, a location and a reason. Following the same four steps each time gives a full answer.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.0] Answer formula for describing and explaining a function: name the function, give the photograph evidence, give its location in accepted notation, and explain why the town performs it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.1] Explain the reason using its site (the land it stands on), situation (its position relative to surroundings), size or hinterland (the area around it that it serves).
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.2] Give several pieces of evidence for each function, so that one doubtful clue does not weaken the answer.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.3] Illustrative ecclesiastical function: a church with a spire and graveyard in the left middleground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.4] Illustrative industrial function: large flat-roofed units and lorry yards in the right foreground, beside the main road.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.21.5] Illustrative market or commercial function: a square and a main street of shops in the centre middleground, where roads converge at the bridge.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.b] A town's history is visible in its buildings and layout, so the photograph can show which parts are old and which are new.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.0] Older features show early growth: a castle, tower house or other ruin; an old church or abbey ruin with an old graveyard; town walls or gates.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.1] A market square, fair green or market house shows the town's role as a market centre.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.2] Old stone bridges, quays and warehouses on the river, and mills and weirs (low dams across a river) show the town's use of the river.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.3] A railway line and station show growth in the 19th century.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.22.4] Modern housing estates and industrial estates on the edge of town show recent growth.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.23.b] Towns grow where a site offers an advantage, and many of those advantages can be seen on the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.23.0] Reasons for the town's location seen on the photograph: a bridging point, a river or coastal site, converging routes, flat land, a harbour, surrounding farmland for a market town, or a defensive site.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.b] The layout of the streets shows whether a town was designed or grew bit by bit, and the pattern can be named.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.0] A planned town has wide, straight streets, a regular grid, a central square or diamond, and uniform terraces.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.1] An unplanned town has narrow, winding, irregular streets, often of medieval origin.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.2] Wide streets on their own do not show that a town is unplanned, because planned towns also have wide, straight streets.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.3] Linear growth: development along a main road, for example houses strung out beside it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.4] Nucleated growth: development clustered around a centre or junction.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.5] Radial pattern: streets radiating from a bridge or square.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.6] Grid pattern: streets laid out at right angles in regular blocks.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.24.7] Name the pattern on the photograph and use it as evidence for the type of town.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.b] Congestion means roads so full that traffic slows or stops. Several causes can be spotted on a photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.0] Narrow streets in the old core of the town, which cannot carry many vehicles.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.1] On-street parking that narrows the road.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.2] Routes converging on one bridge or junction, so all traffic must squeeze through one point.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.3] Through-traffic passing the town centre, meaning vehicles that are only passing through and do not stop there.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.4] Delivery vehicles, and limited off-street parking.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.25.5] Schools and shopping centres that generate peak traffic.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.b] Traffic management means the measures used to keep traffic moving and safe. Many can be spotted on the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.0] Measures visible on photographs: roundabouts, traffic lights, pedestrian crossings, one-way streets (all parked cars face one way, with arrows on the road), double yellow lines (no parking), bus lanes and cycle lanes.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.1] Further measures: off-street and multi-storey car parks, pedestrianised streets (closed to vehicles), bypasses, relief roads and ring roads, bollards (short posts that block vehicles) and traffic-calming ramps.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.2] Answer formula for a measure: name it, give its location in accepted notation, and explain how it reduces congestion or improves safety.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.3] Answer formula for a problem: name it, locate it, and explain why it causes congestion.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.26.4] A suggested solution, such as a bypass to remove through-traffic, a car park on waste ground, a one-way system or pedestrianisation, must be justified with photograph evidence.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.27.b] This applies the formulas to the invented Rivertown photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.27.0] Illustrative problem: all routes converge on the single bridge in the centre middleground, and on-street parking on the main street narrows the road.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.27.1] Illustrative measures: a roundabout at the end of the bridge, and a car park beside the square.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.27.2] Illustrative solution: a relief road crossing the river further downstream, using the open fields.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.b] Land use changes as a town grows. The photograph can be compared with the map to show what a place used to be.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.0] Land-use change is found by comparing the map with the photograph, which are dated differently, or by comparing a location's present use with a listed change.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.1] Listed changes include agricultural to housing, industrial to residential, green space to recreation, open space to residential, farmland to relief road, and green space to waste ground.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.2] Method: find the location, identify its present use, then infer its earlier use from the map or from surrounding clues.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.3] Clues to earlier use: new estates on the edge with fields beyond, cleared sites, and new roads cutting across field patterns.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.4] Illustrative: the map shows fields where the photograph shows the left background housing estate, so the change is agricultural to residential.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.28.5] Second pair, same method: a site shown as industrial on the map and as housing on the photograph is recorded as industrial to residential.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.b] A growing town leaves signs on its edges, which the photograph can show.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.0] New estates on the edge of the town.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.1] Ribbon development (houses built in a line along a road) along approach roads.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.2] Industrial estates and retail parks on the outskirts.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.3] New roundabouts and bypasses.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.29.4] Sprawl is the spreading of a town into the countryside. There is more on this wider urban issue in the Urban Geography chapter.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.b] A site is chosen or rejected on evidence from the photograph.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.0] Choose a site on the photograph for a proposed development, such as a shopping centre, factory, car park or playground, and give three reasons, each with photograph evidence.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.1] For example, reasons to look for: available flat land; access to main roads; nearness to the population served; space for parking; a position away from a flood-prone river bank.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.2] For industry, a site away from housing is suitable.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.30.3] To show a given site is unsuitable, name the reason it fails these tests and give the photograph evidence for it.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.b] The season can be worked out from what plants, animals and people are doing. Reason from the evidence rather than guessing a month.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.0] Deciduous trees (trees that lose their leaves in autumn) in full leaf indicate summer; bare trees indicate winter.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.1] Crops growing or ripening, golden fields and harvested stubble (the short stalks left after harvest) indicate late summer to autumn.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.2] Ploughed fields indicate autumn or spring.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.3] Silage or hay bales in fields indicate summer.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.4] Cattle grazing outdoors indicate the grazing season, roughly spring to autumn, because Irish cattle are usually housed indoors over winter.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.31.5] People in light clothing, and outdoor activity on beaches or pitches, indicate warm weather.

[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.b] Shadows point away from the sun. Use the camera direction from the map to turn a shadow direction into a compass direction.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.0] Ireland is in the northern hemisphere, so the midday sun is due south and midday shadows point north, where they are at their shortest.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.1] The sun rises in the east and sets in the west, roughly. Morning shadows point west; evening shadows point east.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.2] Long shadows mean a low sun, which occurs early or late in the day, or in winter, so long shadows alone do not give the hour.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.3] Other clues: busy streets and full car parks in business hours, school car parks full or empty, rush-hour traffic, and street lights on.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.4] Illustrative: the camera points north, so left is west and right is east. Building shadows fall to the left, which is west, so the sun is in the east: morning.
[geo-aerial-photographs-s1.32.5] Illustrative: the shadows are long, so the sun is low and it is early morning.
