CANDIDATES geo-economic-activities-s2: The mobility of modern economic activities and factors in their location
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.b] Firms try to locate where total transport costs are lowest. This least-cost principle of industrial location was set out by Alfred Weber in 1909, and it depends on whether processing makes the materials lighter or heavier.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.0] Bulk-reducing industries are those where the raw material loses weight in processing, so the plant locates near the raw material because carrying the heavy input a long way would waste money. Examples: alumina refining, dairy processing, fish processing, mining.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.1] Bulk-gaining industries are those where the product gains weight or bulk in production, so the plant locates near the market because the finished product is the costly thing to move. Examples: brewing, soft drinks bottling, bakeries.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.2] Test for an exam location question: if the inputs lose weight in processing, the plant is pulled towards the raw material; if the finished product is heavier or bulkier, it is pulled towards the market.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.b] Location questions use a set of working terms. Knowing them makes the location factors easier to follow.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.0] Footloose industry: one that is not tied to a raw material or a market, so it is free to choose its location. The factors below explain where footloose activities choose to locate.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.1] Branch plant: a factory or office owned by a company headquartered elsewhere, usually carrying out routine production.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.2] Headquarters (HQ): the head office where a company's main decisions are made. Research and development (R&D): the work of designing new products and processes.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.3] Greenfield site: undeveloped land on which a new plant is built. Brownfield site: land already built on, often a former industrial site that may need clearing before reuse.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.4] Industrial estate or business park: a serviced area of land set aside for firms, with roads and utilities already in place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.5] Cluster, or agglomeration: firms in the same or related industries grouped together, sharing skills, suppliers and research.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.6] Backward linkages are links to a firm's suppliers; forward linkages are links to its customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.7] Infrastructure: the transport, energy, water, communications and waste systems that firms depend on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.b] A location factor is any condition that makes one place more suitable than another for an activity. The first three are the inputs a firm needs to make its product.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.0] Raw materials: weight-losing raw materials pull processing towards their source or towards a port where they arrive, which keeps the cost of moving heavy loads down.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.1] Energy: industry once clustered on coalfields, where the fuel was. National electricity grids freed most industry from this, but energy-hungry activities such as alumina refining and data centres still need large, reliable and affordable power.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.2] Labour: the quantity, skills and education, cost, productivity and flexibility of the workforce.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.3] High-technology and service firms seek graduates because their work needs advanced skills; standardised assembly (repeating the same simple steps) seeks low wages.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.4] Irish examples for labour: the Intel plant at Leixlip and the Galway cluster, both described below.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.b] Once a product is made it has to reach its customers, and firms need to receive inputs. Markets, transport links and communications decide how cheaply and quickly that happens.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.0] Markets: the size and wealth of the market, and access to the EU single market (the EU area with no trade barriers between members). Bulk-gaining and perishable goods locate near the market; services locate near their customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.1] Ports: deep water lets bulk carriers (large ships carrying loose cargo) berth. Dublin Port is Ireland's busiest port. Port of Cork and Shannon Foynes Port are also major ports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.2] Rosslare Europort: direct ferry routes to France grew after Brexit (2021) to avoid the UK land bridge, the route across Britain to continental Europe.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.3] Whitegate, Cork Harbour: Ireland's only oil refinery, opened 1959.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.4] Airports: air freight suits light, high-value, time-sensitive goods such as pharmaceuticals, medical devices and microchips, because the high price of the goods can bear the high cost of air transport.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.5] Dublin Airport handled over 30 million passengers in 2023; its North Runway opened in 2022.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.6] Freight modes: road offers door-to-door flexibility over short and medium distances; rail carries heavy loads on fixed routes; sea is the cheapest way to move bulk over long distances; air is the fastest but the most expensive.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.7] Motorways, rail, broadband and fibre (fibre-optic cable) connect firms to markets and suppliers; for services, broadband and fibre matter most. One environmental challenge of air transport is its CO2 emissions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.b] A firm also needs money and a welcoming policy environment. Governments, and the EU above them, can make a location more or less attractive through money, tax and rules.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.0] Capital: investment money comes from banks, shareholders, government grants and venture capital (money from investors who back new, risky businesses).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.1] IDA Ireland was set up in 1949 and became an independent agency in 1969. It attracts firms by offering grants, serviced sites (land with utilities already supplied) and advance factories (buildings built ready for a firm to move into).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.2] Corporation tax (the tax on company profits) was 12.5% from 2003. Very large groups pay 15% from 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.3] Enterprise Ireland (1998) supports Irish-owned firms. Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980) supports enterprise in Gaeltacht areas.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.4] Regional policy steers firms towards less-developed areas through grants and incentives.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.5] Planning permission (official approval from the local authority) is needed before a site can be built on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.6] EU state-aid rules limit the subsidies governments may give firms, while EU funds support regional development.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.7] Trade agreements reduce the barriers that firms face when selling across borders, which makes a location inside the agreement more attractive.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.b] After a firm picks a general area it must find a suitable piece of land, and workers and the plant both need services. The site and its services decide whether the plant can actually be built and run.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.0] Site: flat, well-drained land, large enough for expansion, at a cheap price, so that building is easy and the plant can grow later.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.1] Site: a water supply, and facilities for effluent (liquid waste) and waste disposal.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.2] Site: hazardous industry needs distance from homes, so that accidents or pollution do not harm residents.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.3] Services: water, electricity, gas, broadband, waste treatment, housing and schools. Workers must be able to live near the plant, which is why housing and schools count as location factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.b] Some location factors are about people and history rather than land and transport. Firms gather together, stay put out of habit, or follow the pull of a pleasant place to live.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.0] Agglomeration: firms in the same industry gather to share skilled workers, suppliers, research and universities, so each firm gains from the others being nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.1] Cork Harbour (pharmaceuticals) and Galway (medical technology) grew into clusters in this way.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.2] Personal and historical factors: the choices of founders and the history of a site can decide a location, whatever the economics of the place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.3] Industrial inertia: an industry stays where it is after the original reason for locating there has gone.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.4] Quality of life: attractive places to live help firms recruit skilled staff, because graduates can choose where to work.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.b] Local opposition and environmental regulation can stop or delay a project. Corrib gas shows how long a delay can be.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.0] Corrib gas was discovered in 1996. Its terminal (the plant that receives and processes the gas) is at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.1] The Shell to Sea campaign opposed the project, and the Rossport Five were jailed in 2005.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.2] Gas first flowed in December 2015, after a long delay caused by local opposition and changes to planning.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.b] The weight of each factor has changed over time as transport, energy supply and technology have changed. The pattern runs from resources, to customers, to skills and quality of life.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.0] In the 19th century, raw materials and power were the main location factors, so industry sat on or near coalfields and sources of materials.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.1] In the 20th century, markets and labour became the main factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.2] Today, footloose activities (those free to choose a location) chase skills, tax, connectivity and quality of life.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.b] A bulk-reducing heavy industry located on a deep-water estuary, where imported raw material arrives by sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.0] Aughinish Alumina opened in 1983 on Aughinish Island near Askeaton, Co. Limerick, on the south shore of the Shannon Estuary. It is Europe's largest alumina refinery.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.1] Bulk-reducing: about two to three tonnes of bauxite (the ore, or raw rock, that alumina is made from) make one tonne of alumina, so the refinery sits at the raw material's port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.2] Bauxite is imported by sea, mainly from Guinea and Brazil. The alumina (aluminium oxide) is shipped to aluminium smelters elsewhere; the refinery does not make aluminium.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.3] Deep, sheltered water in the Shannon Estuary, Ireland's deepest natural sheltered water, allows large bulk carriers to berth.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.4] The site is a flat island location away from towns, which suits a large heavy industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.5] Government support for heavy industry is a further factor in its siting.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.6] Problems: bauxite residue, known as red mud, is stored in large disposal areas on site.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.7] The company has been owned by the Russian company Rusal since 2007.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.b] The same estuary that serves Aughinish has also been used for energy, because deep water lets fuel arrive by ship.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.0] Moneypoint power station, Co. Clare, is on the Shannon Estuary and imported coal by sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.1] Coal burning at Moneypoint ended in 2025, so the estuary's energy role has changed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.b] Intel is footloose in the sense that its products are not tied to a raw material or a market, so it chose Leixlip from many possible sites. Its choice shows which factors matter to a high-technology firm.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.0] Intel's Leixlip plant opened in 1989. It is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, worth billions of euro.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.1] Skilled graduates are available, so with Maynooth University nearby, which meets the plant's need for a skilled workforce.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.2] IDA support and low corporation tax attracted the investment. There is more on this in Capital, government and EU policy.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.3] Access to the EU single market from an English-speaking base.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.4] The plant lies beside the M4 motorway and close to Dublin Airport, so goods and staff can move easily.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.5] It occupies a large greenfield site (undeveloped land) with a supply of electricity and water.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.6] Footloose does not mean likely to leave: the large fixed investment makes relocation unlikely.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.7] Global job cuts announced in 2024 and 2025 affected Leixlip, showing that even a large plant depends on parent-company decisions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.b] Cork Harbour combines deep water, industrial land and skilled graduates, so pharmaceutical firms have gathered there. Their closeness to each other reinforces the pattern.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.0] Pfizer, Janssen (Johnson & Johnson) and Novartis are at Ringaskiddy; Eli Lilly is at Kinsale, Co. Cork.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.1] The Port of Cork has deep-water berths at Ringaskiddy, which suits the import and export of pharmaceutical goods.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.2] Large flat sites are zoned for industry (set aside for it in the local plan), with water supply and waste facilities.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.3] Skilled labour comes from University College Cork (UCC) and Munster Technological University (MTU). MTU was formed in 2021 from Cork IT and IT Tralee.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.4] IDA support, tax and clustering reinforce the pattern.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.5] Pharmaceuticals and chemicals are Ireland's largest goods exports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.6] Older heavy industry in the same harbour: Irish Steel at Haulbowline closed in 2001.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.b] Galway shows how the closure of one firm can feed the growth of another industry. Skilled people left behind became the base for a cluster making medical devices.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.0] Boston Scientific opened in Galway in 1994. Medtronic, Parkmore, Galway followed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.1] The cluster draws on skilled engineers left by the closure of DEC's plant. The Dell and DEC block below gives the date.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.2] University of Galway research (renamed in 2022) supports the cluster, and a network of suppliers has grown around the firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.3] There is more on this in Irish Regions — Core and Peripheral, in the peripheral Irish region subsection.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.b] Food and drink show both sides of Weber's idea. Processing that makes the product lighter stays at the source, while brewing, which makes the product heavier, stays near its customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.0] Kerry Group grew from the North Kerry Milk Products co-operative at Listowel, founded in 1972. Its headquarters are in Tralee, Co. Kerry.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.1] Dairy processing is bulk-reducing: plants at Listowel and Tralee sit near the supply of milk.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.2] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland's largest fishing port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.3] Fish processing plants at Killybegs handle pelagic fish (fish that live near the surface) such as mackerel and herring, a bulk-reducing process that locates at the port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.4] Guinness at St James's Gate, Dublin, was founded in 1759 by Arthur Guinness on a 9,000-year lease.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.5] Brewing is bulk-gaining because water is added to the product, so the brewery sits near its market.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.6] Industrial inertia: Guinness still brews at St James's Gate today, long after the original reasons for the site have gone.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.b] Data centres (buildings full of computers that store and process online data) are a modern footloose activity, but they use huge amounts of electricity. For them the power grid has become a limit on location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.0] Data centres used about 21% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.1] EirGrid (the electricity grid operator) stated in 2022 that it would not connect new data centres in the Dublin region until about 2028, so the electricity grid now limits their location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.2] Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, Google and Meta operate data centres in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.3] Why Ireland: a cool, mild climate reduces cooling costs; fibre links to the USA and Europe; existing clusters of technology firms; tax.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.b] Modern economic activities can move more easily than older ones. Five changes have loosened the link between an activity and a fixed place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.0] Lower transport costs, through containerisation (goods packed in standard boxes that move easily between ship, rail and lorry) and air freight, let goods move cheaply over long distances.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.1] Instant communications, through the internet, fibre and video calls, let firms manage plants at a distance.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.2] Standardised production can be done by low-skilled workers, so the work can go anywhere with a cheap labour supply.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.3] The removal of trade barriers, through the EU single market and the World Trade Organization, makes it easier to sell across borders from any member location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.4] Multinationals (firms operating in many countries) can compare countries and move capital quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.b] A country that hosts a branch plant gains quickly, but its gains are not secure. The same mobility that brings the plant can take it away.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.0] Investment brings rapid job creation, but jobs can leave just as quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.1] Branch plants are vulnerable because the decisions are made at a distant headquarters, not in the host country.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.2] Countries compete for investment by offering tax and grants.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.b] A company opens a branch plant in another country when the move helps its own plans. These are the strategies behind such decisions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.0] Expansion into new markets, for example, so that customers are served from nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.1] Getting inside a trade bloc (a group of countries trading freely together), which gives tariff-free access to the EU. A tariff is a tax on imports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.2] Lower costs, because cheaper land, labour or tax raises profit.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.3] Access to skilled labour, because some work cannot be done without trained staff.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s1: 1. Physical regions
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.b] Geographers divide the earth into regions so that places can be studied as groups instead of one by one. A region is an area of the earth's surface that shares one or more characteristics, called criteria, which make it different from the areas around it.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.0] Physical criteria: climate (the usual weather of a place), relief (the height and shape of the land) and rock type, such as limestone.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.1] Administrative criteria: the boundaries set by government, such as county lines.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.2] Cultural criteria: language and religion, which separate groups of people.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.3] Socio-economic criteria: wealth, employment and development (how advanced a place's economy and living standards are).
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.4] Urban criteria: a city and its hinterland (the surrounding area that depends on the city for services and jobs).
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.5] These five types of region are the five subsections of this chapter, and this subsection deals with physical regions.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.b] Regions can be big or small, and they can be defined by one criterion or by several. Their edges also differ: some are fixed by law, others fade gradually.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.0] Regions exist at different scales. A local region is a county or a city's hinterland, e.g. Co. Clare; a national region is the West of Ireland; a continental region is North West Europe; a global region is the Islamic world.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.1] A single index (one measurable criterion) defines a region by that one thing: rock type alone gives a karst region (a limestone landscape shaped by water dissolving the rock).
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.2] Multiple indices combine several criteria. For example, a peripheral region (one on the edge of a country's economic activity, far from its centre) can be defined by income, unemployment and out-migration (people leaving the area) together.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.3] Physical and cultural regions usually merge into their neighbours through transition zones (a gradual belt of change) rather than ending at a sharp line.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.4] Administrative regions have exact boundaries, fixed by law, so you can say precisely which side of the line a place lies on.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.5] Regions also change over time as their criteria change.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.b] Climate is the usual pattern of weather in a place over many years. A climatic region is a large area with similar temperature, precipitation (rain, snow and other moisture falling from the sky) and seasonal pattern, usually with similar natural vegetation and soils.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.0] The world can be divided into climatic regions. This subsection studies the cool temperate oceanic type in detail.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.1] Cool temperate oceanic: Ireland and North West Europe, which is why Ireland is the case studied here.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.2] Warm temperate oceanic: the Mediterranean type, with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, so it differs from Ireland.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.3] Hot desert: e.g. the Sahara.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.b] The cool temperate oceanic climate is mild and wet, with no extremes of heat or cold. These are the features to describe, with the Irish figures (Met Éireann long-term averages).
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.0] Mild winters, because of the warm sea: mean January (coldest month) temperatures are about 4-7°C.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.1] Cool summers, so it is never hot: mean July (warmest month) temperatures are about 14-16°C.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.2] The annual range of temperature (the gap between the warmest and coldest month) is small, only about 10°C, and snow is rare on low ground.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.3] Rain falls in every month, so there is no dry season.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.4] The weather is changeable, with prevailing (most frequent) south-westerly winds.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.b] Ireland's climate comes from several causes working together: where it sits on the globe, the ocean beside it and the winds that cross it. Each cause explains one feature of the climate.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.0] Latitude: Ireland lies between about 51.5°N and 55.5°N, in the mid-latitudes, where the sun's angle is moderate, so it is neither very hot nor very cold.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.1] North Atlantic Drift: a warm ocean current, the extension of the Gulf Stream, keeps winters mild and coasts free of ice.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.2] Maritime influence (the effect of being close to the sea): the sea heats and cools slowly, so the annual temperature range is small.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.3] Prevailing south-westerly winds blow off the Atlantic, carrying its mild, moist air over Ireland.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.4] Frequent Atlantic depressions (areas of low pressure that bring wind and rain) and their fronts (the boundaries between warm and cold air) bring rain. These weather systems are taught in the Weather and Climate chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.5] Relief rainfall: moist air is forced up the western mountains, cools and releases its rain on their western slopes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.b] The climate is not the same everywhere in Ireland. Because the wet south-westerly winds arrive from the Atlantic, the west gets the most rain and the east the least.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.0] The west is wetter than the east: about 1,000-1,250 mm of rain a year across much of the west, against about 750 mm in the driest parts of the east, around Dublin.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.1] Over 2,000 mm a year falls on many western mountains, because moist air is forced to rise over them.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.2] The east lies in a rain shadow (a drier area on the sheltered side of high ground), protected from the south-westerly winds by the western uplands.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.3] Wet days (days with measurable rain): about 150 a year on the east and south-east coasts, rising to about 225 a year in parts of the west.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.4] The south-east is the sunniest region, because it is sheltered from the wet south-westerlies.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.5] The north is slightly cooler than the south, and coasts are milder in winter than the inland Midlands, because the sea warms them.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.b] North West Europe is the international setting for this climate, because the same conditions that affect Ireland affect its neighbours. Further east the climate changes step by step.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.0] The same climate covers Ireland, Britain, north-west France (Brittany), Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark and coastal Norway.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.1] All of these face the Atlantic, share the North Atlantic Drift and lie in the path of westerly depressions, which is why they have the same climate.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.2] Moving east, away from the Atlantic, winters become colder, summers warmer, the annual range larger and rainfall lower, with a summer maximum (most rain falls in summer). This is the continental influence (the effect of being far from the sea).
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.3] The change is gradual, so the boundary of a climatic region is a transition zone, not a line.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.b] Climate shapes more than the weather. In a cool temperate oceanic region it also influences what grows naturally and how the land is farmed.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.0] Natural vegetation is deciduous forest (trees that lose their leaves in winter), mainly oak and ash.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.1] Soils are brown earth, the typical soil under deciduous forest.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.2] Grass grows for most of the year, because winters are mild and rain falls in every month. This favours pastoral farming (rearing animals on grass) in Ireland, mainly cattle and dairying.
[geo-region-concept-s1.8.b] Geomorphology is the study of landforms (natural features of the land) and how they formed. A geomorphological region is an area with a distinctive landscape produced by the same rock type, geological structure (how rocks are arranged and folded) or landforming processes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.8.0] Three settings are studied here: the Burren (a karst region), Munster ridge and valley (South Munster) and the Northern European Plain.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.b] The Burren in north-west Co. Clare is a karst region on Carboniferous limestone, a rock that water slowly dissolves. Formation of its landforms is taught in the notes on Landforms associated with particular rock types; here each landform is only named.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.0] The name comes from the Irish Boireann, meaning rocky place.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.1] Limestone pavement (a bare, flat expanse of rock): blocks called clints, separated by fissures (cracks) called grikes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.2] Turloughs: seasonal lakes in hollows, which fill in winter and drain in summer.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.3] Caves: Aillwee Cave and Doolin Cave, which holds the Great Stalactite, about 7.3 m long.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.4] Mullaghmore: a hill of folded, terraced limestone, an example of how the rock forms stepped slopes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.5] Very few surface rivers, because drainage is underground: water sinks through the cracks in the limestone.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.6] Glaciation: granite erratics (boulders carried by ice and dropped far from their source) from Connemara rest on the limestone, showing that ice scraped off the soil and exposed the pavement.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.b] The limestone shapes life on the Burren as well as its landforms: it affects which plants grow, how farmers use the land and why visitors come.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.0] Plants from Arctic-alpine and Mediterranean environments grow side by side, for example the spring gentian and mountain avens.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.1] Winterage: cattle graze the upland limestone in winter, because the rock stores heat and the ground is dry.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.2] Poulnabrone, a Neolithic (New Stone Age) portal tomb (dolmen, a stone tomb of upright stones with a large capstone), stands on the limestone pavement.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.3] Burren National Park protects part of the area near Mullaghmore.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.4] The Burren and Cliffs of Moher UNESCO Global Geopark (an area recognised internationally for its geological heritage): tourism is a major activity.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.b] South Munster is a region of Old Red Sandstone ridges alternating with Carboniferous limestone valleys. Its parallel pattern comes from folding of the rocks and then erosion.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.0] Armorican folding (a period of crustal folding) about 300 million years ago, on an east-west trend, produced anticlines (upfolds) and synclines (downfolds).
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.1] Differential erosion (different rocks wearing away at different rates) left the harder sandstone standing as ridges and wore the softer limestone down into valleys.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.2] Ridges (anticlines of sandstone): Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains, the Knockmealdown Mountains and the Caha Mountains.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.3] Valleys (synclines of limestone): the Blackwater valley and the Lee valley.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.4] Trellised drainage (a pattern of rivers following parallel valleys, with short links across them): rivers flow east along the valleys. The River Blackwater then turns sharply south at Cappoquin, Co. Waterford, and reaches the sea at Youghal. The Lee flows east to Cork Harbour.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.5] Drowned valleys at the coast, flooded by the sea, form rias (long, narrow inlets): Bantry Bay, Dingle Bay and the Kenmare River.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.6] Human consequences: towns, routes and fertile farmland lie in the valleys, while the ridges carry rough grazing, forestry and bog.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.b] The Northern European Plain is a vast lowland, mostly low, flat or gently rolling, stretching from western France into Russia. Ice and wind shaped its surface, and its flat, fertile land supports many people.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.0] Countries crossed: France (north), Belgium, the Netherlands, northern Germany, Denmark and Poland, and into Russia.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.1] Glacial deposits (material left by ice sheets), including moraines (ridges of rock debris), outwash sands (sand washed out from melting ice) and lakes, shape the north of the plain.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.2] A belt of loess, a wind-blown silt, forms fertile soils along the southern edge.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.3] Loess soils are fertile, so they support crops such as wheat and sugar beet.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.4] The Rhine and Elbe flow north to the North Sea; the Oder and Vistula flow north to the Baltic, which shows the plain slopes gently north.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.5] Polders: land reclaimed from the sea in the Netherlands. The Afsluitdijk, completed in 1932, closed off the Zuiderzee.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.6] Human consequences: fertile farmland, easy movement for transport because the land is flat, dense population and major cities, including Berlin, Warsaw, Hamburg and Amsterdam.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.b] Each of the three regions is defined by a different criterion, which shows how geomorphology can be used to define regions.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.0] The Burren is defined by its rock type, Carboniferous limestone, because that rock produces its karst landforms.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.1] Munster ridge and valley is defined by its geological structure, folded sandstone and limestone, because the folds gave alternating ridges and valleys.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.2] The Northern European Plain is defined by its relief and its surface deposits, glacial and loess, because it is flat and its surface is made of these materials.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.3] The Wicklow Mountains and the Leinster batholith (a huge mass of granite) are granite uplands, also classed as geomorphological regions; their landforms are taught in the notes on Landforms associated with particular rock types.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s3: 3. Cultural regions
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.b] A cultural region is an area where people share one or more cultural traits that set them apart from neighbouring areas. Language and religion are the traits the syllabus uses to define them.
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.0] Culture is the shared way of life of a group: its language, religion, customs, music, sport and sense of identity (feeling that you belong to that group).
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.1] Language and religion are the two cultural traits the syllabus uses. Both are easy to map because census data (the official count of the population, taken every few years) records them.
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.2] Cultural regions rarely match political borders (the lines between states). One state can contain several, as Belgium and Switzerland do, and one region can cross several states, as the Basque Country and the Islamic world do.
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.3] Their edges are usually transition zones, where the shared trait gradually thins out rather than stopping at a line, because people and customs mix across any boundary.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.b] The Gaeltacht is the officially recognised area where Irish is, or was, the community language (the language people use in everyday life). It is not one continuous region.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.0] It lies in seven counties: Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford and Meath.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.1] Main areas: Gaoth Dobhair (Gweedore), Co. Donegal; Conamara and the Aran Islands (Oileáin Árann), Co. Galway, the largest Gaeltacht area; Corca Dhuibhne (the Dingle Peninsula), Co. Kerry; Múscraí and Oileán Chléire (Cape Clear), Co. Cork; An Rinn (Ring), Co. Waterford; Ráth Chairn and Baile Ghib, Co. Meath.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.2] The pockets are scattered, mainly along the western seaboard (the Atlantic coast), rather than joined into one block.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.3] Remote, mountainous areas with poor farmland and poor communications (roads and transport links) had the least contact with English-speaking trade, towns and administration, which is why Irish survived there.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.4] The Gaeltacht therefore overlaps with the peripheral West of Ireland (the outlying, less developed area far from the main centres of population and wealth). There is more on this in the socio-economic regions subsection.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.5] The State created a cultural region deliberately: the two Meath Gaeltachtaí (Gaeltacht areas) were formed in the 1930s by resettling Irish-speaking families from Connemara, Ráth Chairn among them.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.b] The State backs the Irish language with laws, institutions and media, because the Gaeltacht is small and under pressure. Several trends still threaten it.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.0] Irish is the first official language under Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution, which gives the language legal standing.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.1] Údarás na Gaeltachta, set up in 1980, promotes employment and the language in the Gaeltacht, so that people have jobs and can stay in their communities.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.2] Raidió na Gaeltachta (1972) and TG4 (first broadcast in 1996 as Teilifís na Gaeilge) broadcast in Irish, so people can hear and watch their language daily.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.3] State language policy is also set out in the Official Languages Act 2003.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.4] Irish-medium schools (where lessons are taught in Irish) and summer colleges bring learners into the Gaeltacht and sustain the language there.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.5] Threats: out-migration of young people (leaving to find work), in-migration of English speakers and holiday homes, and English-language media and tourism, all of which bring English into daily life.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.6] Census 2022 (whole State): about 1.9 million people said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system. This shows a big gap between ability and everyday use.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.7] The Gaeltacht has shrunk and been redrawn since 1850, so its boundaries have changed. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.b] Belgium is split by a language border that runs east to west across the country, creating three language areas and a bilingual capital.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.0] Flanders, in the north: Dutch-speaking. Flemish is the Dutch spoken in Flanders.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.1] Wallonia, in the south: French-speaking, because it lies on the Latin-speaking side of the border.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.2] A small German-speaking community in the east, around Eupen, so Belgium has three language areas in all.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.3] The Brussels-Capital Region is officially bilingual (French and Dutch). It lies inside Flanders but is mostly French-speaking.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.4] The border marks the old boundary between Germanic-speaking and Latin-speaking peoples (the two language families of northern and southern Europe), and was fixed by law in 1962–63.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.5] More than half of Belgians live in Flanders.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.b] Because the two main language groups live in separate areas, language shapes how Belgium is governed and how its people live. It also lines up with a difference in wealth.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.0] Belgium became a federal state in 1993, which means power is shared between a central government and regional and community governments.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.1] It has three regions (Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels-Capital) and three language communities (Flemish, French, German-speaking), each with its own parliament.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.2] Schools, media and political parties are divided by language, so the two main groups have separate public lives.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.3] In 1968 the university at Leuven split: its French-speaking section moved to a new town, Louvain-la-Neuve, in Wallonia.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.4] After the 2010 election Belgium went 541 days without a new government, because Flemish and French-speaking parties could not agree.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.5] The language divide matches an economic divide: Flanders, with the port of Antwerp and newer industries, became the wealthier region, and many Flemish people resent money transferred to Wallonia, whose coal and steel industries in the Sambre–Meuse valley declined.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.b] Several other European regions are defined mainly by language. Exams use them in matching questions, so know what marks each one out.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.0] The Basque Country (Euskara): Basque is unrelated to any other European language, and the region crosses the border of Spain and France, so one cultural region lies in two states.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.1] Catalonia, north-east Spain: Catalan is the language and Barcelona is the capital. An unofficial independence referendum (a public vote on breaking away from Spain) was held there on 1 October 2017.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.2] Wales (Welsh) and Brittany (Breton) are Celtic-language regions, so each is defined by a Celtic language such as Welsh or Breton.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.3] Switzerland has four national languages: German, French, Italian and Romansh, so it holds several language regions within one state.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.b] Religion divides Ireland in two ways. In the Republic it is changing as the Catholic share falls, and in Northern Ireland it is tied to politics and to where people live.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.0] The Republic is mainly Roman Catholic, but in Census 2022 about 69% were Roman Catholic, down from about 78% in Census 2016.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.1] About 14% said they had no religion in Census 2022, and that share is rising.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.2] Immigration has added other religions, such as Orthodox Christians and Muslims, because people bring their faith with them.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.3] The Plantation of Ulster (the settling of Ulster with colonists), begun in 1609, brought Protestant settlers from Britain and created a Protestant majority in much of the north-east of Ulster.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.4] Religion became tied to political identity: mostly Catholic nationalists (who want a united Ireland) and mostly Protestant unionists (who want to stay in the United Kingdom).
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.5] In Belfast, religion defines segregated residential areas, separated in places by peace walls (peace lines), barriers built between the neighbourhoods.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.6] Census 2021 showed, for the first time, more Catholics than Protestants in Northern Ireland.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.7] The partition (the division of Ireland), the Troubles and the Good Friday Agreement shaped this situation. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.b] The Islamic world is a region defined by religion that stretches from Morocco to Indonesia. To explain it, you need to know where Islam began, how far it reaches and how it spread.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.0] Islam was founded by the Prophet Muhammad in Arabia in the 7th century. Mecca and Medina, in present-day Saudi Arabia, are its holiest cities.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.1] The Islamic calendar begins with the Hijra, Muhammad's move from Mecca to Medina, in 622.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.2] The Islamic world stretches from Morocco in North Africa, through the Middle East and Central Asia, to Pakistan, Bangladesh and Indonesia.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.3] Indonesia has the largest Muslim population of any country, so Islam is not confined to the Arab world.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.4] Muslims are almost a quarter of the world's population, so Islam is the second-largest religion after Christianity.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.5] Arab conquest carried Islam across North Africa and into Spain, beginning in 711; trade routes carried it to South and South-East Asia; the Ottoman Turks later carried it into the Balkans (the region of south-east Europe).
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.b] A religion makes a region when shared beliefs and practices leave visible and lasting marks on the lives and landscape of the people. In the Islamic world these are the following.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.0] A shared faith built on the Five Pillars (the five duties of a Muslim): declaration of faith; prayer five times a day, facing Mecca; giving to charity; fasting in Ramadan; and the pilgrimage to Mecca (the Hajj).
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.1] Arabic is the language of the Qur'an (the holy book of Islam), read across the region, so it unites people who speak different everyday languages.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.2] Mosques (places of worship) and minarets (the towers beside them) mark the landscape.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.3] Islamic law (Sharia) shapes the laws of some states, so religion affects government as well as worship.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.4] Shared customs of dress, food and calendar, such as the Islamic calendar that begins with the Hijra, make everyday life similar across the region.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.5] Sunni Muslims are the large majority overall. Shia Muslims are the majority in Iran and Iraq, and rivalry between the two branches of Islam adds to conflict in the Middle East.
[geo-region-concept-s3.9.b] The edges of the Islamic world are not fixed. History and migration have left Muslim communities beyond its core, and in some places Muslim rule has ended.
[geo-region-concept-s3.9.0] Ottoman rule (rule by the Ottoman Turks) left Muslim communities in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Albania and Kosovo, in the Balkans.
[geo-region-concept-s3.9.1] Spain was under Muslim (Moorish) rule until Granada fell to the Christian kingdoms in 1492.
[geo-region-concept-s3.9.2] Migration has created large Muslim communities in western Europe: North Africans in France, Turks in Germany, and Pakistanis and Bangladeshis in Britain.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.b] Europe divides into three broad Christian regions. Each has its roots in a split within the Church, and the divisions have sometimes led to conflict.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.0] Roman Catholic south and centre: Italy, Spain, Portugal, Poland, Ireland and southern Germany.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.1] Protestant north, so a region shaped by the Reformation: Scandinavia, northern Germany and Britain.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.2] Orthodox east and south-east: Greece, Russia, Romania and Serbia.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.3] The Catholic and Orthodox churches split, which is why Europe has two eastern and western Christian traditions, with Rome and Constantinople at the centre of the two traditions.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.4] The Protestant Reformation (the movement that broke away from the Catholic Church) began in Germany in 1517, with Martin Luther. Vatican City, in Rome, is the centre of the Catholic Church.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.5] The break-up of Yugoslavia in the 1990s set Orthodox Serbs, Catholic Croats and Muslim Bosniaks against each other in war in Bosnia, showing how religious differences can fuel conflict.
