[geo-population-migration-s1.0.b] Geographers describe a population with a small set of measures. Learn each definition exactly, because every later block uses them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.0] Crude birth rate: live births per 1,000 people per year. It is 'crude' because it counts the whole population, not just women of childbearing age.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.1] Crude death rate: deaths per 1,000 people per year.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.2] Natural change: birth rate minus death rate. A positive result is natural increase (more births than deaths); a negative result is natural decrease (more deaths than births).
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.3] Net migration: immigrants (people moving in) minus emigrants (people moving out). It is positive when more people arrive than leave.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.4] Total fertility rate: the average number of children per woman, which shows family size better than the crude birth rate.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.5] Replacement level: a total fertility rate of about 2.1 children per woman in developed countries, the level at which a population replaces itself without migration.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.6] Infant mortality rate: deaths of children under one year old per 1,000 live births.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.7] Life expectancy: the average number of years a newborn is expected to live.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.b] A population can only change in two ways: people are born or die, and people move in or out. The census, a regular count of everyone, shows which of the two drove the change.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.0] Population change equals natural change plus net migration. In any period between two censuses (an inter-censal period), the larger of the two components explains the growth or decline.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.1] The census is a count of every person on one night. In Ireland it is held every five years and run by the Central Statistics Office (CSO).
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.2] The Irish census records age, sex, place of residence, birthplace, nationality, religion, ethnicity, Irish language, housing, work and commuting.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.3] Census 2016 to Census 2022: the population grew by about 360,000 (about 7.6%). Natural increase contributed about 170,000 and net migration about 190,000, so net migration was the slightly larger component.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.b] The five-year rhythm of the Irish census has been broken a few times. Each break had a specific cause.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.0] The 1926 census was the first held by the Irish Free State (the Irish State of that time).
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.1] The 1976 census was cancelled; a special census was held in 1979 instead.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.2] The 2001 census was postponed to 2002 by foot-and-mouth disease (an animal disease outbreak).
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.3] The 2021 census was postponed to 2022 by COVID-19 and held on 3 April 2022.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.b] Governments collect census data because it tells them how many people there are, how old they are and where they live. That information shapes decisions on money and services.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.0] Planning schools, hospitals and housing: counts of children, patients and households show where services are needed.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.1] Pensions: the age structure (how many people are in each age group) sets the cost of pensions; the State pension age has been 66 since 2014.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.2] Transport and infrastructure: population size and location show where roads, rail and water services are needed, so money goes where demand is.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.3] Constituency boundaries: the population of each constituency (the area that elects TDs, members of the Dail) determines the number of TDs it elects.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.4] Funding and workforce planning: funding is allocated to regions by population, and population forecasts show future labour supply and the need for workers from abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.b] Two ideas describe where people live. They are different, so keep them apart: distribution is where people live. Density is the number of people per square kilometre of land.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.0] Distribution is the spread of people across an area, so a map can show crowded and empty places. Density turns that into a number you can compare.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.1] Density = population ÷ area in km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.2] Illustrative example (invented figures): a region of 400 km² with 120,000 people has a density of 120,000 ÷ 400 = 300 people per km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.b] Physical factors are features of the natural environment. They decide how easy it is to farm, build and live in a place.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.0] Relief (the shape and height of the land): lowlands attract more people than high mountains, which are costly to farm and build on.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.1] Climate: temperate climates (mild, without extremes) support dense settlement; places that are too hot, too cold or too dry have fewer people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.2] Soils and water supply: fertile soils and reliable water support farming and settlement.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.3] Natural resources: coalfields drew industrial populations in the 19th century, because mines and factories needed workers close by.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.b] Human factors are choices and developments made by people. They work alongside the physical factors to decide where populations cluster.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.0] Economic opportunity: jobs attract people, and regions with few jobs lose their young people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.1] Transport routes: roads, rail lines and navigable water (rivers deep enough for boats) attract settlement along them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.2] Cities and ports: cities and ports offer jobs and services, so they draw large populations.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.3] History: colonial ports and plantations (large farms for cash crops, set up by colonial powers) shaped where people settled.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.4] Government policy: policy can direct population into or out of particular areas.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.b] Low-density areas have few people per square kilometre. The reasons are mostly the opposite of the factors that attract people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.0] High, steep or cold land is difficult to farm, build on and reach.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.1] Deserts and dense forest offer little water, farmland or access.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.2] Poor soils and remoteness limit farming, and remote areas lack markets and services.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.3] Few jobs cause young people to leave, so the population falls and density drops.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.b] World population is very uneven. Most people crowd into a few regions, while large areas are almost empty.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.0] Dense: East Asia, South Asia, Europe, the north-eastern USA and the Nile valley, where farming, trade or industry support many people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.1] Sparse: the Sahara (a hot desert), the Amazon Basin (a vast rainforest), Siberia, northern Canada, Greenland and Antarctica.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.2] About 90% of the world's people live in the northern hemisphere.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.3] Most people live near coasts and rivers, where water and transport are easy to reach.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.b] Europe has the same unevenness on a smaller scale: a crowded core and a thinly settled edge.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.0] Dense core: the Benelux countries (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg), the Rhine-Ruhr (an industrial region of western Germany), south-east England, the Po valley (northern Italy) and the Paris region.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.1] Sparse periphery (the outer edge): northern Scandinavia, the Scottish Highlands and interior Spain.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.2] The Netherlands has over 500 people per km² (2020s), one of Europe's most densely populated countries.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.3] The EU average is about 110 people per km² (2020s), so the Netherlands is more than four times as dense.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.b] Ireland's people are not spread evenly. They gather around Dublin and the east coast, and thin out towards the west.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.0] The Greater Dublin Area (Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow) has about 2.1 million people, about 40% of the State's population (Census 2022).
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.1] Co. Dublin has about 1.46 million people at about 1,600 people per km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.2] The population is concentrated in the east and in the five cities, where jobs and services are found.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.3] Low density in the west and north-west, notably Mayo (about 25 people per km²), Leitrim and Roscommon, and in the uplands (high ground).
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.b] Today's eastern, urban pattern is not how Ireland always looked.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.0] Before the Famine (the Great Famine of 1845-49) the population was rural and weighted to the west.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.1] Since then the population has shifted to an eastern, urban pattern.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.2] Dublin's commuter counties of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow (counties whose people travel into Dublin to work) have grown rapidly.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.3] Every county's population grew between 2016 and 2022.
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.b] Density is population divided by area, so it rises when people are added to an area that does not grow.
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.0] The State's average density is about 73 people per km² (Census 2022).
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.1] Density is rising because the population has grown from natural increase (more births than deaths) and net immigration since the 1990s, while the land area is fixed.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.b] For most of history the world's population grew very slowly. Then growth took off, and it is only now easing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.0] World population grew slowly until about 1800, then rapidly, producing an exponential J-curve (a graph line that climbs ever more steeply, shaped like a J) driven by falling death rates. The growth rate is now slowing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.1] Milestones: about 1 billion around 1800, 2 billion in 1927, 3 billion in 1960 and 4 billion in 1974.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.2] Milestones continued: 5 billion in 1987, 6 billion in 1999, 7 billion in 2011 and 8 billion on 15 November 2022 (UN).
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.3] Global fertility is about 2.3 children per woman (early 2020s), down from about 5 in the 1950s.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.4] China and India each have over 1.4 billion people (2023); India overtook China as the most populous country in 2023 (UN estimate).
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.b] Growth is not even across the world. Some regions are growing fast while others have stopped growing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.0] Rapid growth: sub-Saharan Africa (the part of Africa south of the Sahara), where fertility is high.
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.1] Slow growth, stagnation (no growth) or decline: Europe and East Asia, where fertility is low.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.b] The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) shows how birth and death rates, and so population growth, change as a country develops. It has five stages, and stage 1 is where every society starts.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.0] Birth rate high, death rate high, and little growth. 'Stationary' means the population stays roughly the same size because births and deaths nearly cancel out.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.1] Example: no country is in stage 1 today; the best examples are pre-industrial societies (societies before factories and modern medicine).
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.b] In stage 2 the death rate drops quickly while births stay high, so the population expands fast. This stage is where the death rate falls most.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.0] Birth rate high; death rate falling rapidly, and the death rate falls most in this stage; growth is rapid because many more people are born than die.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.1] The death rate falls because of clean water and sanitation (safe disposal of sewage), vaccination, a better food supply and medical care.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.2] Example: Niger, because its death rate has fallen while its birth rate stays very high.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.b] In stage 3 the birth rate begins to fall too, so the gap between births and deaths narrows and growth slows.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.0] Birth rate falling; death rate low; growth slows because births are no longer far above deaths.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.1] Example: India, with a total fertility rate of about 2.0 (2019-21).
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.b] By stage 4 both rates are low. The population is large but steady, because births and deaths nearly balance.
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.0] Birth rate low, death rate low, and little growth.
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.1] Examples: Ireland and the USA. Ireland is in stage 4.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.b] Stage 5 goes beyond the original steady state: the birth rate drops below the death rate, so the population begins to shrink.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.0] Birth rate below death rate, so natural change is negative (natural decrease) and the population falls.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.1] Examples: Germany, Italy and Japan.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.b] Niger is the example of stage 2: very high fertility and a population dominated by children and teenagers.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.0] Niger is a West African, landlocked (no coastline) country, largely in the Sahel (the dry belt south of the Sahara) and Sahara.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.1] Population is about 27 million (2023), growing by over 3% a year (early 2020s).
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.2] Total fertility rate is about 6 children per woman (early 2020s), among the highest in the world.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.3] Median age (the age that splits the population into a younger half and an older half) is about 15, among the lowest in the world.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.4] Its population pyramid (a graph of the population by age and sex) has a very wide base, because so many children are born.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.5] Most people live in the south, where rainfall allows farming; the north is Sahara desert.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.b] Japan is the opposite of Niger. It has very few births and many older people, so its population is falling.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.0] Japan has the world's oldest population: about 29% of people are aged 65 or over (2023), the highest share in the world.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.1] Population peaked at about 128 million around 2008-2010, was about 124 million in 2024, and is falling.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.2] Total fertility rate is about 1.2 (2023), well below the replacement level of about 2.1.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.3] Few immigrants by European standards, so immigration does little to offset the fall.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.4] Its population pyramid is top-heavy, with a narrow base (few children) and a wide top (many older people).
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.b] Moving from stage 2 to stage 3 means families choose to have fewer children. Several changes in society make smaller families sensible.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.0] Female education and paid work: women have later and fewer children.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.1] Contraception (methods of preventing pregnancy) is widely available, so couples can choose family size.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.2] Falling infant mortality removes the need for 'insurance' births (having extra children in case some die), as parents can expect their children to survive.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.3] Urbanisation (growth of towns and cities): children cost more and earn less in towns and cities, unlike on farms where they can work.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.4] Pensions replace children as old-age security, so parents no longer need many children to support them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.5] Later marriage, and weaker religious influence on family size.
[geo-population-migration-s1.23.b] A country with falling numbers and a rising share of older people faces economic strain. Governments have a few ways to respond.
[geo-population-migration-s1.23.0] Challenges: a shrinking workforce, labour shortages, rising pension and health costs, closing schools and rural depopulation (the loss of people from the countryside).
[geo-population-migration-s1.23.1] Responses: immigration, pro-natalist incentives (rewards that encourage couples to have more children) to raise birth rates, and later retirement.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.b] The DTM is a useful simplification, but it does not fit every country. Know why.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.0] The model is based on European experience, so it may not describe other regions.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.1] It ignores migration, although moving people can change a country's population sharply.

[geo-population-migration-s1.24.2] Developing countries' death rates fell much faster than Europe's, because medicine was imported, so their growth has been faster.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.3] Some countries may not complete the transition, and it has no fixed time scale.
[geo-population-migration-s1.25.b] A population pyramid is a graph that shows how a population is made up by age and sex. Planners and geographers read it to understand a country at a glance.
[geo-population-migration-s1.25.0] A population pyramid shows age cohorts (groups of people of similar age) in five-year bands, with males on the left and females on the right, as percentages or numbers.
[geo-population-migration-s1.25.1] Uses: planning services, forecasting future needs, comparing countries, and showing past events such as baby booms, wars and emigration.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.b] The overall shape of a pyramid tells you the stage a country has reached: how many children are born and how long people live.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.0] Wide base and narrow top: high fertility and short life expectancy, as in Niger.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.1] Straight sides: a stable population with low birth and death rates.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.2] Narrow base and wide top (top-heavy): an ageing population, as in Japan.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.3] A developed country's pyramid is typically straight-sided or top-heavy, while a developing country's is wide-based.
[geo-population-migration-s1.27.b] Beyond the overall shape, bulges and notches in the sides record events in a country's past.
[geo-population-migration-s1.27.0] Bulges (a band wider than those around it) show baby booms or immigration of young adults.
[geo-population-migration-s1.27.1] Notches (a band narrower than those around it) show emigration, low birth years or war.
[geo-population-migration-s1.27.2] More women than men at the oldest ages, because women live longer.
[geo-population-migration-s1.28.b] Children and older people usually do not earn a wage, so they depend on the working-age population. The dependency ratio measures how heavy that load is.
[geo-population-migration-s1.28.0] Dependency ratio = (people aged 0-14 + people aged 65 and over) ÷ people aged 15-64 × 100.
[geo-population-migration-s1.28.1] Young dependency ratio = people aged 0-14 ÷ people aged 15-64 × 100. Old-age dependency ratio = people aged 65 and over ÷ people aged 15-64 × 100.
[geo-population-migration-s1.28.2] Illustrative example (invented figures): 30,000 aged 0-14, 10,000 aged 65 and over and 80,000 aged 15-64 give (30,000 + 10,000) ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 50.
[geo-population-migration-s1.29.b] The ratio is not the same everywhere inside a country. Migration within a country changes who lives in towns and who lives in the countryside.
[geo-population-migration-s1.29.0] Urban dependency ratios are usually lower than rural ones.
[geo-population-migration-s1.29.1] Young working adults migrate to cities for jobs and college, leaving older people, and sometimes children, in rural areas, which raises the share of dependants there.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.b] When the old-age dependency ratio (the number of people aged 65 and over for every 100 people aged 15-64) rises, there are more people to support and fewer workers to pay for it.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.0] Higher pension costs, because more people claim pensions for longer.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.1] More demand for health care, nursing homes and carers, because older people need more care.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.2] A smaller tax base (the pool of workers paying tax) from fewer workers.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.3] Pressure to raise the pension age, so that people work and pay tax for longer.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.4] Changing housing and transport needs, for example more homes suited to older people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.5] Planning responses in Ireland: home-care supports, pension reform, more geriatric services (health care for older people), encouraging immigration of workers, and age-friendly towns.
[geo-population-migration-s1.31.b] A country with many children has the opposite pressure: it must provide for the young now, but it gains workers later.
[geo-population-migration-s1.31.0] Demand for schools, maternity and child health care, and for future jobs and housing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.31.1] A future workforce, which is the basis of the demographic dividend (the economic boost a country can get when many young people grow up and start working). There is more on this in the overpopulation notes.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.b] Birth rates differ between countries because of economic and social conditions. These factors link income, culture and society.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.0] Income and economic development: birth rates fall as income rises.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.1] Status and education of women: educated women with status tend to have fewer children.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.2] Religion and culture, because beliefs and traditions shape ideas of a normal family size.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.3] Access to contraception, because it lets couples plan how many children to have.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.4] Government policy, for example rules or incentives that encourage or discourage births.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.5] Infant mortality: high infant mortality encourages larger families, because parents have more children to be sure some survive.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.6] Urban or rural living: rural families tend to have more children.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.b] Death rates depend on how healthy and safe people's lives are. The main causes of death differ between rich and poor countries.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.0] Medical technology: vaccines and antibiotics (medicines that kill bacteria), which prevent or cure diseases that once killed many people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.1] Sanitation, diet, income and health services, because clean water, enough food and access to care all help people survive.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.2] Lifestyle diseases such as heart disease and cancer are the main causes of death in rich countries.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.3] Conflict, famine and disease, such as malaria and HIV/AIDS, are the main causes of death in poor countries.

[geo-population-migration-s1.34.2] The result is an older population with a higher median age.

[geo-population-migration-s1.35.2] Mosquito nets and malaria treatment, because mosquitoes spread malaria.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.3] More doctors and clinics, so illness is treated sooner.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.4] Better nutrition, so people are stronger against disease.

[geo-population-migration-s1.36.0] A good diet and housing, which keep people healthy.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.1] Effective health services and vaccination, which treat and prevent illness.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.2] Clean water, so water-borne disease is rare.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.3] Fewer smokers, so fewer deaths from smoking-related disease.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.4] Safer work, so fewer deaths from accidents.

[geo-population-migration-s1.40.3] More women in paid employment, later marriage and later first births.

[geo-population-migration-s1.41.b] Life expectancy rose and infant mortality fell to very low levels.
[geo-population-migration-s1.41.0] Life expectancy at birth is about 82-83 years (early 2020s).
[geo-population-migration-s1.41.1] Women live about 4 years longer than men, which is why they outnumber men at the oldest ages.
[geo-population-migration-s1.41.2] Infant mortality is about 3 per 1,000 live births (2020s).
[geo-population-migration-s1.42.b] Migration is the second component of population change. Irish migration switched direction several times, and the next points give only the outline.
[geo-population-migration-s1.42.0] Heavy net emigration (more people leaving than arriving) in the 1950s and 1980s; net emigration of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961, which the 1954 Report of the Commission on Emigration and Other Population Problems examined.
[geo-population-migration-s1.42.1] Net immigration in the 1970s, from the mid-1990s to 2008, and again from the mid-2010s. There is more on this in Changing migration patterns in Ireland.
[geo-population-migration-s1.43.b] As fertility fell and people lived longer, Ireland's population got older. It also became more diverse.
[geo-population-migration-s1.43.0] Census 2022: about 776,000 people aged 65 and over (about 15% of the population), up about 22% since 2016.
[geo-population-migration-s1.43.1] Average age rose from 37.4 in 2016 to 38.8 in 2022.
[geo-population-migration-s1.43.2] About one-fifth of the population is under 15.
[geo-population-migration-s1.43.3] The pyramid has moved from a young, wide-based shape to an ageing one.
[geo-population-migration-s1.43.4] About one in five people (just over 1 million) was born outside Ireland.
[geo-population-migration-s1.44.b] Where and how Irish people live has changed as well as how many they are.
[geo-population-migration-s1.44.0] Census 1971 was the first census in which more people lived in towns than in rural areas.
[geo-population-migration-s1.44.1] By Census 2016 about 63% of the population was urban.
[geo-population-migration-s1.44.2] The population has shifted to the east. There is more on this in Rural-to-urban migration in developed and developing regions.
[geo-population-migration-s1.44.3] Households are smaller, and more people live alone, for example because families are smaller and people live longer.
[geo-population-migration-s1.45.b] The EU shows the ageing pattern across a whole group of countries: few births, long lives and growth that comes from outside.
[geo-population-migration-s1.45.0] The EU has 27 member states and about 450 million people (2024).
[geo-population-migration-s1.45.1] Fertility is below replacement: the EU total fertility rate is about 1.4 (2023), well under 2.1, so each generation is smaller.
[geo-population-migration-s1.45.2] Natural decrease occurs in many member states; immigration supplies most of the EU's growth.
[geo-population-migration-s1.45.3] The EU median age is about 44.5 (2023), and about 21% of its population is aged 65 or over (2023).
[geo-population-migration-s1.46.b] Germany is a stage 5 example in the EU, where the population would shrink without incoming people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.46.0] Deaths have exceeded births every year since 1972, so Germany has natural decrease.
[geo-population-migration-s1.46.1] Population is about 84 million (2020s), and growth comes from immigration.
[geo-population-migration-s1.47.b] Italy is another stage 5 country, with one of the EU's weakest birth rates and oldest populations.
[geo-population-migration-s1.47.0] Italy has among the EU's lowest fertility rates and oldest populations: its total fertility rate is about 1.2 (2023).
[geo-population-migration-s1.47.1] Population is about 59 million (2024), so Italy is a large country with an ageing base.
[geo-population-migration-s1.47.2] There is more on Italy's regional pattern in A Peripheral European Region: the Mezzogiorno.
[geo-population-migration-s1.48.b] Bulgaria shows a population that shrinks for two reasons at once: few births and people leaving.
[geo-population-migration-s1.48.0] Population was about 9 million in 1989 and about 6.5 million at Census 2021.
[geo-population-migration-s1.48.1] Causes: emigration to western Europe after the fall of communism (1989) and EU entry (2007), together with low fertility.
[geo-population-migration-s1.49.b] Fertility can fall far below replacement level, and the contrast with the highest rates is stark.
[geo-population-migration-s1.49.0] South Korea's total fertility rate is about 0.7 (2023), the lowest in the world.
[geo-population-migration-s1.49.1] Compare Niger's total fertility rate of about 6 children per woman, which shows how far apart countries can be.

CANDIDATES geo-graph-skills-s2: Census of population data
[geo-graph-skills-s2.0.b] A census of population is an official count of every person in the country on one night, with information collected on each person and household. Because everyone is counted on the same night, the results give a comparable picture of the whole country.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.0.0] In Ireland the census is carried out by the Central Statistics Office (CSO, the State's statistics agency), and taking part is required by law, so every household must complete the form.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.0.1] Ireland's census is de facto (based on where people are on the night): it counts everyone present in the State on census night, including visitors, wherever they usually live. The form also records each person's usual residence, so results can be given both ways.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.0.2] The census is normally held every five years on a Sunday night in April. It used to fall in years ending in 1 and 6, but the 2021 census was postponed to 2022, so the next is in 2027.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.0.3] The 2001 census was postponed to 2002 because of foot-and-mouth disease (an animal disease outbreak).
[geo-graph-skills-s2.0.4] The 2021 census was postponed to 2022 because of COVID-19. The next census is planned for 2027.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.0.5] The first census of the Irish Free State (the State as it was from 1922) was held in 1926.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.1.b] Counting a whole country on one night needs a careful system for delivering and collecting forms, and people will only answer honestly if their details are protected.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.1.0] Enumerators (field staff who visit homes) deliver a household form to every dwelling before census night and collect it afterwards.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.1.1] One form is completed per household, with a page for each person in it, so each person's details are recorded separately.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.1.2] Communal establishments (places where people live together) such as hotels, hospitals and prisons are covered separately, and vacant dwellings (empty homes) are recorded, so that every building is accounted for.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.1.3] Information is used only for statistics, and personal forms are kept closed for 100 years, which protects people's privacy.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.1.4] After 100 years the returns become historical records. The 1901 and 1911 census returns are freely available online from the National Archives of Ireland and are used for family history.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.2.b] The form asks about people, work, housing and the household, which gives planners the information they need on how people live.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.2.0] Personal topics: age, sex and marital status; relationships within the household; place of birth, citizenship, ethnicity and religion; ability to speak Irish; other languages; education level.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.2.1] Work topics: occupation, industry and unemployment; commuting (the daily journey to work or school), covering means of travel and journey time.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.2.2] Housing topics: type, age and tenure of the home (tenure means how it is held: owned with or without a mortgage, or rented); rent paid; heating; vacancy.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.2.3] Household topics: internet access, car ownership, health and disability, and carers (people who look after others).
[geo-graph-skills-s2.2.4] Census 2022 asked for the first time about working from home and about renewable energy sources at the home, such as solar panels.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.2.5] Census 2022 included an optional time capsule page whose contents will be released after 100 years.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.3.b] Only a few national totals are needed, and each must be tied to the census it comes from. Take care to keep the Republic's figures apart from Northern Ireland's.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.3.0] Census 2011: about 4.59 million people.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.3.1] Census 2016, held on 24 April 2016: about 4.76 million people.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.3.2] Census 2022, held on 3 April 2022: 5,149,139, about 5.15 million people.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.3.3] Between the 2016 and 2022 censuses the population rose by about 387,000, about 8%.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.3.4] Northern Ireland has its own census, run by NISRA (Northern Ireland Statistics and Research Agency). Its latest was held on 21 March 2021 and counted about 1.9 million people.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.3.5] Ireland's census figures cover the State, not the island. The Republic's latest census was 2022 and Northern Ireland's was 2021, so their figures must not be merged.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.4.b] Results come out in stages and at several scales, from the whole State down to a few streets, so that users can find the level of detail they need.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.4.0] Preliminary results are published within months of census night, because they are quick to produce; detailed results by theme follow over the next year or two.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.4.1] Results are published for the State, the provinces, counties, cities, towns, Electoral Divisions (EDs, small local administrative areas) and Small Areas.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.4.2] Small Areas are the smallest units, roughly a street or estate.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.4.3] Results appear in online tables, interactive maps on the CSO website, county profiles and infographics (pictures and charts that summarise figures).
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.b] Governments and businesses plan ahead using census numbers, because the data shows how many people of each kind live where.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.0] Schools: the number of young children shows how many school places are needed, so new schools can be built where children live.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.1] Healthcare and nursing homes: the size of the older population shows the places needed, because older people use these services most.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.2] Housing: household numbers and vacancy show how many homes are needed, so planners can tell whether to build more.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.3] Transport: commuting flows (how many people travel from where to where) show where roads and public transport are needed, because they reveal busy routes.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.4] Pensions and social welfare: the age structure shows future demand, because more older people means more pensions to be paid.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.5] Government targets resources at disadvantaged areas, which the figures help to identify, and plans regional and county development.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.6] Businesses use the figures to choose where to locate shops and services, so that they open where there are enough customers.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.5.7] Researchers and students use the data; tracking the Irish language and historical and genealogical (family history) research use census returns.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.6.b] The census also decides how Ireland's Dáil (the lower house of parliament) is divided into constituencies, the areas whose voters elect TDs (Teachtaí Dála, members of the Dáil).
[geo-graph-skills-s2.6.0] Dáil constituencies are reviewed after each census so that each TD represents roughly the same number of people, which keeps votes fair across the country.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.6.1] The Constitution requires one TD for every 20,000 to 30,000 people.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.6.2] Constituencies must be revised at least once every 12 years, with regard to census figures.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.6.3] The Electoral Commission (An Coimisiún Toghcháin) carries out the review after each census.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.7.b] A census is a powerful source but not a perfect one, so any conclusions drawn from it need to be treated with care. Other sources fill the gaps between censuses.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.7.0] A census is a snapshot of one night, so it is out of date between censuses, and detailed results take a long time to appear.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.7.1] There may be an undercount (fewer people recorded than really live there): homeless people and people avoiding the count can be missed.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.7.2] Answers are self-reported (people fill in the form themselves) and may be inaccurate.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.7.3] A census is very expensive and raises privacy concerns, because it collects personal details about every household.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.7.4] Electoral Divisions and Small Areas change over time, which makes comparisons between censuses difficult because the areas are no longer the same.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.7.5] Between censuses, civil registration (the official recording of life events) records births, deaths and marriages; the CSO publishes annual population and migration estimates; the Labour Force Survey (a regular sample survey of work and employment) gives other population data.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.8.b] Demographic structure is the make-up of a population by age, sex and other characteristics, such as marital status, nationality or ethnicity, religion, language, education, occupation, household size and urban or rural location.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.8.0] Age structure has three groups: young dependents (0 to 14), working age (15 to 64) and old dependents (65 and over). Dependents are people who are mostly supported by the working-age group.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.8.1] Dependency ratio = (0 to 14 plus 65 and over) ÷ (15 to 64) × 100. It divides by the working-age group, not by the total population.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.8.2] A high young dependency ratio means many schools are needed; a high old dependency ratio means many pensions and healthcare places are needed.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.8.3] Illustrative Rivertown ED, later census: 0 to 14 = 1,050; 15 to 64 = 3,120; 65 and over = 660; total 4,830.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.8.4] Illustrative dependency ratio = (1,050 + 660) ÷ 3,120 × 100 = 54.8, about 55 dependents per 100 people of working age.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.8.5] Illustrative share aged 65 and over = 660 ÷ 4,830 × 100 = 13.7%.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.8.6] Census data is drawn as a population pyramid (a bar chart of age and sex groups) in the way taught in the notes on Figure interpretation; the meaning of pyramid shapes is taught in the notes on Changing population characteristics.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.9.b] Sex structure compares the numbers of males and females. It is measured with a ratio, and it can show the effects of life expectancy and migration.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.9.0] Sex ratio = males ÷ females × 100, giving the number of males per 100 females.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.9.1] Women outnumber men in the older age groups because of longer life expectancy (women tend to live longer).
[geo-graph-skills-s2.9.2] Local imbalances arise from migration, e.g. young women moving to cities for work.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.9.3] Illustrative Rivertown ED, later census: males 2,390 and females 2,440 (total 4,830); sex ratio = 2,390 ÷ 2,440 × 100 = 98 males per 100 females.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.10.b] A population changes in two ways: people are born and die, and people move in and out. Splitting the change into these two parts shows which is driving it.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.10.0] Population change = natural increase + net migration.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.10.1] Natural increase = births minus deaths, so it is positive when more babies are born than people die.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.10.2] Net migration = immigrants (people moving in) minus emigrants (people moving out).
[geo-graph-skills-s2.10.3] Comparing two censuses, with births and deaths from civil registration (the official record of births and deaths), gives the estimated net migration: net migration = total change minus natural increase.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.10.4] Illustrative Rivertown ED, six years apart: population 4,200 at the earlier census and 4,830 at the later census, a change of +630.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.10.5] Illustrative percentage change = 630 ÷ 4,200 × 100 = 15%.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.10.6] Illustrative: births 380 and deaths 210 give a natural increase of 170, so net migration = 630 − 170 = +460, meaning more people moved in than out.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.11.b] Raw totals are hard to compare between places of different size, so geographers also use measures that scale the figures, such as rates per 1,000 people.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.11.0] Crude birth rate and crude death rate are the births and deaths per 1,000 people per year. They are called crude because they use the whole population without adjusting for age.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.11.1] Population density is the number of people per km², so it shows how crowded an area is.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.11.2] Percentage of the population in an age group = people in the group ÷ total population × 100.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.11.3] Illustrative Rivertown ED, census year: 65 births; crude birth rate = 65 ÷ 4,830 × 1,000 = 13.5 per 1,000.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.12.b] The census totals show a long-term pattern for the State: a fall, a low point and then growth. Only the headline trend is given here.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.12.0] The State's population fell steeply after the Great Famine (the potato failure of the 1840s) and was at its lowest in the 1961 census, at about 2.8 million.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.12.1] For comparison, the population of the whole island was about 8.2 million in 1841.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.12.2] After 1961 the population grew, and Census 2022 was the first to show it passing 5 million since the 1851 census.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.12.3] Present features: growth driven by both natural increase and immigration; an ageing population, with a growing share aged 65 and over; rising cultural diversity, with many non-Irish citizens.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.12.4] Demographic transition (the shift in birth and death rates as a country develops), fertility and mortality change and Ireland's twentieth-century history are in the notes on Changing population characteristics and the notes on Changing migration patterns in Ireland.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.12.5] Migration patterns, policy and reasons are in the migration notes, from Changing migration patterns in Ireland to Rural-to-urban migration.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.13.b] A local-area study applies the census to a place you know, by following a fixed sequence from choosing the area to judging the data. Following the steps in order keeps the study complete.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.13.0] Step 1: choose the area, an Electoral Division, Small Area, town or county.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.13.1] Step 2: find its census tables for two or more censuses on the CSO website or interactive map, so that you can compare change over time.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.13.2] Step 3: record the total population, age groups, sex, households, nationality and commuting, because these are the figures the calculations in step 4 need.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.13.3] Step 4: calculate the changes (number and percentage), the dependency ratio, the sex ratio and the share in each age group.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.13.4] Step 5: present the results with graphs: a line graph of population over several censuses, bar or pie charts of age groups, a population pyramid if five-year age bands are available, or a choropleth map (a map shaded by value) of Electoral Divisions.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.13.5] Step 6: describe the trends; step 7: explain them; step 8: note the limitations of the data.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.13.6] Illustrative Rivertown: a line graph of population over several censuses (any earlier values are invented and must be labelled illustrative), a bar or pie chart of the three age groups, and a pyramid only if five-year bands are available.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.14.b] Once a trend is described, the next step is to give reasons for it. Local trends usually come from housing, jobs and migration.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.14.0] New housing estates and commuter growth near cities and along motorways bring rising population, young families and a high young dependency ratio.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.14.1] Rural decline in remote areas brings a falling population, an ageing population and more single-person households, because younger people tend to leave.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.14.2] Other causes include immigration, the closure or opening of a major employer, and town-centre decline set against suburban growth.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.14.3] Illustrative Rivertown: the population growth and high young dependency fit a new housing estate on the edge of town (the aerial photograph of Rivertown shows one; see the notes on Aerial photograph interpretation) that attracts young families who commute, together with positive net migration.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.14.4] There is more on this in the notes on Rural-to-urban migration in developed and developing regions and the Urban Geography chapter.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.15.b] Two further uses of the census link to other parts of the course: language and the study of how cities spread.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.15.0] The census records ability to speak Irish and how often it is spoken: daily within the education system, daily outside it, weekly, less often, or never. The cultural detail is in the notes on Language as a cultural indicator.
[geo-graph-skills-s2.15.1] Census data is also used in the GIS (geographical information systems, computer mapping) study of urban sprawl (the outward spread of a city into the countryside); there is more on this in the notes on Geographical information systems (GIS).

CANDIDATES geo-graph-skills-s1: Figure interpretation
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.b] Every figure question starts the same way: read the labels and units before reading any value. Most lost marks come from a wrong unit, year or column, because a correct reading of the wrong one still scores nothing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.0] Check the title, both axis labels (the words along the horizontal and vertical lines), the key or legend (the box explaining colours or symbols), the units (%, thousands, millions, €m, hectares, mm, °C, per 1,000), the years shown and the source line. Each one changes what a number means.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.1] Read a bar or line value by running a ruler from the top of the bar or point across to the vertical axis, estimating between gridlines, and quoting the value with its unit.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.2] In a table, find the row and the column and read the cell where they meet. Check the totals row before using a figure, so you know whether a number is a part or the whole.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.3] Compare two years by reading neighbouring columns. To spot the one that did not change, read across the whole row, because the unchanged value is the one that stays the same between years.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.4] For highest and lowest, scan every value, not just the first and last, because the extreme can sit in the middle. For a ranking, list the values from largest to smallest.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.5] To count how many items are above an average line, count the bars or points whose tops lie above it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.6] For 'twice as much', double the smaller value and look for the value that matches it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.7] An infographic (a single page combining headline numbers, icons, small charts and county maps) has several panels. Read each panel's own label and unit: headline figures are often totals, while panels may show percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.b] A description states what the figure shows. Using exact trend words and quoting numbers turns a vague answer into a complete one.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.0] Trend vocabulary: increase, rise or growth; decrease, fall or decline; steady, constant or no change; fluctuate (go up and down); peak (highest point); trough (lowest point); gradual or rapid (sharp); levelling off (the change slows and flattens).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.1] The overall trend is the long-term direction. A short-term change, such as a single year's dip, is described separately so it does not hide the main pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.2] To describe a trend, state the overall direction, the start and end values with their years and units, and the period of fastest change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.3] Name any peak, trough or reversal (a change of direction), and quantify the change as the difference, the percentage change, or both.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.4] Point out anomalies: values that break the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.b] Once a trend is described, the next marks come from comparing it with another, saying why it happened and judging whether the figure can be trusted.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.0] To compare two series (two lines or two sets of bars), say which is higher, when the lines cross, whether the gap widens or narrows, and the difference in a given year. For example, net migration = immigration minus emigration.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.1] To explain a trend, link the pattern to one cause from the relevant chapter, in one clause. Explaining means saying why the figure looks as it does, not just what it shows.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.2] To evaluate a figure, check whether an axis that does not start at zero or has uneven intervals exaggerates change, because a small rise then looks large.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.3] Also check whether unevenly spaced dates distort the trend, whether the sample (the group measured) is small, and whether the source is reliable and recent.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.4] Percentages can hide small totals: a large percentage rise may be a small change in numbers, because a small starting number is easy to double.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.b] These worked values come from the invented (illustrative) population of six towns, in thousands: Town A 18, Town B 32, Town C 25, Town D 41, Town E 12, Town F 29. Each statistic has a short method that you can repeat on any data set.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.0] Total (sum): add every value. 18 + 32 + 25 + 41 + 12 + 29 = 157 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.1] Difference: subtract the smaller value from the larger and say which is larger. Town B (32) exceeds Town C (25) by 7 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.2] Mean (average) = total of the values ÷ number of values: 157 ÷ 6 = 26.2 thousand, to one decimal place (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.3] Median (the middle value): put the values in order (12, 18, 25, 29, 32, 41); with an even number of values, average the middle two: (25 + 29) ÷ 2 = 27 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.4] Mode: the most frequent value. The six towns have no mode because no value repeats.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.5] Range = highest value minus lowest value: 41 − 12 = 29 thousand (illustrative), which shows how spread out the values are.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.b] Percentages let you compare parts of different-sized wholes, but the method matters: dividing by the wrong number, or mixing up a percentage with a percentage point, gives a wrong answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.0] Percentage of a total = part ÷ total × 100. Illustrative: Town D's share of the six towns is 41 ÷ 157 × 100 = 26.1%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.1] Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100. A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.2] Always divide by the old (earlier) value, because the change is measured against where you started. Illustrative Country P, broadband households: 20% in 2000 and 90% in 2025, so the percentage change is (90 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 350%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.3] Percentage points: when both values are already percentages, the change is a difference in percentage points. A rise from 20% to 30% is a 10 percentage-point increase, but a 50% increase in the percentage change, (30 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.4] When a question asks by what percentage a percentage changed, the subtraction is the usual reading: give the answer in percentage points and add the percentage change if unsure. Use the percentage-change formula when the values are numbers rather than percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.5] Missing value X: parts of a whole add to 100% (pie chart or percentage table) or to a stated total (stacked bar, a bar split into parts, or a total row), so X = total minus the sum of the known parts.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.6] Illustrative Country Q: if all segments except 'other' total 85%, then other = 100 − 85 = 15%, because the parts of a pie chart must reach 100%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.7] To find a number from a percentage: number = percentage ÷ 100 × total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.b] Raw numbers are hard to compare between places of different size, so geographers use rates, densities and ratios. How you present the answer matters too.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.0] Rate per 1,000 = number ÷ population × 1,000, which lets places of different size be compared. A birth rate is births ÷ population × 1,000.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.1] Density = number ÷ area, given as people per km².
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.2] Ratio: compare two values as a ratio, e.g. 2 : 1. Illustrative: Town D (41) is more than three times the size of Town E (12), a ratio of about 3.4 : 1.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.3] 'Twice as much' means two times the other value; 'half' means divided by two.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.4] Round to the precision the data uses, or to one decimal place where needed, and keep the unit and the multiplier (thousands, millions) in every answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.5] In a calculation question, show the working: a correct answer with working is safe, and the working can earn marks if the answer slips.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.b] When you are asked to draw a graph from a small table of data, marks are given for each of these rules, so each one is worth doing even when the shape of the graph is easy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.0] Use graph paper, a sharp pencil and a ruler; draw the graph large enough to fill most of the space.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.1] The title says what the data is, where and when, taken from the wording of the question.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.2] The horizontal (x) axis shows categories or time; the vertical (y) axis shows values. Label both axes with units.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.3] The scale starts at zero, is evenly divided, and its interval is chosen so the largest value fits (e.g. steps of 5 or 10 units).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.4] On a line graph, time intervals are spaced in proportion to the years. If the data is for 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2015, the last gap is half the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.5] Plot every value accurately. Bars have equal widths and equal gaps; points are joined with ruled straight lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.6] Use a key or legend (a box saying what each colour or shading means) whenever more than one colour or shading is used, and shade or colour each category distinctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.b] Illustrative data: population of six towns, in thousands, from the totals block above. Following the rules in order shows how each mark is earned.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.0] Graph choice: a bar chart, because the values are one measure for separate towns.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.1] Title: 'Population of six towns (thousands), illustrative'.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.2] x-axis: 'Town', with Towns A to F in order.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.3] y-axis: 'Population (thousands)', from 0 to 45 in steps of 5, so the largest value (41) fits.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.4] Six bars of equal width with equal gaps, each drawn to its value: A 18, B 32, C 25, D 41, E 12, F 29.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.5] A single data series (one set of values) needs no key; check each bar top against the scale before finishing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.b] The question 'draw a suitable graph' is answered by naming the graph type, then giving one sentence of justification. The type depends on what the data is. Each example below is illustrative.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.0] Values for separate categories, e.g. the populations of six towns: bar chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.1] Change over time, e.g. the percentage of an urban population from 1980 to 2020: line graph (a bar chart is usually also accepted).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.2] Parts of a whole, or percentage shares adding to 100%, e.g. car sales by fuel type in one year: pie chart or divided bar.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.3] Two related variables for the same places, e.g. income and birth rate for twenty countries: scatter graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.4] Three components that add to 100%, e.g. sand, silt and clay at a soil site: triangular graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.5] Age and sex structure of a population: population pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.6] Monthly temperature and rainfall for one place: climate graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.7] Values for areas on a map, e.g. population density by region: choropleth map (a map with areas shaded by value).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.b] Bar charts show amounts for separate categories. They come in several forms, depending on whether you are comparing categories, groups within a category, or parts of a total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.0] Simple bar chart: separate categories, one bar per category, equal widths and equal gaps; vertical or horizontal.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.1] Multiple (clustered or comparative) bar chart: two or more bars side by side per category, e.g. males and females, or two years; a key is needed to tell the bars apart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.2] Compound (stacked or divided) bar chart: each bar is split into components, and the top of the whole bar is the total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.3] To read a component in a compound bar, take the top of its segment minus the bottom of its segment.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.4] Percentage compound bar: every bar reaches 100%, so the segments show shares rather than totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.5] Illustrative Region R, tree planting: total 2,000 hectares, of which conifers 1,350 ha. Broadleaf = 2,000 − 1,350 = 650 ha, a share of 650 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 32.5%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.b] A histogram looks like a bar chart but is used for continuous data, while a line graph is the standard way to show one variable changing over time.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.0] Histogram: like a bar chart but for continuous data grouped in classes (e.g. age groups or rainfall amounts), drawn with no gaps between bars because one class runs straight into the next.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.1] Line graph (trend graph): one variable over time; plot the points at the correct years and join them with ruled lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.2] Multiple line graph: two or more series on the same axes, with a key so each line can be identified.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.3] The gradient (steepness) of a line shows the rate of change: a steeper section means faster change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.4] Illustrative Country P, households with broadband: 2000 20%, 2005 38%, 2010 61%, 2015 74%, 2020 86%, 2025 90%. The rise is continuous; the fastest change is 2005 to 2010 (+23 percentage points), and the rise slows to +4 points from 2020 to 2025 as the line levels off.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.5] Illustrative Country P: the change from 2000 to 2025 is +70 percentage points, which is not the same as the 350% percentage change above, because one is a subtraction and the other is measured against the old value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.b] A pie chart shows how a whole is split into parts. Because a full circle is 360° and the parts add to 100%, each 1% takes up 3.6° of the circle.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.0] Pie chart: parts of a whole. Degrees = percentage × 3.6, because 360° ÷ 100 = 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.1] For raw numbers rather than percentages: degrees = value ÷ total × 360. Do not multiply raw numbers by 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.2] Drawing steps: draw a circle with a compass, draw a radius (a line from the centre to the edge) to 12 o'clock, then measure each angle clockwise with a protractor, largest segment first.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.3] Shade, key or label each segment with its percentage, and check that the angles add to 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.4] Illustrative Country Q, electricity sources (gas 40%, wind 25%, solar 10%, coal 5%, hydro 5%, other 15%): angles 144°, 90°, 36°, 18°, 18° and 54°, totalling 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.5] Two pie charts compare the shares in two years, not the totals, unless the circles are drawn in proportion to the totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.6] Divided (percentage) bar: a single bar split into shares that add to 100%, an alternative to a pie chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.b] A pictogram uses icons to stand for quantities. A population pyramid is a special graph showing how many people of each age and sex a country has.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.0] Pictogram: each icon stands for a fixed quantity stated in the key, so check the key first; a part-icon is a fraction of that quantity.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.1] Population pyramid: a back-to-back horizontal bar chart, which is a histogram on its side.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.2] Age groups, usually in 5-year bands, sit on the central vertical axis: 0 to 4 at the bottom and the oldest group at the top.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.3] Males are drawn on the left and females on the right. The horizontal axis shows the percentage (or number) of the population, not age, scaled outward from zero on both sides.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.4] Reading a pyramid: a wide base means a high birth rate; a narrow top means low life expectancy; a narrowing base means a falling birth rate; bulges and indentations suggest baby booms, migration, war or famine.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.5] Describe the dependent groups (the young and the old, who rely on others) and the working-age group when reading a pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.6] Drawing one band (illustrative, Country Z): males aged 20 to 24 are 3.5% and females 3.4%, so at the 20 to 24 level draw a bar 3.5 units long left of the centre axis and one 3.4 units long to the right.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.7] The shapes of pyramids and the stages of demographic transition (how a country's birth and death rates change over time) are taught fully elsewhere. There is more on this in Changing population characteristics.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.b] A climate graph combines two kinds of data for one place on a single set of axes: rainfall as bars and temperature as a line, across 12 months.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.0] Months J to D run along the bottom axis. Rainfall is shown as bars against a left-hand axis in mm.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.1] Mean (average) monthly temperature is shown as a line, plotted at the middle of each month, against a right-hand axis in °C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.2] From the graph, read the hottest and coldest months, the wettest and driest months, and the seasonal pattern (rain all year, or a wet and dry season).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.3] Calculations from the graph: annual temperature range = maximum mean monthly temperature minus minimum; mean annual temperature = sum of the 12 monthly means ÷ 12; total annual rainfall = sum of the 12 bars.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.4] Illustrative Station M, mean monthly temperature (°C) J to D: 5, 5, 7, 9, 12, 14, 16, 16, 14, 11, 8, 6. Rainfall (mm) J to D: 110, 80, 85, 60, 65, 70, 70, 85, 90, 115, 110, 120.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.5] Illustrative Station M results: warmest in July and August (16°C); coldest in January and February (5°C); annual range 16 − 5 = 11°C; mean annual temperature 123 ÷ 12 = 10.3°C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.6] Illustrative Station M: total rainfall 1,060 mm; wettest month December (120 mm); driest month April (60 mm). Rain falls in every month with a winter maximum, and the mild winters and cool summers resemble a cool temperate oceanic pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.7] Identifying a climate type from a climate graph is taught elsewhere. There is more on this in Physical regions and Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.b] A scatter graph tests whether two measurements for the same set of places are related, for example whether land value changes with distance from a city centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.0] Scatter graph: two variables for the same set of places. The independent variable (the one that may cause the change) goes on the x-axis; each place is plotted as a point, and the points are not joined.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.1] Draw a line of best fit by eye through the middle of the points, so it shows the general direction.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.2] Positive correlation: both variables rise together. Negative correlation: one rises as the other falls. No correlation: no clear pattern. Strength is described as strong or weak.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.3] Outliers, or anomalies, are points that lie away from the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.4] Correlation does not prove cause: two things can move together without one causing the other.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.5] Illustrative distance from a city centre and land value at eight sites: 1 km €900 per m²; 2 km €700; 3 km €620; 4 km €450; 5 km €400; 6 km €250; 7 km €260; 8 km €150.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.6] Illustrative: the result is a strong negative correlation, with the 7 km site a slight anomaly because it is higher than the 6 km site. A one-clause explanation: accessibility falls with distance from the centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.b] A triangular graph is used when a total is split into exactly three parts. Each point is placed using all three values at once.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.0] Triangular graph: shows three components that always total 100%, e.g. the percentage employed in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.1] Each side is a 0 to 100% scale. Read each component along the gridlines parallel to the side where its 0 is, following the direction in which its numbers run.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.2] Check that the three readings add to 100, which confirms you have read the scales correctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.3] To plot a point: find the first value on its scale, follow its gridline in, and mark where it meets the second value's gridline; check the point with the third value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.4] A point clustered near a corner is dominated by the component that corner represents.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.5] Illustrative employment structure: Country X is primary 10%, secondary 25%, tertiary 65%; it sits near the tertiary corner, typical of a developed economy. Country Y is primary 60%, secondary 15%, tertiary 25%; it sits near the primary corner, typical of a less developed economy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.b] Statistical maps put values onto a map so you can see how they vary from place to place. The choropleth is the main one; four other types each show a different kind of data.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.0] Choropleth map: areas (counties, regions or countries) shaded by value class; darker shading means a higher value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.1] Classes must not overlap and must cover every value, e.g. 0 to 49, 50 to 99, 100 to 149, 150 and over. A key is essential.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.2] Choropleth maps suit densities and percentages. Their weakness is that they imply the value is even across each area and hide variation inside it, and the boundaries look like sudden changes.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.3] Illustrative regional population densities (people per km²): North 35, East 160, South 85, West 40, Midlands 60. North and West are the lightest shade and East the darkest; the empty 100 to 149 class still appears in the key.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.4] Dot map: each dot stands for a fixed number, showing the distribution of a value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.5] Isoline map: lines join places of equal value, e.g. isobars, isotherms, isohyets or contours.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.6] Flow-line map: arrow width is proportional to the amount moving, e.g. migration, traffic or trade.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.7] Proportional symbols: circles or bars sized in proportion to the value, placed on the map.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.b] Sometimes a table is a better way to present data than a graph, and tables, graphs and maps all feed into the Geographical Investigation.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.0] Use a table rather than a graph when many exact values must be given. A table cannot show a trend at a glance.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.1] Graphs, maps and tables are expected in the presentation of results in the Geographical Investigation report. There is more on this in The Geographical Investigation (20%).
