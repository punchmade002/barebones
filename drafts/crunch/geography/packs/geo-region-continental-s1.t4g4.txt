CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s1: 1. The rock cycle and rock formation
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.b] Rock is never permanent. It is continually formed, modified, destroyed and reconstituted (built again from the broken pieces), so the three rock types are linked in one cycle. Two sets of forces drive it, one working from inside the earth and one at its surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.0] Endogenic (internal) forces, driven from within the earth, form and modify rock through magma (molten rock below the surface), heat and pressure.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.1] Exogenic (external) forces, working at the surface, destroy rock through weathering (breakdown of rock in place by weather) and erosion (wearing away and carrying off of rock fragments).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.2] Deposition (dropping of carried sediment, the loose fragments of rock) reconstitutes rock, because the sediment is later turned into new sedimentary rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.b] The cycle is easiest to learn as a circular flow chart in which each rock type is reached from another by a named process. Short-cuts across the circle show that rock does not have to follow the full loop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.0] 1. Magma cools and crystallises (forms crystals as it hardens) to make igneous rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.1] 2. Weathering and erosion break igneous rock down into sediment, because rock at the surface is attacked by weather.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.2] 3. Deposition, compaction and cementation turn the sediment into sedimentary rock, because the layers are squeezed and glued together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.3] 4. Heat and pressure change sedimentary rock into metamorphic rock, because buried rock is squeezed and heated without melting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.4] 5. Melting turns the metamorphic rock back into magma, and the cycle starts again.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.5] Short-cuts: any rock can be weathered into sediment, and any rock can be metamorphosed (changed by heat and pressure) or melted, so the arrows do not all follow one loop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.6] Diagram: draw a circular flow chart with magma and the three rock types as the nodes, and label each arrow with its process, for example crystallisation or compaction and cementation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.b] The rock cycle does not run at random. It is tied to the moving plates (the large slabs that make up the earth's crust), which decide where each stage happens.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.0] Rock is created at plate boundaries and at hotspots (fixed points of rising magma), because magma reaches the crust there.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.1] Rock is metamorphosed at collision zones, where plates push together and the heat and pressure are great.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.2] Rock is destroyed or recycled at subduction zones, where one plate sinks beneath another and the rock melts back into magma.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.b] Igneous rock forms when molten rock cools and crystallises. Magma is molten rock below the surface; lava is molten rock at the surface. Where the rock cools decides how big its crystals grow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.0] Crystal size depends on cooling rate: slow cooling gives large crystals because they have time to grow, and fast cooling gives small ones.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.1] Plutonic (intrusive) rock is igneous rock that cools slowly deep in the crust, so its crystals are large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.2] Volcanic (extrusive) rock is lava that cools quickly at the surface, so its crystals are small.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.3] Uses: granite is used as building stone, kerbs, worktops and headstones; basalt is crushed for road chippings and aggregate (crushed stone used in construction).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.b] Granite is a plutonic rock. When magma pushes into surrounding rock and cools there, it forms masses called intrusions, and their shape gives them different names.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.0] Granite is a plutonic rock built from quartz, feldspar and mica crystals, which are large enough to see because the magma cooled slowly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.1] Batholiths are huge dome-shaped masses of plutonic rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.2] Dykes are vertical intrusions that cut across the beds (layers) of the rock around them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.3] Sills are horizontal intrusions that lie along bedding planes (the horizontal surfaces between layers).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.4] Plutonic rock reaches the surface only after the rock above it has been eroded away, because it formed deep underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.b] Volcanic rock forms at the surface, so it cools fast. Which kind forms depends on the plate setting, the tectonic setting, in which the magma reaches the crust.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.0] Basalt is the commonest volcanic rock and forms from runny lava, which flows easily before it sets.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.1] Basalt forms at constructive (divergent) boundaries, where plates move apart, and at hotspots.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.2] Andesite and granite batholiths form above subduction zones at destructive boundaries, where one plate sinks beneath another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.b] Sedimentary rock is formed from sediment laid down in layers (strata), usually in seas, lakes, river basins and deserts. Loose sediment then has to be turned into solid rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.0] Sediment comes from weathered and eroded fragments of older rock, from the remains of plants and animals, or from minerals precipitated from water (that is, coming out of solution as solids).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.1] Sediment builds up in subsiding basins (areas of sinking land), on continental shelves (the shallow sea floor around a continent) and along trailing (passive) margins, where no plate boundary lies at the coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.2] Lithification is the conversion of loose sediment into solid rock by compaction and cementation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.3] Compaction: the weight of overlying layers squeezes out water and air, packing the grains together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.4] Cementation: minerals such as silica, calcite and iron oxide, carried by groundwater, bind the grains together like glue.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.b] Because sedimentary rock is laid down in layers from material at the earth's surface, it often contains fossils and shows bedding planes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.0] Many sedimentary rocks contain fossils, the remains or traces of past life, because living things were buried in the sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.1] Bedding planes are horizontal surfaces separating one layer from the next.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.2] Joints are vertical cracks that cut through the layers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.3] Many sedimentary rocks are porous, holding water in the spaces between grains. Porous is not the same as permeable (letting water pass through).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.b] Sedimentary rocks are sorted into three groups by the material they are made from.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.0] Clastic rocks are made of broken fragments: sandstone from sand, and shale from mud and clay.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.1] Organic rocks are made of the remains of living things: limestone from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures, and coal from plant remains that accumulated in swamps.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.2] Chemical rocks form from minerals that come out of solution: rock salt and other evaporites form as seawater evaporates and leaves its dissolved minerals behind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.b] Each Irish sedimentary rock records the conditions in which it formed, such as the climate and the setting at that time. For a longer answer, give the conditions as well as the rock and its place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.0] Carboniferous limestone (Carboniferous period, about 360 to 300 million years ago) formed when Ireland lay near the equator under a warm, shallow, clear sea, where shells and skeletons built up the deposit. It covers most of the central lowland and forms the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.1] Old Red Sandstone (Devonian period) formed in hot, semi-arid (very dry) conditions from sand and pebbles laid down by rivers; its red colour comes from iron oxide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.2] Old Red Sandstone forms the Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtees and the Comeraghs.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.3] Shale and sandstone of the Cliffs of Moher, Co. Clare, are layers of shale, siltstone and sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.4] Chalk lies beneath the basalt along the Antrim coast, so the chalk is the older rock and the lava flowed over it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.5] Coal at Arigna, Co. Roscommon, and Castlecomer, Co. Kilkenny, formed from plant remains in swamps; both are former coal-mining areas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.6] Rock salt is mined at the Kilroot salt mine, near Carrickfergus, Co. Antrim.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.b] Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by great heat and/or pressure without melting. The original rock is called the parent rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.0] The two factors that cause metamorphism are heat and pressure.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.1] Minerals recrystallise (re-form as new crystals) while the rock stays solid, so the rock usually becomes harder and denser.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.2] The rock may develop banding or cleavage (a tendency to split along flat surfaces).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.b] Metamorphism happens on two scales, depending on whether the heat and pressure come from a single intrusion or from the movement of plates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.0] Thermal (contact) metamorphism: rock beside an igneous intrusion is baked by its heat, forming a ring of altered rock called a metamorphic aureole; it is small in scale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.1] Regional metamorphism: heat and pressure act over huge areas during plate collision and mountain building; it is large in scale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.b] Each metamorphic rock comes from a particular parent rock, and the pairs can be asked either way round.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.0] Limestone becomes marble; Connemara marble is green and is quarried in Connemara, Co. Galway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.1] Sandstone becomes quartzite, so the parent of quartzite is a sedimentary rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.2] Shale (mudstone) becomes slate, and with more heat and pressure becomes schist, so schist is the more strongly altered rock. Slate is quarried on Valentia Island, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.3] Granite becomes gneiss, so gneiss is a metamorphic rock formed from an igneous parent.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.4] Quartzite forms Errigal, Co. Donegal; Croagh Patrick, Co. Mayo; the Great Sugar Loaf, Co. Wicklow; and the Twelve Bens, Connemara.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.5] Mica schist is found in Donegal and Connemara, and a metamorphic aureole of mica schist surrounds the Leinster granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.6] Gneiss at Inishtrahull, off Co. Donegal, is about 1.7 billion years old and is the oldest rock in Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.b] Ireland's rock types form a recognisable pattern across the island, and each reflects an event in its geological past.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.0] Limestone floors the central lowland, and Old Red Sandstone forms the ridges of Munster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.1] Granite outcrops (shows at the surface) in Wicklow, Donegal and the Mournes; basalt covers Co. Antrim; metamorphic rocks lie in Donegal, Connemara and Mayo.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.2] Plutonic example: the Leinster batholith, the granite core of the Wicklow Mountains, is about 400 million years old and is the largest granite mass in Ireland and Britain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.3] The Mourne granite, in the Mourne Mountains, Co. Down, is much younger, about 56 million years old.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.4] Volcanic example: the Antrim plateau was built by lava flows about 60 million years ago, as the Atlantic began to open. The Giant's Causeway, Co. Antrim, is basalt columns.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.5] The closing of the Iapetus Ocean during Caledonian mountain building produced the Leinster and Donegal granites and regional metamorphism.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.b] North America has two very different edges. The western edge is an active plate margin, where plates are moving and rock is made and changed. The eastern edge is a trailing (passive) margin, where no plate boundary lies at the coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.0] Active western margin: Pacific plates subduct (sink) beneath or slide past North America.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.1] Volcanic rock: the Cascade Range volcanoes, including Mount St Helens in Washington state (erupted 1980), lie above a subducting oceanic plate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.2] Plutonic rock: the Sierra Nevada granite batholith, California.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.3] Regional metamorphism affects the western mountain belts.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.4] The San Andreas Fault, California, is a transform boundary where plates slide past each other.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.5] Trailing eastern margin: there is no plate boundary at the coast, so there are no active volcanoes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.6] Because the margin is quiet, thick sediments accumulate on the Atlantic coastal plain, on the continental shelf and in the Mississippi delta.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.7] The Appalachians are old, worn-down fold mountains of sedimentary and metamorphic rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.b] Rocks can be told apart by what they look like, and photo questions rely on this. Each rock below has one or two clues that separate it from the others.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.0] Granite: speckled, with large interlocking crystals, because it cooled slowly underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.1] Basalt: dark and fine-grained because it cooled quickly; it may form columns, as at the Giant's Causeway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.2] Sandstone: gritty grains, often reddish or buff, and layered, because it is made of cemented sand laid down in beds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.3] Limestone: pale grey, and it may show fossils because it is made from shells and skeletons.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.4] Marble: white or coloured, with sparkling crystals and no fossils, which separates it from limestone, its parent rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.5] Quartzite: pale, very hard and glassy.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.6] Slate: dark grey, and it splits into thin sheets, which is the cleavage (splitting) that metamorphism can produce.

CANDIDATES geo-rivers-s4: 4. Human interaction: river processes
[geo-rivers-s4.0.b] Rivers are useful, so people change them. Every change alters how the river's processes work: its velocity (speed of flow), erosion (wearing away of bed and banks), transport (carrying of material), deposition (dropping of material) and flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.0] Reasons for interfering include making electricity, supplying water, irrigation (watering crops), navigation (letting boats travel), draining farmland and protecting towns from flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.1] The three measures studied here are hydro-electric dams, canalisation and flood control, each with Irish and international examples.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.b] A hydro-electric dam turns the energy of falling water into electricity. It does this by holding the river back and then releasing it in a controlled way.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.0] The dam stores water in a reservoir (an artificial lake behind the dam), and the water is then released so that it falls through turbines (wheels that spin and drive generators).
[geo-rivers-s4.1.1] The drop in height of the water (the head) gives the power, so a greater drop means more energy from the same water.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.b] Upstream of a dam the river meets still water in the reservoir. This changes its processes completely, because a river can only carry material while it keeps moving.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.0] Velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load (the sand, silt and stones it was carrying) there.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.1] The reservoir slowly fills with sediment, a process called sedimentation, which reduces its capacity (how much water it can hold).
[geo-rivers-s4.2.2] A delta (a fan of deposited sediment) can build where the river enters the reservoir, because that is where the flow slows and drops its load.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.b] Below the dam the river has lost its sediment and its natural rhythm of floods and low flows. Both changes alter erosion and deposition.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.0] Water released from the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam. This is called clear-water erosion.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.1] The floodplain (the flat land beside the river) and delta receive no new silt, so deltas and coasts erode because nothing replaces the material lost.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.2] Discharge (the volume of water flowing past a point) is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.3] Less flooding means less deposition on the floodplain, because floodwater is what normally lays down silt there.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.4] Where water is diverted out of the old channel, the bypassed channel carries much less water.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.5] Dams block migrating salmon and eels, which cannot pass the wall. Fish passes (routes around the dam) and trapping and transporting fish around dams are responses.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.b] The Shannon Scheme is one power station at Ardnacrusha, near Limerick, fed by a weir and a canal. It is not a series of dams.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.0] It was built 1925–1929 by the German firm Siemens for the Irish Free State, and it was Ireland's first large hydro-electric station.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.1] Parteen Weir (a low barrier across the river) diverts most of the Shannon's water into a headrace canal to Ardnacrusha. A tailrace (the channel carrying used water away) returns it to the river near Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.2] Lough Derg's level is controlled by Parteen Weir, so the lough acts as the scheme's reservoir.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.3] The original channel between Parteen and Limerick, the Old River Shannon, carries much less water because most of it is diverted.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.4] Salmon and eel stocks declined. The ESB built a fish pass, and it traps silver eels (adult eels migrating downstream) upstream and transports them past the dam.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.b] Ardnacrusha is not the only Irish example. Four more dams and power stations can be named in an answer.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.0] Poulaphouca dam, River Liffey: created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, Co. Wicklow. It generates electricity and supplies drinking water to Dublin.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.1] Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid dams, River Lee, Co. Cork.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.2] Cathaleen's Fall power station, River Erne, at Ballyshannon, Co. Donegal.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.b] The Aswan High Dam is the main international case study. It holds back the Nile in a huge reservoir to give Egypt control over a river that once flooded and shrank unpredictably.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.0] Aswan High Dam, River Nile, Egypt, was completed in 1970. Its reservoir is Lake Nasser, which extends into Sudan.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.1] Benefits are flood control, year-round irrigation (which allows more than one crop a year), hydro-electricity, and protection from drought.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.b] The dam's effects follow from the same processes as any dam: trapped sediment, clear-water erosion and regulated flow. They then spread into farming, fishing, health and settlement.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.0] Silt is trapped in Lake Nasser, so farmers downstream rely on artificial fertilisers instead of the natural silt that floods once left.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.1] The river bed below the dam is scoured (worn away) by clear water, which has spare energy to erode.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.2] The Nile delta coast is eroding, e.g. at the Rosetta promontory (a headland on the delta coast), because no new sediment arrives to replace what the sea removes.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.3] The sardine fishery off the delta in the Mediterranean declined sharply.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.4] Irrigated land suffers waterlogging (soil saturated with water) and salinisation (a build-up of salt in the soil).
[geo-rivers-s4.7.5] Perennial irrigation canals (canals that hold water all year) spread the disease schistosomiasis (bilharzia).
[geo-rivers-s4.7.6] Nubian people were displaced, and the temples of Abu Simbel were moved to higher ground.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.b] The Three Gorges Dam is a second international example, built on the Yangtze to produce power and to reduce floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.0] Three Gorges Dam, Hubei province, China, is the world's largest power station by installed capacity.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.1] It was completed in 2006 and fully operational in 2012, and over one million people were relocated.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.2] It aims to reduce flood peaks downstream, because the Yangtze floods of 1998 killed thousands.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.3] It traps sediment that once reached the river's lower course and delta, so those areas receive less of the sediment they depended on.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.b] Canalisation modifies an existing river channel. It is not the same as building a canal.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.0] Methods are straightening, deepening and widening the channel, sometimes lining it with concrete, cutting through meanders (bends in the river), dredging (scooping sediment from the bed) and building locks and weirs for navigation.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.1] Purposes are navigation, faster drainage of land, and flood control.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.b] Making a river straighter and smoother makes the water move faster. Almost every other effect follows from that rise in velocity.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.0] A straighter, shorter course has a steeper gradient (slope) and less friction (drag from the bed and banks), so velocity rises.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.1] Water and floods are passed downstream faster, so flood peaks downstream are higher and arrive sooner.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.2] Faster flow has more energy, so it erodes the bed and banks.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.3] Where the channel widens or the flow slows again, deposition increases and dredging is needed to keep the channel clear.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.4] Meanders, oxbow lakes (cut-off loops of old meander), pools and riffles (deeper and shallower stretches of the bed) are lost, and the river is cut off from its floodplain.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.b] Canalisation has been used for drainage and navigation in Ireland and for shortening and straightening big rivers abroad.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.0] Arterial drainage (deepening and straightening main rivers to drain land) was carried out by the Office of Public Works (OPW) under the Arterial Drainage Act 1945, e.g. the Moy and Boyne schemes. Rivers were deepened and straightened mainly to drain farmland, not to protect towns.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.1] Arterial drainage effects were changes to fish spawning beds, bank erosion and downstream flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.2] Mississippi: cut-offs (new short channels across bends) made by the US Army Corps of Engineers shortened the river.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.3] Rhine: the Upper Rhine was straightened in the 19th century under Johann Gottfried Tulla, which sped flood waves downstream and caused the bed to erode downward.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.4] Navigation in Ireland: locks and weirs on the Shannon Navigation control water levels and slow the flow.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.b] Flood control measures respond to the causes of river flooding, so it helps to know what those causes are.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.0] Prolonged or intense rain, and saturated ground (soil that is already full of water and cannot absorb more), send water into rivers faster than they can carry it.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.1] Urban surfaces and drains speed runoff (water flowing over land into rivers). Deforestation and drainage of bogs and farmland upstream add to it.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.2] Building on floodplains puts homes in the path of floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.b] Hard engineering uses built structures to hold floodwater back or move it away. Each one changes river processes, and some pass the problem on downstream.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.0] Embankments and levees (raised banks) confine flow to the channel, so deposition raises the bed and the banks must be raised again.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.1] With embankments and levees the floodplain gets no silt, and failure gives sudden, severe floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.2] Flood walls and demountable (removable) barriers protect towns.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.3] Dams and reservoirs store floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.4] Dredging and deepening increase channel capacity, but the flow is faster and downstream flooding can increase.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.5] Relief (bypass) channels take floodwater around the town.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.b] Soft engineering works with the river instead of against it. It slows water or gives it space, so natural processes carry on.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.0] Floodplain zoning uses planning controls on building, so that homes are kept off land that floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.1] Afforestation (planting trees) upstream works through interception (leaves catching rain), which slows runoff.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.2] Wetlands and bogs can be restored, and washlands (land that is allowed to flood) give the river more room.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.3] Flood forecasting and warning give people time to prepare.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.4] Effect on processes: natural flooding and floodplain deposition continue.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.b] Irish floods show hard engineering, dam management and flood-risk planning in practice.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.0] Cork, November 2009: the River Lee flooded when the ESB released large volumes from Inniscarra dam during very heavy rain because the reservoir was full.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.1] The releases were not the sole cause, because the rain was exceptional. The University College Cork and the city water treatment works were flooded, and many homes lost their water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.2] Clonmel, Co. Tipperary: an OPW flood relief scheme on the River Suir uses demountable barriers that are put in place when floods are forecast.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.3] December 2015: Storm Desmond caused prolonged flooding on the Shannon, in towns such as Athlone.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.4] CFRAM (Catchment Flood Risk Assessment and Management) is the OPW programme that maps flood risk.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.b] The Mississippi is the international case study for levees. It shows both what they protect and how they can fail.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.0] The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 led to the US federal levee and floodway system run by the US Army Corps of Engineers.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.1] The 1993 flood breached many levees along the upper Mississippi and Missouri.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.2] In August 2005 Hurricane Katrina caused levee and floodwall failures in New Orleans, and about 80% of the city flooded.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.3] Levees stop flood silt reaching the delta, which contributes to the loss of Louisiana's coastal wetlands.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.b] The Netherlands shows a soft-engineering response to flooding: instead of building higher walls, it gives the river space.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.0] Room for the River (Ruimte voor de Rivier), Netherlands, started after near-disastrous high water on the Rhine and Maas in 1993 and 1995.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.1] Measures include moving dykes (embankments) back, lowering floodplains and digging side channels so the river can spread out safely.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.b] Exams expect a judgement, so weigh what these measures achieve against what they cost.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.0] Benefits are protection of homes, electricity, navigation and water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.1] Costs are problems moved downstream, sediment starvation (rivers and deltas losing the sediment they need), habitat loss, high cost, and a false sense of security.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.2] There is a trend towards working with natural processes.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-activities-s2: The mobility of modern economic activities and factors in their location
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.b] Firms try to locate where total transport costs are lowest. This least-cost principle of industrial location was set out by Alfred Weber in 1909, and it depends on whether processing makes the materials lighter or heavier.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.0] Bulk-reducing industries are those where the raw material loses weight in processing, so the plant locates near the raw material because carrying the heavy input a long way would waste money. Examples: alumina refining, dairy processing, fish processing, mining.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.1] Bulk-gaining industries are those where the product gains weight or bulk in production, so the plant locates near the market because the finished product is the costly thing to move. Examples: brewing, soft drinks bottling, bakeries.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.0.2] Test for an exam location question: if the inputs lose weight in processing, the plant is pulled towards the raw material; if the finished product is heavier or bulkier, it is pulled towards the market.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.b] Location questions use a set of working terms. Knowing them makes the location factors easier to follow.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.0] Footloose industry: one that is not tied to a raw material or a market, so it is free to choose its location. The factors below explain where footloose activities choose to locate.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.1] Branch plant: a factory or office owned by a company headquartered elsewhere, usually carrying out routine production.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.2] Headquarters (HQ): the head office where a company's main decisions are made. Research and development (R&D): the work of designing new products and processes.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.3] Greenfield site: undeveloped land on which a new plant is built. Brownfield site: land already built on, often a former industrial site that may need clearing before reuse.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.4] Industrial estate or business park: a serviced area of land set aside for firms, with roads and utilities already in place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.5] Cluster, or agglomeration: firms in the same or related industries grouped together, sharing skills, suppliers and research.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.6] Backward linkages are links to a firm's suppliers; forward linkages are links to its customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.1.7] Infrastructure: the transport, energy, water, communications and waste systems that firms depend on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.b] A location factor is any condition that makes one place more suitable than another for an activity. The first three are the inputs a firm needs to make its product.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.0] Raw materials: weight-losing raw materials pull processing towards their source or towards a port where they arrive, which keeps the cost of moving heavy loads down.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.1] Energy: industry once clustered on coalfields, where the fuel was. National electricity grids freed most industry from this, but energy-hungry activities such as alumina refining and data centres still need large, reliable and affordable power.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.2] Labour: the quantity, skills and education, cost, productivity and flexibility of the workforce.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.3] High-technology and service firms seek graduates because their work needs advanced skills; standardised assembly (repeating the same simple steps) seeks low wages.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.2.4] Irish examples for labour: the Intel plant at Leixlip and the Galway cluster, both described below.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.b] Once a product is made it has to reach its customers, and firms need to receive inputs. Markets, transport links and communications decide how cheaply and quickly that happens.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.0] Markets: the size and wealth of the market, and access to the EU single market (the EU area with no trade barriers between members). Bulk-gaining and perishable goods locate near the market; services locate near their customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.1] Ports: deep water lets bulk carriers (large ships carrying loose cargo) berth. Dublin Port is Ireland's busiest port. Port of Cork and Shannon Foynes Port are also major ports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.2] Rosslare Europort: direct ferry routes to France grew after Brexit (2021) to avoid the UK land bridge, the route across Britain to continental Europe.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.3] Whitegate, Cork Harbour: Ireland's only oil refinery, opened 1959.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.4] Airports: air freight suits light, high-value, time-sensitive goods such as pharmaceuticals, medical devices and microchips, because the high price of the goods can bear the high cost of air transport.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.5] Dublin Airport handled over 30 million passengers in 2023; its North Runway opened in 2022.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.6] Freight modes: road offers door-to-door flexibility over short and medium distances; rail carries heavy loads on fixed routes; sea is the cheapest way to move bulk over long distances; air is the fastest but the most expensive.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.3.7] Motorways, rail, broadband and fibre (fibre-optic cable) connect firms to markets and suppliers; for services, broadband and fibre matter most. One environmental challenge of air transport is its CO2 emissions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.b] A firm also needs money and a welcoming policy environment. Governments, and the EU above them, can make a location more or less attractive through money, tax and rules.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.0] Capital: investment money comes from banks, shareholders, government grants and venture capital (money from investors who back new, risky businesses).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.1] IDA Ireland was set up in 1949 and became an independent agency in 1969. It attracts firms by offering grants, serviced sites (land with utilities already supplied) and advance factories (buildings built ready for a firm to move into).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.2] Corporation tax (the tax on company profits) was 12.5% from 2003. Very large groups pay 15% from 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.3] Enterprise Ireland (1998) supports Irish-owned firms. Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980) supports enterprise in Gaeltacht areas.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.4] Regional policy steers firms towards less-developed areas through grants and incentives.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.5] Planning permission (official approval from the local authority) is needed before a site can be built on.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.6] EU state-aid rules limit the subsidies governments may give firms, while EU funds support regional development.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.4.7] Trade agreements reduce the barriers that firms face when selling across borders, which makes a location inside the agreement more attractive.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.b] After a firm picks a general area it must find a suitable piece of land, and workers and the plant both need services. The site and its services decide whether the plant can actually be built and run.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.0] Site: flat, well-drained land, large enough for expansion, at a cheap price, so that building is easy and the plant can grow later.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.1] Site: a water supply, and facilities for effluent (liquid waste) and waste disposal.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.2] Site: hazardous industry needs distance from homes, so that accidents or pollution do not harm residents.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.5.3] Services: water, electricity, gas, broadband, waste treatment, housing and schools. Workers must be able to live near the plant, which is why housing and schools count as location factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.b] Some location factors are about people and history rather than land and transport. Firms gather together, stay put out of habit, or follow the pull of a pleasant place to live.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.0] Agglomeration: firms in the same industry gather to share skilled workers, suppliers, research and universities, so each firm gains from the others being nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.1] Cork Harbour (pharmaceuticals) and Galway (medical technology) grew into clusters in this way.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.2] Personal and historical factors: the choices of founders and the history of a site can decide a location, whatever the economics of the place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.3] Industrial inertia: an industry stays where it is after the original reason for locating there has gone.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.6.4] Quality of life: attractive places to live help firms recruit skilled staff, because graduates can choose where to work.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.b] Local opposition and environmental regulation can stop or delay a project. Corrib gas shows how long a delay can be.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.0] Corrib gas was discovered in 1996. Its terminal (the plant that receives and processes the gas) is at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.1] The Shell to Sea campaign opposed the project, and the Rossport Five were jailed in 2005.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.7.2] Gas first flowed in December 2015, after a long delay caused by local opposition and changes to planning.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.b] The weight of each factor has changed over time as transport, energy supply and technology have changed. The pattern runs from resources, to customers, to skills and quality of life.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.0] In the 19th century, raw materials and power were the main location factors, so industry sat on or near coalfields and sources of materials.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.1] In the 20th century, markets and labour became the main factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.8.2] Today, footloose activities (those free to choose a location) chase skills, tax, connectivity and quality of life.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.b] A bulk-reducing heavy industry located on a deep-water estuary, where imported raw material arrives by sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.0] Aughinish Alumina opened in 1983 on Aughinish Island near Askeaton, Co. Limerick, on the south shore of the Shannon Estuary. It is Europe's largest alumina refinery.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.1] Bulk-reducing: about two to three tonnes of bauxite (the ore, or raw rock, that alumina is made from) make one tonne of alumina, so the refinery sits at the raw material's port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.2] Bauxite is imported by sea, mainly from Guinea and Brazil. The alumina (aluminium oxide) is shipped to aluminium smelters elsewhere; the refinery does not make aluminium.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.3] Deep, sheltered water in the Shannon Estuary, Ireland's deepest natural sheltered water, allows large bulk carriers to berth.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.4] The site is a flat island location away from towns, which suits a large heavy industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.5] Government support for heavy industry is a further factor in its siting.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.6] Problems: bauxite residue, known as red mud, is stored in large disposal areas on site.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.9.7] The company has been owned by the Russian company Rusal since 2007.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.b] The same estuary that serves Aughinish has also been used for energy, because deep water lets fuel arrive by ship.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.0] Moneypoint power station, Co. Clare, is on the Shannon Estuary and imported coal by sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.10.1] Coal burning at Moneypoint ended in 2025, so the estuary's energy role has changed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.b] Intel is footloose in the sense that its products are not tied to a raw material or a market, so it chose Leixlip from many possible sites. Its choice shows which factors matter to a high-technology firm.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.0] Intel's Leixlip plant opened in 1989. It is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, worth billions of euro.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.1] Skilled graduates are available, so with Maynooth University nearby, which meets the plant's need for a skilled workforce.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.2] IDA support and low corporation tax attracted the investment. There is more on this in Capital, government and EU policy.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.3] Access to the EU single market from an English-speaking base.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.4] The plant lies beside the M4 motorway and close to Dublin Airport, so goods and staff can move easily.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.5] It occupies a large greenfield site (undeveloped land) with a supply of electricity and water.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.6] Footloose does not mean likely to leave: the large fixed investment makes relocation unlikely.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.11.7] Global job cuts announced in 2024 and 2025 affected Leixlip, showing that even a large plant depends on parent-company decisions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.b] Cork Harbour combines deep water, industrial land and skilled graduates, so pharmaceutical firms have gathered there. Their closeness to each other reinforces the pattern.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.0] Pfizer, Janssen (Johnson & Johnson) and Novartis are at Ringaskiddy; Eli Lilly is at Kinsale, Co. Cork.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.1] The Port of Cork has deep-water berths at Ringaskiddy, which suits the import and export of pharmaceutical goods.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.2] Large flat sites are zoned for industry (set aside for it in the local plan), with water supply and waste facilities.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.3] Skilled labour comes from University College Cork (UCC) and Munster Technological University (MTU). MTU was formed in 2021 from Cork IT and IT Tralee.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.4] IDA support, tax and clustering reinforce the pattern.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.5] Pharmaceuticals and chemicals are Ireland's largest goods exports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.12.6] Older heavy industry in the same harbour: Irish Steel at Haulbowline closed in 2001.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.b] Galway shows how the closure of one firm can feed the growth of another industry. Skilled people left behind became the base for a cluster making medical devices.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.0] Boston Scientific opened in Galway in 1994. Medtronic, Parkmore, Galway followed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.1] The cluster draws on skilled engineers left by the closure of DEC's plant. The Dell and DEC block below gives the date.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.2] University of Galway research (renamed in 2022) supports the cluster, and a network of suppliers has grown around the firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.13.3] There is more on this in Irish Regions — Core and Peripheral, in the peripheral Irish region subsection.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.b] Food and drink show both sides of Weber's idea. Processing that makes the product lighter stays at the source, while brewing, which makes the product heavier, stays near its customers.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.0] Kerry Group grew from the North Kerry Milk Products co-operative at Listowel, founded in 1972. Its headquarters are in Tralee, Co. Kerry.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.1] Dairy processing is bulk-reducing: plants at Listowel and Tralee sit near the supply of milk.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.2] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland's largest fishing port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.3] Fish processing plants at Killybegs handle pelagic fish (fish that live near the surface) such as mackerel and herring, a bulk-reducing process that locates at the port.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.4] Guinness at St James's Gate, Dublin, was founded in 1759 by Arthur Guinness on a 9,000-year lease.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.5] Brewing is bulk-gaining because water is added to the product, so the brewery sits near its market.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.14.6] Industrial inertia: Guinness still brews at St James's Gate today, long after the original reasons for the site have gone.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.b] Data centres (buildings full of computers that store and process online data) are a modern footloose activity, but they use huge amounts of electricity. For them the power grid has become a limit on location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.0] Data centres used about 21% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.1] EirGrid (the electricity grid operator) stated in 2022 that it would not connect new data centres in the Dublin region until about 2028, so the electricity grid now limits their location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.2] Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, Google and Meta operate data centres in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.15.3] Why Ireland: a cool, mild climate reduces cooling costs; fibre links to the USA and Europe; existing clusters of technology firms; tax.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.b] Modern economic activities can move more easily than older ones. Five changes have loosened the link between an activity and a fixed place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.0] Lower transport costs, through containerisation (goods packed in standard boxes that move easily between ship, rail and lorry) and air freight, let goods move cheaply over long distances.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.1] Instant communications, through the internet, fibre and video calls, let firms manage plants at a distance.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.2] Standardised production can be done by low-skilled workers, so the work can go anywhere with a cheap labour supply.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.3] The removal of trade barriers, through the EU single market and the World Trade Organization, makes it easier to sell across borders from any member location.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.16.4] Multinationals (firms operating in many countries) can compare countries and move capital quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.b] A country that hosts a branch plant gains quickly, but its gains are not secure. The same mobility that brings the plant can take it away.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.0] Investment brings rapid job creation, but jobs can leave just as quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.1] Branch plants are vulnerable because the decisions are made at a distant headquarters, not in the host country.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.17.2] Countries compete for investment by offering tax and grants.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.b] A company opens a branch plant in another country when the move helps its own plans. These are the strategies behind such decisions.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.0] Expansion into new markets, for example, so that customers are served from nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.1] Getting inside a trade bloc (a group of countries trading freely together), which gives tariff-free access to the EU. A tariff is a tax on imports.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.2] Lower costs, because cheaper land, labour or tax raises profit.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.3] Access to skilled labour, because some work cannot be done without trained staff.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.4] Following competitors into a cluster, so as to share the skills and suppliers already there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.18.5] Tax planning, because a low-tax country leaves the firm more profit.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.b] The strategies that open plants have opposites that close them. A parent company closes a branch plant when the plant no longer suits its plans.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.0] Rationalisation: cutting surplus capacity after a fall in demand or a merger.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.1] Relocation to lower-cost countries, because production costs are lower there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.2] Outsourcing: contract manufacturers make the goods instead of the company's own plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.3] Automation (machines replacing workers) reduces the number of workers needed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.4] Mergers and takeovers, after which a parent firm may close a duplicate plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.19.5] A product reaching the end of its life cycle, because falling demand leaves the plant with no future output.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.b] A branch plant does not have to close when its routine work moves away. It can survive by changing what it does.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.0] A plant can survive by moving up the value chain (the steps that add more value to a product) into more R&D, advanced products and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.20.1] Intel's continuing investment at Leixlip is the example: Fab 34, opened in 2023, makes advanced chips using extreme ultraviolet lithography, a chip-making technique.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.b] Dell and DEC are two short examples of plants that closed or moved, and of how a closure can affect a region.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.0] Dell opened in Limerick (Raheen) in 1990.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.1] In January 2009 Dell announced that manufacturing would move to Łódź, Poland, with about 1,900 jobs lost in Limerick. It kept sales and service functions in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.2] Poland joined the EU on 1 May 2004, with nine other states, so its plants lay inside the enlarged EU with lower costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.3] DEC closed its Galway plant in 1993, and its skilled workforce later helped attract the medtech cluster.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.21.4] There is more on Dell in The MNC and change subsection of The Global Economy — Multinationals and Globalisation.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.b] A product passes through stages from launch to decline, and its best production location changes at each stage. This is how the product life cycle shapes where plants are found.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.0] Introduction: the product is developed near headquarters and R&D, in a core region with highly skilled labour.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.1] Growth: branch plants are opened in other developed countries near large markets; Ireland gained many.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.2] Maturity: the standardised product is mass-produced in low-wage locations, because it no longer needs high skills.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.22.3] Decline: the plant closes or production is concentrated in the cheapest plant.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.b] Apple's Cork site shows a plant changing its role as the company's production methods change, rather than closing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.0] Apple opened at Hollyhill, Cork, in 1980 and first made computers there.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.1] As Apple's manufacturing moved to contract manufacturers in Asia, the Cork site shifted mainly to operations, support and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.23.2] The Cork site is now Apple's European headquarters, with about 6,000 staff (2020s).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.b] A host country is not helpless against mobile firms. It can aim to make its jobs harder to move.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.0] Attract firms early in new life cycles, for example in pharmaceuticals, medical technology and software, so the country has the work while it is new and growing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.1] Invest in education and research so that jobs are harder to move: Science Foundation Ireland, set up in 2000 and made a statutory body (one created by law) in 2003, funds research; it merged into Research Ireland in 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.24.2] Universities supply the graduates that keep high-technology firms in place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.b] Computing and the internet have freed many services from a fixed place. Delivering a service at a distance means it can be located almost anywhere.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.0] Computing and the internet have made many services footloose, and Ireland became a European base for software and internet firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.1] Teleservices are services delivered at a distance by phone or internet, such as customer support, technical support and remote data processing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.2] Teleservices can locate anywhere with broadband, including peripheral areas (those far from the main centres).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.3] Low-skilled teleservice work moves easily to cheaper countries or is automated.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.25.4] There is more on IT and teleservices in The MNC and change subsection of The Global Economy — Multinationals and Globalisation.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.b] E-commerce has three effects on where activities locate: on warehouses, on town-centre shops and on bank branches, because customers no longer need to travel.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.0] E-commerce is buying and selling goods and services over the internet.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.1] Warehouses and delivery depots locate near motorways and airports, so that parcels can be moved quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.2] Pressure on town-centre shops, and bank branch closures, follow the growth of online trade.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.26.3] There is more on e-commerce and an MNC's operations in The MNC and change subsection of The Global Economy — Multinationals and Globalisation.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.b] After COVID-19 many jobs could be done away from a city office. This gives rural areas a chance to attract work, but only if they have good broadband.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.0] Remote and hybrid working (part at home, part in the office) grew after COVID-19, so some jobs can be done from home or a local hub rather than a city office.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.1] National Broadband Plan: the contract was signed in 2019 with National Broadband Ireland to bring high-speed broadband to rural premises.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.2] Connected Hubs: a national network of remote-working hubs launched in 2021.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.3] Our Rural Future, rural development policy, 2021.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.27.4] Broadband availability differs from region to region, and a region's coverage affects whether it can attract remote-working jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.28.b] New technology continues to change where work is done and how many people are needed.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.28.0] Artificial intelligence and automation are future drivers of location and job change.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.28.1] Automation can reduce the labour-cost advantage of low-wage locations, favouring places with skills and research, because machines make cheap labour less important.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.29.b] Map and photograph questions ask why an activity might or might not locate at a point, using evidence from the map or photograph.

[geo-economic-activities-s2.29.1] Explain why the factor matters to the activity.

[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.b] For a factory or multinational, look on the map for the features that supply land, transport, workers and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.0] Flat land and an edge-of-town site, where there is room and land is cheaper.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.1] A national primary road or motorway, a railway, and a port or airport, for moving goods.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.2] A town labour force and services: schools, hospital and housing.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.30.3] Water and an existing industrial estate, because services and planning approval for industry are already in place.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.b] A fish processing plant needs to be close to where fish are landed, because fish is a bulk-reducing, perishable raw material.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.0] A pier, harbour or fishing port, on the coast.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.1] Roads to markets, so that fresh fish reaches buyers quickly.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.2] Water and a labour force, because processing needs both.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.31.3] Low-lying land, because it is flat enough to build on and close to the coast.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.32.b] A hotel depends on attractions that draw visitors, together with the services that let them reach and use the hotel.
[geo-economic-activities-s2.32.0] Coast, beach and scenery; lakes or mountains; antiquities (ancient sites and monuments).
[geo-economic-activities-s2.32.1] A golf course, and water sports, because these give guests activities.
