CANDIDATES geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3: 3. Landforms from volcanic and plutonic structures
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.b] Magma (molten rock) that rises into the crust cools in one of two ways, and the way it cools decides what landform results. Both ways are endogenic (internal) processes, driven from inside the Earth.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.0] Extrusive (volcanic) activity: magma reaches the surface and cools quickly as lava, ash and other material, building landforms on the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.1] Intrusive (plutonic) activity: magma cools slowly underground, forming bodies of rock that become landforms only when the rock above them is eroded away.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.2] Plutonic features are not formed by exogenic processes (surface processes such as weathering and erosion); erosion only reveals them, because the magma had already cooled into rock underground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.b] Rock is rarely one solid block. Cracks called joints run through it, and they decide where weathering and erosion do their work.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.0] A joint is a crack in rock along which no movement has taken place, which is what separates it from a fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.1] In igneous rock (rock formed from cooled magma), joints form by contraction as the rock cools.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.2] In granite, joints also form by expansion when the overlying rock is removed and pressure on the granite is released.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.3] Weathering and erosion work along joints, so the joint pattern shapes the landform.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.b] Not all eruptions come from a single volcano. When lava escapes along a long crack, it can build a wide, high, flat landform instead of a cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.0] 1. Basic lava (runny lava that flows easily) pours out of long cracks in the crust, called fissures, rather than from a single vent (an opening in the crust).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.1] 2. Each lava flow spreads widely before it cools, because the lava is runny.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.2] 3. Repeated flows build up, one on top of another, so the plateau grows higher with each eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.3] 4. The result is a high, flat-topped plateau with steep edges.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.4] Example: the Antrim plateau, Co. Antrim.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.5] Diagram: cross-section of stacked lava flows forming a flat-topped plateau with steep edges.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.b] The Antrim plateau is an Irish extrusive example, and the granite Mournes are its intrusive neighbour from the same period. Keep the two apart.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.0] The Antrim plateau is made of basalt (a dark volcanic rock) in layers from fissure eruptions about 60 million years ago, linked to the opening of the North Atlantic.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.1] Along the Antrim coast, dark basalt lies on top of white chalk, which the basalt protected from erosion.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.2] The Mourne Mountains, Co. Down, are granite intrusions (granite is a plutonic rock that cooled underground) of the same period as the Antrim basalts, much younger than the Leinster granite.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.3] Slieve Donard is the highest peak of the Mournes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.b] Basalt columns form in a lava flow as it cools and contracts (shrinks). They are a joint pattern, not a volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.0] Columnar jointing forms when a thick lava flow cools slowly and evenly, so the whole flow shrinks in a regular pattern.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.1] As the flow contracts, cracks form at about 120° to each other, which is why the pattern is regular.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.2] The cracks produce columns at right angles to the cooling surface, mainly six-sided, because cracks meeting at about 120° enclose six-sided shapes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.3] Sea erosion exposes the columns on the north Antrim coast, where about 40,000 interlocking basalt columns, most of them six-sided, form the Giant's Causeway, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.4] Fingal's Cave, Isle of Staffa, Scotland, has columns from the same volcanic period.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.5] The Giant's Causeway is a cooled lava flow, not a volcano, and its columns did not form from growing crystals.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.6] Diagram: labelled sketch of hexagonal columns standing at right angles to the cooling surface of the flow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.b] A shield volcano is built by runny lava, which is why it is broad and low rather than tall and steep. Viscosity is how thick a liquid is: low viscosity means it flows easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.0] 1. Low-viscosity (runny) basic lava flows a long way before it cools.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.1] 2. Mostly effusive eruptions (lava pours out steadily rather than exploding) build a broad cone with gentle slopes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.2] 3. Successive flows spread out from the central vent, so the volcano is wide rather than steep.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.3] Example: Mauna Loa and Kilauea, Hawaii.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.4] Diagram: cross-section of a broad, low cone with gentle slopes and a summit crater (the bowl-shaped hollow at the top).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.b] Hawaii is the setting to use for shield volcanoes, with its own record-sized examples and two named lava types.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.0] Hawaii's shield volcanoes sit over a hot spot (a hot area under the crust where magma rises), which is why volcanoes form there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.1] Mauna Loa is the largest active volcano on Earth by volume, for example, which shows how far runny shield-volcano lava can spread.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.2] Mauna Kea is more than 10,000 m high when measured from its base on the sea floor, so most of it lies under the sea.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.3] Pahoehoe lava has a smooth, ropy surface; aa lava has a rough, blocky surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.b] A composite volcano is the opposite of a shield. Its magma is too thick to flow far, so it piles up into a steep cone built of alternating layers.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.0] 1. Viscous (thick, sticky) acidic or intermediate magma is too thick to flow far.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.1] 2. Explosive eruptions alternate with lava flows, so the cone is built of layers of ash and lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.2] 3. Over time these layers build a steep cone with a crater at the summit.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.3] 4. The cone also has a main vent and secondary vents.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.4] Examples: Mount Fuji, Japan; Vesuvius, Italy; Mount Etna, Sicily; Mount St Helens, USA.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.5] Diagram: cross-section of a steep cone showing alternating ash and lava layers, the crater, the main vent and secondary vents.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.b] The smallest volcanic cone is built from loose fragments blown out in short bursts rather than from flowing lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.0] A cinder cone is a small, steep cone built mainly of pyroclastic fragments (rock blown out by explosions) during short, explosive eruptions.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.1] Example: Parícutin, Mexico, which began in a farmer's cornfield in 1943.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.2] Diagram: cross-section of a small, steep cone of loose fragments around a central crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.b] Some eruptions are so large that they destroy the volcano's own summit. The result is a huge basin called a caldera.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.0] A caldera is a large basin formed when the summit of a volcano collapses into the magma chamber (the underground reservoir of magma) emptied by a huge eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.1] A caldera is much larger than a crater and forms by collapse, not by explosion alone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.2] A caldera may fill with water to form a crater lake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.3] Crater Lake, Oregon, USA: Mount Mazama collapsed about 7,700 years ago, and the caldera now holds the lake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.4] Kilauea, Hawaii, has a summit caldera.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.5] Santorini, Greece, and Krakatoa, Indonesia, are further calderas; Anak Krakatau has grown inside the Krakatoa caldera.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.b] Erosion can leave a volcano's hardened core behind after the rest of the cone has gone, because the core is harder than the cone around it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.0] 1. Magma solidifies in the vent of a volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.1] 2. The softer cone around the vent is eroded away.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.2] 3. The resistant plug (rock that resists erosion well) is left standing as a steep-sided hill.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.3] Examples: Slemish, Co. Antrim; Castle Rock, Edinburgh, Scotland, a volcanic plug whose crag-and-tail shape was later shaped by ice.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.4] Diagram: cross-section of a plug standing above the eroded cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.b] Volcanic heat underground also produces smaller surface features, where hot water, steam and gas escape without any lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.0] A geyser erupts hot water and steam from the ground at intervals.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.1] A hot spring is hot water that rises to the surface and flows out without erupting, so it differs from a geyser.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.2] A fumarole is a vent that emits gases and steam.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.3] Example: Old Faithful, Yellowstone, USA, is a geyser.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.b] Iceland is the setting to use for fissure eruptions, geysers and new islands, and it shows how destructive a fissure eruption can be.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.0] The Laki fissure eruption of 1783 poured out huge lava flows; its gases caused crop failure and famine in Iceland, so the harm came from more than the lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.1] Geysir and Strokkur are geysers in Iceland, for example.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.2] Surtsey, a new island formed off southern Iceland from 1963.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.12.3] Hekla and Eyjafjallajökull are also Icelandic volcanoes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.13.b] The biggest plutonic landform is the batholith, a mass of rock that cooled deep underground and only becomes a landform once the rock above it has been stripped away.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.13.0] A batholith is a huge dome-shaped mass of magma that cooled slowly deep underground into granite.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.13.1] Heat from the magma altered the surrounding rock into a metamorphic aureole (metamorphic means changed by heat), a ring of altered rock around the batholith, for example mica schist.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.13.2] When the overlying rock is eroded, the granite is exposed and forms upland areas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.b] The Leinster batholith is Ireland's intrusive case study. It is the largest granite mass in Ireland and forms the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.0] The granite intruded about 400 million years ago, during the Caledonian mountain-building period.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.1] It is the largest granite mass in Ireland and runs from south Co. Dublin south-west through Wicklow and Carlow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.2] The overlying slate was eroded, exposing the granite as the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.3] A band of mica schist, the metamorphic aureole, surrounds the granite.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.4] The granite uplands have rounded summits, such as Lugnaquilla below.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.14.5] Lugnaquilla, at 925 m, is the highest mountain in Wicklow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.15.b] Magma that is forced between layers of rock, rather than rising to the surface, can bend those layers up or down. Which way the layers bend gives the two features their names.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.15.0] A laccolith forms when magma is forced between layers of rock, arching the layers above into a dome, so a laccolith bulges up.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.15.1] Henry Mountains, Utah, USA, are laccoliths.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.15.2] A lopolith is a saucer-shaped intrusion that sags downwards in the centre, so a lopolith sags down.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.b] Two sheet-shaped intrusions differ only in direction. A sill runs along the rock layers, while a dyke cuts across them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.0] A sill is a horizontal sheet of magma injected between the layers of rock (along a bedding plane, the boundary between layers), parallel to them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.1] When exposed, a resistant sill forms cliffs, ledges and waterfalls, because it resists erosion better than the layers around it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.2] Fair Head, Co. Antrim, is a dolerite sill with columnar jointing.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.3] Great Whin Sill, northern England, carries part of Hadrian's Wall along its crest.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.4] A dyke is a vertical or near-vertical sheet of magma cutting across the layers.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.5] A dyke harder than the surrounding rock stands up as a wall-like ridge because the softer rock erodes around it; a softer dyke is eroded into a trench.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.16.6] The Isle of Arran, Scotland, has a dyke swarm (a group of many dykes).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.17.b] Plutonic features end up as high ground because of differential erosion, meaning different rocks wear away at different speeds.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.17.0] Plutonic rocks such as granite are usually more resistant than the rocks around them, so they wear away more slowly.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.17.1] Once the surrounding cover is removed, the plutonic rock stands as upland while the weaker rock is lowered, because the weaker rock erodes faster.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.17.2] Diagram: one block of crust showing a batholith, laccolith, lopolith, sill, dyke and volcanic plug, each labelled.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.18.b] Devon and Cornwall in south-west England are the international example of granite uplands, parts of one exposed batholith.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.18.0] Dartmoor and Bodmin Moor are granite uplands, parts of one large batholith exposed by erosion, so they are upland for the same reason as the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.18.1] Haytor, Dartmoor, is a tor, a granite outcrop rising above the moor.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.18.2] St Austell, Cornwall, is a centre of china clay (kaolin) quarried from decomposed granite.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s1: 1. The rock cycle and rock formation
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.b] Rock is never permanent. It is continually formed, modified, destroyed and reconstituted (built again from the broken pieces), so the three rock types are linked in one cycle. Two sets of forces drive it, one working from inside the earth and one at its surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.0] Endogenic (internal) forces, driven from within the earth, form and modify rock through magma (molten rock below the surface), heat and pressure.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.1] Exogenic (external) forces, working at the surface, destroy rock through weathering (breakdown of rock in place by weather) and erosion (wearing away and carrying off of rock fragments).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.2] Deposition (dropping of carried sediment, the loose fragments of rock) reconstitutes rock, because the sediment is later turned into new sedimentary rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.b] The cycle is easiest to learn as a circular flow chart in which each rock type is reached from another by a named process. Short-cuts across the circle show that rock does not have to follow the full loop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.0] 1. Magma cools and crystallises (forms crystals as it hardens) to make igneous rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.1] 2. Weathering and erosion break igneous rock down into sediment, because rock at the surface is attacked by weather.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.2] 3. Deposition, compaction and cementation turn the sediment into sedimentary rock, because the layers are squeezed and glued together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.3] 4. Heat and pressure change sedimentary rock into metamorphic rock, because buried rock is squeezed and heated without melting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.4] 5. Melting turns the metamorphic rock back into magma, and the cycle starts again.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.5] Short-cuts: any rock can be weathered into sediment, and any rock can be metamorphosed (changed by heat and pressure) or melted, so the arrows do not all follow one loop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.6] Diagram: draw a circular flow chart with magma and the three rock types as the nodes, and label each arrow with its process, for example crystallisation or compaction and cementation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.b] The rock cycle does not run at random. It is tied to the moving plates (the large slabs that make up the earth's crust), which decide where each stage happens.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.0] Rock is created at plate boundaries and at hotspots (fixed points of rising magma), because magma reaches the crust there.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.1] Rock is metamorphosed at collision zones, where plates push together and the heat and pressure are great.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.2] Rock is destroyed or recycled at subduction zones, where one plate sinks beneath another and the rock melts back into magma.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.b] Igneous rock forms when molten rock cools and crystallises. Magma is molten rock below the surface; lava is molten rock at the surface. Where the rock cools decides how big its crystals grow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.0] Crystal size depends on cooling rate: slow cooling gives large crystals because they have time to grow, and fast cooling gives small ones.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.1] Plutonic (intrusive) rock is igneous rock that cools slowly deep in the crust, so its crystals are large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.2] Volcanic (extrusive) rock is lava that cools quickly at the surface, so its crystals are small.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.3] Uses: granite is used as building stone, kerbs, worktops and headstones; basalt is crushed for road chippings and aggregate (crushed stone used in construction).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.b] Granite is a plutonic rock. When magma pushes into surrounding rock and cools there, it forms masses called intrusions, and their shape gives them different names.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.0] Granite is a plutonic rock built from quartz, feldspar and mica crystals, which are large enough to see because the magma cooled slowly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.1] Batholiths are huge dome-shaped masses of plutonic rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.2] Dykes are vertical intrusions that cut across the beds (layers) of the rock around them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.3] Sills are horizontal intrusions that lie along bedding planes (the horizontal surfaces between layers).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.4] Plutonic rock reaches the surface only after the rock above it has been eroded away, because it formed deep underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.b] Volcanic rock forms at the surface, so it cools fast. Which kind forms depends on the plate setting, the tectonic setting, in which the magma reaches the crust.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.0] Basalt is the commonest volcanic rock and forms from runny lava, which flows easily before it sets.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.1] Basalt forms at constructive (divergent) boundaries, where plates move apart, and at hotspots.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.2] Andesite and granite batholiths form above subduction zones at destructive boundaries, where one plate sinks beneath another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.b] Sedimentary rock is formed from sediment laid down in layers (strata), usually in seas, lakes, river basins and deserts. Loose sediment then has to be turned into solid rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.0] Sediment comes from weathered and eroded fragments of older rock, from the remains of plants and animals, or from minerals precipitated from water (that is, coming out of solution as solids).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.1] Sediment builds up in subsiding basins (areas of sinking land), on continental shelves (the shallow sea floor around a continent) and along trailing (passive) margins, where no plate boundary lies at the coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.2] Lithification is the conversion of loose sediment into solid rock by compaction and cementation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.3] Compaction: the weight of overlying layers squeezes out water and air, packing the grains together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.4] Cementation: minerals such as silica, calcite and iron oxide, carried by groundwater, bind the grains together like glue.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.b] Because sedimentary rock is laid down in layers from material at the earth's surface, it often contains fossils and shows bedding planes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.0] Many sedimentary rocks contain fossils, the remains or traces of past life, because living things were buried in the sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.1] Bedding planes are horizontal surfaces separating one layer from the next.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.2] Joints are vertical cracks that cut through the layers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.3] Many sedimentary rocks are porous, holding water in the spaces between grains. Porous is not the same as permeable (letting water pass through).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.b] Sedimentary rocks are sorted into three groups by the material they are made from.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.0] Clastic rocks are made of broken fragments: sandstone from sand, and shale from mud and clay.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.1] Organic rocks are made of the remains of living things: limestone from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures, and coal from plant remains that accumulated in swamps.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.2] Chemical rocks form from minerals that come out of solution: rock salt and other evaporites form as seawater evaporates and leaves its dissolved minerals behind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.b] Each Irish sedimentary rock records the conditions in which it formed, such as the climate and the setting at that time. For a longer answer, give the conditions as well as the rock and its place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.0] Carboniferous limestone (Carboniferous period, about 360 to 300 million years ago) formed when Ireland lay near the equator under a warm, shallow, clear sea, where shells and skeletons built up the deposit. It covers most of the central lowland and forms the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.1] Old Red Sandstone (Devonian period) formed in hot, semi-arid (very dry) conditions from sand and pebbles laid down by rivers; its red colour comes from iron oxide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.2] Old Red Sandstone forms the Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtees and the Comeraghs.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.3] Shale and sandstone of the Cliffs of Moher, Co. Clare, are layers of shale, siltstone and sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.4] Chalk lies beneath the basalt along the Antrim coast, so the chalk is the older rock and the lava flowed over it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.5] Coal at Arigna, Co. Roscommon, and Castlecomer, Co. Kilkenny, formed from plant remains in swamps; both are former coal-mining areas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.6] Rock salt is mined at the Kilroot salt mine, near Carrickfergus, Co. Antrim.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.b] Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by great heat and/or pressure without melting. The original rock is called the parent rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.0] The two factors that cause metamorphism are heat and pressure.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.1] Minerals recrystallise (re-form as new crystals) while the rock stays solid, so the rock usually becomes harder and denser.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.2] The rock may develop banding or cleavage (a tendency to split along flat surfaces).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.b] Metamorphism happens on two scales, depending on whether the heat and pressure come from a single intrusion or from the movement of plates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.0] Thermal (contact) metamorphism: rock beside an igneous intrusion is baked by its heat, forming a ring of altered rock called a metamorphic aureole; it is small in scale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.1] Regional metamorphism: heat and pressure act over huge areas during plate collision and mountain building; it is large in scale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.b] Each metamorphic rock comes from a particular parent rock, and the pairs can be asked either way round.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.0] Limestone becomes marble; Connemara marble is green and is quarried in Connemara, Co. Galway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.1] Sandstone becomes quartzite, so the parent of quartzite is a sedimentary rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.2] Shale (mudstone) becomes slate, and with more heat and pressure becomes schist, so schist is the more strongly altered rock. Slate is quarried on Valentia Island, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.3] Granite becomes gneiss, so gneiss is a metamorphic rock formed from an igneous parent.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.4] Quartzite forms Errigal, Co. Donegal; Croagh Patrick, Co. Mayo; the Great Sugar Loaf, Co. Wicklow; and the Twelve Bens, Connemara.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.5] Mica schist is found in Donegal and Connemara, and a metamorphic aureole of mica schist surrounds the Leinster granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.6] Gneiss at Inishtrahull, off Co. Donegal, is about 1.7 billion years old and is the oldest rock in Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.b] Ireland's rock types form a recognisable pattern across the island, and each reflects an event in its geological past.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.0] Limestone floors the central lowland, and Old Red Sandstone forms the ridges of Munster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.1] Granite outcrops (shows at the surface) in Wicklow, Donegal and the Mournes; basalt covers Co. Antrim; metamorphic rocks lie in Donegal, Connemara and Mayo.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.2] Plutonic example: the Leinster batholith, the granite core of the Wicklow Mountains, is about 400 million years old and is the largest granite mass in Ireland and Britain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.3] The Mourne granite, in the Mourne Mountains, Co. Down, is much younger, about 56 million years old.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.4] Volcanic example: the Antrim plateau was built by lava flows about 60 million years ago, as the Atlantic began to open. The Giant's Causeway, Co. Antrim, is basalt columns.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.5] The closing of the Iapetus Ocean during Caledonian mountain building produced the Leinster and Donegal granites and regional metamorphism.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.b] North America has two very different edges. The western edge is an active plate margin, where plates are moving and rock is made and changed. The eastern edge is a trailing (passive) margin, where no plate boundary lies at the coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.0] Active western margin: Pacific plates subduct (sink) beneath or slide past North America.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.1] Volcanic rock: the Cascade Range volcanoes, including Mount St Helens in Washington state (erupted 1980), lie above a subducting oceanic plate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.2] Plutonic rock: the Sierra Nevada granite batholith, California.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.3] Regional metamorphism affects the western mountain belts.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.4] The San Andreas Fault, California, is a transform boundary where plates slide past each other.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.5] Trailing eastern margin: there is no plate boundary at the coast, so there are no active volcanoes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.6] Because the margin is quiet, thick sediments accumulate on the Atlantic coastal plain, on the continental shelf and in the Mississippi delta.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.7] The Appalachians are old, worn-down fold mountains of sedimentary and metamorphic rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.b] Rocks can be told apart by what they look like, and photo questions rely on this. Each rock below has one or two clues that separate it from the others.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.0] Granite: speckled, with large interlocking crystals, because it cooled slowly underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.1] Basalt: dark and fine-grained because it cooled quickly; it may form columns, as at the Giant's Causeway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.2] Sandstone: gritty grains, often reddish or buff, and layered, because it is made of cemented sand laid down in beds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.3] Limestone: pale grey, and it may show fossils because it is made from shells and skeletons.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.4] Marble: white or coloured, with sparkling crystals and no fossils, which separates it from limestone, its parent rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.5] Quartzite: pale, very hard and glassy.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.6] Slate: dark grey, and it splits into thin sheets, which is the cleavage (splitting) that metamorphism can produce.

CANDIDATES geo-rivers-s4: 4. Human interaction: river processes
[geo-rivers-s4.0.b] Rivers are useful, so people change them. Every change alters how the river's processes work: its velocity (speed of flow), erosion (wearing away of bed and banks), transport (carrying of material), deposition (dropping of material) and flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.0] Reasons for interfering include making electricity, supplying water, irrigation (watering crops), navigation (letting boats travel), draining farmland and protecting towns from flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.1] The three measures studied here are hydro-electric dams, canalisation and flood control, each with Irish and international examples.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.b] A hydro-electric dam turns the energy of falling water into electricity. It does this by holding the river back and then releasing it in a controlled way.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.0] The dam stores water in a reservoir (an artificial lake behind the dam), and the water is then released so that it falls through turbines (wheels that spin and drive generators).
[geo-rivers-s4.1.1] The drop in height of the water (the head) gives the power, so a greater drop means more energy from the same water.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.b] Upstream of a dam the river meets still water in the reservoir. This changes its processes completely, because a river can only carry material while it keeps moving.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.0] Velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load (the sand, silt and stones it was carrying) there.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.1] The reservoir slowly fills with sediment, a process called sedimentation, which reduces its capacity (how much water it can hold).
[geo-rivers-s4.2.2] A delta (a fan of deposited sediment) can build where the river enters the reservoir, because that is where the flow slows and drops its load.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.b] Below the dam the river has lost its sediment and its natural rhythm of floods and low flows. Both changes alter erosion and deposition.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.0] Water released from the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam. This is called clear-water erosion.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.1] The floodplain (the flat land beside the river) and delta receive no new silt, so deltas and coasts erode because nothing replaces the material lost.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.2] Discharge (the volume of water flowing past a point) is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.3] Less flooding means less deposition on the floodplain, because floodwater is what normally lays down silt there.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.4] Where water is diverted out of the old channel, the bypassed channel carries much less water.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.5] Dams block migrating salmon and eels, which cannot pass the wall. Fish passes (routes around the dam) and trapping and transporting fish around dams are responses.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.b] The Shannon Scheme is one power station at Ardnacrusha, near Limerick, fed by a weir and a canal. It is not a series of dams.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.0] It was built 1925–1929 by the German firm Siemens for the Irish Free State, and it was Ireland's first large hydro-electric station.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.1] Parteen Weir (a low barrier across the river) diverts most of the Shannon's water into a headrace canal to Ardnacrusha. A tailrace (the channel carrying used water away) returns it to the river near Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.2] Lough Derg's level is controlled by Parteen Weir, so the lough acts as the scheme's reservoir.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.3] The original channel between Parteen and Limerick, the Old River Shannon, carries much less water because most of it is diverted.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.4] Salmon and eel stocks declined. The ESB built a fish pass, and it traps silver eels (adult eels migrating downstream) upstream and transports them past the dam.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.b] Ardnacrusha is not the only Irish example. Four more dams and power stations can be named in an answer.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.0] Poulaphouca dam, River Liffey: created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, Co. Wicklow. It generates electricity and supplies drinking water to Dublin.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.1] Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid dams, River Lee, Co. Cork.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.2] Cathaleen's Fall power station, River Erne, at Ballyshannon, Co. Donegal.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.b] The Aswan High Dam is the main international case study. It holds back the Nile in a huge reservoir to give Egypt control over a river that once flooded and shrank unpredictably.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.0] Aswan High Dam, River Nile, Egypt, was completed in 1970. Its reservoir is Lake Nasser, which extends into Sudan.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.1] Benefits are flood control, year-round irrigation (which allows more than one crop a year), hydro-electricity, and protection from drought.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.b] The dam's effects follow from the same processes as any dam: trapped sediment, clear-water erosion and regulated flow. They then spread into farming, fishing, health and settlement.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.0] Silt is trapped in Lake Nasser, so farmers downstream rely on artificial fertilisers instead of the natural silt that floods once left.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.1] The river bed below the dam is scoured (worn away) by clear water, which has spare energy to erode.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.2] The Nile delta coast is eroding, e.g. at the Rosetta promontory (a headland on the delta coast), because no new sediment arrives to replace what the sea removes.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.3] The sardine fishery off the delta in the Mediterranean declined sharply.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.4] Irrigated land suffers waterlogging (soil saturated with water) and salinisation (a build-up of salt in the soil).
[geo-rivers-s4.7.5] Perennial irrigation canals (canals that hold water all year) spread the disease schistosomiasis (bilharzia).
[geo-rivers-s4.7.6] Nubian people were displaced, and the temples of Abu Simbel were moved to higher ground.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.b] The Three Gorges Dam is a second international example, built on the Yangtze to produce power and to reduce floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.0] Three Gorges Dam, Hubei province, China, is the world's largest power station by installed capacity.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.1] It was completed in 2006 and fully operational in 2012, and over one million people were relocated.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.2] It aims to reduce flood peaks downstream, because the Yangtze floods of 1998 killed thousands.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.3] It traps sediment that once reached the river's lower course and delta, so those areas receive less of the sediment they depended on.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.b] Canalisation modifies an existing river channel. It is not the same as building a canal.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.0] Methods are straightening, deepening and widening the channel, sometimes lining it with concrete, cutting through meanders (bends in the river), dredging (scooping sediment from the bed) and building locks and weirs for navigation.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.1] Purposes are navigation, faster drainage of land, and flood control.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.b] Making a river straighter and smoother makes the water move faster. Almost every other effect follows from that rise in velocity.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.0] A straighter, shorter course has a steeper gradient (slope) and less friction (drag from the bed and banks), so velocity rises.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.1] Water and floods are passed downstream faster, so flood peaks downstream are higher and arrive sooner.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.2] Faster flow has more energy, so it erodes the bed and banks.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.3] Where the channel widens or the flow slows again, deposition increases and dredging is needed to keep the channel clear.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.4] Meanders, oxbow lakes (cut-off loops of old meander), pools and riffles (deeper and shallower stretches of the bed) are lost, and the river is cut off from its floodplain.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.b] Canalisation has been used for drainage and navigation in Ireland and for shortening and straightening big rivers abroad.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.0] Arterial drainage (deepening and straightening main rivers to drain land) was carried out by the Office of Public Works (OPW) under the Arterial Drainage Act 1945, e.g. the Moy and Boyne schemes. Rivers were deepened and straightened mainly to drain farmland, not to protect towns.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.1] Arterial drainage effects were changes to fish spawning beds, bank erosion and downstream flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.2] Mississippi: cut-offs (new short channels across bends) made by the US Army Corps of Engineers shortened the river.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.3] Rhine: the Upper Rhine was straightened in the 19th century under Johann Gottfried Tulla, which sped flood waves downstream and caused the bed to erode downward.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.4] Navigation in Ireland: locks and weirs on the Shannon Navigation control water levels and slow the flow.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.b] Flood control measures respond to the causes of river flooding, so it helps to know what those causes are.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.0] Prolonged or intense rain, and saturated ground (soil that is already full of water and cannot absorb more), send water into rivers faster than they can carry it.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.1] Urban surfaces and drains speed runoff (water flowing over land into rivers). Deforestation and drainage of bogs and farmland upstream add to it.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.2] Building on floodplains puts homes in the path of floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.b] Hard engineering uses built structures to hold floodwater back or move it away. Each one changes river processes, and some pass the problem on downstream.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.0] Embankments and levees (raised banks) confine flow to the channel, so deposition raises the bed and the banks must be raised again.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.1] With embankments and levees the floodplain gets no silt, and failure gives sudden, severe floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.2] Flood walls and demountable (removable) barriers protect towns.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.3] Dams and reservoirs store floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.4] Dredging and deepening increase channel capacity, but the flow is faster and downstream flooding can increase.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.5] Relief (bypass) channels take floodwater around the town.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.b] Soft engineering works with the river instead of against it. It slows water or gives it space, so natural processes carry on.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.0] Floodplain zoning uses planning controls on building, so that homes are kept off land that floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.1] Afforestation (planting trees) upstream works through interception (leaves catching rain), which slows runoff.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.2] Wetlands and bogs can be restored, and washlands (land that is allowed to flood) give the river more room.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.3] Flood forecasting and warning give people time to prepare.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.4] Effect on processes: natural flooding and floodplain deposition continue.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.b] Irish floods show hard engineering, dam management and flood-risk planning in practice.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.0] Cork, November 2009: the River Lee flooded when the ESB released large volumes from Inniscarra dam during very heavy rain because the reservoir was full.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.1] The releases were not the sole cause, because the rain was exceptional. The University College Cork and the city water treatment works were flooded, and many homes lost their water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.2] Clonmel, Co. Tipperary: an OPW flood relief scheme on the River Suir uses demountable barriers that are put in place when floods are forecast.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.3] December 2015: Storm Desmond caused prolonged flooding on the Shannon, in towns such as Athlone.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.4] CFRAM (Catchment Flood Risk Assessment and Management) is the OPW programme that maps flood risk.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.b] The Mississippi is the international case study for levees. It shows both what they protect and how they can fail.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.0] The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 led to the US federal levee and floodway system run by the US Army Corps of Engineers.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.1] The 1993 flood breached many levees along the upper Mississippi and Missouri.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.2] In August 2005 Hurricane Katrina caused levee and floodwall failures in New Orleans, and about 80% of the city flooded.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.3] Levees stop flood silt reaching the delta, which contributes to the loss of Louisiana's coastal wetlands.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.b] The Netherlands shows a soft-engineering response to flooding: instead of building higher walls, it gives the river space.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.0] Room for the River (Ruimte voor de Rivier), Netherlands, started after near-disastrous high water on the Rhine and Maas in 1993 and 1995.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.1] Measures include moving dykes (embankments) back, lowering floodplains and digging side channels so the river can spread out safely.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.b] Exams expect a judgement, so weigh what these measures achieve against what they cost.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.0] Benefits are protection of homes, electricity, navigation and water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.1] Costs are problems moved downstream, sediment starvation (rivers and deltas losing the sediment they need), habitat loss, high cost, and a false sense of security.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.2] There is a trend towards working with natural processes.
