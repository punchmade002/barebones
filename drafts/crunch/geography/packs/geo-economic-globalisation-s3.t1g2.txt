TARGET geo-economic-globalisation-s3: 3. Patterns in world trade
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.b] Trade is the buying and selling of goods and services between countries. Exports leave a country and imports arrive in it, and data questions use these words constantly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.0] Visible trade is goods that can be seen and counted, such as cars, food and computer chips.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.1] Invisible trade is services, which cannot be held or counted in the same way, such as software, financial services, tourism and aircraft leasing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.2] Balance of trade: the value of exports minus the value of imports, which gives one number that shows whether a country sells more than it buys.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.3] A trade surplus means exports are greater than imports; a trade deficit means imports are greater than exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.b] The balance of trade is only part of the picture of a country's dealings with the world. The balance of payments is the wider record.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.0] Balance of payments: the record of all money flows between a country and the rest of the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.1] It includes trade in goods and services, plus investment flows (money put into businesses abroad), profits sent home, borrowing and transfers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.2] It is a wider measure than the balance of trade, which records only exports and imports, so the two are not the same thing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.b] A country that keeps buying more from abroad than it sells pays a price at home, while exports bring benefits. Each effect follows from where the money goes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.0] A persistent trade deficit (imports greater than exports year after year) means money flows out of the country to pay for the extra imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.1] The country may have to borrow or sell assets (things it owns, such as businesses or property) to pay for those imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.2] Home industries and jobs can be lost as cheaper imports replace domestic production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.3] The currency (the country's money) may weaken when a country persistently imports more than it exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.4] Exports bring jobs, tax revenue, foreign earnings and economic growth, because foreign buyers pay money into the country.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.b] A country that sells to many different countries is safer than one that sells mainly to one. This is the advantage of many export partners.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.0] A country with many export partners is less dependent on any one market.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.1] A recession (a fall in economic activity), tariff or crisis in one country, such as the US tariffs imposed in 2025, then does less damage to its overall trade, because the other markets keep buying.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.b] Governments can open their markets to imports or restrict them. Protectionism is the restricting approach, and international bodies try to set common rules.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.0] Protectionism restricts imports to protect home industries from foreign competition, so home producers face less pressure from cheaper foreign goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.1] Barriers to trade include tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on the quantity imported), subsidies (payments that help home producers compete with imports), and regulations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.2] Trade blocs (groups of countries that agree trade rules among themselves) remove barriers between their own members, so members trade with each other more easily than with outsiders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.3] GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade) was signed in 1947 to reduce tariffs between countries, so goods cost less to trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.4] The World Trade Organisation (WTO) was set up on 1 January 1995, with headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland; it has over 160 member countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.5] The WTO sets the rules of world trade and settles disputes between members, so countries have a common referee.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.6] Fair trade gives producers in developing countries a guaranteed minimum price and a premium (an extra payment), shown by the Fairtrade mark.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.b] World trade has grown much faster than world output since 1950, and most countries now depend on imports and exports. Several changes made this possible.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.0] Falling trade barriers, through GATT and the WTO, made it cheaper to trade across borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.1] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard-sized steel boxes) began with the first container ship voyage, the Ideal X of Malcolm McLean, USA, in 1956; containers made loading and shipping faster and cheaper.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.2] Shanghai, China, has been the world's busiest container port since 2010.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.3] Air freight and information and communication technology (ICT) speed up trade in high-value goods and services, for example by moving them quickly and sharing information instantly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.4] Multinational companies (MNCs, firms that operate in many countries) trade components between their own plants in different countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.5] The entry of China and Eastern Europe into world trade added large new producers and markets, so there were more goods to sell and more buyers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.b] The three triad areas and the rest of the world are linked by shipping routes and by products whose parts come from many countries. Because they are so linked, a disruption in one place spreads worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.0] One product links many countries: the iPhone and Intel chip supply chains (the series of countries and firms that make a product's parts and assemble it) each pass through several countries. There is more on this in One multinational company (MNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.1] The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, and the Panama Canal, opened in 1914, are chokepoints (narrow passages that much shipping must use) on the shipping routes linking the triad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.2] In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given blocked the Suez Canal for about six days, holding up hundreds of ships.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.3] From late 2023 attacks on shipping in the Red Sea forced many ships to sail around the Cape of Good Hope, Africa.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.4] The 2008-09 global financial crisis caused world trade to fall sharply in 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.5] COVID-19 (2020-21) disrupted supply chains, for example in the 2021 chip shortage.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.b] Most of the world's trade and production is concentrated in three areas. The trading triad is North America, Europe and the Pacific Rim, the three core economic areas of the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.0] The triad produces most of the world's manufactured goods, such as cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.1] It holds the headquarters of most multinational companies (MNCs), so company decisions are made there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.2] Most world trade takes place among the three areas, and most foreign direct investment (money that firms invest in businesses and factories in other countries) flows between them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.b] The USA is the triad's North American member and the dominant economy within it. Its trade is shaped by its size, its trade bloc and its use of tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.0] The USA is the world's largest economy and its largest importer, so its demand matters to exporters everywhere.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.1] It has had a trade deficit in goods every year since the mid-1970s, which means it imports more goods than it exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.2] NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement: USA, Canada, Mexico) took effect on 1 January 1994.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.3] USMCA (United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement) replaced NAFTA on 1 July 2020, so the three countries kept a trade bloc.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.4] Mexico overtook China in 2023 as the largest source of US imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.5] From 2018 the USA put tariffs (taxes on imports) on many Chinese goods in the US-China trade war.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.6] In 2025 the USA raised tariffs on imports from most countries, which raised the price of goods entering the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.b] Europe is one of the three triad areas, and it trades as a bloc. The EU is a union of countries that trade freely with one another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.0] The EU is a single market (members trade as if in one market) of 27 member states, since the UK left the EU.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.1] The single market began on 1 January 1993, removing barriers to trade between members, so goods move freely between them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.2] The EU is the largest trading bloc by value, with strong trade with the USA and China, for example in goods sold between the blocs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.3] The euro, the shared currency, was introduced in 1999; euro notes and coins followed in 2002.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.4] In July 2025 the EU and USA agreed a 15% US tariff on most EU goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.b] In the triad, the Pacific Rim means mainly East and South-East Asia, together with Australia. India is not part of it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.0] Members include, for example, Japan, China, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, the ASEAN countries and Australia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.1] ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) was founded in 1967 as a group of South-East Asian countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.2] APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) was founded in 1989.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.3] RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership) has 15 Asia-Pacific members, including China, Japan, South Korea, Australia and the ASEAN members.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.4] RCEP was signed in November 2020 and came into force from 2022; its members hold about 30% of the world's population and GDP (the total value of goods and services a country produces in a year).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.5] The Asian Tigers are South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, fast-growing Asian economies. China and Japan are not Tigers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.b] Japan is a Pacific Rim economy that grew after the Second World War by exporting manufactured goods. Its story shows how a country with few raw materials can grow by selling manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.0] After the Second World War Japan's growth was based on exporting manufactured goods, mainly cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.1] Japan has few raw materials, so it must import most of its oil, coal and iron ore.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.2] Japan was the world's second-largest economy from the late 1960s until China overtook it in 2010; Germany overtook Japan in 2023.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.3] Toyota, based in Toyota City, uses just-in-time production, which delivers parts as they are needed rather than storing them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.4] Other major Japanese companies are, for example, Honda, Sony and Nissan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.5] To avoid trade barriers, Japanese firms opened factories inside the EU and USA; Nissan's car plant at Sunderland, north-east England, opened in 1986, inside the European Economic Community (EEC, the EU's earlier name).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.6] Japan's growth has been slower since the 1990s, so it is no longer expanding as quickly as it did after the war.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.b] China moved from a closed economy to the world's largest exporter of goods, which is why it is called the workshop of the world. Government reforms opened it to trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.0] Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms, from 1978, opened China's closed economy to foreign trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.1] The first Special Economic Zones (SEZs, areas with special rules to attract foreign firms) were set up in 1980, including Shenzhen beside Hong Kong.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.2] Shenzhen is in mainland China and is not part of Hong Kong; it grew from a small town to over 17 million people (2020 census).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.3] China joined the WTO on 11 December 2001, which gave it a place under world trade rules.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.4] China has been the world's largest exporter of goods since 2009, and it overtook Japan as the second-largest economy in 2010.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.5] China runs a huge trade surplus, because it sells so much abroad; meaning its exports greatly exceed its imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.6] The Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, links China to Asia, Africa and Europe through ports, railways and roads.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.7] The trade war with the USA, begun in 2018, also affects China; the US side is in the North America notes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.b] South Korea is the standard example of a newly industrialised country (NIC, a country that has recently built a large manufacturing economy). It went from poverty to high income by selling manufactured goods abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.0] South Korea was war-damaged by the Korean War (1950-53) and was one of the world's poorest countries in the early 1960s.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.1] Under President Park Chung-hee (1961-79) it industrialised through exports and rose from poverty to high income.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.2] Its economy is dominated by chaebol, family-controlled business groups such as Samsung (founded 1938), Hyundai, LG and SK.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.3] Ulsan is home to Hyundai's shipyards, among the world's largest, and to a Hyundai car plant, as an example of an industrial city built on exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.4] South Korea is a world leader in shipbuilding, cars, electronics and memory chips, and Samsung makes smartphones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.5] South Korea is one of the Asian Tigers and joined the OECD (the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) in 1996.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.14.b] Not every region shares in the triad's trade. Some areas are left on the edges of world trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.14.0] Africa, much of Latin America and South Asia hold only a small share of world trade, mostly in raw materials. There is more on this in How the global economy is organised.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.b] An international division of labour means that countries and regions specialise in different stages of production and different types of work. The first pattern grew out of colonial rule.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.0] In the old, colonial pattern, colonies and developing countries exported raw materials and food, while Europe and later the USA manufactured goods and sold them back.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.1] Sudan's Gezira Scheme, from 1925, grew irrigated cotton for the British (Lancashire) textile mills.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.2] India exported raw cotton and tea to Britain under colonial rule, as an example of raw materials and food leaving a colony.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.3] Ghana (then the Gold Coast) exported cocoa; Cote d'Ivoire and Ghana together grow over half of the world's cocoa today, still mostly exported unprocessed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.b] From the 1960s-70s the pattern changed. Multinational companies began to split production, placing each stage where it suited them best.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.0] Multinational companies moved assembly and routine manufacturing to newly industrialised countries (NICs) and low-wage countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.1] The core (the rich, powerful economies) kept headquarters, research and development (R&D, work on new products and technology), design, finance and marketing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.2] The periphery (the poorer countries on the edge of the world economy) still supplies raw materials.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.3] As an example, Apple's packaging carried the line 'Designed by Apple in California. Assembled in China.'
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.4] Intel's R&D is at Hillsboro, Oregon; its fabs (chip factories) include Leixlip in Ireland; assembly and test take place in Malaysia and Vietnam. There is more on this in One multinational company (MNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.b] The pattern is not fixed. Services are now divided between countries, and countries move up or spread to new places.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.0] Services are now divided too: Bengaluru (Bangalore), India, is an IT services hub for firms such as Infosys, Wipro and Tata Consultancy Services, and IT and call centres are located in India.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.1] China is moving into high-value products instead of only low-cost assembly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.2] Low-cost assembly is spreading to Vietnam, Bangladesh and India, because firms look for cheaper places to assemble.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.b] Ireland is a very open economy: its exports are larger than its GDP. It has moved from supplying food to the UK to hosting high-value manufacturing and services by multinational companies (MNCs).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.0] Goods exports passed 200 billion euro in 2024 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office), a record.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.1] Most of this export value is produced by MNC plants, not by Irish-owned firms, so large exports reflect MNC production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.2] Main goods exports: pharmaceuticals and chemicals (the largest category), medical devices (a cluster at Galway, with firms such as Medtronic and Boston Scientific), computer chips and electronics, and food and drink (beef, dairy and whiskey).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.3] Main services exports: computer services (Ireland is one of the world's largest exporters of them), financial services, aircraft leasing and tourism.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.4] Irish-based lessors (companies that own aircraft and lease them to airlines) manage over half of the world's leased commercial aircraft, from Dublin and Shannon.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.5] Ireland has a large goods trade surplus, because it exports far more goods than it imports, the largest of it with the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.b] Where Irish goods go reflects who makes them. The main market is the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.0] The USA is the largest single market for Irish goods, taking about a third of them, which makes it Ireland's biggest buyer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.1] Other main markets, such as EU partners Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands, then the UK and China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.2] US pharmaceutical and technology MNCs export from their Irish plants back to the USA and worldwide, which is why the USA dominates.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.3] In early 2025 Irish exports to the USA rose sharply as US buyers stockpiled medicines (bought extra in advance) before new tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.b] Ireland's trade with the UK changed after the UK left the EU. Brexit is the usual reason to give.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.0] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition period (when the old trade rules still applied) ended on 31 December 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.1] Brexit brought customs checks (inspections of goods at the border) on trade with the UK and took the UK out of the EU single market.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.2] A weaker pound makes Irish goods dearer for UK buyers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.3] Irish firms have diversified into other markets, so they depend less on the UK.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.b] Ireland's export success depends on a narrow base, which brings risk.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.0] Ireland depends on a few US MNCs and on the US market, so a problem for those firms or that market hits Irish exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.1] US tariffs from 2025 expose Irish exports to the USA to new barriers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.b] Data-response questions give charts and tables of trade and ask you to calculate. The method is the same every time.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.0] Read values from the chart or table and state their units before calculating.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.1] Balance of trade for one country: subtract imports from exports; a negative result is a trade deficit.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.2] Difference between two countries: subtract the smaller value from the larger, so the answer is positive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.3] Total exports: add the values for every partner, so none is missed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.4] Use a bar chart to compare values between countries and a line graph to show change over time, because each suits that kind of data.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s2: 2. Spatial variations in development
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.b] Development (how wealthy and well-off people are, in income, health and education) is not spread evenly. It varies between continents, countries, regions within a country and districts within cities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.0] The Brandt Line, from the Brandt Report North–South: A Programme for Survival (1980), separates the rich North from the poorer South. It dates from 1980, so it is not a current UN classification.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.1] The Brandt Commission that wrote the report was chaired by the former West German Chancellor Willy Brandt. The line runs roughly along 30°N, dipping south so that Australia and New Zealand fall in the North.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.2] The North (North America, Europe, Russia, Japan, Australia and New Zealand) is richer than the South (Latin America, Africa and most of Asia).
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.3] The line is a simplification because some countries in the South are now rich: Singapore, South Korea and the Gulf states.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.4] The terms First World and Third World are outdated. The critique of these labels belongs to the Global Interdependence option.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.5] Global inequality is extreme: the richest tenth of the world's people own about 76% of global wealth, and the poorest half own about 2% (World Inequality Report 2022). The justice angle is in the Global issues and a justice perspective note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.b] The Human Development Index (HDI) combines income, health and education into one score per country. Mapping it shows the same North–South pattern as the Brandt Line, with important exceptions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.0] Very high HDI is found in places such as Europe, North America, Australia, Japan and the Gulf.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.1] High and medium HDI cover most of Latin America, East and South-East Asia and North Africa.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.2] Low HDI is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (Africa south of the Sahara), with Afghanistan, Yemen and Haiti outside Africa. Examples in Africa include Niger, Chad, South Sudan, Somalia and the Central African Republic. Haiti is the poorest country in the Americas.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.3] The named rankings and categories are in the Measuring economic development note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.b] Even inside one rich trading bloc, development differs sharply. GDP per head (the value of a country's output divided by its population) shows a richer core and a poorer edge.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.0] Luxembourg has the highest GDP per head in the EU, Ireland the second-highest, and Bulgaria the lowest (Eurostat, early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.1] Luxembourg's GDP per head is inflated by cross-border commuters, who add to its output but live elsewhere, so it overstates living standards there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.2] The EU's core is a richer north-west belt, the Blue Banana, and its periphery (the edge, far from the main centres) lies in the east and south. There is more on core and periphery in the Socio-economic regions note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.3] Within member states, Italy's Mezzogiorno (the poorer south of Italy) is about 22% of Italy's GDP, with GDP per head a little over half that of the Centre-North in recent years.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.4] Romania and Bulgaria contain many of the EU's less developed regions, which fits Bulgaria's lowest GDP per head.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.5] An EU less developed region has GDP per head under 75% of the EU average. Such regions receive most of the EU's cohesion funding (money paid to narrow the gap between richer and poorer regions).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.b] Ireland is rich overall, but incomes differ by region. Dublin pulls ahead because of the jobs and functions concentrated there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.0] The Dublin region has the highest disposable income per person (income left after tax, available to spend) in the State. The Border and Midland regions are consistently among the lowest (CSO county income figures, 2010s to 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.1] County Dublin has about 1.45 million of the State's about 5.1 million people (Census 2022), so it holds about 28% of the population.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.2] Dublin's advantages are its capital and port functions, multinational and financial-services investment, universities and the airport. Together these provide most of the high-value jobs, which is why incomes are highest there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.3] The West and North-West are peripheral, with remoteness, weaker infrastructure, dependence on farming and small firms, and out-migration (people leaving to find work elsewhere).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.4] Peripheral location does not by itself mean poverty: the West is peripheral within a very rich country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.b] The State has tried to narrow the gap between Dublin and the rest. Policy combines regional bodies, planning targets and spending on transport and broadband.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.0] The BMW region (Border, Midland and Western) held Objective 1 status, the EU's top category for regions needing extra funding, for 2000 to 2006.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.1] Three regional assemblies (regional bodies of local councillors) have operated since 2015, so regional planning has its own bodies: Northern and Western; Southern; and Eastern and Midland.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.2] Project Ireland 2040, launched in February 2018, combines the National Planning Framework (the planning strategy) and the National Development Plan (the investment plan).
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.3] The National Planning Framework aims for the Northern and Western and Southern regions together to grow as much as the Eastern and Midland region, so that growth is balanced.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.4] It also aims for half of future population growth to go to the five cities: Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.5] Metrolink is a planned metro line from Swords, via Dublin Airport, to Dublin city centre. The National Broadband Plan (contract signed 2019) aims to bring high-speed broadband to rural homes.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.6] Transport investment can reduce regional income differences: better access attracts firms to regional towns, cuts costs and commuting times, and widens the job market. It also reduces congestion, shortens journeys and lowers emissions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.b] National averages hide local differences. Rich countries contain poor districts, and large countries contain regions at very different levels.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.0] Even rich places contain pockets of deprivation, and poor regions exist within richer countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.1] India: Census 2011 literacy was about 94% in Kerala and about 62% in Bihar. Fertility (children per woman) was about 1.8 in Kerala and about 3.0 in Bihar (NFHS-5, 2019 to 2021).
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.2] Brazil: the poor North-East contrasts with the rich South-East, an example of a poor region inside one country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.b] Physical factors affect development by limiting or supporting farming, health and transport. They influence development but do not determine it.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.0] The Sahel, the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara (Mali, Niger, Chad and Burkina Faso), has suffered repeated droughts since the 1970s, which limit farming and cause famine, because crops fail without reliable rain.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.1] In Ethiopia, outside the Sahel, drought worsened by civil war led to the 1984 to 1985 famine, followed by Live Aid (the 1985 charity appeal) in 1985.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.2] Extreme heat and tropical storms add difficulty, while temperate climates (mild, without extremes) suit productive farming.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.3] Climate does not decide development: Singapore is tropical, yet it is one of the world's richest countries. Botswana shows that a poor start can be overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.4] Malaria and other tropical diseases reduce life expectancy, the productivity of workers and children's schooling, because ill people cannot work or attend school. The malaria figure is in the Measuring economic development note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.5] Mountains, deserts and poor soils limit farming and make transport costly, while fertile lowlands and river plains support dense populations and farming surpluses.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.b] Where a country lies, what lies under it and what hazards strike it all shape its wealth. These can help or hold back development.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.0] Landlocked countries (with no coast) depend on neighbours' roads and ports, which raises trade costs. Landlocked developing countries include Chad, Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, Malawi, Uganda, Afghanistan and Bolivia.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.1] Coastal countries with good harbours trade more easily, because ships can reach them directly. Peripheral location adds to transport costs for Ireland and for the EU's outlying regions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.2] Natural resources can fund development: Botswana's diamonds were discovered at Orapa in 1967 and are mined by Debswana, a joint venture of the government and De Beers.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.3] Norway's oil has been extracted from the North Sea since the early 1970s, and its sovereign wealth fund (a state-owned investment fund), the Government Pension Fund Global, is one of the world's largest.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.4] Resources can also bring the resource curse of corruption, conflict and dependence on one export. Nigeria's oil from the Niger Delta dominates its exports, and oil spills and conflict have affected the Delta.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.5] The DR Congo produces about 70% of the world's cobalt (early 2020s), yet it is one of the poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.6] Haiti's earthquake of 12 January 2010, magnitude 7.0, struck near Port-au-Prince and killed over 100,000 people (estimates vary).
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.7] Floods, cyclones and droughts destroy infrastructure and set poor countries back for years, while rich countries recover faster.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.b] Social factors concern the people themselves: how many there are, how educated and healthy they are, and how women are treated. Each affects how productive a country can become.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.0] Rapid population growth spreads spending on schools and health thinly and creates more young dependants (children supported by workers). Niger has the world's highest fertility rate, about 6 to 7 children per woman (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.1] A falling birth rate can give a demographic dividend, a larger share of working-age people to drive growth.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.2] An educated workforce raises productivity and attracts investment. Low literacy, especially among women, holds development back, as the Kerala and Bihar contrast shows.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.3] Disease, malnutrition and HIV/AIDS reduce the workforce and life expectancy, and health spending raises productivity.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.4] Where girls are educated and women work outside the home, families are smaller, children are healthier and household incomes are higher.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.5] Brain drain: doctors, nurses and graduates leave poor countries, which loses skills. Remittances (money sent home by migrants) help offset this; India is the world's largest recipient of remittances (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.6] Water, sanitation, electricity, roads and internet are both a cause and a result of development: richer places can afford them, and having them makes growth easier.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.b] Cultural factors are the beliefs, customs and social divisions of a society. They can widen or narrow people's opportunities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.0] Attitudes to women's roles and girls' education matter because they shape schooling: early marriage reduces schooling and raises fertility.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.1] Social structures and inequality limit opportunity for some groups: caste in India, land concentration in Latin America (a few owners holding most land) and ethnic divisions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.2] Many languages within borders drawn in the colonial period can make national unity and education harder. A world language such as English can help, as in India's IT sector.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.3] The colonial cultural legacy, which still shapes society, includes European languages, education aimed at a small elite and a culture of exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.4] Attitudes to enterprise, saving and education differ between societies: South Korea places a high value on education. Traditional farming practices and land tenure (how land is owned and held) can slow change.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.b] Governments set the conditions for growth: peace, honest institutions and sensible policy attract investment, while conflict and mismanagement drive it away.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.0] Political stability and good governance attract investment and allow long-term planning. Botswana became independent in 1966 and has held regular elections as a multi-party democracy since independence.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.1] War destroys infrastructure, displaces people and scares off investors, so growth stops. Syria's civil war from 2011 displaced millions. South Sudan became independent in July 2011 and entered civil war in December 2013; There is more on Sudan in the Case study of a developing economy note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.2] Corruption (misuse of office for private gain) diverts public money and aid. Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index ranks Denmark at or near the top and Somalia and South Sudan at or near the bottom (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.3] Colonial history left borders, export economies and weak institutions; the detail is in the Case study of a developing economy note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.4] Government economic policy matters: education, infrastructure, openness to trade and investment, and industrial policy (state support for chosen industries), as in South Korea, China and Ireland, contrast with mismanagement, as in Zimbabwe and Venezuela.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.5] Debt repayments take money from schools and hospitals, and rich countries' farm subsidies and tariffs (taxes on imports) disadvantage poor exporters. The detail is in the Global issues and a justice perspective note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.6] EU membership and funds helped Ireland, Spain and Portugal, all major recipients of EU regional and cohesion funds, to converge with richer states (catch up with them).
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.b] Levels of development are not permanent. Countries can move up or down, and theory suggests why gaps can persist or close.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.0] Development is not fixed: countries can rise, as Ireland, South Korea, China and Botswana have, or fall, as Zimbabwe, Venezuela and Syria have.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.1] A falling HDI is usually caused by war, economic collapse or disease, because each destroys incomes, health or schooling.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.2] Cumulative causation: core regions keep growing because they attract capital and skilled migrants, so success feeds further success.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.3] Spread effects: growth can sometimes spread from the core to the periphery. Full core–periphery theory is in The Global Economy: Multinationals and Globalisation chapter; models of development are in the Global Interdependence option.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.b] South Korea went from war-torn poverty to a high-income industrial economy. Its growth came from planning, industry and education rather than resources.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.0] The Korean War (1950 to 1953) destroyed so much that it left the South among the world's poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.1] From the 1960s, under Park Chung-hee, the government planned export-led industrialisation (growth by making goods to sell abroad): textiles first, then steel, shipbuilding, cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.2] Chaebol, large family-controlled conglomerates (groups of many firms) such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG, drove the industrial growth.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.3] The government invested heavily in education. South Korea joined the OECD (a club of rich countries) in 1996.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.4] The 1997 Asian financial crisis hit South Korea hard, but it recovered. UNCTAD (the UN trade and development body) reclassified it as a developed country in 2021, and it is now a very high HDI country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.5] Its growth came from industry and education, not from natural resources.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.b] China's rise began with deliberate reform. It has lifted huge numbers out of poverty, but the gains are uneven.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.0] Economic reforms began in 1978 under Deng Xiaoping, known as opening up (allowing trade and foreign investment).
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.1] Special Economic Zones (areas with special rules to attract foreign business) were set up from 1980, the first at Shenzhen, beside Hong Kong.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.2] China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, which gave its exporters easier access to world markets.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.3] The World Bank estimates that close to 800 million Chinese were lifted out of extreme poverty since 1978 (World Bank, 2022).
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.4] China has been the world's second-largest economy since 2010, overtaking Japan, but it is upper-middle income, not a developed country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.5] Coastal provinces are much richer than the western interior, and hundreds of millions of rural people migrated to coastal cities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.b] Botswana began as one of the world's poorest countries and put its diamond revenue into roads, schools and clinics. It shows that a poor start can be overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.0] Botswana became independent in 1966 as one of the world's poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.1] Diamond revenue funded roads, schools and clinics, so the money was turned into development, and stable democratic government and comparatively low corruption helped.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.2] Botswana is now an upper-middle-income country, so its start as one of the poorest was overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.3] HIV/AIDS sharply reduced life expectancy in the 1990s and 2000s; it recovered with free antiretroviral treatment.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.4] Dependence on diamonds is a weakness of the economy, because the country relies on one resource.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.b] Ireland moved from stagnation and emigration in the 1950s to one of the richest EU states. Policy change, foreign investment and EU membership drove it, with a serious setback in 2008.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.0] Heavy emigration in the 1950s, a sign of stagnation: net emigration was about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.1] T. K. Whitaker's Economic Development (1958) led to the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958), which offered free trade, grants and tax breaks for foreign firms.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.2] Free secondary education came in 1967, and Ireland joined the EEC (the forerunner of the EU) on 1 January 1973.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.3] The Celtic Tiger boom ran from the mid-1990s to 2007. Free secondary education, free trade and foreign investment helped drive it.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.4] A property crash and banking crisis from 2008 led to an EU–IMF bailout of €85 billion, agreed in November 2010; Ireland exited the programme in December 2013.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.b] Development can also go into reverse. These three countries show economic mismanagement, dependence on one export and war pulling living standards down.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.0] Zimbabwe: land seizures from 2000 cut farm output, so the economy suffered; hyperinflation (prices rising out of control) peaked in 2008, and the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned in 2009.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.1] Venezuela: the economy depended on oil. After oil prices collapsed in 2014, the economy shrank by well over half, and more than 7 million people emigrated (2024).
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.2] Syria: its HDI fell because of the civil war that began in 2011, reversing decades of progress.
