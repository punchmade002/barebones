TARGET geo-option-geoecology-s4: 4. One major biome in detail: hot desert or tropical rainforest
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.0.b] A biome is a large world region with a distinctive climate, soil and community of plants and animals adapted to those conditions. In the hot desert option, everything you write follows from one starting point: the climate.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.0.0] Climate is the main control, because it shapes the soil, and climate and soil together shape the vegetation, which in turn determines which animals can live there.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.0.1] Biotic inter-relationships (the links between living things) include food chains and webs, which run from producers (plants, which make their own food) to herbivores (plant eaters) and carnivores (meat eaters), with decomposers (organisms that break down dead matter) returning nutrients to the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.0.2] Plants and animals also compete for scarce water, which is the key shortage in a desert.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.0.3] The hot desert is one of the biome belts that follow the world climate belts: tundra, boreal forest, temperate deciduous forest, temperate grassland, Mediterranean, hot desert, savanna and tropical rainforest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.0.4] On mountains, biomes change with altitude in vertical zones, because temperature falls as you climb.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.1.b] Hot deserts are the driest places on earth outside the poles, and they sit in predictable bands. Learn the rainfall limit, the latitudes and a set of named deserts with their continents.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.1.0] Annual rainfall is under 250 mm, which is the limit that defines a hot desert. Hot deserts lie roughly between 15 and 30 degrees north and south of the equator, mainly on the west sides of continents and in continental interiors (the middle of large landmasses).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.1.1] The Sahara, North Africa, is the world's largest hot desert, at about 9 million km², crossing Morocco, Algeria, Libya, Egypt, Mauritania, Mali, Niger, Chad and Sudan.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.1.2] Other hot deserts: the Arabian Desert; the Thar (India and Pakistan); the Namib (south-west Africa); the Atacama (Chile); the Sonoran and Mojave (south-west USA and north-west Mexico); the Great Victoria and Simpson Deserts (Australia).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.1.3] Map cue: sketch the desert belts on either side of the equator, and label the west coasts of the continents where they reach the sea.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.2.b] Aridity (extreme dryness) has five separate causes, and different deserts get different combinations. Give each cause its own explained point, with the mechanism and a named example.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.2.0] 1. Subtropical high pressure: air that rose at the equator sinks at about 30 degrees north and south (this circulation is the Hadley cell). Sinking air warms and dries, so clouds cannot form.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.2.1] 2. Offshore trade winds blow from the dry interior of the land out over the sea, so they bring no moisture to the coast.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.2.2] 3. Cold ocean currents off west coasts cool the air above them. The cooled lower air is stable (a temperature inversion, with cold air under warmer air), so it cannot rise to form rain, and fog forms instead.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.2.3] Examples of cold currents beside deserts: the Canary Current (western Sahara), the Benguela Current (Namib), the Humboldt Current (Atacama) and the California Current (Baja California and the Sonoran coast).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.2.4] 4. Rain shadow: mountains force moist air to rise, so it cools and rains on the windward side (the side facing the wind), then descends dry on the leeward side (the sheltered side).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.2.5] Rain shadow examples: the Andes shelter the Atacama from the east; the Sierra Nevada shelters the Mojave, including Death Valley, from Pacific moisture.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.2.6] 5. Continentality: the interior of a large continent is far from the sea, so winds have lost most of their moisture before they arrive.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.3.b] Desert temperatures are extreme in two ways: very hot by day in summer, and with a huge swing between day and night. Both come from the lack of cloud and moisture in the air.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.3.0] Summer daytime temperatures often exceed 40 °C, because little cloud blocks the sun.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.3.1] Clear skies and dry air give intense insolation (incoming solar radiation) by day and rapid heat loss by night. So the diurnal (daily) range is very large, and nights can be near freezing in winter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.3.2] A diurnal range of 20 to 30 °C or more is common.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.3.3] The annual range (the gap between the warmest and coolest month) is larger than in the tropics.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.3.4] The highest air temperature officially recognised by the World Meteorological Organization is 56.7 °C, at Furnace Creek, Death Valley, California, on 10 July 1913.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.4.b] Rain in a desert is rare, and when it comes it behaves differently from rain elsewhere. Evaporation, humidity, wind and fog complete the picture.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.4.0] Rain is scarce and unreliable. When it falls it is often short, intense convectional downpours (rain from warm air rising rapidly) that cause flash floods, because the hard, dry ground cannot absorb it quickly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.4.1] Potential evaporation (the water that would evaporate if it were available) far exceeds rainfall, and humidity is low.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.4.2] Strong, hot, dusty winds blow across the bare surface, because there is little vegetation to slow them.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.4.3] Coastal fog deserts: the Namib, among the oldest deserts in the world, receives much of its moisture as fog from the cold Benguela Current.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.4.4] The Atacama, Chile, is the driest non-polar desert on earth; some of its weather stations have recorded no rain for years at a time.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.5.b] The picture of endless sand dunes is misleading. Knowing the three surface types and the dry river bed word wadi lets you describe the landscape accurately.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.5.0] Most of the desert is rock and gravel, not sand dunes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.5.1] Hamada: a bare rock plateau. Reg: a stony gravel plain. Erg: a sand sea with dunes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.5.2] Wadis are dry river beds that flow only after rain.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.5.3] Wind erosion landforms are taught separately. There is more on this in Erosion by wind.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.b] The soil of the hot desert is an aridisol (a dry-climate soil). Its features follow from the lack of water and the lack of plants, and it is a classic contrast with the rainforest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.0] Aridisols are thin and immature, because weathering (the breakdown of rock) is slow without water and is mainly physical.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.1] They are coarse, sandy or stony, with very little humus (decayed plant matter), because few plants grow. This is why they are pale grey, yellow or red in colour.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.2] Little leaching (washing of nutrients down through the soil) takes place. Capillary rise (water drawn up through the soil, then evaporating) brings salts and calcium carbonate to the surface.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.3] This forms salt crusts (salinisation) and lime hardpans called calcrete or caliche (calcification).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.4] Wind removes fine particles from the surface, so the stones are left behind as a desert pavement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.5] The soils are alkaline. Where irrigated they are fertile, because nutrients have not been leached out; they are farmed in the Nile valley in Egypt and in California's Imperial Valley, irrigated from the Colorado River.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.6] Alluvial soils (deposited by rivers) from rivers that rise outside the desert, such as the Nile, are an exception to the aridisol pattern, because the river brings fresh sediment from outside the desert.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.6.7] Contrast with rainforest: desert soils are poor in humus but can be fertile when irrigated, whereas rainforest soils stay infertile beneath lush forest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.7.b] With so little water, plants cannot form a continuous cover. The pattern of where they grow is itself a response to drought.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.7.0] Cover is sparse, with much bare ground, so biomass (the total mass of living matter) and diversity are low.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.7.1] Plants are widely spaced so that they do not compete for the same scarce water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.7.2] Creosote bush in the Mojave and Sonoran Deserts limits competitors with evenly spaced plants, roots and chemicals, and individual bushes can be very long-lived.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.7.3] Vegetation is concentrated, because water is there, along wadis (dry river beds that flow after rain) and at oases (places where groundwater reaches the surface).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.8.b] Xerophytes are plants adapted to drought. The first strategy is to store water when it rains, as succulents do in their stems or leaves.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.8.0] Succulents store water in their stems or leaves, such as the cacti below, so they can survive the long gaps between rains.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.8.1] Saguaro cactus, found only in the Sonoran Desert of Arizona and Sonora, Mexico: its pleated stem expands to store water after rain, and it has spines and shallow roots spread widely just below the surface.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.8.2] The saguaro can grow over 12 m tall and live over 150 years. Its white flower is the state flower of Arizona.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.8.3] Cacti are native only to the Americas. Euphorbias in African deserts look similar through convergent evolution (unrelated plants developing the same features in similar conditions).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.8.4] Spines in place of leaves cut water loss (less surface to evaporate from) and deter animals; a thick, waxy cuticle (the outer coating of the plant) reduces evaporation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.9.b] Other plants cut water loss or reach water by their roots and leaves. Each feature below solves a different part of the drought problem.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.9.0] Few, sunken stomata (the pores through which leaves lose water vapour); in many succulents they open only at night (Crassulacean acid metabolism, or CAM, photosynthesis), so less water is lost in the heat of the day.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.9.1] Long tap roots reach groundwater: mesquite, in the south-west USA and Mexico, has tap roots that can reach tens of metres down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.9.2] Shallow, widespread roots catch brief rain before it evaporates or drains away, so the plant can take in water quickly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.9.3] Acacia trees in the Sahara and Arabian deserts have deep roots, small leaves and thorns.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.9.4] Small or light-coloured leaves, or leaves shed in drought, reduce water loss because less leaf surface is exposed to the sun.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.9.5] Welwitschia mirabilis, Namib Desert, where fog is the main moisture: it has only two leaves, which grow throughout its life; it absorbs moisture from fog; some plants are thought to be over 1,000 years old.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.9.6] Halophytes (plants that tolerate salty soils) cope with the salts that rise to the surface. Some plants in coastal deserts harvest moisture from fog.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.10.b] Some desert plants do not endure drought at all. They avoid it, by surviving as seeds until rain arrives.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.10.0] Ephemerals (short-lived plants): their seeds lie dormant for years, then after rain germinate, flower and set seed within weeks, producing a desert bloom.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.10.1] In the Atacama, Chile, a flowering desert (desierto florido) appears in unusually wet years, often linked to El Niño (a climate pattern in the Pacific Ocean).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.10.2] Death Valley had a widely reported superbloom in 2016.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.11.b] The dromedary (one-humped) camel of the Sahara and Arabia is the standard desert animal, with a whole set of adaptations to heat, thirst and sand. Name the feature and the reason for each.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.11.0] Its hump stores fat, not water. It can go many days without drinking, then drink over 100 litres in a few minutes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.11.1] It lets its body temperature rise by several degrees during the day, saving the water that sweating would use, and thick fur on its back shades it from the sun.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.11.2] It has long eyelashes and closable nostrils that keep out sand, and broad, padded feet that spread its weight on sand.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.11.3] Very concentrated urine and dry droppings reduce water loss (dromedary camel; kangaroo rat).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.12.b] Other desert animals escape the heat by being active at night or underground, find water in food or fog, or breed fast when rain comes. Pair each animal with its adaptation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.12.0] Fennec fox, Sahara: the smallest fox, with very large ears that radiate heat so it stays cool; nocturnal (active at night); burrows by day; fur on the soles of its feet for hot sand.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.12.1] Kangaroo rat, North American deserts: gets water from its seed diet and from its metabolism (the chemical reactions in its body); nocturnal; seals its burrow by day to keep humidity in.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.12.2] Namib fog-basking beetle: stands head-down on dune crests at dawn so that fog condenses on its back and runs to its mouth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.12.3] Thorny devil lizard, Australian deserts: grooves in its skin channel water to its mouth, so it can drink from any damp surface.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.12.4] Spadefoot toad, Sonoran Desert: estivates (lies dormant) underground through the dry months and emerges after summer rain; its eggs and tadpoles develop extremely quickly, before the pools dry up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.12.5] Addax antelope, Sahara: pale coat, which reflects sunlight; gets most of its water from plants; now critically endangered.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.12.6] Desert locust: breeds rapidly after rain, when food is available, and can form huge swarms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.13.b] Even in a sparse biome, species depend on each other. Desert food chains are simple, but there are close partnerships around the cactus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.13.0] Lesser long-nosed bats pollinate (carry pollen between) saguaro and agave flowers at night.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.13.1] Gila woodpeckers dig nest holes in saguaros; when the holes are abandoned, elf owls and other birds use them.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.13.2] Food chains are short, because there are few producers; seed-eating rodents are prey for snakes, owls and foxes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.14.b] People adapt to the desert as well as plants and animals do. The syllabus also asks for a European example, and Spain provides one.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.14.0] Tuareg (Sahara) and Bedouin (Arabia) are pastoral nomads (herders who move with their animals) who herd camels and goats.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.14.1] Oasis farming grows date palms, whose roots reach groundwater, so they can grow where rain cannot support crops.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.14.2] Tabernas Desert, Almería province, Andalusia, south-east Spain: often called Europe's only desert, a semi-arid area in the rain shadow of surrounding mountains.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.14.3] Its dry, open landscape explains why the Tabernas Desert was used as a film set for spaghetti westerns in the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.15.b] A climate graph shows temperature as a line and rainfall as bars for each month. The values below are invented to show the method.

[geo-option-geoecology-s4.15.1] Total rainfall: the sum of the twelve monthly rainfall bars, for example 150 mm (invented illustrative values); a total under 250 mm points to a hot desert.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.15.2] Seasonality: describe when the rain falls and when temperatures peak, for example a single short wet season in late summer (invented illustrative values).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.16.b] The tropical rainforest is a biome: a community of plants and animals adapted to a hot, wet equatorial climate and to nutrient-poor soils. This set of blocks stands alone, so you can answer any biome question from it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.16.0] Climate is the main control, because it shapes the soil, and climate and soil together shape the vegetation, which in turn shapes the animals.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.16.1] Biotic inter-relationships (the links between living things): food chains and webs run from producers (plants) to herbivores and carnivores, with decomposers returning nutrients to the soil. Species compete for light, and many pollinators and seed dispersers depend on the plants they feed on.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.16.2] The rainforest is the biome of the equatorial belt, one of the biome belts that follow the world climate belts.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.16.3] Mountains show vertical zones of biomes instead, because temperature falls with altitude.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.17.b] Rainforest is found in a belt on either side of the equator, in three main regions plus some smaller areas. It covers little land but holds a very large share of the world's species.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.17.0] A dense, evergreen, broadleaved forest in the hot, wet equatorial climate, mainly between about 10 degrees north and south of the equator.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.17.1] Amazon Basin, South America: the largest, covering about 5.5 million km², with roughly 60% of it in Brazil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.17.2] Congo Basin, Central Africa: the second largest, including the Democratic Republic of the Congo.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.17.3] South-East Asia: Indonesia (including Sumatra and Borneo), Malaysia and Papua New Guinea.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.17.4] Smaller areas: Central America, West Africa, eastern Madagascar and north-east Australia (the Daintree, Queensland).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.17.5] Rainforests, though small in area, cover only about 6% of the earth's land surface but are widely estimated to hold over half of the world's land plant and animal species. This is a widely quoted estimate.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.17.6] Map cue: shade the equatorial belt, then mark the three main basins and regions.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.18.b] Temperature in the rainforest is high and almost constant, because the sun stays high in the sky all year. The daily swing is bigger than any seasonal one.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.18.0] Mean monthly temperatures are about 26 to 28 °C all year, so there is no cold season.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.18.1] The annual range is only about 2 to 3 °C, because the sun stays high and day length barely changes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.18.2] The sun is overhead at the equator twice a year, at the equinoxes in March and September.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.18.3] The daily range is greater than the annual range, and there are no seasons in the temperate sense (no summer and winter).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.19.b] Rain falls in every month, and the cause is a daily cycle of heating and convection (warm air rising). The equatorial low-pressure belt drives it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.19.0] Annual rainfall is over 2,000 mm, falling throughout the year and in places far more. Manaus, in the central Amazon, Brazil, receives about 2,300 mm a year.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.19.1] Stage 1: because the sun is high, intense morning heating warms the ground and the moist air above it, which rises.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.19.2] Stage 2: the rising air cools and cumulonimbus clouds (tall rain clouds) build up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.19.3] Stage 3: heavy afternoon thunderstorms follow, almost daily.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.19.4] The low-pressure belt of rising air along the equator, the ITCZ (Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, also called the doldrums), drives this; some areas have a short drier season when it shifts.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.19.5] Relative humidity (the amount of water vapour in the air) is often over 80%.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.19.6] A large share of the Amazon's rain is water recycled from the forest by evapotranspiration (water given off by plants and evaporated from the ground and leaves).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.20.b] The lush forest grows on poor soil. It survives because nutrients are locked in the living plants and recycled very quickly, rather than stored in the ground.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.20.0] Latosols are deep, red or yellow, laterised (leached of most minerals except iron and aluminium by heavy rain), acidic and infertile, with little humus. There is more on this in Soil processes and the global pattern of soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.20.1] Litter (fallen leaves and dead matter) decomposes within weeks in the heat and humidity.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.20.2] Shallow roots and mycorrhizal fungi (fungi growing on the roots) take up nutrients almost at once and pass them to the trees.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.20.3] Most nutrients are held in living vegetation rather than the soil, so the nutrient cycle is rapid and closed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.20.4] Rainforest soils are infertile despite the lush forest; hot desert soils are infertile in humus but can be fertile when irrigated.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.21.b] The forest is arranged in layers, each with different light and conditions. Learn each layer with its approximate height.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.21.0] Emergents rise above the canopy, at about 45 to 60 m and more: kapok, the Brazil nut tree in the Amazon, and dipterocarps in South-East Asia.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.21.1] The canopy, at about 25 to 40 m, is a dense, continuous layer that intercepts most sunlight and rain and holds most of the animal life.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.21.2] The understorey of young trees and palms reaches up to about 15 m.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.21.3] The shrub layer grows in the dim light below.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.21.4] The forest floor is dark and humid; only about 2% of the sunlight reaching the canopy arrives there.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.21.5] Diagram cue: a labelled vertical profile of the layers, with approximate heights.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.22.b] Rainforest plants are adapted to heavy rain, thin soils and competition for light. Each feature below answers one of those pressures.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.22.0] Buttress roots (wide, wall-like roots at the base) support tall trees such as kapok (Ceiba) and the Brazil nut, which have shallow roots.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.22.1] Shallow roots catch nutrients from the litter layer, because that is where the nutrients are.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.22.2] Drip tips and waxy leaves shed heavy rain, so the leaves do not stay waterlogged.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.22.3] Large leaves in the understorey capture the little light that reaches it, because the canopy above blocks most of it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.22.4] Thin, smooth bark, because the trunk needs no thick insulation in the constant warmth, and a smooth surface makes it harder for epiphytes to cling.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.22.5] Evergreen, with leaves shed and replaced all year. When a tree falls it opens a light gap, and young trees grow rapidly upwards into it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.23.b] In a crowded forest, many plants reach the light by climbing or growing on other plants, and some cope with poor soil by trapping insects or living as parasites.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.23.0] Lianas are woody climbers, rooted in the soil, that climb trees to reach the light, so they do not need to build thick trunks.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.23.1] Epiphytes (plants growing on other plants) such as orchids, bromeliads and ferns grow on branches and take moisture from the air and rain. Bromeliads hold pools of water in their leaf rosettes, which tree frogs use.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.23.2] Strangler figs start as epiphytes high in the canopy, where there is light, and enclose their host tree.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.23.3] Pitcher plants (Nepenthes) in the nutrient-poor soils of South-East Asia trap and digest insects.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.23.4] Rafflesia arnoldii, a parasite on vines in Sumatra and Borneo, has the largest single flower in the world, about 1 m across, and smells of rotting meat to attract flies.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.24.b] Rainforest has extremely high species diversity of plants and animals. The pattern is many species but few individuals of each.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.24.0] Hundreds of tree species can grow in a single hectare of Amazon rainforest, compared with a handful in an Irish wood.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.24.1] Many species grow in each hectare, but each species is represented by few individuals, so a tree's nearest neighbour of the same species may be far away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.24.2] The climate is warm and wet all year with no cold or dry season to survive, so plants grow all year round and many species can coexist.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.24.3] The layers of the forest create many separate niches (ways of living, each with its own light, food and shelter), from the sunny emergents to the dark forest floor, so more species can live side by side.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.24.4] This richness depends on the closed nutrient cycle: when the forest is cleared, both the species and the nutrients stored in them are lost together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.25.b] Most animals live in the canopy (arboreal, meaning tree-living), where the food and sunlight are. Their adaptations suit life in the trees.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.25.0] Spider monkeys and howler monkeys, Amazon: prehensile (gripping) tails used as a fifth limb; howler monkeys are among the loudest land animals.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.25.1] Three-toed sloth: its very slow metabolism suits a low-energy leaf diet; algae growing in its fur give camouflage; it descends to the ground only about once a week.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.25.2] Toucan, South and Central America: its large, light bill reaches fruit at the tips of branches and helps it lose heat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.25.3] Orangutan, found only in Borneo and Sumatra: the largest tree-living mammal, with long arms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.25.4] Harpy eagle, Central and South America: hunts monkeys and sloths in the canopy.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.26.b] Beyond the canopy, forest animals rely on camouflage, warning colours and swimming. Place each animal on the correct continent.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.26.0] Poison dart frogs, Central and South America: bright colours warn predators of their toxic skin, so predators learn to leave them alone.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.26.1] Jaguar: the largest cat in the Americas, with a spotted coat that breaks up its outline in the dappled light of the forest floor; a strong swimmer that hunts along rivers.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.26.2] Many animals are strong swimmers or live in water, because rivers flood the forest for months each wet season: the anaconda, a huge snake, hunts in flooded forest and rivers, and the Amazon river (pink) dolphin swims among the flooded trees.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.26.3] Capybara, South America: the world's largest rodent, a semi-aquatic grazer that escapes predators by diving into water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.26.4] Many animals are nocturnal (active at night), which avoids daytime predators and competition, and some canopy animals glide between trees rather than climbing down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.26.5] Okapi, Congo rainforest: a forest relative of the giraffe, with striped legs for camouflage in the dim forest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.26.6] Gorillas and bonobos live in the Congo Basin; bonobos are found only south of the Congo River.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.27.b] Rainforest plants and animals depend on each other closely. Pollination and seed dispersal link them, and some partnerships are mutualism (both partners gain).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.27.0] Pollination by insects, birds and bats, and seed dispersal by fruit bats, birds and other animals link plants and animals closely.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.27.1] Mutualism: Azteca ants live inside Cecropia trees, which give them shelter and food; in return the ants attack herbivores and clear climbing plants from the tree.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.27.2] Leafcutter ants cut leaves to grow the fungus they eat, so the leaves are food for the fungus, not the ants.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.27.3] Brazil nut: pollinated mainly by large orchid bees and dispersed by agoutis, rodents that bury the nuts; trees produce poorly in plantations away from intact forest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.28.b] Indigenous people have long lived in the forest by methods that let it recover.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.28.0] The Yanomami (Brazil and Venezuela border), the Kayapó (Brazilian Amazon) and the Mbuti (Ituri forest, Democratic Republic of the Congo) are hunter-gatherers.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.28.1] Hunting, gathering and shifting cultivation (slash and burn, clearing a patch and moving on) at low density, because small numbers of people take little, let the forest and soil recover.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.28.2] There is more on commercial felling in Human alteration of biomes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.29.b] The syllabus asks for European examples where appropriate, but this biome has none.
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.29.0] There is no tropical rainforest in Europe. There is more on Europe's role in the trade that drives felling in Human alteration of biomes.

[geo-option-geoecology-s4.30.0] Annual range: the warmest monthly mean minus the coolest, for example 28 °C minus 25 °C gives an annual range of 3 °C (invented illustrative values).
[geo-option-geoecology-s4.30.1] Total rainfall: the sum of the twelve monthly rainfall bars, for example 2,400 mm (invented illustrative values); rain falls in every month, with no dry season.

CANDIDATES geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5: 5. Climatic environments and climate change
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.0.b] A climatic environment (or climate region) is a large area with a similar pattern of temperature, precipitation and seasons, usually matched by similar vegetation and soils. Weather is the short-term state of the atmosphere, while climate is the average pattern over many years, so a climate region is defined by long-term averages, not by one day's weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.0.0] The Köppen system, devised by Wladimir Köppen, a German-Russian climatologist, divides the world mainly by temperature and precipitation thresholds (cut-off values) that match vegetation, so each climate type has a typical plant cover.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.0.1] Its broad groups are tropical, dry, warm temperate, cool temperate (continental) and polar, so a place is placed in a group by its temperature and rainfall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.0.2] Climates are arranged in belts by latitude (distance north or south of the equator), then modified by distance from the sea, ocean currents, relief (the height and shape of the land), and whether a place lies on the east or the west side of a continent.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.0.3] Controls on climate: latitude and insolation (incoming solar energy); pressure belts and planetary winds with their seasonal shift; air masses (large bodies of air with uniform temperature and moisture); ocean currents; continentality (distance from the sea); altitude and relief.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.0.4] Diagram: a world map of the main climate regions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.1.b] The equatorial (tropical rainforest) climate lies close to the equator, about 0-10 degrees N and S. It is found mainly in the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia (Indonesia and Malaysia). Its temperature is the steadiest on earth, and the reasons come from the sun's position.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.1.0] Temperatures are high all year, with monthly means of about 26–28 °C.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.1.1] The annual range (the difference between the warmest and coolest month) is tiny, about 2–3 °C, but the daily range is larger than the annual range: afternoon highs of about 30–32 °C and night lows of about 22–24 °C.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.1.2] The sun is always high in the sky and is overhead at the equator at the equinoxes (March and September), so there is little change in heat input through the year. Day length is also about 12 hours all year.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.1.3] Cloud cover reflects sunlight and stops temperatures rising much above the low 30s °C, which is why maximum temperatures here are lower than in deserts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.1.4] Pressure is permanently low in the equatorial trough (the ITCZ, or doldrums, the belt where the trade winds from north and south meet), so winds are light and variable.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.2.b] Rain falls here every month, and nearly all of it comes from one process, convection (warm air rising, cooling and condensing into cloud). That process also produces a predictable daily pattern of weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.2.0] Rainfall is over 2,000 mm a year (Manaus, Brazil, about 2,300 mm) and falls in every month.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.2.1] There are often two wetter peaks after the equinoxes, when the overhead sun and the ITCZ pass over.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.2.2] The rain is convectional: intense heating and very moist air from warm seas and forest transpiration (water given off by plant leaves) build towering clouds that produce heavy afternoon thunderstorms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.2.3] Trade winds converge at the ITCZ, which lifts and reinforces the moist air, so rising air is supplied from two directions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.2.4] Relative humidity (how much water vapour the air holds compared with the most it could hold) is often over 80%, and the air is humid at night too.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.2.5] Daily cycle: a clear, calm, humid morning; cumulus (heaped, fluffy cloud) building by late morning; cumulonimbus (towering storm cloud) and torrential rain with thunder in the afternoon; clearing in the evening.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.2.6] The climate has no temperate-style seasons, because temperature and rainfall barely change through the year.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.3.b] The constant heat and rain support the richest plant cover on land, and that in turn shapes how people use the region.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.3.0] Tropical rainforest grows on latosols (deep, reddish tropical soils). There is more on this in One major biome in detail.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.3.1] Forest clearance for farming and timber is the main human pressure on the rainforest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.4.b] A monsoon climate has a seasonal reversal of wind direction, bringing a very wet summer and a dry winter. The word monsoon comes from the Arabic word for season. The classic monsoon climate is in South and South-East Asia, especially India, Bangladesh and Myanmar; weaker monsoon climates occur in northern Australia and West Africa.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.4.0] The hot season before the rains (March–May): temperatures often exceed 40 °C on the northern plains.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.4.1] The wet south-west monsoon, from June to September: slightly cooler than the hot season because of cloud and rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.4.2] The cool dry season (October–February): pleasant, with cool nights in the north.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.4.3] The south-west monsoon normally reaches Kerala around 1 June and covers all of India by mid-July. It brings about three-quarters of India's annual rainfall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.5.b] The summer monsoon starts with the land heating faster than the sea. Four linked steps then pull moist air onto India.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.5.0] 1. Intense heating of the land and of the high land to the north (the Tibetan Plateau) creates low pressure over north-west India.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.5.1] 2. The ITCZ (the belt of low pressure where trade winds meet) moves north.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.5.2] 3. The south-east trade winds cross the equator and are deflected by the Coriolis force (the turning effect of the earth's rotation on moving air) into south-west winds, which gather moisture over the Indian Ocean to the south.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.5.3] 4. The subtropical jet stream (a fast, high-altitude river of air) shifts north of the Himalayas, the mountains to the north, allowing the monsoon to burst suddenly. There is more on this in India.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.6.b] Rain is not spread evenly across India: relief and distance from the moisture source decide who gets most. In winter the wind reverses and blows from the land, which is why the dry season follows.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.6.0] Relief rain (rain from moist air forced up high ground) falls on the Western Ghats and on the north-east hills, the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya; Mawsynram and Cherrapunji, in the Khasi Hills, are among the wettest places on earth.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.6.1] The Deccan lies in a rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of high ground), and rainfall decreases north-westwards along the Ganges plain, with the Thar Desert in Rajasthan the driest region.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.6.2] Kolkata is wetter than Delhi, which shows how rainfall falls away from the moisture source.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.6.3] In winter the land cools and high pressure builds over the interior of Asia; dry north-east winds blow off the land.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.6.4] The Himalayas, the mountains to the north, block cold air from the interior of Asia from reaching India.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.6.5] The east coast of southern India (the Tamil Nadu coast) gets winter rain from the north-east monsoon after it has crossed the Bay of Bengal, because the wind picks up moisture over the sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.7.b] Farmers depend on the monsoon arriving on time and in the right amount, so any variation has serious effects.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.7.0] Late, weak or broken monsoons bring drought; very heavy ones bring floods.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.7.1] Cyclones (intense tropical storms) in the Bay of Bengal are also a hazard: the Odisha super cyclone of October 1999 killed about 10,000 people.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.7.2] El Niño years (when the central and eastern Pacific warms) often bring a weaker Indian monsoon.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.7.3] Kharif crops such as rice are sown with the monsoon rains; rabi crops such as wheat rely on stored water and irrigation, because they grow in the dry season.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.8.b] This climate lies on west coasts between about 40° and 60° in the path of the westerlies (the prevailing winds that blow from the west). Because the sea warms and cools slowly, it gives mild winters and cool summers.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.8.0] Locations: north-west Europe, including Ireland, Britain, north-west France (Brittany), Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark and coastal Norway; also British Columbia and the Pacific north-west of the USA, southern Chile, New Zealand and Tasmania.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.8.1] Winters are mild and summers are cool, with a small annual range: Ireland's mean January is about 4–7 °C and its mean July about 14–16 °C, an annual range of about 10 °C.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.8.2] Frost and snow are infrequent on low ground, because the sea keeps winters mild.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.8.3] The climate of Ireland is also described at Core level in the note Physical regions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.9.b] Rain falls in every month, and the pattern is wet, mild and changeable. Most of it comes from fronts (the boundaries between warm and cold air) and from hills in the way of the wind.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.9.0] Rain falls in all months, with a maximum in autumn and winter and many wet days: about 150 a year in the east and south-east of Ireland and up to about 225 in parts of the west.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.9.1] Annual rainfall is about 750 mm in the driest east (around Dublin), about 1,000–1,250 mm across much of the west, and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.9.2] Most rain is frontal (cyclonic, produced where warm air rises over cold air in a depression), with relief rain on west-facing mountains; amounts fall from west to east because the air has already lost moisture.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.9.3] The weather is changeable because depressions (low-pressure systems) and ridges of high pressure alternate.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.9.4] Occasional anticyclones (high-pressure systems with sinking air) bring dry spells, most often in summer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.9.5] Vegetation is deciduous woodland (trees that shed leaves in winter) and grass on brown earths (fertile, mid-brown soils).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.b] Each feature of the climate has a cause in the atmosphere or ocean. Wind, sea, pressure systems and air masses work together.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.0] Prevailing south-westerly winds bring maritime air (air that has crossed the sea, so it is mild and moist) off the Atlantic.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.1] The North Atlantic Drift, the extension of the Gulf Stream (a warm ocean current), warms winter air and seas.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.2] The sea heats and cools slowly, moderating the seasons, so winters are not very cold and summers are not hot.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.3] Depressions form frequently on the polar front (where warm tropical and cold polar air meet) and are steered by the jet stream (fast high-altitude winds), so they bring bursts of rain across the region.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.4] Tropical and polar maritime air masses (the warm one from the south, the cold one from the north, both moist) are the most common.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.5] Eastwards the oceanic influence weakens, because the sea is further away, and the climate grades into the continental climate of central and eastern Europe. There is more on this in Physical regions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.11.b] The Mediterranean climate lies on the west sides of continents between about 30° and 40° (some places to about 45°). It has hot summers and mild winters.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.11.0] Locations: the lands around the Mediterranean Sea (for example southern Italy, southern Spain and Greece), central and southern California, central Chile, the Cape Town area of South Africa, and south-western and southern Australia (around Perth and Adelaide).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.11.1] Summers are hot: lowland July means are about 25 °C or more, for example in southern Italy.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.11.2] Winters are mild: coastal January means are about 9–12 °C, and frost is rare on the coasts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.11.3] Inland and upland areas have hotter summers and colder winters than the coast, because the sea has less moderating effect there.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.b] Winter rain and summer drought both come from the seasonal shift of the pressure belts. The summer subtropical high (a belt of high pressure with sinking air) is the key to the explanation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.0] Rainfall is moderate (commonly about 400–900 mm), under 600 mm a year on parts of the Puglia lowlands and Sicily, and most of it falls from October to March.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.1] Summers are dry, with drought for several months; rain often falls in intense downpours that cause flash floods and soil erosion.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.2] In summer the subtropical high (the Azores High in the Mediterranean, the North Pacific High in California) moves poleward over the region, bringing sinking, warming air, clear skies and no rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.3] In winter the pressure belts shift towards the equator, so the westerlies and their depressions bring rain; the warm Mediterranean Sea also spawns its own winter depressions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.4] On some of these coasts cold currents (the California, Humboldt, Benguela and Canary currents) keep summers cooler and drier.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.5] The sirocco is a hot, dry, dusty wind from the Sahara; the mistral and the bora are cold winds.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.13.b] Dry summers shape both the plants that survive and the dangers people face.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.13.0] Drought-tolerant vegetation: olives, vines and cork oak, and maquis scrub (low, tough evergreen shrubs); citrus grows where it is irrigated.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.13.1] Hazards: summer water shortage, and wildfires in hot, dry, windy summers.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.13.2] The Mati wildfire near Athens, Greece, in July 2018 killed about 100 people, one of the deadliest wildfires in Europe.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.13.3] Human response: water storage and irrigation, and tourism. There is more on this in Climate and economic development.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.14.b] The continental climate lies in the interiors of large northern-hemisphere continents between about 40° and 60° N, far from the sea. With no ocean to moderate the temperature, it has the largest annual ranges on earth.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.14.0] Locations: central and eastern Europe into Russia (Moscow) and Ukraine, the steppes of Ukraine and southern Russia, Central Asia, the Canadian Prairies, and the US Midwest and Great Plains.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.14.1] There is no large equivalent in the southern hemisphere, which has too little land at these latitudes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.14.2] Winters are very cold, with monthly means well below 0 °C for several months: Moscow about −7 °C in January and Winnipeg about −16 °C in January.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.14.3] Summers are warm to hot: Moscow about 19 °C and Winnipeg about 20 °C in July.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.14.4] The annual range is large and increases with distance from the sea; Verkhoyansk has an annual range of over 60 °C.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.14.5] Spring and autumn are short.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.b] Precipitation is lower than on oceanic coasts and peaks in summer, the opposite of Ireland. The reasons are the land itself, the distance from moisture and the pressure systems.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.0] Precipitation is low to moderate (roughly 250–750 mm) and decreases into the interior: Moscow receives about 600–700 mm a year, with a summer maximum.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.1] The summer maximum comes from convectional thunderstorms (rising currents of heated air) as the land heats up; winter precipitation falls as snow, which lies for months.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.2] Continentality: the land heats and cools quickly, and there is no sea to moderate the temperature.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.3] Distance from the oceans: the westerlies have lost most of their moisture crossing Europe, and in North America the Rockies block Pacific air from reaching the interior.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.4] The winter Siberian High, a cold, dry, clear thermal anticyclone (high pressure caused by intense cooling of the land), sits over the continent; North America has an equivalent.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.5] Summer low pressure over the heated land draws in some moist air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.16.b] Extreme temperatures and a short growing season create hazards, but the open grasslands also support large-scale farming.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.16.0] Hazards: blizzards; cold waves of arctic air; summer heatwaves, drought and dust storms, as in the Dust Bowl of the 1930s on the Great Plains; floods when the spring snowmelt comes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.16.1] Vegetation is temperate grassland (steppe and prairie) on chernozems, the black, humus-rich soils of the steppes of Ukraine and southern Russia, the Prairies and the Pampas.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.16.2] Human response: wheat and maize belts on the grassland; boreal forest (northern coniferous forest) further north.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.17.b] Hot deserts lie about 15–30° N and S on the west sides of continents, for example the Sahara, Arabian, Namib, Atacama, Sonoran and Mojave, and Australian deserts. They have the highest temperatures on earth and very little rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.17.0] Rainfall is very low (under about 250 mm) and unreliable because of sinking air in the subtropical high pressure belt, offshore trade winds (blowing from land to sea), cold currents and rain shadows.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.17.1] The highest air temperatures on earth were recorded: 56.7 °C at Furnace Creek, Death Valley, on 10 July 1913, a WMO record, because of clear skies and high sun.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.17.2] The daily range is very large, often 20–30 °C or more, because dry, cloudless air lets heat escape at night.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.17.3] Fog forms on coasts with cold currents, because the cold water chills the air above it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.17.4] The Sahara covers about 9 million km², the world's largest hot desert; the Atacama is the driest non-polar desert.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.17.5] Cold currents on desert coasts: the Canary (Sahara), Benguela (Namib), Humboldt (Atacama) and California (Sonoran coast).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.b] Climate is not the same everywhere because the controls on it vary from place to place. Each kind of change across space has its own cause.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.0] From equator to pole (latitude): temperature falls and seasonal contrasts grow with latitude.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.1] From coast to interior: continentality increases the annual range; Ireland's annual range is about 10 °C, while Verkhoyansk's is over 60 °C.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.2] From west to east coasts: cold currents cool and dry west coasts, while warm currents such as the North Atlantic Drift warm them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.3] With altitude: temperature falls with height, so high mountains have a vertical sequence of climates, and snow lies on high ground even near the equator.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.4] Across a mountain range: the windward side gets relief rain, while the leeward side lies in a rain shadow, as the Deccan does in India.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.5] Climate boundaries are transition zones that shift: the Sahel lies between the desert and wetter land and moves with the ITCZ from year to year, with rainfall of roughly 200–600 mm.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.b] Climate changes over very different timescales, and each timescale has its own natural causes. Human activity has added a further cause in recent times.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.0] Timescales: long-term change over millions of years (plate movement, mountain building); glacial cycles over tens to hundreds of thousands of years; centuries (the Little Ice Age); years (El Niño and volcanic eruptions); and recent human-caused warming.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.1] Milankovitch orbital cycles (regular changes in the earth's orbit and tilt, described under Ice Ages causes below).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.2] Changes in solar output (the energy the sun gives off), which means the earth receives more or less heat.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.3] Volcanic eruptions: sulphate aerosols (tiny airborne particles) reflect sunlight and cool the earth for a year or two; after Mount Pinatubo, Philippines, erupted in June 1991, global average temperature fell by about 0.5 °C for about a year (1992).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.4] Plate tectonics: moving continents and opening or closing seaways change ocean currents, so heat is redistributed around the world.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.5] Changes in ocean circulation, because currents carry heat around the globe.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.6] Feedbacks (effects that amplify a change): the ice–albedo feedback (albedo is how much sunlight a surface reflects), and CO2 and methane released or absorbed by oceans and permafrost (permanently frozen ground).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.20.b] Since the Industrial Revolution people have become a cause of climate change.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.20.0] The enhanced greenhouse effect (extra heat trapped by added greenhouse gases) comes from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and agriculture; land-use change and aerosols also alter the climate. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.b] Nobody measured temperature in the distant past, so scientists use natural records that preserve clues, called proxies. Each source reaches back a different distance in time.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.0] Ice cores: bubbles of ancient air record past CO2, and oxygen isotopes (forms of oxygen of different weight) record past temperature; Antarctic ice cores (EPICA Dome C) record about 800,000 years of climate and CO2.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.1] Ocean-floor sediments, because layers of mud build up over time and keep a record of past conditions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.2] Tree rings, because trees grow a new ring each year and the rings record the conditions of that year.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.3] Pollen preserved in peat bogs and lake muds; Irish bogs record changes in vegetation, and therefore climate, since the Ice Age.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.4] Glacial landforms, the features left by ice, show where ice once lay.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.5] Historical records such as harvest dates, frost fairs and ships' logs; instrument records since the 19th century.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.b] The first of the three climate-change settings is the Ice Ages. The present era has swung repeatedly between very cold and warmer conditions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.0] The Quaternary glaciation began about 2.6 million years ago; since then the earth has swung between long glacial periods and shorter, warm interglacials.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.1] Over the last 800,000 years the cycle has been about 100,000 years long; we now live in an interglacial.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.2] At the Last Glacial Maximum (the coldest point of the last glacial period), about 20,000 years ago, ice sheets covered much of North America and northern Europe, including Scandinavia, Britain and Ireland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.3] Global sea level was about 120 m lower than today, because water was locked in ice, exposing land bridges between islands and continents (for example between Britain and continental Europe).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.4] Global average temperature was several degrees colder, so the climate belts were pushed towards the equator.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.23.b] Ireland was covered by ice, and the landscape still carries the evidence. The effects of the Ice Ages also reached well beyond the ice itself.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.23.0] Ireland had two main cold stages, the Munsterian and the Midlandian. The Munsterian ice covered almost all of Ireland; the Midlandian ice had gone by about 10,000 years ago.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.23.1] The evidence is in the landscape of drumlins, eskers, corries and U-shaped valleys, all landforms made by ice.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.23.2] Isostatic rebound (land rising after the weight of ice is removed) and raised beaches are after-effects of the ice.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.23.3] Beyond the ice, climates were drier and windier and deserts expanded, which is why loess was later blown about.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.23.4] Loess (wind-blown dust) was blown from outwash plains (the gravel plains laid down by meltwater beyond the ice).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.23.5] Sea level fell, and plants and animals migrated towards the equator to escape the cold and recolonised the land afterwards.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.b] Small changes in the earth's orbit start an ice age, and feedbacks make the change much bigger. The orbital changes alter the distribution of sunlight, not the total amount.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.0] Milankovitch cycles, named after Milutin Milanković, a Serbian scientist who calculated them in the early 20th century, change the distribution of sunlight by latitude and season more than its total amount.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.1] Eccentricity (the shape of the orbit) varies over about 100,000 years; obliquity (the tilt, varying between about 22° and 24.5°) over about 41,000 years; precession (the wobble of the axis) over roughly 19,000–26,000 years.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.2] When northern summers are cool, snow survives the winter and ice sheets grow.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.3] Feedbacks amplify the small orbital change: more ice raises albedo (reflects more sunlight), and colder oceans absorb more CO2, lowering greenhouse gases.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.4] Ice cores show CO2 near 180 ppm (parts per million) at glacial maxima and about 280 ppm in interglacials.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.5] Changes in the Atlantic circulation, which means a change in how heat is carried to the north.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.6] Land near the North Pole allows ice sheets to build, because snow can pile up on land but not on open sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.25.b] Within the last thousand years there were warm and cold spells that are recorded in writing. Both are regional, mainly in the North Atlantic and Europe, and approximate in date.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.25.0] Medieval Warm Period, roughly AD 950–1250: a regional warm phase mainly in the North Atlantic and Europe. Vines were grown further north in England than in the colder centuries that followed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.25.1] Norse settlers led by Erik the Red settled Greenland from about AD 985; the settlements were abandoned in the 15th century as the climate cooled.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.25.2] Little Ice Age, roughly the 14th to the mid-19th century, coldest in the 17th century: a regional cold phase mainly in the North Atlantic and Europe, with Alpine glaciers advancing.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.25.3] The River Thames in London froze in severe winters, and frost fairs were held on the ice, the last in 1814.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.25.4] The Maunder Minimum (about 1645–1715), a period with very few sunspots, coincided with some of the coldest decades.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.25.5] In Ireland the Great Frost of the winter of 1739–40 was followed by famine in 1740–41.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.25.6] The eruption of Tambora, Indonesia, in April 1815 led to 1816 being the year without a summer, with harvest failures across Europe and north-eastern North America.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.26.b] The second climate-change setting is the warming of the 20th century and the present. The evidence comes from many independent measurements.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.26.0] CO2 was about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution and is over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s; it has been measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.26.1] Global average surface temperature has risen about 1.1–1.2 °C since the late 1800s (IPCC).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.26.2] 2024 was the warmest year on record and the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels (Copernicus).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.26.3] Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.26.4] Global sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.26.5] Glaciers are retreating in most mountain regions, so this is further evidence of warming.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.26.6] Ireland's average temperature has risen about 0.9 °C since 1900 (EPA and Met Éireann).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.27.b] Warming happens because more greenhouse gases hold more heat in the climate system. Feedbacks then speed it up.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.27.0] The enhanced greenhouse effect: more CO2, methane and nitrous oxide absorb more outgoing long-wave radiation (heat given off by the earth), so the heat budget is out of balance and the earth warms until a new balance is reached.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.27.1] Most of the extra heat goes into the ocean, so the sea warms and expands.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.27.2] Ice–albedo feedback: melting ice exposes dark ocean, which absorbs more heat; this is why the Arctic is warming fastest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.27.3] Water vapour feedback: warmer air holds more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas, so warming leads to more warming.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.27.4] Permafrost thaw releases methane and CO2, so warming leads to more greenhouse gases.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.27.5] Reduced ocean CO2 uptake as the water warms, so more CO2 stays in the air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.28.b] A warmer atmosphere and ocean change how climate is spread across the world. The effects follow from more heat and more water vapour in the system.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.28.0] A more intense hydrological cycle (the cycle of evaporation, rain and runoff): heavier downpours and more severe droughts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.28.1] More intense heatwaves: the European heatwave of summer 2003 caused an estimated 70,000 or so extra deaths.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.28.2] A poleward shift of climate belts and storm tracks, and an expansion of the subtropical dry zones.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.28.3] A possible weakening of the Atlantic circulation (AMOC, the ocean conveyor that carries warm water northwards).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.28.4] Stronger tropical cyclones, because a warmer ocean supplies more heat to the storm.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.28.5] Sea-level rise from the thermal expansion of warmer water and the melting of land ice.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.29.b] Ireland is affected through temperature, rainfall and storms. The world has responded through scientific assessment and international agreement.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.29.0] Ireland: warmer temperatures; wetter winters and more flooding; a risk of summer drought, as in the summer of 2018; stronger storms and coastal erosion.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.29.1] The IPCC (the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) was set up in 1988; its Sixth Assessment Report (2021) stated that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.29.2] The Paris Agreement of 2015 aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue 1.5 °C. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.30.b] The third climate-change setting is El Niño. To understand it you first need the normal pattern across the equatorial Pacific, known as the Walker circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.30.0] Strong south-east trade winds push warm surface water westward across the Pacific, piling up a warm pool near Indonesia and northern Australia, where sea level is higher.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.30.1] Warm, moist air rises over the warm pool, producing low pressure and heavy rain. Air returns eastward at height and sinks over the cool eastern Pacific, giving high pressure and dry conditions on the coast of Peru and northern Chile.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.30.2] Cold, nutrient-rich water upwells (rises from depth) off Peru (the Humboldt Current), supporting the Peruvian anchoveta fishery, the largest single-species fishery in the world.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.30.3] The Walker circulation is named after Gilbert Walker, a British scientist who worked in India in the early 20th century.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.31.b] In an El Niño year the normal pattern breaks down. The warm water, and with it the rain, moves east.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.31.0] Every 2–7 years the trade winds weaken or even reverse, and the warm water flows back east towards South America.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.31.1] The thermocline (the boundary between warm surface water and cold deep water) deepens in the east and upwelling stops; sea-surface temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific rise well above normal; the zone of rising air and rain shifts east.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.31.2] It usually begins around the end of the year and lasts about 9–12 months, sometimes longer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.31.3] El Niño is a natural variation; scientists are still researching whether global warming changes it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.31.4] It was named by Peruvian fishermen after the Christ child because the warm water appeared around Christmas. Together with the Southern Oscillation, the see-saw of air pressure across the Pacific studied by Gilbert Walker, it is called ENSO.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.32.b] Because the rain moves east, regions that are normally wet or dry swap roles. These distant effects are called teleconnections.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.32.0] Heavy rain and floods in coastal Peru and Ecuador, which are normally dry, because the zone of rising air and rain has shifted east.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.32.1] Collapse of the anchoveta catch and the fishmeal industry, with seabird deaths, because the cold, nutrient-rich water no longer upwells.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.32.2] Drought in Indonesia, the Philippines and eastern Australia, with crop failure, bushfires, forest fires and haze.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.32.3] Often a weaker Indian monsoon and drought in southern Africa, so these are El Niño effects far from the Pacific.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.32.4] Fewer Atlantic hurricanes and more in the central and eastern Pacific, so the hurricane pattern shifts with the warm water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.32.5] Global average temperature rises temporarily, and strong El Niño years set temperature records.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.32.6] Effects on Ireland and Europe are weak and uncertain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.33.b] Past El Niño events show how large the effects can be.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.33.0] 1972–73: an El Niño, combined with overfishing, caused the collapse of the Peruvian anchoveta fishery.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.33.1] 1982–83 and 1997–98: very strong El Niños. In 1997–98 coastal Peru and Ecuador had severe floods, and drought in Indonesia allowed huge forest fires whose smoke haze spread across South-East Asia.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.33.2] 2015–16: one of the three strongest El Niños on record.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.33.3] 2023–24: helped make 2023 and 2024 the warmest years on record.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.34.b] La Niña is the reverse of El Niño: the normal Pacific pattern becomes stronger than usual, and the weather effects swap over.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.34.0] Stronger trade winds, a cooler than normal eastern Pacific and stronger upwelling, so cold water reaches the surface off Peru.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.34.1] Heavier rain in Indonesia and in Queensland, Australia, drought in Peru and the south-western USA, and more Atlantic hurricanes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.34.2] The strong La Niña of 2010–11 brought record rain and floods to Queensland, Australia, including the Brisbane flood of January 2011.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.35.b] El Niño is on the syllabus because it shows two parts of the climate system acting as one.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.35.0] It shows the atmosphere and ocean acting as one linked system, because a change in ocean temperature alters the winds and rain above it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.35.1] It shows how a change in ocean circulation changes weather around the world (teleconnections).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.35.2] Monitoring by buoys and satellites in the equatorial Pacific gives months of warning, so countries can prepare for floods and drought.
