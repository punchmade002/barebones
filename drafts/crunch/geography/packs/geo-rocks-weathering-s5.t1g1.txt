TARGET geo-rocks-weathering-s5: 5. Mass movement (mass wasting) processes and the factors governing them
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.b] Slopes are never completely still. Mass movement is the way weathered material shifts downhill, and everything else in this subsection (the processes, the factors and the case studies) is a variation on it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.0] Mass movement (also called mass wasting) is the downslope movement of weathered material, such as regolith (the loose broken rock and soil that covers solid rock), soil, rock, mud, peat or snow, under the influence of gravity.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.1] No transporting agent such as a river, glacier or wind is needed, because gravity does the work. Weathering (the breakdown of rock where it lies) supplies the material that moves.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.2] Gravity pulls material downslope. Friction (grip between surfaces), cohesion (particles sticking together) and plant roots resist it. Movement happens when the downslope force exceeds the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.3] Processes are classed, so that they can be compared, by speed (slow or fast), by water content (dry or wet) and by type of movement (heave, slide, flow or fall).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.b] Mass movement is not even across the landscape or through the year. It varies in space and in time, which is the syllabus idea of processes differing in intensity (how strong) and frequency (how often).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.0] In space, it is most intense on steep, wet and unvegetated slopes, because steepness increases the pull downslope while water and bare ground reduce the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.1] In time, its frequency rises after heavy rain, snowmelt, earthquakes and seasonal freeze-thaw (the repeated freezing and thawing of water in rock cracks).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.b] The slowest mass movement, moving soil by a few millimetres to centimetres a year. It is too slow to watch, so you recognise it from its effects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.0] Soil particles are lifted at right angles to the slope by wetting (swelling) or by freezing (frost heave, the upward push of freezing ground).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.1] On drying or thawing, the particles drop vertically under gravity, so each cycle moves them a little further downslope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.2] Evidence: terracettes (small steps on grassy slopes), bent tree trunks, tilted fences, walls and poles, soil piled up behind walls, and cracked roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.3] Diagram: draw the zigzag path of a particle, lifted at right angles to the slope and dropped vertically.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.b] Solifluction is a slow flow of waterlogged soil in cold regions near ice. It works because frozen ground below stops water draining away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.0] In periglacial areas (cold areas bordering ice sheets and glaciers) the surface layer thaws in summer above permanently frozen ground, called permafrost.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.1] The saturated layer cannot drain, because the frozen ground beneath is impermeable, so it flows slowly downhill, forming lobes (tongue-shaped bulges) and terraces.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.2] Solifluction was widespread in Ireland at the end of the last glaciation, when the climate was periglacial.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.b] Rockfall and landslide both move solid rock quickly. The difference is that rockfall is a free fall from a cliff, while a landslide is a slide along a plane.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.0] Rockfall: blocks loosened by freeze-thaw, or undercut by the sea or a river (worn away at the base), fall freely from a steep cliff. They collect as scree (also called talus) at the foot.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.1] Landslide (rockslide): a mass of rock or debris slides suddenly along a flat plane, such as a bedding plane (a layer boundary in sedimentary rock) or joint (a crack) sloping in the same direction as the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.2] Landslides often follow heavy rain, which lubricates the plane so the rock slips more easily, or an earthquake, which shakes it loose.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.b] A slump is a fast slide in which the moving mass turns as it goes, because the surface it slides on is curved rather than flat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.0] Saturated material, often clay over or under permeable rock (rock that lets water through), slides along a curved (concave) slip plane and rotates backward.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.1] The slump leaves a crescent-shaped scar (scarp) at the top, a back-tilted block and a bulging toe at the foot.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.2] Slumps are common on clay cliffs undercut by the sea, because the sea removes the support at the base, for example on the Antrim coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.3] A slump moves on a curved plane. A landslide moves on a flat plane.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.b] These three fast flows each carry a wet or loose mass downhill: mud, volcanic mud, or snow and ice. All are triggered by a sudden supply of water or by disturbance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.0] Mudflow: fine material (clay, silt, weathered rock or volcanic ash) saturated by heavy rain or snowmelt flows rapidly downhill like a liquid, often along valleys. It can bury settlements.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.1] Lahar: a volcanic mudflow of ash and water, from rain or from melted snow and ice on the volcano. It can travel very fast and far. The word is from Indonesia.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.2] Avalanche: a sudden, rapid slide of snow and ice down a steep mountain slope, triggered by heavy snowfall, rapid thaw or disturbance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.3] Ice avalanches occur when part of a glacier collapses, such as at Marmolada in 2022.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.b] A bog burst is a fast mass movement of peat, common in the uplands of Ireland. Peat (partly rotted plant matter that builds up in waterlogged ground) on a slope fills with water and then slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.0] Blanket bog (a thick layer of peat covering a hillside) on a slope becomes saturated after prolonged or intense rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.1] The peat gains weight, and its base loses grip on the underlying mineral soil or rock. The peat bursts and flows downhill as a black slurry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.2] A bog burst blocks rivers, kills fish, and destroys roads, bridges and land.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.3] Bog bursts are common in the uplands of western and north-western Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.b] Whether a slope moves, and how fast, depends on a set of factors. The first three are the slope angle, the water in the ground and what the slope is made of.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.0] Gradient (slope angle): the steeper the slope, the greater the downslope pull of gravity and the faster the movement. Gentle slopes favour creep; steep slopes favour falls and slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.1] Water content: some water adds cohesion, but saturation fills the pores (the tiny gaps between particles), adds weight, reduces friction and acts as a lubricant, so material flows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.2] Heavy or prolonged rain and snowmelt trigger most fast movements, because they saturate the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.3] Clay becomes slippery and plastic (easily moulded) when wet. Jointed or bedded rock dipping towards the slope slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.4] Permeable rock over impermeable clay or shale (rock that water cannot pass through) concentrates water at the junction, as on the Antrim coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.b] The remaining factors either hold a slope together or remove what supports it. Some are natural, some come from the climate, and one is human.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.0] Vegetation: roots bind soil and plants absorb water, so bare slopes move more easily.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.1] Climate and weathering: freeze-thaw loosens rock, heavy seasonal rainfall adds water, and periglacial climates produce solifluction.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.2] Undercutting: erosion by the sea or a river at the base of a slope removes support, so the material above is left unsupported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.3] Loading: extra weight at the top of a slope, from rock, buildings or spoil (waste rock), increases the downslope force.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.4] Tectonic activity: earthquakes and volcanic eruptions shake loose material and trigger landslides and lahars.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.5] Human activity also affects slope stability. There is more on this in Human interaction: mass movement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.b] The Antrim coast is an Irish example of landslides and slumps. Its layers of rock, hard on top of soft, explain why the slopes fail.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.0] Location: the Antrim coast between Larne and Cushendall, along the Antrim Coast Road (A2).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.1] Geology: hard basalt lies on chalk, which lies on soft Lias clay with other mudstones.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.2] Rainwater soaks through the jointed basalt and chalk and saturates the clay, which becomes slippery and plastic.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.3] The rock above slumps and rotates seaward in rotational slides, and clay flows out as mudflows. These have repeatedly blocked the coast road.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.4] Garron Point is a classic landslip site on this coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.5] Responses, so that the road stays open: engineers have built retaining walls and drainage, and fitted rock-catch fences and netting to protect the road.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.11.b] Two Irish bog bursts show how rain-soaked peat can fail, one recent and one historic.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.11.0] Pollatomish (Dooncarton Mountain), Co. Mayo, 19 September 2003: an intense thunderstorm with very heavy rain triggered about 40 landslides and peat slides in a few hours.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.11.1] At Pollatomish, houses, roads, a bridge and a graveyard were damaged, and several families were evacuated.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.11.2] Knocknageeha, near Rathmore, Co. Kerry, December 1896: a bog burst swept away a house, killing a family of eight. It is the classic Irish bog burst.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.b] Italy gives two international examples: mudflows after heavy rain at Sarno, and a landslide into a reservoir at Vajont.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.0] Sarno and neighbouring towns, Campania, southern Italy, 5-6 May 1998: after prolonged heavy rain, loose volcanic ash and soil on steep limestone slopes became saturated and slid off as fast mudflows into the towns below. About 160 people were killed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.1] Vajont Dam, northern Italy, 9 October 1963: a huge landslide from Monte Toc slid into the reservoir (the lake held back by the dam). The wave it sent over the dam destroyed the town of Longarone, killing about 2,000 people. The dam itself survived.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.2] Weak clay layers in the valley side, and the filling of the reservoir which saturated the slope, caused the Vajont slide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.b] South America gives a lahar triggered by a volcano and mudslides triggered by torrential rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.0] Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia, 13 November 1985: a small eruption melted part of the summit ice cap. Lahars rushed down river valleys and buried the town of Armero, killing about 23,000 people.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.1] Warnings had been given, but Armero was not evacuated.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.2] Vargas state, on the coast of Venezuela, December 1999: days of torrential rain triggered mudslides and debris flows on the steep mountains above coastal towns, killing thousands of people.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.b] Recent glacier collapses in the Alps are mass movements of ice, rock and mud. They show glacier retreat acting as a new cause.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.0] Blatten, Lötschental, canton of Valais, Switzerland, 28 May 2025: the Birch Glacier collapsed, sending a huge mass of ice, rock and mud onto the village and burying most of it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.1] The village had been evacuated days earlier, after geologists detected rockfalls onto the glacier.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.2] The debris blocked the River Lonza, forming a lake upstream, which means a flood risk.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.3] Marmolada glacier, Dolomites, Italy, 3 July 2022: during a heatwave a large section of the glacier collapsed as an ice avalanche, killing 11 people.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s3: 3. Weathering processes
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.b] Rock at the surface is attacked by weather, water, plants and animals. Weathering is the first step in wearing landscapes down, and it must be kept separate from erosion.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.0] Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place (in situ) at or near the earth's surface, by weather, water, plants and animals. Erosion is the wearing away and removal of material by a moving agent such as a river, the sea, ice or wind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.1] Weathering involves no transport: the rock breaks down where it lies, so no moving agent is involved. This prepares the rock for erosion and for mass movement (the downhill movement of loose material under gravity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.2] Regolith is the layer of loose, weathered rock fragments lying on top of solid bedrock (the unbroken rock beneath).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.3] Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals (the chemical substances the rock is made of).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.4] Chemical weathering changes or dissolves the minerals in the rock, so the rock itself is altered, not just broken.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.5] Biological weathering by plants and animals works through both physical and chemical means.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.b] Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process. It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock (rock with natural cracks called joints).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.1] 2. At night the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.2] 3. By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.3] 4. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular (sharp-edged) fragments.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.4] It is most effective on mountains in upland Ireland, on high mountains and in periglacial areas (cold areas where the ground is frozen for much of the year). Freeze-thaw is active on Irish mountain summits such as Carrauntoohil, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.5] Result: scree (talus) slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields (areas covered in broken angular rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.b] In hot deserts the rock surface swings between hot days and cold nights. Both processes here are physical weathering driven by that heating and cooling, called insolation (heating by the sun).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.0] Exfoliation, or onion weathering, is an insolation process in hot deserts such as the Sahara, where the daily temperature range (the gap between day and night temperature) is large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.1] The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day, and cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.2] Stress builds up, and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion, leaving rounded boulders.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.3] Many geographers stress that some moisture, such as dew, must be present for exfoliation to work effectively.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.4] Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.b] Rock can also break because the forces on it change, or because growing crystals push it apart. Neither process changes the minerals, so both are physical.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.0] Pressure release (also called unloading or sheeting): when the overlying rock is eroded, the weight pressing on buried rock such as granite is removed, so it expands upward and cracks into sheets parallel to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.1] The sheets peel off to leave domes, such as Half Dome, Yosemite, California, USA, a granite dome shaped by sheeting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.2] Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores (tiny holes) and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.3] Salt crystallisation is common in hot deserts and on coasts, where salt water is available and evaporates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.b] Plants and animals break rock down in two ways. Their physical action is a form of physical weathering, and the acids they produce cause chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.0] Physical: plant roots grow into cracks and widen them, and burrowing animals expose fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.1] Chemical: decaying plants produce humic acids, and lichens and mosses release acids that attack rock surfaces.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.b] Carbonation is a chemical weathering process. It dissolves limestone, and it works along joints and bedding planes (the flat surfaces between layers of rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.0] Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.1] The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble, so it is carried away in solution (dissolved in water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.2] Word equation: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.0] Hydrolysis: water reacts with feldspar (a mineral in granite) and turns it into clay (kaolin, or china clay); the quartz grains (another mineral in granite) are left as sand, and the granite crumbles.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.1] China clay from the hydrolysis of granite is quarried around St Austell, Cornwall, England, and on the edge of Dartmoor.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.2] Oxidation: oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron in the rock to form iron oxide (rust), which gives red, yellow or brown colours and weakens the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.3] Hydration: some minerals absorb water and swell, setting up stress in the rock; anhydrite, for example, becomes gypsum.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.b] Weathering does not act at the same speed or in the same way everywhere. Climate decides which type of weathering dominates, and rock type decides how vulnerable the rock is.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.0] Climate: temperature and rainfall decide which type of weathering dominates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.1] Warm, wet climates favour fast chemical weathering because, by Van 't Hoff's rule, the rate of chemical reactions roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.2] Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, and hot deserts favour exfoliation and salt crystallisation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.3] Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.4] Rock type (its mineral composition): limestone is vulnerable to carbonation, granite to hydrolysis, and quartzite (a very hard rock) resists both.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.b] Beyond climate and rock type, four more factors affect how quickly rock weathers. Most work by letting water reach the rock or by exposing fresh rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.0] Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults (breaks in the rock where blocks have moved) let water in, so heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.1] Vegetation and soil cover: roots and humic acids speed weathering, and soil holds moisture against the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.2] Time: older surfaces are more weathered, because weathering has had longer to act.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.3] Relief (the steepness and shape of the land): steep slopes remove debris and expose fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.4] Aspect (the direction a slope faces): shady slopes stay frozen longer, so freeze-thaw is stronger on them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.b] Temperature links the processes above. It matters in two different ways, and the type of weathering that results depends on the temperature pattern of the place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.0] Temperature acts in two ways: as a trigger, through the expansion and contraction that cause stress, and as a control on the rate of chemical reactions.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.1] Freeze-thaw depends on temperature crossing the freezing point, and exfoliation and granular disintegration depend on a large daily range; both need changes in temperature, not just warmth.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.3] Method: name the process and its type, state the temperature condition it needs, explain the stress or rate change it produces, and link this to the result, such as scree from freeze-thaw.

CANDIDATES geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1: 1. The geography of volcanoes
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.b] A volcano is an opening (vent) in the Earth's crust, the thin rocky outer layer, through which magma, gases and fragments escape, together with the landform built around it. Magma is molten rock below the surface; lava is magma that has reached the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.0] Magma chamber: the reservoir of magma beneath the volcano, where the molten rock collects before it rises.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.1] Main vent: the pipe (conduit) that carries magma up to the crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.2] Secondary (parasitic) vent: a smaller opening on the flanks (sides) of the cone, which lets magma escape away from the summit.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.3] Crater: the hollow at the top of the main vent.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.4] Cone: the hill of the volcano, built up over many eruptions from alternating layers of lava and ash.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.5] Lava flow: lava moving across the surface away from the vent, such as the flows drawn down the side of the cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.6] Diagram: draw a labelled cross-section showing the magma chamber, main vent, secondary vent, crater, cone of alternating lava and ash layers, lava flow, ash cloud and volcanic bombs, with the crust around the chamber.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.b] An eruption throws out liquid, gas and solid material. Each one is a hazard in its own right, so you need to know the names and what each does.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.0] Lava: molten rock that flows from the vent, so it can bury land and buildings around the volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.1] Gases: water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide, which are released as the magma rises and the pressure on it falls.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.2] Pyroclastic material (tephra): solid fragments of rock thrown into the air. It includes ash, lapilli (cinders), volcanic bombs and pumice.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.3] Ash: the finest pyroclastic particles, which are light enough to fall over wide areas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.4] Volcanic bombs: large lumps of lava thrown out of the vent while still partly molten.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.5] Pumice: a light, porous rock (full of tiny holes) formed from frothy lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.6] Pyroclastic flows: fast, very hot avalanches of gas, ash and rock moving down the slopes, so they destroy anything in their path.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.7] Lahars: volcanic mudflows of ash and water, for example when an eruption melts snow or ice.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.b] The silica content of magma (silica is the mineral material that makes rock) controls how runny the lava is, how much gas it traps, and so how the volcano erupts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.0] Basic (basaltic) lava is low in silica and hot, so it is runny (low viscosity, meaning it flows easily).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.1] Gases escape easily from basic lava, so eruptions are gentle (effusive, meaning lava pours out rather than blasting).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.2] Acidic lava is high in silica and cooler, so it is viscous (thick and sticky).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.3] Acidic lava traps gas, so pressure builds up and eruptions are explosive.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.4] Low-viscosity lava spreads widely and builds shield volcanoes (broad, gently sloping); viscous lava piles up into steep cones. The names and shapes of the landform types belong to the volcanic and plutonic landforms notes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.b] Volcanoes are grouped by how likely they are to erupt again. The groups depend on how long it has been since the last eruption and whether another is expected.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.0] Active: a volcano that has erupted recently and may erupt again soon. Examples: Mount Etna, Sicily, Italy; Kilauea, Hawaii; Stromboli, Italy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.1] Dormant (sleeping): a volcano that has not erupted for a long time but could erupt again. Example: Mount Fuji, Japan, last erupted in 1707.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.2] Extinct: a volcano not expected ever to erupt again. Example: Slemish, Co. Antrim, the remains of an extinct volcano, linked with St Patrick.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.b] Most volcanoes lie in narrow belts that follow plate boundaries (the lines where the huge slabs of crust, called plates, meet), not at random. Each type of boundary gives a different result.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.0] Constructive (divergent) boundaries: basic magma rises to fill the gap where plates separate, mostly under the sea along mid-ocean ridges (underwater mountain chains).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.1] Iceland is where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level; the North American and Eurasian plates are moving apart there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.2] Destructive (convergent) boundaries: oceanic crust is subducted (pushed down beneath another plate), the descending plate melts, and acidic magma rises to form explosive volcanoes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.3] Destructive boundaries form volcanoes in fold-mountain chains (mountains crumpled up where plates collide), such as the Andes where the Nazca plate is subducted beneath the South American plate.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.4] They also form volcanoes in island arcs (curved chains of volcanic islands) such as Japan and Indonesia.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.5] Continental-continental collision and transform (conservative) boundaries, where plates slide past each other, have few or no volcanoes, so magma rarely reaches the surface there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.b] A belt of volcanoes circles the Pacific Ocean, because most of the plate boundaries around that ocean are destructive.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.0] The Ring of Fire is a belt of volcanoes and earthquakes around the edges of the Pacific Ocean, mainly along destructive boundaries.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.1] It contains about three-quarters of the world's active volcanoes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.2] Examples: Mount St Helens (Cascade Range, Washington State, USA); Mount Fuji, Japan; Mount Pinatubo, the Philippines; Krakatoa, Indonesia; volcanoes of the Andes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.b] A hot spot is a plume of magma rising from deep in the mantle (the hot layer beneath the crust), away from plate boundaries. It explains volcanoes in places where no plates meet.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.0] As a plate moves over a stationary hot spot, a chain of volcanoes forms, with the volcanoes getting older with distance from the hot spot, because each one is carried away from the magma supply.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.1] Hawaii sits in the middle of the Pacific plate over a hot spot, so it is not on a plate boundary. The Pacific plate moves north-west, so the islands get older to the north-west.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.2] The active volcanoes Kilauea and Mauna Loa are on the Big Island in the south-east, over the hot spot.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.3] Other hot spots: Yellowstone, USA; Iceland, which is on a constructive boundary and also over a hot spot.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.b] Ireland has no active volcanoes now, but it had huge eruptions in the past. These were linked to the opening of the North Atlantic Ocean, which is the syllabus's national setting.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.0] Ireland lies in the middle of the Eurasian plate, far from a plate boundary, so it has no active volcanoes today.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.1] About 60 million years ago, North America and Greenland separated from Europe as the Atlantic opened. The crust stretched and cracked.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.2] Basic lava poured out of long fissures (cracks in the ground) in north-east Ireland, flow upon flow, in fissure eruptions. These lavas formed the Antrim basalts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.3] Basalt is an extrusive (volcanic) rock, meaning it formed from lava on the surface. The Antrim basalts came from fissures, not from a single cone volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.4] Landforms from these lavas include the Antrim plateau and the Giant's Causeway.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.b] Eruptions can kill people and damage land, buildings, travel and even the climate. Named examples show how large the damage can be.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.0] Loss of life, and destruction of buildings, farmland and infrastructure by lava, pyroclastic flows and lahars. In AD 79 Vesuvius, near Naples, Italy, buried the Roman towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum under ash and pyroclastic flows.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.1] In 2018, lava from Kilauea's lower East Rift Zone destroyed hundreds of homes in the Leilani Estates area of Hawaii.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.2] Ash fall can collapse roofs under its weight, for example when thick ash builds up on them, destroy crops and contaminate water supplies.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.3] Ash clouds disrupt air travel. In April 2010 an eruption under the Eyjafjallajökull ice cap, Iceland, closed much of European airspace for about a week, and about 100,000 flights were cancelled.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.4] Volcanic tsunamis (huge sea waves): the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa, Indonesia, caused tsunamis that killed about 36,000 people.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.5] The undersea Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai volcano, Tonga, erupted on 15 January 2022 and caused a tsunami across the Pacific.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.6] Gases and ash can cool the global climate for a year or two. After Mount Pinatubo, the Philippines, erupted in 1991, sulphur dioxide in the upper atmosphere cooled global average temperatures by about 0.5°C over the following year or so.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.7] Economic losses and evacuation of populations, because homes, farms and businesses are lost and people must leave the danger zone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.b] Volcanoes are dangerous, but they also give people land, energy, income and materials. This is why many people live close to them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.0] Fertile soils from weathered volcanic ash and lava, which break down into minerals plants can use. On the slopes of Mount Etna and Vesuvius they support vineyards and orchards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.1] Geothermal energy (heat from underground) and hot water: in Iceland geothermal energy heats about nine in ten homes and generates electricity.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.2] Tourism: volcanoes, geysers (jets of hot water and steam) and hot springs attract visitors, such as the Blue Lagoon and the geysers at Geysir, Iceland.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.3] Minerals and building materials, such as sulphur, pumice and basalt, which can be quarried or mined from volcanic rock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.4] New land: Surtsey, an island that emerged from the sea off southern Iceland in 1963.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.b] Mount St Helens is the full case study to learn, with date, place, plate setting, events, effects and response. The eruption was sideways, not vertical, which makes it unusual.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.0] Place: Cascade Range, Washington State, USA. The Juan de Fuca plate is subducted beneath the North American plate, a destructive boundary.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.1] For weeks before 18 May 1980, magma pushed the north flank (side) outwards into a bulge.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.2] A magnitude 5.1 earthquake triggered the collapse of the north flank as a huge landslide.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.3] The collapse released a sideways (lateral) blast that flattened forests.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.4] The summit lost about 400 m of height, leaving a horseshoe-shaped crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.b] The eruption did great damage, but the number of deaths was low because people had been kept away from the danger zone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.0] Ash fell across several US states.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.1] Lahars (mudflows of ash and water) flowed down river valleys, including the Toutle River, destroying bridges and homes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.2] 57 people died, not thousands.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.3] Most people had been kept out of a restricted zone, which kept the death toll low.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.b] Volcanoes usually give warning signs, because magma must move upwards before an eruption. Scientists measure these signs to judge when a volcano is becoming dangerous.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.0] Seismometers (instruments that record earthquakes) pick up swarms of small earthquakes as magma rises.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.1] Ground deformation: tiltmeters (which measure changes in slope), GPS and satellite radar measure swelling or bulging of the cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.2] Gas emissions: changes in the amount and type of gas, especially sulphur dioxide.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.3] Temperature: thermal imaging and satellites detect heating of the ground or of crater lakes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.4] Changes in groundwater and springs, for example because rising magma heats the water underground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.5] Studying the deposits and records of past eruptions shows how often a volcano erupts and which areas are at risk. Hazard maps show these areas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.6] Scientists can often forecast that an eruption is likely, but not its exact time, size or style.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.b] Eruptions cannot be stopped, but their effects can be limited. The main approaches are to watch the volcano, keep people out of danger, and sometimes to fight the lava itself.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.0] Monitoring by volcano observatories, so that warning signs are picked up early.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.1] Hazard zoning keeps settlement out of high-risk zones, so fewer people and buildings are in the path of an eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.2] Evacuation plans and warning systems, and public education.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.3] Diverting or slowing lava flows with barriers and channels, or cooling it with water.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.4] Heimaey, Iceland, 1973: the Eldfell eruption on Heimaey, Westman Islands, threatened the harbour. About 5,000 residents were evacuated overnight, mostly by the fishing fleet. Sea water was pumped onto the advancing lava to cool and halt it, saving the harbour.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.5] Pinatubo, the Philippines, 1991: scientists from the US Geological Survey and the Philippine volcano institute monitored earthquakes, gas emissions and swelling, and forecast the eruption. Tens of thousands of people were evacuated, including from the US Clark Air Base, saving thousands of lives.
