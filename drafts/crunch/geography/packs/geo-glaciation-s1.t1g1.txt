TARGET geo-glaciation-s1: 1. Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.b] Ireland was glaciated (covered by moving ice) during the Pleistocene, the geological period of repeated ice ages. The landforms in this subsection were shaped by ice in its two main cold stages, and the type of ice involved decides what it can shape.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.0] The older cold stage is the Munsterian; the more recent is the Midlandian. Their names are the ones to use when you are asked to date Irish glaciation.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.1] Munsterian ice covered almost all of the island, so it was more extensive than the Midlandian ice, which left the far south free.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.2] Midlandian glaciation ended about 10,000 years ago, and its ice did not cover the far south of Ireland, so the south kept land the Midlandian ice never reached.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.3] An ice sheet is a continental mass of ice covering whole landscapes, buried hills and all, as in Antarctica and Greenland today.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.4] A valley glacier is confined to a valley, so its path is set by the landscape. It is fed by snow and ice from corries (armchair-shaped hollows high on the mountainside).
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.b] Glacier ice is not frozen water poured into a valley. It is built up slowly from snow that never melts away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.0] Snow that survives the summer is compacted into firn (granular snow, also called nevé), because each year's new snow presses down on the older snow beneath.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.1] Firn becomes glacier ice as the weight of overlying snow squeezes out the air and the ice crystals grow larger and interlock, producing dense solid ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.b] A glacier gains ice at its head and loses it at its snout (its lower end), and the balance between these determines its behaviour. Geographers compare this to a budget of income against spending.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.0] Accumulation zone: the upper part of the glacier, where snow and ice are gained, because more snow falls there than melts.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.1] Ablation zone: the lower part, including the snout, where ice is lost by melting. Ablation simply means loss of ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.2] Equilibrium line: the boundary between the two zones, where gain and loss are equal over a year, so above it the glacier gains and below it the glacier loses.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.b] A glacier is not frozen in place. Gravity and the weight of the ice make it creep downhill by three different mechanisms, and friction makes some parts move faster than others.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.0] Basal slip: meltwater at the base lubricates the bed (the rock floor beneath the ice), so the whole glacier slides over the rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.1] Internal deformation (plastic flow): ice crystals slip past one another under the weight of the ice, so the glacier flows downhill.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.2] Rotational movement: ice in a corrie (the hollow where a glacier begins) rotates within the hollow instead of moving straight down the valley.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.3] Ice moves fastest at the centre of the surface and slowest at the sides and base, where friction with the valley walls and bed is greatest.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.b] The snout's position depends on whether more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts at the snout. This balance decides whether the glacier is getting longer or shorter.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.0] Advance: when more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts, the snout moves forward.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.1] Retreat: when melting exceeds the ice arriving, the snout moves back up the valley, although the ice is still flowing forward. The snout retreats because it melts back faster than the ice feeds it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.2] Heavier snowfall or cooler summers, which reduce melting, cause the glacier to advance.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.3] Less snowfall or warmer summers, which increase melting, cause the glacier to retreat.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.b] Average rate of movement is found by dividing the distance moved by the number of years. The figures below are illustrative, invented for practice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.0] Average rate = distance moved ÷ number of years, given in metres per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.1] Illustrative: a snout that retreats 600 m over 20 years has moved an average of 600 ÷ 20 = 30 m per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.2] Illustrative: a snout that advances 450 m over 9 years has moved an average of 450 ÷ 9 = 50 m per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.b] Glaciers erode mainly by plucking and abrasion, while freeze-thaw supplies rock debris at the edges of the ice. Together these processes carve the corries, valleys and lakes of glaciated uplands.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.0] Plucking (quarrying): meltwater enters joints (cracks) in the bedrock and freezes, bonding the ice to the rock; as the glacier moves on, it tears out blocks, mainly on the downhill side of rock obstacles.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.1] Abrasion: rock fragments frozen into the base of the ice scrape and polish the bedrock, like sandpaper on wood. It produces striations (scratches), polished rock and rock flour.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.2] Rock flour: fine powder ground from the bedrock by abrasion, which meltwater carries away, giving glacial lakes a milky colour.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.3] Freeze-thaw on the rock above and beside the ice: water freezing in cracks expands and breaks off fragments, which fall onto the ice and are carried away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.b] A corrie (also called a cirque) is the armchair-shaped hollow where a mountain glacier begins. Corrie ice feeds valley glaciers, so corries are linked to the valleys below them.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.0] 1. Snow collects in a hollow on the mountainside; nivation (erosion by snow patches) and freeze-thaw enlarge the hollow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.1] 2. The snow turns to ice, and the rotating glacier plucks and abrades the back wall and floor, deepening the hollow and steepening the back wall.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.2] 3. The result is a bowl with a steep back wall and a rock lip, called the threshold, at its lower edge, because the ice erodes the floor more deeply than the rim.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.3] 4. When the ice melts, water collects behind the lip, because the lip dams the hollow, and forms a lake called a tarn.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.4] Examples: Coumshingaun, Comeragh Mountains, Co. Waterford; the Devil's Punchbowl, Mangerton, Co. Kerry.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.5] Diagram: cross-section of a corrie with steep back wall, rock lip and tarn; plan view of the bowl-shaped hollow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.b] Corries do not only deepen hollows; they also eat into the mountain around them. Where corries meet, they leave sharp ridges and peaks standing between them.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.0] 1. Two corries cut back into the mountainside from opposite sides of a ridge.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.1] 2. Their back walls retreat, so the ridge between them is sharpened into a thin, knife-edged arête.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.2] 3. Three or more corries erode back around one summit from different directions.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.3] 4. The summit is left as a sharp, pointed pyramidal peak, also called a horn, because erosion has cut away every side of it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.4] Arête example: Striding Edge, Helvellyn, Lake District, England.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.5] Pyramidal peak examples: Carrauntoohil, Macgillycuddy's Reeks, Co. Kerry, Ireland's highest mountain; the Matterhorn, Swiss-Italian Alps.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.6] Diagram: plan view of two corries backing onto an arête; plan view of a horn ringed by three corries.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.b] A glacier reshapes the river valley it occupies. It turns a narrow V-shaped valley into a wide trough, which is why glaciated valleys look so different from river valleys.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.0] 1. Before glaciation the valley is V-shaped, cut by a river.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.1] 2. A glacier fills the valley and deepens and widens its floor by plucking and abrasion.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.2] 3. The glacier cuts off the river's interlocking spurs (ridges that overlap from alternate sides of a river valley), leaving blunt cliff faces called truncated spurs.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.3] 4. The result is a broad, flat-floored trough with steep sides, which gives the valley its U-shape because the ice cuts down and sideways at once. It is also called a glacial trough.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.4] Examples: Glendalough and Glenmalure, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.5] Diagram: cross-sections of a V-shaped valley beside a U-shaped valley, with truncated spurs labelled.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.b] When a small glacier joins a large one, the two do not cut to the same depth. This leaves the smaller valley stranded high above the main one.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.0] 1. A smaller tributary glacier joins the larger glacier that is filling the main valley.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.1] 2. Because the tributary glacier is smaller, it erodes less deeply, so its valley is left hanging above the main valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.2] 3. When the ice melts, the tributary stream falls over the edge of the hanging valley as a waterfall.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.3] Example: Poulanass Waterfall, falling from a hanging valley into the Glendalough valley, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.4] Diagram: long profile (side view along the valley) of a hanging valley and its waterfall above the main valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.b] A glacier does not erode its valley floor evenly. Where it cuts deeper, it leaves a rock basin (a hollow in the bedrock) that fills with water as a lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.0] 1. Plucking and abrasion overdeepen a section of the valley floor more than the rest, leaving a rock basin.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.1] 2. When the ice melts, the basin fills with water, forming a long, narrow ribbon lake, because the lake takes the shape of the valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.2] Example: Upper Lake, Glendalough, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.3] Paternoster lakes: a chain of rock-basin lakes along a valley floor, separated by bars of harder rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.4] Tarns, ribbon lakes and kettle lakes are all glacial lakes.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.5] Diagram: long profile of an overdeepened valley with a ribbon lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.b] A fjord is a glaciated valley on the coast that the sea has flooded after the ice melted. It forms only where a glacier cut the valley floor below sea level.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.0] 1. A glacier overdeepens a coastal valley so that its floor lies well below sea level.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.1] 2. When the ice melts, the sea floods the valley, leaving a fjord with steep sides.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.2] Examples: Killary Harbour, on the Co. Galway-Co. Mayo border; Sognefjord, Norway.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.3] Diagram: plan view of a fjord with steep sides and the sea at its mouth.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.b] Where ice flows over a bump of hard bedrock, it treats the two sides differently. Abrasion smooths the side it climbs and plucking roughens the side it leaves.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.0] 1. Ice flows over a bedrock outcrop (rock exposed at the surface), and abrasion smooths and polishes its upstream (stoss) side.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.1] 2. On the downstream (lee) side, plucking tears blocks from the rock, leaving a steep, rough face.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.2] 3. The result is a rounded hill with a smooth stoss side and a rough lee side.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.3] Example: Lembert Dome, Yosemite, California, USA.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.4] Striations: fine parallel scratches cut into bedrock by rock fragments held in the base of the ice; they are carved by the ice and show the direction of ice flow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.5] Diagram: cross-section of a roche moutonnee showing the stoss side, lee side and direction of ice flow; plan view of striations on bedrock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.b] A glacier carries rock debris, called its load, in three positions. Where the load sits decides how much work it does and what it looks like when dropped.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.0] Supraglacial load: debris resting on the surface of the glacier, such as rock falling from the valley sides.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.1] Englacial load: debris trapped within the ice, so it is carried inside the glacier, away from the surface and base.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.2] Subglacial load: debris dragged along the base; it is the most abrasive part of the load and does most of the erosion, because it is pressed against the bedrock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.3] Glacial load is angular and unsorted: ice does not sort it by size, and it is not rounded by water as river load is, so sharp edges and mixed sizes survive.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.b] Deposition means dropping the load. Ice that deposits material directly leaves a jumble, and this jumble also affects how water drains across the land afterwards.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.0] Till (boulder clay) is unsorted and unstratified (not laid down in layers), angular material ranging from clay to boulders, deposited directly by ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.1] Boulder clay plains of the Irish Midlands are formed where till is left across the land, for example when ice melts and drops its load over a wide area.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.2] Deranged drainage: no clear pattern, with many lakes and marshes, forms on poorly drained glacial deposits such as till.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.b] A moraine is a ridge or sheet of till. Its name depends on where in or around the glacier it was left.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.0] Lateral moraine: a ridge of till along the side of a glacier, built from debris falling from the valley walls, such as frost-shattered rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.1] Medial moraine: formed where two glaciers join, as their lateral moraines merge and run down the middle of the combined ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.2] Terminal (end) moraine: an arc-shaped ridge of till at the snout, marking the furthest advance or a long pause in retreat, because the snout dumps its load where it stays still.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.3] Recessional moraine: a ridge of till built at the snout during a pause while the glacier retreats.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.4] Ground moraine: a sheet of till left on the bed as the glacier melts back.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.5] Southern Irish End Moraine: marks the southern limit of the Midlandian ice sheet.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.6] Diagram: cross-section of a valley glacier with lateral, medial and terminal moraines labelled; plan view of an arc-shaped terminal moraine.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.b] Drumlins are smooth, oval hills of till, moulded by the ice that flowed over them. Their shape records which way the ice was moving.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.0] 1. Ice deposits or overrides an accumulation of till on the bed, building an irregular mass of material.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.1] 2. Ice flowing over the mass moulds it into an elongated, streamlined hill lying parallel to the direction of ice flow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.2] 3. The blunt, steep end faces up-ice, and the tapered end points down-ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.3] 4. Drumlins occur in swarms, all aligned in one direction, forming a basket of eggs landscape.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.4] 5. Where the sea floods low ground, drumlins become islands, forming drowned drumlins, as in Clew Bay, Co. Mayo, and Strangford Lough, Co. Down.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.5] Example: the drumlin belt across Co. Cavan, Co. Monaghan and Co. Leitrim.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.6] Diagram: plan view of a drumlin swarm with an ice-flow arrow; side profile showing the blunt up-ice end and tapered down-ice end.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.b] Ice can carry very large rocks over long distances. When it melts it drops them, so they end up in places where they do not belong geologically.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.0] Erratic: a boulder carried by ice and dropped far from its source, with a rock type unlike the bedrock beneath it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.1] Example: granite erratics from Connemara resting on the limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.b] Fluvio-glacial material is laid down by meltwater rather than directly by ice, so it differs from till. Exams ask you to sort landforms into three groups, listed here.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.0] Meltwater sorts material by size, so fluvio-glacial deposits are sorted, and layers are stratified (laid down in layers); till is neither.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.1] Exam classification, glacial erosion: corrie, arete, pyramidal peak, U-shaped valley, hanging valley, ribbon lake, fjord, roche moutonnee, striations.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.2] Exam classification, glacial deposition: till, moraines, drumlins, erratics.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.3] Exam classification, fluvio-glacial: eskers, outwash plains, kames, kettle holes.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.b] An esker is a long ridge built by a meltwater stream that once flowed through a tunnel in the ice. The ridge is what is left once the ice around it has gone.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.0] 1. Meltwater flows in a tunnel within or beneath the glacier.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.1] 2. The stream deposits sorted sand and gravel on the tunnel floor as its velocity falls, because slower water can no longer carry its load.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.2] 3. When the ice melts, the deposit is left as a long, winding ridge called an esker.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.3] Example: Esker Riada, a series of eskers across the Midlands from roughly Dublin to Galway, used as an ancient routeway (the Slí Mhór); Clonmacnoise, Co. Offaly, is built beside it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.4] Diagram: plan view of a winding esker ridge; cross-section of a ridge of sorted gravel.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.b] Meltwater leaves the glacier carrying sand and gravel and drops it beyond the ice limit. It builds a broad, flat plain, also called a sandur.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.0] 1. Meltwater spreads sand and gravel beyond the terminal moraine (the ridge of till marking the furthest ice advance) onto flat ground.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.1] 2. The deposit forms a flat, sorted outwash plain.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.2] Example: The Curragh, Co. Kildare, a plain of sand and gravel; it is well-drained grassland used for horse training and an army camp.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.3] Diagram: plan view of an outwash plain lying beyond a terminal moraine.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.b] Meltwater also dumps sand and gravel on and around the ice itself. When the ice is gone, mounds and hollows are left behind.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.0] 1. Meltwater deposits sand and gravel in a hollow on the ice surface or at its margin.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.1] 2. When the ice melts, the deposit is left as a mound called a kame; where it lies against a valley side it forms a kame terrace.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.2] 3. A block of ice buried in outwash melts, leaving a hollow called a kettle hole. When water fills the hollow it becomes a kettle lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.3] Diagram: cross-section of a kame beside a kettle hole.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s3: 3. Weathering processes
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.b] Rock at the surface is attacked by weather, water, plants and animals. Weathering is the first step in wearing landscapes down, and it must be kept separate from erosion.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.0] Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place (in situ) at or near the earth's surface, by weather, water, plants and animals. Erosion is the wearing away and removal of material by a moving agent such as a river, the sea, ice or wind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.1] Weathering involves no transport: the rock breaks down where it lies, so no moving agent is involved. This prepares the rock for erosion and for mass movement (the downhill movement of loose material under gravity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.2] Regolith is the layer of loose, weathered rock fragments lying on top of solid bedrock (the unbroken rock beneath).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.3] Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals (the chemical substances the rock is made of).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.4] Chemical weathering changes or dissolves the minerals in the rock, so the rock itself is altered, not just broken.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.5] Biological weathering by plants and animals works through both physical and chemical means.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.b] Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process. It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock (rock with natural cracks called joints).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.1] 2. At night the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.2] 3. By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.3] 4. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular (sharp-edged) fragments.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.4] It is most effective on mountains in upland Ireland, on high mountains and in periglacial areas (cold areas where the ground is frozen for much of the year). Freeze-thaw is active on Irish mountain summits such as Carrauntoohil, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.5] Result: scree (talus) slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields (areas covered in broken angular rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.b] In hot deserts the rock surface swings between hot days and cold nights. Both processes here are physical weathering driven by that heating and cooling, called insolation (heating by the sun).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.0] Exfoliation, or onion weathering, is an insolation process in hot deserts such as the Sahara, where the daily temperature range (the gap between day and night temperature) is large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.1] The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day, and cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.2] Stress builds up, and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion, leaving rounded boulders.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.3] Many geographers stress that some moisture, such as dew, must be present for exfoliation to work effectively.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.4] Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.b] Rock can also break because the forces on it change, or because growing crystals push it apart. Neither process changes the minerals, so both are physical.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.0] Pressure release (also called unloading or sheeting): when the overlying rock is eroded, the weight pressing on buried rock such as granite is removed, so it expands upward and cracks into sheets parallel to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.1] The sheets peel off to leave domes, such as Half Dome, Yosemite, California, USA, a granite dome shaped by sheeting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.2] Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores (tiny holes) and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.3] Salt crystallisation is common in hot deserts and on coasts, where salt water is available and evaporates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.b] Plants and animals break rock down in two ways. Their physical action is a form of physical weathering, and the acids they produce cause chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.0] Physical: plant roots grow into cracks and widen them, and burrowing animals expose fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.1] Chemical: decaying plants produce humic acids, and lichens and mosses release acids that attack rock surfaces.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.b] Carbonation is a chemical weathering process. It dissolves limestone, and it works along joints and bedding planes (the flat surfaces between layers of rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.0] Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.1] The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble, so it is carried away in solution (dissolved in water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.2] Word equation: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.0] Hydrolysis: water reacts with feldspar (a mineral in granite) and turns it into clay (kaolin, or china clay); the quartz grains (another mineral in granite) are left as sand, and the granite crumbles.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.1] China clay from the hydrolysis of granite is quarried around St Austell, Cornwall, England, and on the edge of Dartmoor.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.2] Oxidation: oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron in the rock to form iron oxide (rust), which gives red, yellow or brown colours and weakens the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.3] Hydration: some minerals absorb water and swell, setting up stress in the rock; anhydrite, for example, becomes gypsum.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.b] Weathering does not act at the same speed or in the same way everywhere. Climate decides which type of weathering dominates, and rock type decides how vulnerable the rock is.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.0] Climate: temperature and rainfall decide which type of weathering dominates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.1] Warm, wet climates favour fast chemical weathering because, by Van 't Hoff's rule, the rate of chemical reactions roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.2] Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, and hot deserts favour exfoliation and salt crystallisation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.3] Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.4] Rock type (its mineral composition): limestone is vulnerable to carbonation, granite to hydrolysis, and quartzite (a very hard rock) resists both.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.b] Beyond climate and rock type, four more factors affect how quickly rock weathers. Most work by letting water reach the rock or by exposing fresh rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.0] Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults (breaks in the rock where blocks have moved) let water in, so heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.1] Vegetation and soil cover: roots and humic acids speed weathering, and soil holds moisture against the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.2] Time: older surfaces are more weathered, because weathering has had longer to act.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.3] Relief (the steepness and shape of the land): steep slopes remove debris and expose fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.4] Aspect (the direction a slope faces): shady slopes stay frozen longer, so freeze-thaw is stronger on them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.b] Temperature links the processes above. It matters in two different ways, and the type of weathering that results depends on the temperature pattern of the place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.0] Temperature acts in two ways: as a trigger, through the expansion and contraction that cause stress, and as a control on the rate of chemical reactions.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.1] Freeze-thaw depends on temperature crossing the freezing point, and exfoliation and granular disintegration depend on a large daily range; both need changes in temperature, not just warmth.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.3] Method: name the process and its type, state the temperature condition it needs, explain the stress or rate change it produces, and link this to the result, such as scree from freeze-thaw.

CANDIDATES geo-rivers-s1: 1. Fluvial processes, patterns and associated landforms
[geo-rivers-s1.0.b] Before looking at how rivers shape the land, you need the vocabulary for the parts of a river system. These words are used to label maps and diagrams.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.0] Source: where the river rises, meaning where it begins. The River Shannon, Ireland's longest river, rises at the Shannon Pot, Co. Cavan.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.1] Mouth: where the river ends by entering the sea, a lake or another river, so it is the opposite end from the source.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.2] Tributary: a smaller river that flows into a larger river, so it adds its water to the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.3] Confluence: the point where a tributary joins the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.4] Drainage basin (catchment): the whole area of land drained by a river and its tributaries, so all rain falling in it ends up in that river system.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.5] Watershed: the high ground that separates one drainage basin from the next, so water on either side flows to a different river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.6] Distributary: a channel that leaves the main river and flows away from it, found on deltas (fan-shaped landforms of deposited material at a river mouth).
[geo-rivers-s1.0.7] Estuary: the tidal lower course of a river, where fresh water meets salt water. The Shannon enters the Atlantic through the Shannon Estuary below Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.b] The long profile is the smooth, concave slope of a river from its source to its mouth. Because the slope changes along the way, the river is divided into three courses, and each course has its own landforms.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.0] The gradient (steepness of the river's slope) is steep near the source and gentle near the mouth, which is what makes the profile concave.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.1] Upper (youthful) course: steep, with V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs (ridges that overlap like the teeth of a zip), waterfalls, rapids and potholes.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.2] Middle (mature) course: meanders (looping bends) develop as the valley widens, because the gentler gradient lets the river swing from side to side.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.3] Lower (old) course: a wide floodplain (flat land beside the river), levees (natural banks built up beside the channel), meanders with oxbow lakes (cut-off loops), and a delta or estuary at the mouth.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.b] A river can only erode or carry material if it has energy. How much it has, and where it uses it, explains why the upper course looks so different from the lower course.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.0] A river's energy depends on its volume (discharge, the amount of water flowing past a point) and its gradient.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.1] Energy left after overcoming friction with the bed and banks is used for erosion (wearing away the land) and transport (carrying material, called the load).
[geo-rivers-s1.2.2] Volume increases downstream because tributaries keep joining the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.3] Vertical erosion (deepening the bed) dominates in the upper course, so valleys there are cut downwards.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.4] Lateral erosion (widening the valley sideways) dominates in the middle and lower courses, so the valley floor there becomes wide.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.5] Headward erosion lengthens a valley at its source, cutting back upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.b] Rivers wear away their bed and banks in four ways. Each works differently, and each is a named process you may be asked to explain.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.0] Hydraulic action: the force of moving water dislodges loose material and forces air into cracks in the bed and banks, widening them until rock breaks away.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.1] Abrasion (corrasion): the load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks like sandpaper. Pebbles swirled in eddies (small whirlpools) drill potholes into the bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.2] Attrition: particles of the load collide with each other and with the bed, so they become smaller and more rounded downstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.3] Solution (corrosion): slightly acidic water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone, carrying it away in solution (dissolved in the water).
[geo-rivers-s1.4.b] Once material has been eroded, the river carries it downstream. The method depends on the size of the particles and the speed of the flow.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.0] Traction: the largest boulders are rolled along the bed, mainly during floods when the flow is strongest.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.1] Saltation: pebbles and sand are too heavy to stay in the flow, so they bounce along the bed in a series of hops.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.2] Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried within the flow, giving the water its brown colour.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.3] Solution: dissolved minerals are carried invisibly in the water.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.4] Competence: the largest particle a river can carry. It rises sharply in flood because the faster, bigger flow can move larger particles.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.5] Capacity: the total load a river can carry. It also rises sharply in flood, so a flooded river carries far more material overall.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.b] A river drops its load when it no longer has the energy to carry it. Understanding why it drops it, and in what order, explains the shape of most depositional landforms.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.0] Deposition (dropping of the load) happens when velocity (speed of flow) falls: on entering a lake or the sea, on the inside of a bend, when floodwater recedes, or on a gentler gradient.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.1] Deposition also happens when the load becomes too great for the river's energy to carry, so the excess is dropped even if the flow has not slowed.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.2] Heavier material is deposited first and the finest last, so deposits are sorted by size. This contrasts with glacial till (material dropped by ice), which is unsorted and angular.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.b] A drainage pattern is the arrangement of a river and its tributaries, shaped by the underlying rock and relief (the height and shape of the land). Each pattern tells you something about the rock or land beneath it.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.0] Dendritic: tree-like, with tributaries joining at acute angles (sharp angles); forms on rock of uniform resistance, so the rivers have no reason to favour any direction.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.1] Trellised: tributaries meet the main rivers at right angles; forms where bands of hard and soft rock alternate. In the ridge-and-valley landscape of Munster, rivers follow the soft limestone valleys and tributaries flow off the sandstone ridges at right angles.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.2] Radial: rivers flow outward from a central high point like the spokes of a wheel, because the ground slopes away in every direction from the high point.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.3] Deranged: no clear pattern; forms on poorly drained glacial deposits (material left by ice), e.g. the drumlin belt of Co. Cavan, with many small lakes and marshes between the drumlins.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.4] Centripetal: rivers flow inward to a central lake or basin, e.g. Lough Neagh, into which rivers drain from all sides.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.5] On an Ordnance Survey map, dendritic streams branch like a tree at sharp angles, trellised streams run parallel with tributaries meeting them at right angles, radial streams spread out from a high point, and centripetal streams converge on a lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.b] In the upper course the river is steep and powerful, so it cuts downwards into its bed. This shapes a narrow valley with a V-shaped cross-section.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.0] 1. The steep gradient gives the river high energy, so it erodes vertically by hydraulic action and abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.1] 2. Weathering (breakdown of rock in place) and mass movement (downhill movement of loose material) loosen the valley sides and move material into the channel, widening the top of the valley.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.2] 3. The result is a narrow floor and steep sides in a V-shape, because the river cuts down faster than it widens the valley.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.3] 4. The river swings around areas of harder rock, leaving spurs (ridges of land) that interlock like the teeth of a zip.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.4] Interlocking spurs are fluvial (made by rivers) and are not to be confused with truncated spurs, which are glacial.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.5] On an Ordnance Survey map, the contours (lines joining points of equal height) form a V that points upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.6] Example: the upper course of the River Liffey in the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.7] Diagram: cross-section of a V-shaped valley; plan view of interlocking spurs with the river winding between them.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.b] A waterfall forms where a river crosses rock of different hardness. Two erosion processes, hydraulic action and abrasion, do the work in the undercutting and plunge-pool stages.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.0] 1. A waterfall forms where a band of resistant rock (rock that wears away slowly) lies over less resistant rock.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.1] 2. Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft rock below faster, undercutting the hard rock and leaving an overhang.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.2] 3. Falling water and its load scour a deep plunge pool at the base by hydraulic action and abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.3] 4. The unsupported overhang collapses, and the waterfall retreats upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.4] 5. Repeated retreat leaves a steep-sided gorge downstream of the falls, because the river has cut back through the rock the falls once occupied.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.5] Example: Niagara Falls, USA-Canada border, with a resistant cap of dolomite over weaker shale; the retreat of the falls has cut the Niagara Gorge below them.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.6] Irish waterfalls: Powerscourt Waterfall, River Dargle, Co. Wicklow, Ireland's highest waterfall at about 121 m; and Torc Waterfall, near Killarney, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.7] Diagram: cross-section showing hard rock cap, soft rock beneath, overhang, plunge pool and direction of retreat.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.b] Rapids and potholes are smaller features of the steep upper course, both made by erosion of the river bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.0] 1. Rapids form where alternating bands of hard and soft rock lie across the river bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.1] 2. Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft bands faster, leaving the hard bands standing as steps.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.2] 3. Fast, turbulent water tumbles over the steps, so the surface is broken and white, forming rapids.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.3] Potholes: hollows drilled into the bed by pebbles swirled in eddies, the grinding action of abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.b] A meander is a looping bend in a river. It is formed by both erosion and deposition, so it is classified under both.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.0] 1. Water flows fastest on the outside of a bend, where it erodes the bank by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a steep river cliff.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.1] 2. Water is slowest on the inside of the bend, where it deposits sand and gravel, forming a gently sloping point bar (slip-off slope).
[geo-rivers-s1.10.2] 3. Helicoidal (corkscrew-shaped) flow carries eroded material from the outer bank to the inner bank of the next bend.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.3] 4. Continued lateral erosion makes the bends more pronounced, so that only a narrow neck of land eventually separates them.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.4] Example: meanders on the River Shannon's floodplain, the Shannon Callows, between Athlone and Portumna.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.5] Diagram: plan view of a meander with the fastest-flow line, river cliff and point bar.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.b] If meanders keep growing, the loops can eventually be cut off from the river. This leaves a lake that later disappears.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.0] 1. During a flood the river cuts through the neck of a meander and takes the straighter, shorter course.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.1] 2. Deposition seals off the old bend, so the water left behind forms a crescent-shaped oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.2] 3. The oxbow lake slowly fills with silt and becomes a meander scar (a dry, curved hollow where the loop used to be).
[geo-rivers-s1.11.3] Example: oxbow lakes on the lower Mississippi, USA.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.4] Diagram: plan sequence showing the cut-off and the formation of the oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.b] Floodplains and levees are built by deposition during floods. They are among the main depositional landforms of the lower course.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.0] Lateral erosion by migrating meanders widens the valley floor, so there is a broad flat area for floodwater to spread over.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.1] When the river floods, it spreads across the valley, loses velocity and deposits a layer of fine silt called alluvium.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.2] Repeated floods build a wide, flat, fertile floodplain; on an Ordnance Survey map, widely spaced contours show it. Example: the Shannon Callows, a wide floodplain of seasonally flooded meadows.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.3] Levees: the coarsest material is dropped first, right beside the channel, because the water slows as soon as it leaves it. Each flood therefore raises the banks a little.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.4] Over time these build into natural embankments called levees, and the river bed itself may rise above the level of the floodplain.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.5] Example: levees along the lower Mississippi, USA, which have been artificially raised for flood control.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.6] Diagram: cross-section of a floodplain showing alluvium layers, levees and the raised channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.b] A delta is a landform of deposition at a river mouth. It builds up when the river drops its load faster than the sea or lake can remove it.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.0] A delta forms where a river carrying a heavy load enters a sea or lake and its velocity drops sharply.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.1] In salt water, fine clay particles clump together (flocculation) and sink faster.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.2] Deposits build up until they block the channel, forcing the river to split into smaller channels called distributaries.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.3] Conditions needed: a large load, and calm water with a small tidal range and weak currents, so that deposits are not removed.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.b] A delta is built up in layers, and deltas take different shapes depending on how the deposits spread. You should know the layers and the named types.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.0] Topset beds: flat, coarser deposits laid down on top of the delta, because the heavier material drops first.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.1] Foreset beds: steeply sloping deposits at the front of the delta, built out into the water so the delta grows forward.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.2] Bottomset beds: fine, flat layers laid down beyond the front of the delta, because the finest material is carried furthest.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.3] Arcuate (fan-shaped) delta, e.g. the Nile delta, Egypt.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.4] Bird's-foot delta: long fingers of deposits along distributaries, e.g. the Mississippi delta, USA.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.5] Estuarine delta: fills a drowned river mouth (a river mouth flooded by the sea).
[geo-rivers-s1.14.6] Lake delta, e.g. the delta between the Upper and Lower Lakes at Glendalough, Co. Wicklow, which divided what was once a single lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.7] Diagram: plan view of a delta with distributaries; cross-section showing topset, foreset and bottomset beds.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.b] Two more depositional features occur near the mouth, or where the load is large: the estuary and the braided channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.0] Estuary: in the tidal river mouth, fresh and salt water meet, and deposition builds mudflats and salt marsh.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.1] Example: the Shannon Estuary below Limerick, where the Shannon enters the Atlantic.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.2] Braided channel: the river splits into several channels around islands of deposited sand and gravel, called eyots or braid bars.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.3] Braided channels form where the load is large and discharge varies, so the river cannot carry all its sediment and drops it as islands in the channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.b] Every fluvial landform is formed by erosion, by deposition, or by both. Sorting them this way is a skill that exams test.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.0] Erosion: V-shaped valley, interlocking spurs, waterfall, gorge, potholes, rapids.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.1] Erosion and deposition: meander, oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.2] Deposition: floodplain, levee, delta, point bar, estuary mudflats.
