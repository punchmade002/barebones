TARGET geo-economic-environment-s5: 5. Conflicts between economic interests and environmental interests
[geo-economic-environment-s5.0.b] Land, sea and resources can be used to make money or can be protected, and often they cannot do both at once. A conflict starts when people with economic interests and people with environmental interests want different things from the same place.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.0.0] Economic interests are jobs, profit, energy supply, tax revenue, regional development (bringing investment to a poorer area) and infrastructure (roads, pipelines, power lines and similar). These are the reasons a project is proposed.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.0.1] Environmental interests are habitats, landscape, heritage (historic and archaeological sites), water and air quality, health, safety and the climate. These are the reasons people object.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.0.2] Interests conflict at local, national and global scales: a local community may oppose a project that the State or the world supports, or the reverse.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.1.b] Each conflict has several groups, called stakeholders (people or bodies affected by a decision), and they rarely agree. Knowing who they are lets you explain who wants what.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.1.0] Developers and companies want to build or extract, and government and State agencies decide, regulate or sometimes carry out the project themselves.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.1.1] Local communities are often divided among themselves, because some residents gain jobs or income while others lose quiet, safety or their way of life.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.1.2] Environmental groups, farmers and fishermen, tourists, the EU and the courts are also involved. Farmers and fishermen depend on the land or sea, and the EU and the courts set and enforce the rules.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.2.b] Conflicts are settled through legal and planning systems, through payment, or through a deal that gives each side part of what it wants.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.2.0] Planning permission (official approval to build) is needed for most developments, and decisions can be appealed to An Coimisiún Pleanála (formerly An Bord Pleanála), the national planning appeals body.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.2.1] An environmental impact assessment (a study of a project's likely effects on the environment) is carried out before approval, and court cases follow where decisions are challenged.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.2.2] EU law, such as the Habitats and Birds Directives, protects habitats and species, so a member state must protect them even when it would prefer to develop.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.2.3] Compensation can be paid, such as payments to turf cutters or to fishermen who give up fishing, to make a restriction acceptable to those who lose by it.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.2.4] Compromise includes route changes, limits on activity, conditions attached to permission and sustainable management (using a resource so that it lasts).
[geo-economic-environment-s5.3.b] The Corrib gas project is a national example of a conflict. Gas from a field under the sea promised energy and money, but bringing it ashore caused a long local dispute.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.3.0] The Corrib gas field lies about 80 km off north-west Co. Mayo and was discovered in 1996.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.3.1] Gas first flowed in December 2015 and, so that it could be used, was processed at the Bellanaboy terminal (the onshore plant that treats the gas); for a time it supplied over half of Ireland's gas.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.3.2] Economic arguments were energy security (less reliance on imported gas), a domestic gas supply, revenue for the State and the companies, and construction jobs.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.4.b] Opposition came from how and where the gas would be brought ashore, not from the field itself.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.4.0] The original plan piped high-pressure, unprocessed gas onshore near homes at Rossport, creating a safety risk for residents because raw gas at high pressure is dangerous close to houses.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.4.1] The Shell to Sea campaign, from 2005, argued that the gas should be processed at sea rather than onshore, which would keep the hazardous raw gas away from people.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.4.2] Sruwaddacon Bay, a protected estuary (a Special Area of Conservation, a site protected under EU law for its habitats), lay on the route of the pipeline.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.5.b] The dispute escalated into jailings and years of protest before the project was altered and completed.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.5.0] The Rossport Five were jailed in June 2005 for refusing to obey a court order not to obstruct pipeline work; they were released after 94 days.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.5.1] Protests were policed heavily for years, and the dispute divided the local community, because some residents backed the project and others opposed it.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.5.2] The onshore route was changed to keep the pipeline further from houses, and a tunnel was bored under Sruwaddacon Bay to carry it, so the protected estuary was not dug up.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.5.3] Shell sold its share in the project in 2018.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.6.b] Fish in Irish waters are a shared resource. Fishing communities want a bigger catch, while conservation rules limit how much can be taken.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.6.0] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland's largest fishing port, so it matters most for jobs; the six State Fishery Harbour Centres are Killybegs, Castletownbere, Dingle, Rossaveel, Dunmore East and Howth. Coastal jobs depend on these harbours.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.6.1] Under the Common Fisheries Policy (1983), total allowable catches (the maximum amount of each species that may be caught) are set by the EU and divided into national quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.6.2] Irish fishermen have long argued that Ireland's share of the catch in its own rich waters is too small, and much of that catch is taken by other EU fleets.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.6.3] Ireland's exclusive economic zone (the sea area where the State has the rights to the resources) extends 200 nautical miles from the coast.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.7.b] Political change after Brexit (the UK leaving the EU) shrank what Irish boats could catch, which hurt fishing incomes.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.7.0] The EU-UK Brexit trade agreement (December 2020) reduced Irish quotas, so Irish boats were allowed to catch less.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.7.1] A scheme paid vessel owners to decommission (scrap) their boats, which cut the size of the fleet to match the smaller quota.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.8.b] When wild salmon numbers collapsed, the State banned a fishing method, which set commercial fishermen against anglers and conservationists.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.8.0] Ireland banned drift-net fishing (fishing with nets left floating in the open sea) for wild salmon at sea from 2007, because wild salmon returning to Irish rivers had fallen sharply.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.8.1] Commercial salmon fishermen received compensation, so that they were not left without income, for losing their livelihood.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.8.2] Anglers (people who fish with rods) and conservation groups supported the ban, because wild salmon sustain angling tourism.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.9.b] Fish stocks cross borders and farms share water with wild fish, so conflicts reach beyond Ireland and affect the sea and the bays.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.9.0] From 2010 Iceland and the Faroe Islands set large mackerel quotas as mackerel moved north-west into their waters, taking a bigger share of a stock Irish boats also fish.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.9.1] The EU, with strong support from Irish fishermen, protested; in 2013 it imposed trade measures on the Faroe Islands over its herring catches, banning imports of Faroese herring and mackerel; the dispute eased in 2014.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.9.2] Salmon farms in western bays are blamed by anglers for harming wild salmon and sea trout, because sea lice (parasites that attach to fish) from the farms spread to wild fish.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.10.b] Mullaghmore is a main national case of tourism against a fragile landscape. A visitor centre was meant to help tourism but was planned in a place valued for being unspoilt.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.10.0] The Burren, Co. Clare, has Carboniferous limestone pavement (a bare, flat surface of limestone), with rare Arctic-alpine and Mediterranean plants growing side by side, such as spring gentian and mountain avens.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.10.1] Mullaghmore is a hill of folded, terraced limestone; the Burren National Park protects the area near it.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.10.2] In the early 1990s the Office of Public Works (OPW, the State body that manages heritage sites and public buildings) began building an interpretative centre (a visitor centre with displays) and car park near Mullaghmore.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.11.b] The two sides disagreed on whether a visitor centre at Mullaghmore would help or harm the Burren, because one side focused on jobs and income and the other on the fragile landscape.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.11.0] Supporters wanted tourism jobs and visitor spending in a poor rural area, and better visitor management, which means directing visitors to one place instead of leaving them to wander.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.11.1] The Burren Action Group argued that traffic, sewage and visitor pressure would damage a fragile, unspoilt landscape, and that any centre should be in nearby villages.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.12.b] The case was settled in the courts and the planning system, so it shows how legal challenge can stop a State project.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.12.0] In 1993 the Supreme Court (Ireland's highest court) ruled that the State had to obtain planning permission like any other developer, so the State was not exempt from the planning rules, and work stopped.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.12.1] In 2000 An Bord Pleanála refused permission, and the partly built site was cleared and restored.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.13.b] Skellig Michael is a remote island of great heritage and wildlife value, which is why increased tourism there is a problem.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.13.0] Skellig Michael, about 12 km off the Iveragh Peninsula, Co. Kerry, has an early Christian monastery of stone beehive huts on a steep rocky island.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.13.1] It has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site (a place recognised by the United Nations as being of outstanding value to humanity) since 1996 and is managed by the OPW.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.13.2] Seabirds, including puffins, Manx shearwaters and storm petrels, breed on the island; Little Skellig holds one of the world's largest gannet colonies.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.14.b] Film fame brought more demand for visits than a fragile island can easily take, so access is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.14.0] Filming for the Star Wars films (from 2014) greatly increased demand for visits.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.14.1] Boat operators and local businesses want more access, because visitors bring them income; conservationists object to the filming and to rising visitor numbers.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.14.2] Access is only by licensed boats in summer, with visitor numbers capped at about 180 a day; landings depend on the weather.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.14.3] Steep steps and the weather make landing a safety issue, which is why landings are limited.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.15.b] The Hill of Tara is a heritage site of national importance, and the land around it is rich in archaeology. This is the heritage side of the conflict over the M3 motorway.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.15.0] The Hill of Tara, Co. Meath, is an ancient ceremonial site and the traditional seat of the High Kings of Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.15.1] The Tara-Skryne valley around the hill is an archaeological landscape (an area where the sites and the land together tell the story of the past); excavation along the planned road route found dozens of sites.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.16.b] The road was meant to solve a transport problem for fast-growing towns, and the best route crossed an important archaeological area.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.16.0] The M3 runs from Clonee to north of Kells, relieving congestion on the N3 for the fast-growing commuter towns of Meath (towns whose residents travel to work elsewhere), such as Navan and Kells.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.16.1] It cuts journey times to Dublin, so commuters save time, which helps regional access and commuters.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.16.2] The route passes through the Tara-Skryne valley, about 2 km from the Hill of Tara.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.17.b] Opponents said the road would destroy archaeology. The road was built, but only after a major find was recorded.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.17.0] Campaign groups, archaeologists and protesters camped on the route argued that the road would destroy an archaeological landscape.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.17.1] In 2007 a large prehistoric ceremonial enclosure was found at Lismullin and declared a national monument; it was excavated and recorded, and then the road was built over it.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.17.2] The M3 opened in 2010, so the road was completed despite the protests.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.18.b] Turf (dried peat cut from bogs) is a traditional fuel, but the bogs it comes from are rare habitats protected by EU law.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.18.0] Turf cutting gives rural families a traditional right and a cheap source of fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.18.1] Raised bogs are rare in Europe and store large amounts of carbon; Ireland holds a large share of the EU's remaining active raised bog (bog that is still growing).
[geo-economic-environment-s5.18.2] The EU Habitats Directive (1992) required Ireland to designate raised bogs as Special Areas of Conservation (SACs), sites protected for their habitats.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.19.b] The ban took away a traditional way of life, so the State offered compensation. Even so, some people protested.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.19.0] After a ten-year derogation (a temporary exemption from the rules) ended, turf cutting was banned on 53 raised-bog SACs from 2011.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.19.1] Compensation: about €1,500 a year for 15 years per turf cutter, or relocation to a bog that is not protected.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.19.2] The Turf Cutters and Contractors Association organised protests, because the ban ended a traditional right, and some cutting continued on some bogs.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.20.b] Wind power helps with climate targets, but turbines are built in places where people live and wildlife lives, so wind farms cause conflict at local and national level.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.20.0] Economic and renewable case: renewable energy targets, income for landowners and community funds. The Climate Action Plan aims for 80% renewable electricity by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.20.1] Local objections: visual impact, noise, shadow flicker (the flickering shadow cast by rotating blades), falling property values, effects on birds, and peat stability.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.20.2] Derrybrien, Slieve Aughty Mountains, Co. Galway: a peat slide of about 450,000 m³ on 16 October 2003, during wind-farm construction, killed fish in the Owendalulleegh River.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.20.3] In 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland €5 million plus €15,000 a day because no proper environmental impact assessment (a study of effects before approval) had been carried out.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.20.4] Offshore wind: the Climate Action Plan sets at least 5 GW (gigawatts, a measure of generating capacity) of offshore wind, which can conflict with fishing grounds and seascapes.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.21.b] Ending milk quotas let farmers produce more milk. The economic gain came with costs for rivers and for the climate.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.21.0] Milk quotas (EU limits on how much milk each farmer could produce) were abolished on 1 April 2015, and the dairy herd grew strongly afterwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.21.1] Economic case: farm incomes and food exports, because more milk means more to sell.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.21.2] Environmental case: nitrates (chemicals from fertiliser and slurry) reaching water damage water quality; agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.21.3] EPA (the Environmental Protection Agency, 2016-2021): only about half of Irish rivers, lakes and estuaries were in good or high ecological status (a measure of water health), with agriculture the main pressure.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.21.4] Sectoral ceilings (limits on emissions set for each sector of the economy, 2022) require agriculture to cut emissions by 25% by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.22.b] Data centres (buildings full of computers that store and process online data) bring investment, but they use so much electricity that they conflict with climate goals.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.22.0] Economic case: investment, multinational jobs and Ireland's technology economy, because the centres bring in big companies.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.22.1] Data centres used about a fifth of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office), and most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.22.2] Environmental and grid case: huge electricity demand strains the grid (the national network that carries electricity) and works against climate targets.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.22.3] In 2022 EirGrid (the operator of the electricity grid) said it would not connect new data centres in the Dublin area until about 2028, because the grid could not supply them.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.23.b] The Amazon is the main global example. Brazil's economic activities clear the forest for farming, timber, mining, roads and power.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.23.0] The Amazon is the world's largest tropical rainforest, with about 60% of it in Brazil.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.23.1] Cattle ranching is the main driver of clearing, and most cleared land becomes pasture; soya farming spreads in the south.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.23.2] Logging removes timber from the forest, so the trees are cut for sale, for example.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.23.3] The Trans-Amazonian Highway, begun in 1970, opened the forest to settlers; clearings along the roads form a fishbone pattern (a central road with side tracks branching off), as in Rondônia.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.23.4] Carajás, Pará, has the world's largest iron-ore mine, run by Vale.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.23.5] The Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River, completed in 2019, was opposed by indigenous groups including the Kayapó.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.24.b] The forest matters to people far from Brazil, so protecting it is a global interest as well as a Brazilian one.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.24.0] The forest holds biodiversity (a huge variety of species), stores large amounts of carbon, recycles rainfall and is home to indigenous peoples.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.24.1] Global interest: clearing the forest releases carbon that adds to climate change, while Brazil's development needs drive clearing.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.25.b] Satellite figures show how clearing fell when protection was strong and rose when it weakened. These are rounded figures for the Brazilian Amazon only.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.25.0] Satellite monitoring by PRODES (Brazil's official programme for measuring deforestation): about 27,800 km² of the Brazilian Amazon was cleared in 2004.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.25.1] Clearing was cut by over 80%, to about 4,600 km², by 2012, showing that protection can work.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.25.2] Clearing rose again to about 13,000 km² in 2021, so the gains were not permanent.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.26.b] Brazil and other countries have tried several ways to slow clearing, but results have depended on which government is in power.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.26.0] Brazil's Forest Code, a law to protect the forest, requires landowners in the Amazon to keep 80% of their land as forest, though this is often not enforced.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.26.1] The soy moratorium (an agreed pause) of 2006: major traders agreed not to buy soya from newly cleared Amazon land.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.26.2] The Amazon Fund of 2008: donors, mainly Norway and also Germany, pay for reduced deforestation.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.26.3] Deforestation rose under President Bolsonaro (2019-2022) and fell under President Lula (2003-2010, and again from 2023), showing that government policy matters.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.27.b] The Amazon conflict was also a conflict between people, sometimes with deadly results.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.27.0] Chico Mendes, a rubber tapper (a worker who collects rubber from trees without felling them) and union leader who campaigned to protect the forest, was murdered at Xapuri, Acre, on 22 December 1988.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.27.1] His murder shows that the conflict over clearing was one between people, not only between economic and environmental interests.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.28.b] Germany is a European example of a global conflict: mining a fuel that heats the climate against protecting a forest and villages.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.28.0] RWE's Hambach open-cast lignite mine (a surface mine for lignite, a soft brown coal) in North Rhine-Westphalia expands into the ancient Hambach Forest.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.28.1] Economic side: the mine supplies power and jobs in the Rhineland. Environmental side: the forest, the villages and the carbon released by burning lignite.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.28.2] Protesters occupied tree houses in the forest for years, to stop the trees being felled; in October 2018 a German court halted the clearing.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.28.3] The remaining forest was later spared under the coal phase-out (the planned ending of coal use).
[geo-economic-environment-s5.29.b] Even after the Hambach ruling, mining continued elsewhere, until national law set an end date for coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.29.0] The village of Lützerath was cleared of protesters in January 2023 to extend the Garzweiler lignite mine.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.29.1] Germany's 2020 law ends coal-fired power by 2038 at the latest, so the mines have a fixed end date.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.30.b] The Three Gorges Dam is a huge project that shows the economic case for damming a river.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.30.0] The Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze River is the world's largest power station by installed capacity (the most electricity it can generate).
[geo-economic-environment-s5.30.1] It provides clean electricity, flood control downstream and improved navigation (easier passage for ships).
[geo-economic-environment-s5.30.2] The dam was completed in 2006 and became fully operational in 2012.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.31.b] The same dam brought heavy costs for people, heritage and the river itself.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.31.0] Over one million people were relocated, because the reservoir flooded their homes.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.31.1] Farmland, towns and archaeological sites were flooded by the reservoir (the lake formed behind the dam).
[geo-economic-environment-s5.31.2] The dam traps sediment that would otherwise reach the river downstream, and the ecology of the river has changed.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.32.b] The conflict between economy and environment also shows up between countries, in the argument over who should cut emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.32.0] Developing countries argue for their right to develop with cheap energy, while the global need is to cut emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.32.1] Fossil-fuel exporting countries protect their revenue from coal, oil and gas, which they would lose if the world used less of them.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.32.2] Belém, Pará, hosted the COP30 climate conference in November 2025.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.33.b] On an Ordnance Survey map question you are asked to show how conflict could arise from where a development is placed. Work through the same steps each time.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.33.0] Identify the development and the features of the site it needs, such as a wind farm on high ground with open views, or a quarry close to a village.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.33.1] Read the map for relevant features, because they show who would be affected: contour lines for height, settlements, roads for access, rivers, and protected areas.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.33.2] Identify the groups for and against: developers and landowners usually support a site, while residents, environmental groups and tourism businesses may object.
[geo-economic-environment-s5.33.3] Conflict arises when a site serves one group's interests while threatening another group's interests; give the reasons for each side.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s4: 4. Sustainable economic development
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.b] Economic activity such as farming, fishing, mining and industry always affects the environment. Sustainable development is the framework within which that environmental impact is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.0] The Brundtland Report, "Our Common Future" (UN World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987), defined it as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.1] It has three pillars: environmental protection, economic growth and social fairness. Sustainable development balances all three rather than maximising one, so a plan that boosts profit but wrecks the environment is not sustainable.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.b] Five working rules turn the definition into practice. Each one protects the environment so that it can keep supporting people in the future.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.0] Renewable resources (ones that regrow or refill naturally) such as fish, forests and water should be used no faster than they regenerate, otherwise they are used up.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.1] Non-renewable resources (ones that cannot be replaced once used) should be used efficiently and recycled, so that they last longer.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.2] Pollution should be kept within what the environment can absorb, so that air, water and soil are not overloaded.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.3] The polluter pays: those who cause environmental damage bear the cost of it, which gives them a reason to cause less.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.4] The precautionary principle: act to prevent serious harm even before the harm is proven, because waiting for proof may be too late.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.b] Sustainable development became an international aim, with world meetings setting out plans for governments to follow.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.0] The Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro (1992) was a later, separate event from the Brundtland Report. It produced Agenda 21, an action plan for sustainable development.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.1] The UN Sustainable Development Goals were adopted in 2015: 17 goals with a target year of 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.b] In Ireland, the main body that controls the environmental impact of industry is the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). It was created by law and works independently of the government departments it advises.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.0] It was set up under the Environmental Protection Agency Act 1992, and the EPA began work in 1993.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.1] It is an independent public body (a State organisation not run by a government minister) with headquarters at Johnstown Castle, Co. Wexford.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.b] The EPA's most important power is licensing. Activities with a high risk of pollution may not operate unless the EPA allows it and sets the conditions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.0] Large industries need an EPA licence: pharmaceutical and chemical plants, power stations, intensive pig and poultry farms, cement works and mines. Waste facilities also need one.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.1] The licence sets emission limits for the activity, which means the maximum pollution it may release into air, water or land.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.2] Integrated pollution control means one licence covers air, water, waste and noise, instead of separate permits for each. This lets the EPA look at the whole impact of a site at once.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.b] To control pollution, the EPA first has to measure it. It tracks the state of the environment and reports what it finds.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.0] The EPA monitors water quality in rivers, lakes, estuaries (where rivers meet the sea), groundwater (water held underground in rock and soil) and bathing waters.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.1] It runs a national air quality network and produces a greenhouse gas inventory (a count of emissions of gases that cause climate change) and projections of future emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.2] Drinking water is supplied by Uisce Éireann.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.3] The State of the Environment report is published every four years, so the public and government can see whether things are improving.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.4] The EPA advises government and the public and funds environmental research.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.5] The EPA took over radiological protection in 2014, including the control of radon (a natural radioactive gas that can seep up from the ground).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.b] A licence is only useful if it is obeyed, so the EPA checks and punishes breaches.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.0] Inspections check that licensed activities keep within their emission limits.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.1] The Office of Environmental Enforcement, set up within the EPA in 2003, prosecutes polluters (takes them to court).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.2] The EPA oversees the environmental work carried out by local authorities (the county and city councils).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.b] The EPA cannot solve every problem alone. Some pollution is hard to see, track or assign to one source.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.0] The EPA cannot inspect everywhere, so some breaches go unchecked.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.1] Diffuse pollution (pollution that comes from many scattered sources rather than one pipe or chimney) from thousands of farms is hard to police.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.2] Local authorities and other bodies share responsibility for environmental control, so the EPA is one part of a wider system.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.b] Control is best done before a project starts. An environmental impact assessment is the study that looks at a project's likely effects in advance.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.0] An environmental impact assessment is a study, required before planning permission is decided for large projects, that predicts a project's effects on the environment and how to reduce them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.1] The developer's report (the developer is the company or body proposing the project) was called an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS). It is now an Environmental Impact Assessment Report (EIAR).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.b] Environmental impact assessment is not optional for big projects. It comes from EU law and is built into Irish planning rules.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.0] The EU Environmental Impact Assessment Directive, 1985, is applied in Irish planning law.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.1] Assessment is required for projects above set sizes, such as motorways, power stations, wind farms, mines and quarries, large intensive farms, pipelines, ports and airports. Small projects below the set sizes do not need one.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.b] An EIAR has to describe the project, the place it will be built and every kind of effect it could have, then say how harm will be reduced.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.0] A description of the project and of the existing environment (the baseline, meaning the condition of the site before anything is built, which later effects are measured against).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.1] Likely effects on people, plants and animals, soil, water, air, climate, landscape, archaeology and heritage, traffic and noise.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.2] Alternatives considered, such as other designs or sites, and the mitigation measures (steps to reduce or prevent harm) proposed to reduce effects.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.3] A non-technical summary for the public, so ordinary people can understand the report.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.4] The developer writes the report, often with consultants. Neither the EPA nor the planning authority writes it.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.b] The assessment follows a set order. It ends with a decision-maker who weighs the report against what the public and experts say.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.0] 1. The developer prepares the EIAR.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.1] 2. The public and bodies such as the National Parks and Wildlife Service make submissions (written comments and objections).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.2] 3. The planning authority or An Coimisiún Pleanála decides whether to grant permission, often with conditions. The board was renamed in 2025 from An Bord Pleanála, the national planning appeals board.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.b] Short questions ask why environmental impact studies are needed. There are four reasons, and Ireland's experience at Derrybrien shows what happens when the law is not followed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.0] They prevent damage before it happens, which is cheaper than paying for clean-up afterwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.1] They require developers to consider alternatives, so a less harmful design or site may be chosen.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.2] They give the public a say in decisions, through submissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.3] They are a legal requirement.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.4] At Derrybrien, Co. Galway, a peat slide on 16 October 2003 occurred during wind-farm construction. In 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland a lump sum of €5 million plus €15,000 a day because no proper environmental impact assessment had been carried out.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.b] Fish are a renewable resource, but only if people leave enough of them to breed. This idea is the basis of sustainable fishing.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.0] Fish are renewable only while catches stay below the rate at which the stock (the population of one kind of fish) breeds and grows.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.1] The maximum sustainable yield is the largest catch that can be taken year after year without reducing the stock.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.b] Overfishing means taking fish faster than stocks can replace themselves. Several causes push fleets to do this.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.0] Modern technology, including sonar (finds fish using sound), satellite navigation, factory ships (which process the catch at sea) and huge nets, lets fleets find and take fish faster than stocks can recover.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.1] Too many boats chase the same stock, so the catch is shared out among more boats and each one fishes harder.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.2] Subsidies (payments from government) keep fleets fishing when stocks are falling.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.3] Young fish are caught before they have had the chance to breed, so the stock cannot renew itself.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.4] Discards: dead or unwanted fish are thrown back into the sea, which wastes the catch.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.5] Illegal fishing takes fish outside the quota system (the limits on how much each country or boat may catch).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.b] The Grand Banks is the classic example of a fishery destroyed by overfishing. It shows how both fish stocks and jobs can be lost.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.0] The Grand Banks, off Newfoundland, Canada, were one of the world's richest cod fisheries for centuries.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.1] Catches rose sharply with factory trawlers (large boats that fish with nets and process the catch on board) from the 1960s, and the stock collapsed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.2] Canada declared a moratorium (ban) on northern cod fishing on 2 July 1992.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.3] About 30,000 people in Newfoundland lost their jobs, the largest single lay-off in Canadian history.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.4] The stock has recovered only slowly, and partly, in the decades since.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.b] North Sea herring is a second example, with a happier ending. It shows that a stock can recover when fishing is stopped and then tightly limited.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.0] North Sea herring was overfished in the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.1] The fishery was closed from 1977 to the early 1980s, which gave the stock time to breed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.2] The stock recovered after the fishery reopened under strict quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.b] In EU waters, fish stocks are managed through the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP). Here it matters as a conservation tool that tries to keep catches sustainable.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.0] The EU Common Fisheries Policy, 1983, sets total allowable catches (TACs, the maximum amount of each stock that may be caught) and divides them into national quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.1] Conservation rules include minimum mesh sizes (so small fish escape the net), minimum landing sizes, closed areas and seasons, and vessel decommissioning (scrapping boats to reduce fleet size).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.2] The CFP reform of 2013 aims to fish at maximum sustainable yield and introduces a landing obligation that ends discards, phased in from 2015 to 2019.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.3] Monitoring uses satellite tracking, the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority (set up in 2007) and fishery protection patrols by the Irish Naval Service.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.4] Ireland's exclusive economic zone (the sea area over which a country controls resources) extends 200 nautical miles from the coast, within which these rules apply.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.5] Further measures include marine protected areas (areas of sea where fishing is restricted) and aquaculture (fish farming) as an alternative to wild catch.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.b] Fishing matters to Irish coastal communities, which depend on the fish stocks around the coast for their living.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.0] Mackerel is the most valuable Irish catch, landed mainly at Killybegs, Co. Donegal, Ireland's largest fishing port.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.1] Killybegs is the base of the pelagic fleet (boats that fish species living in open water, not on the seabed) that catches mackerel and herring.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.2] Ireland has six State Fishery Harbour Centres: Killybegs, Castletownbere, Dingle, Rossaveel, Dunmore East and Howth.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.3] Irish fishing communities depend on these stocks, so conservation limits cause hardship. There is more on this in Conflicts between economic interests and environmental interests.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.b] Mining that happened before modern controls can go on polluting for generations. Avoca is the Irish example.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.0] Copper mining at Avoca, Co. Wicklow, over centuries left acid mine drainage (acidic water that runs out of old mine workings) when mining ended.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.1] The acid drainage still discolours and pollutes the Avoca River long after the mines closed, which shows why closure plans are needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.b] Today a mine has to work within licence rules. Tara Mines shows what controlled mining looks like.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath, is an underground zinc and lead mine, Europe's largest zinc mine, producing since 1977 and owned by Boliden.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.1] It operates under an EPA licence, which sets limits on its emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.2] Tailings (the waste left after the metal has been extracted) are stored in a lined tailings facility, so they do not leak into the ground, and the water is monitored.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.3] Restoration plans are in place for the site.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.b] A responsible mine plans for its end before it opens. Lisheen shows rehabilitation (restoring a site to a safe, useful condition) after closure.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.0] Lisheen, Co. Tipperary, a zinc and lead mine, closed in 2015 and the site has been rehabilitated.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.1] A new mine needs an environmental impact assessment report (EIAR) and a closure and restoration plan backed by a financial bond (money set aside in advance) before it opens, so the clean-up is paid for even if the company fails.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.b] Peat was long treated as a fuel to be dug out. The scale of that harvesting damaged Ireland's bogs.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.0] Bord na Móna, founded in 1946, carried out large-scale industrial harvesting of peat for power stations and fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.1] Harvesting removed raised bogs (domed peatlands formed over thousands of years), released the carbon they had stored and destroyed their habitats.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.b] Peat harvesting has ended, and the focus has moved to repairing the damage and finding new uses for the land.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.0] The Lanesborough and Shannonbridge peat stations closed in December 2020, and Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.1] Cutaway bogs (bogs where the peat has been removed) are being rehabilitated and rewetted, meaning water is returned to them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.2] Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly: a cutaway bog turned into parkland, wetlands and trails.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.3] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo: the peat station closed in 2003 and wind farms now stand on the cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.4] A just transition (a change that is fair to the workers affected) is needed for Midland workers who lost jobs in peat.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.b] The Aral Sea shows how diverting water for farming can destroy a whole environment and the livelihoods that depended on it.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.0] The Amu Darya and Syr Darya, which feed the Aral Sea in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, were diverted from the 1960s to irrigate (water by channels) cotton in the Soviet Union.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.1] The sea shrank to a fraction of its size and split into separate lakes, and its salinity (salt content) rose.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.2] The fishing industry collapsed, and ports such as Moynaq (Uzbekistan) were left stranded far from the water.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.3] Dust storms spread salt and pesticides from the exposed seabed, harming local health.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.4] The Kok-Aral dam (Kazakhstan, 2005) raised the level of the small northern sea, and fishing there has partly revived.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.25.b] Burning fossil fuels pollutes the air and adds to climate change, so a sustainable approach changes the energy source.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.25.0] Switching to renewable energy (energy from sources that do not run out, such as wind) reduces the environmental impact of power generation. There is more on this in Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.b] Farming can be paid and encouraged to protect the environment, not just to produce food.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.0] Agri-environment schemes pay farmers for conservation: REPS (Rural Environment Protection Scheme, 1994), now succeeded by ACRES.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.1] Origin Green, Bord Bia's sustainability programme for Irish food and drink, was launched in 2012.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.2] Nitrates rules limit the amount of nitrogen fertiliser and manure that farms can apply, because the excess can run off and pollute water.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.3] BurrenLIFE (2005 to 2010) paid Burren farmers to manage species-rich limestone grassland through winter grazing (winterage); it continued as the Burren Programme.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.4] Results-based farming in the Burren rewards farmers for measured environmental outcomes, not just for following set practices.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.b] Forests and tourist sites can be used without being destroyed if how they are used is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.0] Forests are replanted after felling, using mixed native broadleaves (a variety of native trees such as oak, rather than one species).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.1] Forests are not planted on deep peat.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.2] Visitor limits and ecotourism (tourism designed to protect nature) reduce pressure on fragile sites.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.3] At Skellig Michael, Co. Kerry, access is controlled through licensed boats and visitor limits.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.b] The best way to deal with waste is to create less of it, which is why there is an order of priority.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.0] Reduce, reuse and recycle, in that order of priority.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.1] Levies (charges) and deposit schemes (a refundable payment on containers) give people and businesses an incentive to reduce waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.2] A circular economy keeps products and materials in use for as long as possible, instead of using them once and throwing them away.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.b] The polluter pays principle is put into effect through taxes and charges that put a price on pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.0] Irish carbon tax, introduced in 2010.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.1] Plastic bag levy, introduced in March 2002, a charge on plastic bags, so shoppers have a reason to use fewer.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.2] Landfill levy, charged on waste sent to landfill, so dumping costs more than reducing waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.3] EU Emissions Trading System, from 2005: companies pay for the emissions they release.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.b] Governments have set targets for cutting emissions, and the future of sustainable development depends on meeting them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.0] The EU and Ireland aim for climate neutrality by 2050 (releasing no more greenhouse gases than are removed).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.1] Ireland's Climate Act 2021 sets a 51% cut in emissions by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.2] The Climate Action Plan sets 80% renewable electricity by 2030 and a retrofit (upgrading insulation and heating) of 500,000 homes to B2 energy rating by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.3] Offshore wind, electric vehicles, public transport and the circular economy are the main routes to lower emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.4] Cutting agricultural emissions, restoring bogs and planting forests as carbon sinks (places that absorb and store carbon).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.b] Meeting these targets will not be easy. Several obstacles stand in the way.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.0] Cost: clean technology, retrofitting and rehabilitation are expensive.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.1] Short-term economic pressure favours quick profit over long-term protection.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.2] Jobs in traditional industries, such as peat and fishing, are at risk.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.3] Global cooperation is needed, because pollution and climate change do not stop at borders.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s1: 1. Renewable and non-renewable resources in the economy
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.b] A resource is anything in the environment that people use to meet their needs. Resources are classed by whether nature can replace them, and this decides whether a country can keep using them for ever.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.0] Resources include energy, minerals, water, soil, forests, fish, land and scenery, so the word covers far more than fuels.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.1] Non-renewable (stock, finite) resources formed over millions of years, so they are used up far faster than they form and will eventually run out. Examples are coal, oil, natural gas, peat, metal ores and uranium.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.2] Renewable (flow) resources are replaced naturally, so they will not run out however much is used. Examples are wind, solar, hydro (running water), wave, tidal and geothermal heat (heat from inside the Earth).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.3] Some resources are renewable only if managed sustainably (used no faster than they are replaced): forests, fish stocks, soils, groundwater and biomass (plant material used as fuel). Overuse turns them into exhausted resources, as happens with fish stocks. There is more on this in Sustainable economic development.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.b] Three kinds of fuel are non-renewable. Fossil fuels and peat formed from once-living material, and uranium is a finite mineral.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas and peat) formed from the remains of plants or marine organisms buried and compressed over millions of years. Burning them releases the carbon they stored.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.1] Peat is non-renewable because a bog (wet ground where dead plants build up as peat) grows only about 1 mm a year, far slower than it is cut.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.2] Nuclear energy from uranium is an alternative to fossil fuels with low carbon emissions, but it is not renewable because uranium is finite.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.b] Energy is the resource that the rest of the economy runs on. This is why a country's energy supply matters so much to its wealth and security.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.0] Primary energy is energy in its raw form, such as oil, gas or wind. Electricity is a secondary form made from primary sources, for example by burning gas or by turning turbines with wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.1] Renewables give a far larger share of electricity than of total energy, because much of total energy is oil used for transport and heating, which renewables supply little of.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.2] Energy powers industry, transport, farming and homes, so resources are central to the economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.3] Energy security means a secure and affordable supply that is not disrupted by price shocks (sudden large rises in price) or supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.4] A country that imports its energy pays a large import bill (the money spent buying energy from abroad) and is exposed to price shocks and supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.b] Ireland has few fossil fuels of its own, so it depends heavily on imported energy. The mix of fuels it uses shows where that dependence lies.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.0] Import dependence (the share of energy bought from abroad): Ireland imported close to 90% of its energy in the years before Corrib gas came ashore in 2015.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.1] The share fell after Corrib began and has risen again as Corrib's output declines. In the early 2020s Ireland still imported about three-quarters of its energy (SEAI, the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.2] Early 2020s primary energy mix (SEAI): oil just under half and the largest single source; natural gas about a third; renewables a little over a tenth; coal and peat small and falling.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.3] Adding oil, gas, coal and peat shows that fossil fuels still supply most of Ireland's primary energy, while renewables are growing.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.b] Oil is Ireland's largest single source of primary energy, and Ireland produces none of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.0] All of Ireland's oil is imported, which adds to the import bill and leaves the country exposed to price shocks.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.1] Oil is used mainly for transport and home heating.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.2] Whitegate, Co. Cork, is Ireland's only oil refinery (the plant that turns crude oil into fuels).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.b] Natural gas is Ireland's second-largest primary energy source and its largest source of electricity. Home gas fields supplied it for decades but are now running down.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.0] Kinsale Head gas field, off Co. Cork, was discovered in 1971 and began production in 1978 as Ireland's first commercial gas field. Production ended in 2020 when the field was depleted (used up), so it is no longer a working field.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.1] Corrib gas field, off north-west Co. Mayo, was discovered in 1996 and first flowed in December 2015. Its gas is processed at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.2] For a time after 2015 Corrib supplied over half of Ireland's gas. Its output is now declining, which is why import dependence has risen again.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.3] Most of Ireland's gas now comes from Britain through interconnector pipelines (cross-border links) from Moffat, Scotland.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.4] Gas is the largest single source of Irish electricity, because gas-fired power stations generate more of it than any other fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.b] Ireland's own coal fields are small and long closed. Moneypoint, the big coal-burning station, ran on imported coal and has now stopped burning coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.0] Ireland's native coalfields are small and long closed. They are Arigna, Co. Roscommon, where mining ended in 1990 and which is now the Arigna Mining Experience, and Castlecomer, Co. Kilkenny.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.1] Moneypoint, Co. Clare, on the Shannon Estuary, is an ESB (Electricity Supply Board) coal-fired station opened in 1985. At about 900 MW (megawatts, a measure of power output) it is Ireland's largest power station.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.2] Moneypoint burned imported coal, brought in by large ships to its deep-water jetty (a landing pier where water is deep enough for big ships).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.3] Moneypoint stopped burning coal in 2025.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.b] Peat, cut from the bogs of the Midlands, was Ireland's own fuel and powered stations for decades. It has now ended, and the cutaway bogs (bogs from which the peat has been removed) are being given new uses.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.0] Bord na Móna, the State peat company, was founded in 1946.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.1] Peat-fired stations at Lanesborough (Lough Ree) and Shannonbridge (West Offaly) closed in December 2020. Bord na Móna ended all peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.2] Edenderry, Co. Offaly, switched from peat to biomass, a renewable fuel of wood and energy crops.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.3] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo: the peat station closed in 2003 and was replaced by wind farms on the cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.4] Cutaway bogs are reused. Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly, has turned a cutaway bog into parkland, wetlands and walking and cycling trails.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.b] Hydroelectric power uses falling water to turn turbines (machines that spin to drive generators). The Shannon Scheme at Ardnacrusha was an early large scheme, and little potential is left for more.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.0] Ardnacrusha, on the River Shannon near Limerick, was built by the German firm Siemens and opened in 1929 as the Shannon Scheme. The ESB was set up in 1927 to run it, and for some years it supplied most of the country's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.1] Other stations: Poulaphouca on the River Liffey, Co. Wicklow; Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid on the River Lee, Co. Cork; and Cathaleen's Fall at Ballyshannon on the River Erne, Co. Donegal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.2] Turlough Hill, Co. Wicklow, opened in 1974 as an ESB pumped-storage station. Water is pumped uphill when demand is low and released through turbines at peak times, so it stores energy for when it is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.3] Little potential is left for large new hydro schemes, because the best sites were already used.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.b] Wind is Ireland's main renewable source. The country's position on the Atlantic edge of Europe gives it an advantage.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.0] Wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s. Renewables, mostly wind, supplied about two-fifths.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.1] Strong, frequent Atlantic winds make the western coasts and uplands the best sites for wind farms.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.2] Bellacorick wind farm, Co. Mayo (1992), was Ireland's first commercial wind farm. Oweninny wind farm was built on the same cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.3] Galway Wind Park, Connemara, completed in 2017, is one of Ireland's largest onshore (on land) wind farms.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.4] Arklow Bank Wind Park, off Co. Wicklow, opened in 2004 as Ireland's first offshore (at sea) wind farm, with seven turbines. Offshore wind is planned to grow strongly, with at least 5 GW targeted by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.b] Ireland's other renewable sources are smaller than wind. Some are growing quickly and some are still at an early stage.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.0] Solar power has grown rapidly since the early 2020s, through large solar farms on farmland and panels on rooftops. SEAI grants support home installations.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.1] The Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is Ireland's wave-energy test site, where wave machines are trialled.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.2] Geothermal energy is limited to low-temperature ground-source heat pumps (devices that take heat from the ground to warm buildings).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.3] Tidal energy harnesses the rise and fall of the sea, and it is at an early stage in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.4] Biomass (wood and energy crops) and biofuels (fuels made from plant material) are the remaining renewable sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.b] Governments set targets to push renewables up and cut emissions. Ireland missed its 2020 target and has set tougher ones for 2030 and 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.0] Ireland's 2020 EU target was 16% of gross final energy consumption (all the energy used by final users such as homes, industry and transport) from renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.1] Ireland reached only about 13.5% and missed the target. It was one of the furthest from its target of any EU member state.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.2] Climate Action Plan: 80% of electricity from renewables by 2030, and at least 5 GW (gigawatts) of offshore wind by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.3] Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021: cut greenhouse gas emissions by 51% by 2030, compared with 2018, and reach climate neutrality (net zero, meaning emissions no higher than what is removed) by 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.b] Ireland's renewable share fell short of its 2020 target for six linked reasons. Several come from how Irish people live and travel, and some from delays in building.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.0] Heavy dependence on cars and road freight for transport, which runs on oil, so renewables supply little of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.1] Oil used for home heating in a dispersed rural settlement pattern (homes spread out, so gas networks and shared heating are hard to provide).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.2] Slow planning and grid connection for wind farms, which delayed new capacity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.3] No large hydro potential left for new schemes, because the best sites were already used.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.4] A late start on offshore wind, which left Ireland with less renewable capacity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.5] Strong economic and population growth raising energy demand, which made the renewable share harder to raise.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.b] Interconnectors are cables or pipelines that link Ireland's energy network to other countries. They help security of supply because power and gas can flow in when Ireland needs them.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.0] The East-West Interconnector, opened in 2012, links Co. Dublin to north Wales by electricity cable.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.1] The Celtic Interconnector, from Co. Cork to Brittany, France, is under construction and due to link Ireland to the EU mainland grid in the late 2020s.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.2] Gas also arrives by interconnector pipelines from Scotland, which is how most of Ireland's gas now reaches it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.b] Knowing which sectors use most energy shows where demand is highest and where savings or renewables matter most.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.0] Transport is the largest energy-using sector in Ireland (SEAI).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.1] Heat for homes and businesses comes next, then electricity generation for industry and services.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.2] Data centres (buildings full of computer servers) used about a fifth of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office). Most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.b] Europe depends heavily on imported energy, and its main oil and gas comes from the North Sea and from suppliers outside the EU.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.0] The EU imports close to three-fifths of its energy (early 2020s, Eurostat, the EU's statistics office).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.1] The North Sea, shared by the UK and Norway, is Europe's main oil and gas province (an area rich in oil and gas).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.2] Norway is not an EU member but is one of the EU's main gas suppliers, by pipeline.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.b] Relying on one supplier for a vital fuel is risky. Europe learned this when Russia, its biggest gas supplier, invaded Ukraine.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.0] Before 2022 Russia supplied about two-fifths of the EU's imported gas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.1] Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022. Gas and electricity prices soared across Europe.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.2] REPowerEU plan, May 2022: cut dependence on Russian fossil fuels by saving energy, importing more liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so ships can carry it, much of it from the USA) and speeding up renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.3] Russia's share of EU gas imports has since fallen sharply.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.17.b] National energy mixes differ widely across Europe. France relies on nuclear power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.17.0] Nuclear power (electricity made from splitting uranium atoms) supplies about two-thirds of France's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.b] Germany is changing its energy mix by moving to renewables and ending nuclear power, though it still burns coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.0] The Energiewende (energy transition) is Germany's long-term switch to renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.1] After the Fukushima disaster in Japan in March 2011 (a nuclear accident), Germany decided to phase out nuclear power. Its last three reactors closed on 15 April 2023.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.2] Germany still burns lignite (brown coal) from huge open-cast mines (mines dug from the surface) in the Rhineland. A 2020 law sets 2038 at the latest as the end of coal-fired power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.19.b] Poland shows a country whose power still rests on coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.19.0] Coal generates well over half of Poland's electricity (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.b] Denmark shows a country that has built its electricity supply around wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.0] Wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity (2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.1] Denmark was an early leader in wind power and is home to the turbine maker Vestas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.b] Two European countries that are not EU members get nearly all their electricity from renewable sources, thanks to their landscapes.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.0] Hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of Norway's electricity, from its mountains, high rainfall and glacial lakes, which give plenty of falling water.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.1] Geothermal and hydro supply almost all of Iceland's electricity, and about 9 in 10 Icelandic homes are heated geothermally.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.b] The EU sets renewable targets for all its members. It met its 2020 target and has raised the goal for 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.0] The EU target of 20% renewable energy by 2020 was achieved, at about 22%.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.1] The revised Renewable Energy Directive (2023, an EU law binding on member states) requires at least 42.5% renewable energy by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.2] Renewables supplied about a quarter of EU gross final energy consumption in 2023, so the EU still has far to go to reach 42.5%.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.b] Despite the growth of renewables, fossil fuels still supply most of the EU's energy. Final energy (the energy used by consumers) is spread across several sectors.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.0] Fossil fuels (oil, gas and coal) still supply most of the EU's energy. Oil is the largest single source, mainly for transport.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.1] Transport, households and industry each take roughly a quarter to a third of final energy. Services take a smaller share.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.b] Energy production and consumption are not spread evenly across Europe. Which role a country plays depends on its industry and its natural resources.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.0] Core industrial countries, such as Germany and France, consume most of the energy, because their large industry and populations need so much.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.1] Resource-rich countries, such as Norway, export energy, because they produce more than they need.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.2] Peripheral countries, such as Ireland, depend on imports, because they have few fuels of their own.
