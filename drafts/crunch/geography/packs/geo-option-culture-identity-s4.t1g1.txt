TARGET geo-option-culture-identity-s4: 4. Everyday expressions of culture and identity
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.b] Culture and identity are the broad concepts. Everyday expressions are the visible parts of culture that people practise, and they are what an essay on this topic is built from.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.0] Culture is a group's shared, learned way of life: its beliefs, customs, language, arts, food and dress. It is learned, not inherited, so each generation has to be taught it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.1] Identity is a person's or group's sense of who they are and where they belong, for example as Irish, Catalan or a member of a county club.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.2] Everyday expressions are the visible, practised parts of culture: people choose them, repeat them and pass them on.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.3] The main everyday expressions in this option are sport, traditions, costume, food, music, art and festivals.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.b] Customs are not just pastimes. They do real work for a group, and these five functions give you the points to explain in an essay.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.0] They mark belonging: taking part in a shared custom shows that a person belongs to the group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.1] They pass culture from one generation to the next, because children learn the games, songs and customs by taking part.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.2] They assert identity when a culture is under pressure, as Irish games and music did during the Gaelic Revival (a movement to bring Irish games and music back into everyday use).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.3] They attract tourists and bring income to the places where they are practised.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.4] They can also mark division: parades in Northern Ireland and football rivalries separate groups as well as unite them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.2.b] Globalisation (the growing connection between countries through trade, media and travel) spreads the same products, sports and customs to many countries, so cultures can become more alike.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.2.0] Fast food, Hollywood films, global sports and global brands are the main channels of Americanisation (the spread of American culture to other countries).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.2.1] McDonald's opened its first Irish restaurant on Grafton Street, Dublin, in 1977, an example of a global brand entering everyday Irish life.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.2.2] Halloween customs returned from the USA in a commercial form, although the festival itself has Irish origins in Samhain (the Celtic festival of 31 October to 1 November).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.3.b] Communities do not simply accept cultural change. Three responses try to keep traditional expressions alive.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.3.0] Revival movements, such as the Gaelic Revival, promote Irish games and music to keep them in everyday use.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.3.1] UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (living traditions and skills rather than buildings or objects) was created by its 2003 convention. Irish harping was added in 2019.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.3.2] State support for traditional music, sport and arts events helps keep these expressions in practice, because funding lets them continue and reach new audiences.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.4.b] Migration (people moving to live in another country) also changes everyday culture, because migrants bring their own customs with them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.4.0] Migrants bring new food, festivals, religious celebrations and sports, which become part of everyday life in their new country.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.4.1] New and existing expressions blend: multicultural festivals and fusion food (dishes that mix cooking traditions) combine traditions from different groups.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.b] The Gaelic Athletic Association (GAA) promotes native Irish games, chiefly hurling and Gaelic football. It is the strongest example of sport as an expression of Irish identity, so be ready to treat it in depth.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.0] The GAA was founded on 1 November 1884 in Hayes' Hotel, Thurles, Co. Tipperary, by Michael Cusack and others, to preserve and promote native Irish games.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.1] Maurice Davin was the GAA's first president.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.2] Players are amateurs, so the games stay rooted in the community, which means they play for their club and county without being paid.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.3] Clubs are based on parishes and teams represent counties, so the GAA reinforces local and county identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.4] The GAA is organised on an all-island basis and has clubs among the Irish diaspora (Irish people living abroad).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.5] Camogie, the women's form of hurling, is run by the Camogie Association, founded in 1904. Ladies' football is the women's form of Gaelic football.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.6] Hurling was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2018. It is a cultural symbol and is not Ireland's national sport by law.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.b] Croke Park is the stadium behind the GAA's best-known rule changes and events, so you can use it to show how sport and national history are linked.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.0] Croke Park, Dublin, holds about 82,300 spectators and is one of Europe's largest stadiums.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.1] Members were banned from playing "foreign games" (soccer, rugby, cricket and hockey), which kept the GAA focused on native Irish games. The ban was removed in 1971.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.2] A rule barring members of the British security forces from the GAA was removed in 2001.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.3] Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920: British forces opened fire at a Gaelic football match in Croke Park, killing 14 people.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.4] This is a different event from Bloody Sunday 1972 in Derry, and there is more on it in the notes on Religion as a cultural indicator.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.5] From 2007 Croke Park hosted rugby and soccer internationals while Lansdowne Road was rebuilt. England played Ireland at rugby there in February 2007.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.7.b] Other sports express identity too, and in Northern Ireland the team a person supports can signal which community they belong to.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.7.0] The Ireland rugby team represents the whole island, including Northern Ireland, so it is an all-island expression of identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.7.1] Sporting loyalties in Northern Ireland are divided between Irish and British teams, so the choice of team can express national identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.b] Traditional music is played informally, and sessions are the main setting in which it is shared.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.0] Traditional instruments include the fiddle, uilleann pipes (Irish bellows-blown bagpipes), tin whistle, flute, concertina, accordion, bodhrán (a hand-held frame drum) and harp.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.1] The harp is Ireland's national symbol and appears on coins and the presidential seal.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.2] Sessions are informal gatherings where musicians play together, which is how tunes and playing styles are passed on.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.3] Uilleann piping was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2017.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.b] Traditional music has an organisation behind it and a yearly festival, and it has a singing and dancing side too.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.0] Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann was founded in 1951 to promote traditional music.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.1] The first Fleadh Cheoil na hÉireann (a national music festival) was held in Mullingar in 1951. It is now held in a different town each August and draws hundreds of thousands of visitors.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.2] Sean-nós ("old style") is unaccompanied traditional singing in Irish, strongest in Conamara.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.3] Sean-nós dancing is the traditional informal dance form that sits alongside the singing tradition.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.10.b] Irish music and dance also reached a world audience, which shows an Irish expression spreading outwards through globalisation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.10.0] Riverdance began as the interval act (the performance between entries) of the Eurovision Song Contest, performed in Dublin on 30 April 1994.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.10.1] It became a worldwide touring show, carrying Irish dance to audiences abroad.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.10.2] Irish popular music with traditional roots combines the traditional instruments and melodies with modern styles.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.b] Festivals bring a community together on a fixed date. These points cover national and religious occasions, and St Patrick's Day shows Irish identity in the diaspora.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.0] St Patrick's Day, 17 March: parades are held across the world, for example in New York, and landmarks are lit green.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.1] St Patrick's Day is a global expression of Irish identity in the diaspora. Dublin's St Patrick's Festival began in 1996.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.2] St Brigid's Day (Lá Fhéile Bríde, around 1 February) became a public holiday in 2023, the first named after a woman.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.3] Samhain (31 October to 1 November) is the Celtic origin of Halloween.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.12.b] Local festivals and fairs tie a place to a custom, and each of these is held at the same time every year.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.12.0] Puck Fair, Killorglin, Co. Kerry, is held every August (10-12 August), when a wild goat is crowned king.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.12.1] Lisdoonvarna Matchmaking Festival, Co. Clare, is held every September.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.12.2] Ballinasloe Horse Fair, Co. Galway, is held every October and is one of Europe's oldest horse fairs. It is also important to Travellers, a group with a distinct everyday culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.13.b] Some festivals look outwards to the diaspora, and others celebrate writing and the arts.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.13.0] The Rose of Tralee, Co. Kerry, has been held since 1959 and is open to women of Irish birth or descent worldwide, so it links Ireland with its diaspora.

[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.13.2] The Galway International Arts Festival has been held since 1978. Wexford Festival Opera has been held since 1951.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-globalisation-s4: 4. How the global economy is organised
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.b] A basic processing unit is a factory carrying out one stage of production: processing a raw material, making a component (a part that goes into a finished product) or assembling a product. Multinational companies (MNCs, firms operating in many countries) place each stage in whichever country suits it best, which is why production is so widely spread.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.0] The Intel and Apple chains, such as those in 'One multinational company (MNC)', are examples of stages spread across several countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.1] Cheap labour suits labour-intensive assembly (work that needs many workers rather than machines), so firms put assembly where wages are low.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.2] Units are placed close to raw materials or to markets, which cuts the distance inputs or finished goods must travel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.3] A factory inside a trade bloc (a group of countries with shared trade rules) sells to the bloc's members without the barriers that apply to imports from outside.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.4] Tax breaks (reduced tax bills) and grants (money from government) lower the cost of operating in a country, so they attract factories.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.5] Falling transport and communication costs make distant factories practical, because a plant far away can still be supplied and managed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.6] Spreading production across many countries means that one disaster or strike does not stop all production, so the firm spreads its risk.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.b] Nike shows how far a firm can spread production: it designs and sells shoes but owns almost none of the factories that make them. Do not say Nike owns its Asian factories.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.0] Founded as Blue Ribbon Sports in 1964 by Phil Knight and Bill Bowerman; renamed Nike in 1971.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.1] Headquarters, design and marketing are in Beaverton, Oregon, USA, so the high-skill, high-value work stays at home.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.2] Footwear is made by independent contract factories (outside firms paid to make the product), mainly in Vietnam (the largest), China and Indonesia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.3] Nike sells its products worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.4] Sweatshop criticism in the 1990s (attacks on factories with very low pay and poor conditions), including of factories in Indonesia, led to factory monitoring and published supplier lists (lists of the factories Nike uses).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.b] Governments attract factories by creating special areas with easy trade rules. In an export processing zone, firms import components and export finished goods free of customs duty (a tax on goods crossing a border), and they receive tax breaks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.0] Shannon Free Zone, Co. Clare, set up in 1959, was one of the world's first export processing zones and Ireland's pioneer. The duty-free shop at Shannon Airport was 1947, so do not swap the dates.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.1] Shenzhen, China, became a Special Economic Zone (a region with special business rules to attract foreign firms) in 1980.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.2] Maquiladoras are assembly plants along Mexico's border with the USA, such as those at Tijuana and Ciudad Juárez, set up from 1965.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.3] A maquiladora imports components duty-free and exports finished goods to the USA, so it is an example of an export processing zone at work.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.b] Spreading production across many countries brings risks as well as savings. The costs fall on supply chains, workers and the environment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.0] Long supply chains (the linked stages from raw material to finished product) are vulnerable to disruption, as the 2021 chip shortage, the Ever Given blockage and the Red Sea diversions showed. There is more on this in 'Patterns in world trade'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.1] Branch plants (factories owned by a firm whose headquarters is elsewhere) close when costs change, and their workers lose jobs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.2] Some plants pay low wages and have poor conditions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.3] Moving components and goods over long distances has an environmental cost.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.4] Responses include reshoring (moving production back to the home country) and friend-shoring (moving it to politically allied countries).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.b] At world scale, regions play different roles in the global economy. The core is the wealthy, powerful centre; the periphery is the poorer edge that supplies it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.0] Core regions have capital (money to invest), MNC headquarters, research and development, skilled labour, good infrastructure and political power.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.1] The core's examples are the USA, western Europe's Blue Banana (Europe's core belt of dense development) and Japan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.2] The periphery supplies raw materials, food and cheap labour, with weak infrastructure and out-migration of people (people leaving to find work elsewhere).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.3] Most of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America and parts of South Asia are periphery.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.b] Some countries sit between the core and the periphery. They are changing their role in the global economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.0] The semi-periphery is made up of newly industrialised countries (NICs, countries that have recently built large manufacturing industries) moving towards the core.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.1] South Korea did so earlier; China, India, Brazil and Mexico are doing so now.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.b] The core did not become rich by accident. An early start, overseas empires and a self-reinforcing cycle of growth built its lead.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.0] Britain industrialised first, from the late 18th century (the Industrial Revolution), followed by western Europe and the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.1] Colonial empires supplied the core with raw materials and markets, and profits were reinvested in the core, which strengthened it further.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.2] Cumulative causation, a model by Gunnar Myrdal (1957), says success attracts more investment, people and services, which increases success.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.3] Backwash effects drain capital and skilled people from the periphery to the core, so the core grows at the periphery's expense.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.4] The Blue Banana runs from south-east England and London through the Benelux countries and the Rhine valley to northern Italy. There is more on this in 'Socio-economic regions'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.b] The periphery's economy was shaped by outside rulers for their benefit, which left it badly placed after independence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.0] Colonial economies were built around exporting one or two raw materials, so they had little other industry.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.1] Railways ran from mines and plantations to ports, rather than linking regions to each other, because their purpose was export.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.2] Borders were drawn by the colonisers, not by the people who lived there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.3] After independence these economies depended on falling commodity prices (the prices of raw materials), were burdened by debt and suffered conflict.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.b] Core and periphery are not fixed. Some places have moved up the ladder, and the North-South model is a simple picture of the pattern.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.0] Spread effects (the benefits of growth passing outwards from the core) and investment can raise peripheral regions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.1] The Asian Tigers and China moved from periphery to semi-periphery or core in about two generations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.2] Ireland moved from the European periphery to a core-like economy through MNC investment. There is more on this in 'Irish Regions - Core and Peripheral'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.3] The Brandt Line, from the Brandt Report of 1980, is a simple North-South model (a line dividing richer northern from poorer southern countries) of the same pattern.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.b] Sub-Saharan Africa is the main region excluded from world manufacturing. It has a very small share of world manufacturing and exports mostly unprocessed raw materials.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.0] Nigeria: crude oil is most of its exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.1] Zambia: copper is most of its exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.2] The Democratic Republic of the Congo produces most of the world's cobalt and exports it raw despite its huge reserves, amid conflict.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.3] Sudan: civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces since April 2023 has caused one of the world's largest displacement crises, with people forced to flee their homes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.4] South Sudan became independent from Sudan in 2011, taking most of the oil fields.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.5] The 2023 war is within Sudan, not a war with South Sudan, because they have been separate countries since 2011.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.b] Many linked problems keep sub-Saharan Africa out of manufacturing. Each one raises costs or risks for firms, or limits what the countries can do for themselves.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.0] Poor infrastructure: roads and ports are inadequate and electricity is unreliable, which makes running a factory costly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.1] Landlocked countries (with no coast): Africa has 16, such as Chad, Niger, Mali and South Sudan, and they face high transport costs to reach ports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.2] Political instability, conflict and corruption scare off investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.3] Low levels of education and skills, and poor health, limit the workforce.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.4] Small, poor home markets give firms little reason to produce locally.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.5] Heavy debt absorbs money that could fund development.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.6] The colonial legacy: economies were set up to export raw materials, and that pattern persists.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.7] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials, so processing at home does not pay; coffee beans enter rich markets with low tariffs, while roasted coffee faces higher ones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.b] Several measures try to help excluded regions join world trade and industry. They target debt, trade barriers and infrastructure.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.0] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative, begun in 1996, gives debt relief (cancelling or reducing debts) through the World Bank and IMF.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.1] The African Continental Free Trade Area, which removes trade barriers between African countries, began trading on 1 January 2021.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.2] Chinese and other foreign investment is building infrastructure.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.3] Fair trade (a system that pays producers a fairer price). There is more on this in 'Patterns in world trade'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.b] Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, MNCs, technology and the movement of people and ideas. It has changed what is traded, who trades and how goods move.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.0] About 80% of world trade is linked to the international production networks of MNCs (UNCTAD, 2013).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.1] Much of it is intra-firm trade, between parts of the same company or with its suppliers in global value chains (production split into stages across countries).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.2] Trade is increasingly in components rather than finished goods, because each stage of production crosses borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.3] Trade in services and digital trade has grown rapidly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.4] New trading powers, led by China, have entered world trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.5] The WTO (World Trade Organization) and trade blocs have lowered barriers, so goods cross borders more easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.6] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard steel boxes) and huge ports have cut the cost of shipping, so distant trade pays.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.7] E-commerce (online selling) brings small sellers into world markets, so they can reach buyers abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.b] Globalisation does not benefit everyone equally. Some groups and regions gain, others lose.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.0] Consumers gain cheaper goods, because production moves to low-cost countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.1] NICs and China gain jobs and growth, because factories locate there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.2] Manufacturing regions in the core lose jobs, because production moves abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.3] Excluded regions fall further behind, because they get little of the new investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.4] Workers in low-wage assembly can face poor and unsafe conditions, as the Rana Plaza collapse showed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.5] Transporting goods over long distances has environmental costs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.b] Since 2018 some governments have pushed back against open trade, raising barriers between major economies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.0] The US-China trade war from 2018, the US tariffs of 2025 and Brexit have raised barriers between major economies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.1] Governments are reshoring strategic industries such as chips (the microchips in electronics), bringing production back home so they do not depend on distant suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.2] Supply-chain worries grew after COVID-19.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.b] India's economy was tightly controlled before 1991, and it opened to globalisation only after a crisis.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.0] Five-year plans from 1951 directed investment and production, so the state, not the market, decided what was made.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.1] Protection (tariffs and rules that shut out imports) shielded Indian industries from foreign competition.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.2] The Licence Raj: firms needed government licences to produce and expand, which limited competition and growth.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.3] A balance-of-payments crisis (when a country cannot pay for its imports and debts) in 1991 forced change.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.4] Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh removed most licences, cut tariffs and opened India to foreign investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.b] Globalisation brought rapid growth, new industries and foreign investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.0] Bengaluru became known as the 'Silicon Valley of India'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.1] IT and business-process outsourcing (hiring firms abroad to run tasks such as customer service) grew rapidly; Tata Consultancy Services (founded 1968), Infosys (founded 1981) and Wipro grew, with Bengaluru as a major hub.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.2] Texas Instruments opened a design centre in Bengaluru in 1985.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.3] Maruti Suzuki, a joint venture (a company shared between partners) with Suzuki of Japan, made its first car in 1983.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.4] India is a leading maker of generic medicines (cheaper copies of branded drugs), earning it the name 'pharmacy of the world'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.5] Make in India, launched in 2014, aimed to attract manufacturing; iPhones are assembled near Chennai and Bengaluru.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.6] Services produce over half of India's GDP (the value of everything it produces), and India is among the world's five largest economies (early 2020s). A growing middle class has emerged.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.7] Hundreds of millions of people have been moved out of extreme poverty, as growth created jobs and income.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.b] The gains from globalisation have been uneven, and it has brought costs for some people and places.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.0] Inequality exists between cities and rural areas, and between states.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.1] Small farmers are exposed to world prices, so they can lose when prices fall.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.2] Farmers' protests in 2020-21 were against three farm laws, which were repealed in November 2021.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.3] Most workers are still in the informal sector (jobs without contracts, regular pay or legal protection).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.4] Pollution and accidents at MNC plants: the Bhopal gas leak at the Union Carbide pesticide plant in December 1984 killed thousands; Union Carbide was a US MNC.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.b] China is a second developing economy, which opened to the world after 1978. It gained enormous growth but also costs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.0] Reforms under Deng Xiaoping from 1978 opened China to foreign trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.1] Special Economic Zones from 1980 attracted foreign investment, such as Shenzhen.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.2] China joined the WTO on 11 December 2001, which gave it easier access to world markets.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.3] China has been the largest goods exporter since 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.4] About 800 million people have been lifted out of extreme poverty since 1978 (World Bank).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.5] Inequality exists between the coast and the interior, because growth centred on coastal zones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.6] Pollution from rapid industrial growth and dependence on exports are costs of this model, because a fall in world demand would hit China's economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.b] Bangladesh shows both sides of globalisation: jobs from the garment industry, and the dangers of low-cost production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.0] Ready-made garments are most of Bangladesh's exports; it is the world's second-largest garment exporter after China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.1] The garment industry provides jobs, especially for women.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.2] Rana Plaza, Savar, near Dhaka, collapsed on 24 April 2013, killing over 1,100 garment workers. It was a building collapse, not a fire.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.3] The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh, signed in 2013 by clothing brands and unions, introduced factory inspections.

CANDIDATES geo-option-culture-identity-s2: 2. Language as a cultural indicator
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.0.b] A cultural indicator is a feature that helps you identify and map a cultural group. Language is one of the strongest, because it is both part of a group's identity and easy to measure.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.0.0] Language carries a group's history, literature, songs, place names and ways of thinking, so losing a language means losing a store of the group's culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.0.1] Language marks who belongs to a group, because sharing a language means sharing a way of speaking and thinking that outsiders do not have.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.0.2] Language is easy to map from census questions on language (a census is the official population count, held at regular intervals), so it is used to define cultural regions (areas with a shared culture).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.b] Nationalism (the belief that a people with a shared culture should govern themselves) has often rested on language. Governments have therefore either promoted a language to build a nation or suppressed one to weaken a group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.0] Many nation states (states whose people share one national identity) were built around a single language, because a common language helps unite the population.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.1] The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge), founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill, led a language revival that fed the Irish independence movement, though it was founded as a non-political body.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.2] Suppressing a language has been used to weaken a group. Basque and Catalan were banned from public life and schools under Franco (1939–1975).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.3] The Welsh Not was a wooden board hung round the neck of children caught speaking Welsh in some 19th-century schools, so children were punished for using their own language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.1.4] Kurdish was banned in public life and broadcasting in Turkey until the early 1990s, which is an example of a state weakening a group by silencing its language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.2.b] Languages carry power as well as culture. The language of the rulers or the wealthy tends to spread, and smaller languages lose ground.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.2.0] Colonial languages (those of the empires that ruled a territory) became official languages of former colonies: English in India and Nigeria, French in Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire, Spanish across most of Latin America, and Portuguese in Brazil.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.2.1] The language of the powerful brings jobs and status, so speakers of minority languages shift to it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.2.2] The Organisation internationale de la Francophonie, founded in 1970, links French-speaking states, which shows how a colonial language can keep a group of countries connected.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.3.b] Place names preserve the language spoken in an area long after it has been replaced. Reading them is a way of tracing a region's linguistic history.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.3.0] Irish place-name elements show the Irish-speaking past of areas that are now English-speaking: baile (townland or homestead; a townland is a small traditional land division), cill (church), dún (fort), inis (island) and cluain (meadow).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.3.1] Examples: Clonmel (Cluain Meala), Kilkenny (Cill Chainnigh), Dundalk (Dún Dealgan) and Ennis (Inis). Each English name is a version of the older Irish one, which is why the Irish elements are still visible in them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.4.b] Language can bind people together or split them apart, and it pays no attention to state borders.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.4.0] A shared national language can unite a state, but language borders can divide it, as Belgium's does. There is more on this in The complexity of regions (ii).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.4.1] French is spoken in France, Belgium (Wallonia), Switzerland (west) and Quebec (Canada), so one language links four different places.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.4.2] Languages cross borders: French is spoken in several states, and Basque is spoken in both Spain and France, so a language community can be split between two states.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.5.b] Languages are grouped by descent, from the largest unit to the smallest. This lets us show how languages that look different are related.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.5.0] A language family is a set of languages descended from a common ancestor. Each family divides into branches, and each branch contains languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.5.1] A dialect is a regional variety of a single language, so it is smaller than a language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.5.2] About 7,000 languages are spoken in the world today, for example across the families below.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.b] The world's languages fall into a small number of large families. Know each family's main languages and the regions where it is spoken.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.0] Indo-European is the largest family, spoken by nearly half the world's people. It covers Europe, the Americas, Iran and South Asia.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.1] Sino-Tibetan: Mandarin and other Chinese languages, Tibetan and Burmese.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.2] Afro-Asiatic: Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic and Hausa.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.3] Niger-Congo: most of sub-Saharan Africa (Africa south of the Sahara), e.g. Swahili, Yoruba and Zulu.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.4] Austronesian: Malay/Indonesian, Filipino, Malagasy, Māori and Hawaiian.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.5] Dravidian: southern India, e.g. Tamil and Telugu.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.6.6] Turkic and Uralic are further families.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.b] Counting speakers depends on whether you count only native speakers (those who learned it as a first language) or second-language speakers as well. That is why different languages come first on different lists.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.0] Mandarin Chinese has the most native speakers.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.1] English has the most speakers in total, about 1.5 billion counting second-language speakers.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.2] Spanish has the second-most native speakers. Hindi is also very widely spoken.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.7.3] Arabic is the language of the Qur'an (the holy book of Islam) and an official language in over 20 states of the Middle East and North Africa.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.8.b] Languages move when people move or when one group gains power over another. Four main routes explain the present world map of languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.8.0] Migration, conquest and empire spread languages: Latin with the Roman Empire, and English, Spanish, French and Portuguese with colonialism.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.8.1] Trade and religion spread languages too: Arabic spread with Islam, because the Qur'an is in Arabic.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.8.2] Today globalisation (the growing links between countries) and the media spread dominant languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.b] Most European languages belong to the Indo-European family, which divides into branches. A sketch map of the branches is a useful diagram in an exam answer.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.0] Germanic: English, German, Dutch (including Flemish, the Dutch spoken in Belgium), Danish, Swedish, Norwegian and Icelandic.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.1] Romance, from Latin (the language of the Roman Empire, so these languages descend from it): French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Romanian and Catalan.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.2] Slavic: Russian, Polish, Czech, Slovak, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Croatian and Slovene.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.3] Celtic: Goidelic (Irish, Scottish Gaelic and Manx) and Brythonic (Welsh, Breton and Cornish).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.4] Hellenic: Greek. Baltic: Latvian and Lithuanian. Albanian is a separate branch.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.5] Non-Indo-European languages in Europe: Finnish, Estonian and Hungarian (Uralic); Turkish (Turkic); Maltese (Semitic, written in the Latin alphabet).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.9.6] Basque is an isolate (a language with no known relatives), unrelated to any other language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.10.b] The EU has to manage many languages in one union. Irish has gained official status there, while English has become the language most Europeans learn as a second language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.10.0] The EU has 24 official languages, so documents and debates may be needed in each.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.10.1] Irish became an official EU language in 2007, with full working-language status from 1 January 2022. Do not say 1973: that is when Ireland joined, not when Irish became official.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.10.2] English is Europe's main second language, and language learning is supported by programmes such as Erasmus exchanges (study periods abroad).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.b] The mass media (television, film, the internet and other means of reaching large audiences) now spread dominant languages, and English is the language most carried by them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.0] Television, film, music, the internet, social media and streaming are dominated by English, through Hollywood films and US and UK music.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.1] About half of all websites are in English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.2] Young people adopt English words and English-language culture, because that is what they see and hear online.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.11.3] Minority-language speakers consume most media in the majority language at home, so even in a Welsh or Irish-speaking household, most of what is watched is in the majority language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.12.b] The spread of English through the media and business changes other languages and puts pressure on them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.12.0] English loanwords (words borrowed from another language) enter other languages, such as the French le weekend (a feature of Franglais, French mixed with English).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.12.1] English is the language of business, science and the internet, so people need it for work and study.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.12.2] The pressure falls on small national languages as well as on minority languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.b] Some states have used law to protect their language from English and other dominant languages. France, Quebec and Iceland are the examples.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.0] France: the Académie française, founded in 1635, works to protect the French language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.1] Toubon Law (1994): French must be used in advertising, product labels, workplace documents and public signage.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.2] French music radio stations must play at least 40% French-language songs (a quota, or fixed minimum; law of 1994, applied from 1996).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.3] Quebec's Charter of the French Language (Bill 101, 1977) made French the sole official language of Quebec, a French-speaking province of Canada, and requires most immigrant children to attend French-language schools.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.13.4] Iceland coins new Icelandic words for new things, e.g. tölva (computer), so it does not need to borrow English words.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.b] A minority language survives better if people can hear and see it every day. Broadcasting in the language gives it a public presence and helps young people use it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.0] Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972, so Irish was heard on the radio every day.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.1] TG4, originally Teilifís na Gaeilge, launched on 31 October 1996 and is based in Baile na hAbhann, Conamara.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.2] S4C, the Welsh-language television channel, launched on 1 November 1982, after Plaid Cymru's former leader Gwynfor Evans threatened a hunger strike in 1980 when the government backed away from its promise.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.3] BBC Alba, the Scottish Gaelic television service, launched in 2008, so Scottish Gaelic gained its own screen presence.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.4] EITB, the Basque public broadcaster, began television in 1982.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.14.5] Social media and online content also let small languages reach young people.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.15.b] Many languages are under threat, and it is the minority languages that are most at risk.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.15.0] A minority language is spoken by a minority of a state's population, e.g. Irish, Welsh, Breton, Basque, Scottish Gaelic and Sámi.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.15.1] About 40% of the world's languages are endangered, meaning they may stop being spoken.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.b] Languages rarely die for one reason. Pressure from outside combines with decisions made inside families, and each cause below is a separate point you can use in an essay.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.0] Conquest and official suppression, because a ruling power can ban a language from schools and public life.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.1] A dominant language is needed for jobs, education and emigration, so people see the minority language as less useful.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.2] Famine and emigration, and the in-migration (arrival) of majority-language speakers, reduce the number of speakers in the heartland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.3] Urbanisation (movement to cities), mass media and globalisation all expose people to the dominant language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.16.4] Parents do not pass the language on, so inter-generational transmission (passing a language from one generation to the next) breaks.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.17.b] Extinct languages can sometimes be brought back, which shows that decline is not always final. Manx and Cornish are the examples.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.17.0] Manx: the last native speaker, Ned Maddrell, died in 1974. The language has since been revived, with a Manx-medium primary school (a school that teaches through Manx).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.17.1] Cornish: the last traditional speaker is usually given as Dolly Pentreath, who died in 1777. Cornish was revived in the 20th century.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.17.2] The Cornish were recognised as a national minority in 2014.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.b] A language survives when the state, schools, media, employers and families all support it. Exams ask for these strategies by heading, so learn each one as a heading you can develop.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.0] Official status in law, such as Article 8 for Irish, which gives the language public standing.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.1] Education through the language: immersion and medium-of-instruction schools (where lessons are taught in the language), and compulsory study of the language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.2] Media, such as broadcasting and online content, so the language is heard daily.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.3] Jobs and economic development in the language's heartland (the area where it is strongest), and planning and housing controls, so speakers can afford to stay.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.4] Language commissioners (officials who enforce language rights) and public-service rights.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.5] Community and family support, culture and festivals, because a language lives only if people use it socially.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.6] Cross-border and EU recognition, so the language has support beyond one state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.18.7] Possible future measures, such as language conditions for housing in the Gaeltacht or more online content, count alongside measures already in place.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.b] Irish is Ireland's national setting for this topic. Its legal status is strong, but the number of people who use it every day is small.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.0] Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution makes Irish the national language and the first official language; English is the second official language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.1] Census 2022: about 1.87 million people (about 40%) said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.2] Census 2022: the Gaeltacht (the Irish-speaking regions) had a population of about 106,000, of whom about 20,000 spoke Irish daily outside education.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.3] Census 1851, the first count: about 1.5 million Irish speakers (about 23%), which shows how far the language has fallen.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.4] Gaeltacht areas lie in seven counties: Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford and Meath.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.19.5] Main areas: Gaoth Dobhair (Co. Donegal); Conamara and the Aran Islands (Co. Galway, the largest); Corca Dhuibhne (Co. Kerry); Múscraí and Oileán Chléire (Co. Cork); An Rinn (Co. Waterford); Ráth Chairn and Baile Ghib (Co. Meath).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.b] The State has created a set of bodies, laws and plans to support Irish, especially in the Gaeltacht. Each is a point you can use under the strategies of official status, commissioners, planning and economic development.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.0] The Gaeltacht Areas Order 1956 and the Department of the Gaeltacht (1956) are the founding state measures for the Gaeltacht.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.1] Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980) is the Gaeltacht development authority. It grant-aids firms in the Gaeltacht, such as on the Gaoth Dobhair industrial estate, and supports Irish-language community projects, so that jobs exist locally.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.2] Official Languages Act 2003 (amended 2021): the public has the right to deal with public bodies in Irish. The 2021 amendment sets a target that 20% of new public-service recruits be competent in Irish by 2030.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.3] An Coimisinéir Teanga (Language Commissioner), set up in 2004, enforces the Act, so the public can complain if their right to Irish is ignored.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.4] Foras na Gaeilge is an all-island body, so it works in both parts of Ireland, set up in 1999 under the Good Friday Agreement to promote Irish.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.5] Gaeltacht Act 2012: 26 Gaeltacht Language Planning Areas, each with a language plan; Gaeltacht Service Towns; Irish Language Networks outside the Gaeltacht.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.6] The 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language 2010–2030 aims to raise daily speakers to 250,000.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.20.7] Northern Ireland's Identity and Language (Northern Ireland) Act 2022 gave official recognition to Irish and Ulster-Scots and created an Irish Language Commissioner.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.b] Schools and cultural events give people the chance to learn and use Irish. They matter most for children, because a language survives only if the young learn it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.0] Irish is a compulsory subject, so all pupils learn it, through primary and post-primary school, with exemptions.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.1] Gaelscoileanna are Irish-medium schools (schools teaching through Irish) outside the Gaeltacht; their umbrella body was founded in 1973.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.2] Gaeltacht summer colleges (coláistí samhraidh) bring learners to live with Irish-speaking families, so they hear Irish in daily life.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.3] Seachtain na Gaeilge is an annual Irish-language festival around St Patrick's Day. The Oireachtas na Gaeilge festival was first held in 1897.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.21.4] TG4 broadcasts Irish-language drama, sport and music programmes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.b] Despite state support, Irish is weak as an everyday community language. The 2007 study set the figure that shows how fragile the Gaeltacht is.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.0] The Comprehensive Linguistic Study of the Use of Irish in the Gaeltacht (2007) found that Irish is viable as a community language only where at least 67% speak it daily.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.1] Its 2015 update warned that Irish could cease to be the community language in most Gaeltacht districts within about ten years.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.2] Evaluation: Irish has high ability, meaning many people can speak it, but low daily use.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.22.3] The history of Gaeltacht boundaries since 1850: there is more on this in The complexity of regions (ii).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.b] Welsh is the international setting for this topic. Its history shows how law, industry and a government decision each weakened the language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.0] The Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 annexed (took over) Wales to England and barred Welsh speakers from public office unless they used English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.1] The 1847 government reports on education in Wales, known as the Treachery of the Blue Books, blamed Welsh for the people's backwardness.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.2] Coal and iron industries in the 19th century drew large numbers of English and Irish workers into south Wales, spreading English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.23.3] The flooding of the Welsh-speaking village of Capel Celyn in the Tryweryn valley in 1965, for a reservoir to supply Liverpool, went ahead despite almost all Welsh MPs opposing it, and became a symbol of Welsh powerlessness.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.24.b] Welsh speakers did not accept decline passively. Campaigners pressed the government for rights and services in Welsh.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.24.0] Cymdeithas yr Iaith Gymraeg (the Welsh Language Society) was founded in 1962 after Saunders Lewis's radio lecture Tynged yr Iaith (The Fate of the Language).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.24.1] The society used civil disobedience (deliberately breaking rules in protest), e.g. painting over English-only road signs.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.24.2] The campaign that secured S4C, described under media that protect minority languages, was part of the same activism.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.b] Activism was followed by a series of laws that gradually gave Welsh equal standing with English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.0] The Welsh Language Act 1967, a first step, gave Welsh limited equal validity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.1] The Welsh Language Act 1993 gave Welsh and English equal treatment in the public sector and created the Welsh Language Board.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.2] The Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave Welsh official status and led to a Welsh Language Commissioner from 2012.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.3] The National Assembly for Wales (1999), renamed the Senedd in 2020, makes laws through Welsh and English.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.25.4] The Cymraeg 2050 strategy (2017) sets a target of 1 million Welsh speakers by 2050.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.26.b] As with Irish, schooling and youth organisations are how Welsh is passed to the next generation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.26.0] Welsh is compulsory in all schools in Wales up to age 16, and Welsh-medium schools are found across Wales.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.26.1] Urdd Gobaith Cymru (a youth movement founded in 1922) and the National Eisteddfod (a Welsh festival of culture) promote Welsh among the young.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.b] The Census 2021 figures show a decline in Welsh speakers, and the heartland faces pressures such as second homes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.0] Census 2021: about 538,000 people aged 3 and over (17.8%) could speak Welsh, down from 19.0% in 2011. Wales has a population of about 3.1 million.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.1] The highest shares are in Gwynedd (about 64%) and the Isle of Anglesey (over half), in the north-west heartland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.2] Second homes and English in-migration push up house prices in Welsh-speaking rural areas, so local Welsh speakers struggle to afford homes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.3] From April 2023 Welsh councils may charge up to a 300% council-tax premium on second homes, as a response to this pressure.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.4] Evaluation: Welsh is often cited as the most successful Celtic-language revival, but its decline in the heartland shows the limits of revival despite statutory support (support set out in law).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.27.5] There is more on Welsh political history in A European regional case study of identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.b] Basque, spoken in northern Spain and south-west France, was suppressed under Franco and has been strongly supported since. It is the main comparison with Irish and Welsh.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.0] The Statute of Gernika (1979) created the Basque Autonomous Community, a self-governing region of Spain, where Basque is co-official with Spanish.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.1] The school models are A (Spanish-medium, with Basque as a subject), B (both languages) and D (all through Basque, with Spanish as a subject). Model D is now chosen for most pupils.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.2] Ikastolak are Basque-medium schools that began as a clandestine (secret) movement under Franco.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.3] Euskaltzaindia, the Royal Academy of the Basque Language, founded in 1919, created standard Basque, Batua, from 1968, so that speakers of different dialects share one written form.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.4] Basque television is provided by EITB, described under media that protect minority languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.28.5] Evaluation: use of Basque is rising among the young, which suggests the school models are working.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.b] Breton, the Celtic language of Brittany in France, shows what happens when the state gives little support. It contrasts with Wales and the Basque Country.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.0] Brittany was united with France in 1532, so it has long been part of a state where French dominates.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.1] Article 2 of the French Constitution, added in 1992, states that the language of the Republic is French.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.2] Since 2008 Article 75-1 states that regional languages belong to France's heritage.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.3] France signed the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 1999 but has not ratified it (formally approved it so that it becomes binding).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.4] Diwan: Breton-medium immersion schools (where pupils are taught entirely in Breton), the first opened in 1977, run as a voluntary association, so they depend on volunteers rather than the state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.5] In 2021 France's Constitutional Council struck down parts of a new law, the Molac Law, that allowed immersion teaching in public schools.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.29.6] Breton has declined sharply, and most of its speakers are elderly, so few young people are learning it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.30.b] Māori shows that a language can be revived with state backing and early-years schooling. It is a brief non-European example.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.30.0] Te reo Māori became an official language of New Zealand in 1987 under the Māori Language Act.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.30.1] Kōhanga reo (language nests), preschools run entirely in Māori, began in 1982, so children learn the language from the earliest age.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.31.b] Beyond individual states, Europe-wide bodies also recognise minority languages.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.31.0] The Council of Europe adopted the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 1992, which gives minority languages recognition across Europe.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.31.1] Irish's EU status is set out in Irish and the EU, and English as Europe's second language.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.b] This is a data skill from the Ordinary-level examination: a bar chart of languages spoken in Ireland by age group. If you practise it, invent clearly labelled illustrative data.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.0] Find the tallest bar to identify the age group that speaks the language most.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.1] Add the percentages for one language across the groups to find its combined share.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.2] A promotion idea for Irish should target the group where the chart shows the lowest use, and link the language to jobs, travel or culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s2.32.3] An advantage of speaking another language is access to jobs with multinational companies, to travel, and to understanding another culture.
