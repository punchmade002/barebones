TARGET geo-economic-environment-s2: 2. Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.b] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas formed from ancient living things) supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy (the energy taken straight from nature before conversion) in the 2020s. Their production is concentrated in a few places, so most countries must import them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.0] The Middle East (e.g. Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq) holds about half the world's proven oil reserves (oil known to be recoverable). Venezuela has the largest proven reserves of any single country.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.1] The USA has been the world's largest oil producer since the late 2010s, through shale oil and gas (trapped in rock and released by fracking, which fractures the rock with pressurised liquid).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.2] Russia is one of the largest gas exporters. Qatar is a leading exporter of liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so that ships can carry it).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.3] China produces and consumes about half the world's coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.4] Fossil fuels are traded over long distances by tanker, pipeline and LNG ship, so importers depend on exporters for their energy security (a steady, affordable supply of energy).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.b] Energy use is very uneven across the world. It follows wealth and industry, and the pattern is shifting towards Asia.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.0] Developed countries use far more energy per person than developing countries. Demand is now growing fastest in Asia, notably China and India, because their economies and populations are growing.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.1] The USA, Canada and Australia use many times more energy per person than India or most of sub-Saharan Africa.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.2] China has been the world's largest CO2 (carbon dioxide) emitter in total since the mid-2000s, but the USA emits far more CO2 per person.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.b] Burning a fossil fuel releases the carbon and impurities locked inside it. Each pollutant causes a different problem, so keep them apart.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.0] Carbon dioxide (CO2) is the main greenhouse gas released, and it drives global warming.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.1] Sulphur dioxide (SO2), mainly from coal and heavy oil (thick, impure oil), is a cause of acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.2] Nitrogen oxides (NOx), mainly from vehicles and power stations, are also a cause of acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.3] Smoke, fine particles (soot, tiny solid specks that can be breathed deep into the lungs) and carbon monoxide are released as well.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.b] The greenhouse effect is the natural trapping of heat by gases in the atmosphere. Burning fossil fuels adds extra gas to it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.0] CO2 traps heat in the atmosphere, so extra CO2 from burning fossil fuels enhances the greenhouse effect and causes global warming. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.b] Acid rain is a transboundary problem (one that crosses national borders): the countries that cause it are often not the ones that suffer.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.0] Normal rain is slightly acidic, at about pH 5.6, because CO2 dissolves in it naturally. Acid rain is rain with a pH below 5.6, not below pH 7.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.1] SO2 and NOx dissolve in water vapour to form sulphuric and nitric acids. These fall as wet deposition in rain, or as dry deposition of acidic particles and gases.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.2] Sources include coal-fired power stations, heavy industry and vehicles, because all of them burn fuels that contain sulphur or release nitrogen oxides.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.3] Emissions from Britain and Germany were carried by the prevailing south-westerly winds (the usual wind direction) to Norway and Sweden, which is why Scandinavia suffered from other countries' pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.4] The Black Triangle, the border region of former East Germany, Poland and Czechia, had lignite-burning power stations (lignite is a low-grade, smoky coal) that caused severe forest death in the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.b] Acid rain harms living things, buildings and people. The damage can appear far from the source, because the wind carries the pollution across borders.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.0] Forest damage: needle and leaf loss, and nutrients leached (washed out) from the soil. German forests, such as the Black Forest, were reported dying in the 1980s (Waldsterben, meaning forest death).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.1] Lake acidification: thousands of Scandinavian lakes were acidified and lost their fish. Aluminium released from the soil by the acid kills fish.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.2] Limestone and marble buildings and statues dissolve, through the same process as the chemical weathering of limestone (carbonation, where weak acid reacts with the rock).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.3] Corrosion of metal, for example, and respiratory (breathing) problems in people.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.b] Because acid rain crosses borders, responses had to be international as well as national. They work either by cutting emissions or by repairing the damage.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.0] The 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution, signed at UNECE (the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe) in Geneva, was an international agreement on air pollution that crosses borders.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.1] EU directives (laws binding on member states) limit emissions from large power stations.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.2] Flue-gas desulphurisation (scrubbers, which remove SO2 from the smoke before it leaves the chimney) is used at power stations. Moneypoint, Co. Clare, opened in 1985 and had scrubbers fitted in the late 2000s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.3] Low-sulphur fuels cut SO2 at source. Catalytic converters (exhaust devices that reduce NOx) became compulsory on new petrol cars in the EU in 1993.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.4] Sweden and Norway have limed acidified lakes (added lime to neutralise the acid) since the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.5] Countries switched from coal to gas and renewables, which release little or no SO2.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.6] Outcome: European SO2 emissions have fallen by well over three-quarters since 1990, and many lakes are recovering.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.b] Smog is smoke mixed with fog. It builds up when smoke from home fires cannot escape upwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.0] Burning coal and turf in homes releases smoke and fine particles, because the fuel burns incompletely in an open fire.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.1] In calm, cold winter weather an anticyclone (a high-pressure system with settled air) creates a temperature inversion, where a layer of warm air sits above cold air near the ground.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.2] The inversion traps the smoke near the ground, because the warm layer stops it rising and blowing away. This forms smog.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.3] Smog causes bronchitis, asthma, heart and lung disease, and extra deaths, because the smoke and fine particles are breathed in.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.b] Smog led governments to ban smoky fuels in city areas. London's disaster in 1952 was the earlier precedent, and Dublin's ban is the Irish case study.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.0] London's Great Smog, in December 1952, caused about 4,000 deaths at the time. Later estimates are far higher.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.1] The Great Smog led to the UK Clean Air Act 1956, which created smokeless zones (areas where smoky fuel may not be burned).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.2] Dublin suffered severe winter smogs in the early 1980s from household coal burning, which led to the ban.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.3] The sale of smoky (bituminous) coal was banned in Dublin from 1 September 1990. Black smoke levels fell by about 70%.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.4] The ban covered smoky coal only, so smokeless fuel and turf could still be burned in Dublin at first.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.5] A study published in The Lancet in 2002 estimated that the ban was linked to about 350 fewer deaths a year in Dublin from respiratory and heart disease.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.6] The ban was extended to Cork in 1995, and in stages to other cities and large towns as low-smoke zones. National rules from 2022 further restrict the sale of smoky solid fuels and wet wood.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.b] Beyond the air pollutants, getting and using fossil fuels harms land, water and rivers.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.0] Oil spills during transport, for example from tankers, pollute the sea and coasts. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.1] Landscape damage comes from coal mining and peat cutting, which scar and strip the land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.2] Thermal pollution of rivers is caused by the cooling water of power stations, which is returned to the river warmer than it was taken.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.b] Wind turns turbine blades that drive a generator. Turbines are built onshore on land and offshore at sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.0] Ireland: wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s, from onshore farms such as Galway Wind Park, Connemara (2017), and from Arklow Bank Wind Park, off Co. Wicklow (2004), Ireland's first offshore wind farm.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.1] Denmark: wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.2] Advantage: strong, frequent Atlantic winds give Ireland a good wind resource, and wind is the main renewable source in Irish electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.3] Disadvantage: output varies with the wind, and local residents object on grounds of noise, shadow flicker (flickering shadows cast by moving blades) and visual impact.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.4] Disadvantage: Derrybrien, Co. Galway, suffered a peat slide of about 450,000 m³ on 16 October 2003 during construction of a wind farm on bog. The EU Court of Justice fined Ireland in 2019 for the lack of a proper environmental impact assessment (a study of the likely harm before building).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.b] Hydroelectric power uses falling water to turn turbines, through three main methods.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.0] Dams and reservoirs release stored water through turbines at a controlled rate.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.1] Run-of-river schemes use the river's natural flow, so they need little or no stored water.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.2] Pumped storage pumps water uphill at times of low demand and releases it at peak times, so it acts like a store of energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.3] Ireland: Ardnacrusha on the River Shannon (Shannon Scheme, opened 1929, built by Siemens) and Turlough Hill, Co. Wicklow (1974), the country's pumped-storage station. Poulaphouca on the Liffey, Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid on the Lee, and Cathaleen's Fall at Ballyshannon on the Erne also generate power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.4] Advantage: output can be raised quickly at peak times (when demand is highest), as at Turlough Hill.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.5] Disadvantage: Ireland has little hydro potential left for large new schemes.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.6] Norway: hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of its electricity, for example.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.7] Three Gorges Dam, Yangtze River, China: the world's largest power station by installed capacity. It was completed in 2006, fully operational in 2012, and over one million people were relocated. It gives flood control downstream but traps sediment (silt that would otherwise travel downriver).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.b] Photovoltaic panels convert sunlight into electricity, and solar thermal panels use sunlight to heat water.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.0] Ireland: solar power has grown rapidly since the early 2020s, through large solar farms on farmland and panels on rooftops. SEAI (Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland) grants help homes to install panels.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.1] Disadvantage: Ireland's cloud cover and short winter days limit output, because panels need strong daylight.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.b] Waves and tides drive turbines or barrages (dams built across an estuary). The three technologies are different, so do not mix them up.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.0] Wave: the Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is a wave test site.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.1] Tidal barrage: La Rance, Brittany, France, a barrage across the Rance estuary, opened in 1966. It was one of the first and largest tidal power stations.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.2] Tidal stream: SeaGen in Strangford Lough, Co. Down, was installed in 2008 as the world's first commercial-scale tidal-stream turbine, using the fast tidal flow through the narrows. It has since been removed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.3] Advantage: tides are predictable, because they follow the moon's cycle. Disadvantages: high cost and marine impacts (harm to sea life and habitats).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.b] Geothermal energy uses heat from the Earth's interior, to heat water or to generate steam.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.0] Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (a plate boundary where the crust is thin) over a hotspot (a rising plume of hot rock). The Hellisheiði power station generates electricity from geothermal water, and about 9 in 10 Icelandic homes are heated by it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.1] Ireland uses low-temperature heat pumps only, which draw modest heat from the ground.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.2] Advantage: output does not depend on the weather. Disadvantage: high-temperature sources are found only where the crust is hot, as in Iceland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.b] Biomass is organic material (from plants or animal waste) burned or converted to energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.0] Sources: wood chip, energy crops such as willow and miscanthus, and biogas from slurry and food waste produced by anaerobic digestion (rotting without oxygen).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.1] Biofuels (fuels made from plants) are blended into petrol and diesel, by law in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.2] Edenderry, Co. Offaly, was converted from peat to biomass, an example of a power station switching fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.3] Advantage: carbon-neutral in principle, because the crops absorb as much CO2 as they release when burned. Disadvantage: energy crops compete with food production for land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.b] Nuclear power uses fission, the splitting of uranium atoms, to release heat that makes steam to drive turbines. Nuclear is not renewable.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.0] France: nuclear power supplies about two-thirds of its electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.1] Advantages: low carbon emissions, so little CO2, plus reliable and large output.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.2] Disadvantages, such as radioactive waste, accident risk, very high cost, and the cost of decommissioning (safely shutting down and dismantling) old reactors.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.3] Chernobyl, Ukraine (then USSR), 26 April 1986: a reactor exploded, and radioactive fallout spread across Europe, including Ireland. A 30 km exclusion zone remains.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.4] Fukushima Daiichi, Japan, 11 March 2011: an earthquake and tsunami knocked out cooling and caused meltdowns.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.b] Ireland has never had a nuclear power station. A combination of law, public opinion and practical limits explains why.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.0] The Electricity Regulation Act 1999 bans the use of nuclear fission to generate electricity in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.1] The ESB (Electricity Supply Board) planned a nuclear power station at Carnsore Point, Co. Wexford, in the 1970s. It was abandoned after large public protests and was never built.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.2] Other reasons: Ireland's small electricity grid, the high cost, the problem of waste disposal, and fears raised by Chernobyl.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.3] Sellafield, Cumbria, England, a nuclear reprocessing site on the Irish Sea coast, has discharges that Irish governments have long objected to, which adds to Irish unease about nuclear power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.b] Renewable energy comes from sources that are naturally replaced. These advantages apply to wind, hydro, solar, wave, tidal and geothermal power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.0] Renewables are inexhaustible, so supply is secure: the sun, wind, water and tides will not run out.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.1] They produce little or no CO2, SO2 or NOx, so they cause less global warming and acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.2] They reduce the fossil-fuel import bill and improve energy security.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.3] They create jobs, often in rural areas.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.4] Once a plant is built, the fuel is free.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.5] They help meet EU and national targets and avoid penalties for missing them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.b] Renewables also have costs and limits, and these explain why fossil fuels are still widely used.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.0] Renewables are intermittent (not constant), because wind and sun vary, so backup or storage is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.1] The initial cost of building a plant is high.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.2] Turbines and dams have visual impact, and cause noise and local objections.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.3] Remote sites need grid connections (cables linking them to the electricity network).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.4] Some habitats are damaged: birds are killed by turbines, and dams flood valleys. Peat slides can also happen at wind farms on bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.b] Ireland imports a large share of its energy, so renewable energy matters for both its environment and its economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.0] Ireland imported about three-quarters of its energy in the early 2020s, at a cost of billions of euro a year. Renewables reduce this import bill and the exposure to price shocks (sudden rises in world fuel prices).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.1] Ireland must meet its 2030 targets for renewable electricity and emissions. The Climate Action Plan aims for 80% renewable electricity by 2030, and missing EU targets exposes Ireland to the cost of buying compliance.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.2] Energy security improves through interconnectors (cables that link electricity grids): the East-West Interconnector to Wales (2012) and the Celtic Interconnector to France (due in the late 2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.3] Renewables provide jobs and income in rural areas, because wind farms, solar farms and dams are built there.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.4] Green power attracts multinational companies that want renewable electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.5] Cleaner air and water follow from burning less fossil fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.b] Map questions ask you to choose and justify a wind-farm site on an Ordnance Survey map, and to explain how conflict may arise. Match each feature of the site to a need of the wind farm.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.0] A wind farm needs a high, exposed upland or coastal site with strong winds, because output depends on wind speed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.1] It should be away from houses, to limit noise and shadow flicker.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.2] It needs an access road close by for construction, and a connection close to the electricity grid, by transmission line (the high-voltage cable that carries power).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.3] It should avoid protected areas and deep peat, because peat can slide when disturbed. It is best placed on land of low farming value.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.4] Conflict arises when a site lies near homes or in a protected or scenic area, so the site must balance wind strength against these constraints.

CANDIDATES geo-mapwork-s1: Map interpretation
[geo-mapwork-s1.30.1] 19th and 20th century sites include canals, railways and stations, mills, coastguard stations, later housing and industrial estates.
[geo-mapwork-s1.30.2] What it shows: the growth of planned towns and estates, and of transport and industry.
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.b] Many Irish place names are built from Irish words that describe the land or what stood there. Knowing the elements helps you read the history and site of a place from its name.
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.0] Settlement and building elements: baile/bally (townland or settlement), cill/kil (church), ráth/rath and lios/lis (ringfort), dún/dun (fort), cathair/caher (stone fort), áth/ath (ford), droichead (bridge).
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.1] Land elements: cnoc/knock (hill), sliabh/slieve (mountain), ard (height), drom/drum (ridge), gleann/glen (valley), mullach/mullagh (summit).
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.2] Water, vegetation and other elements: loch/lough (lake), inis/inch (island), cluain/clon (meadow), doire (oak wood), carraig/carrick (rock).
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.3] Size elements: beag/beg (small), mór/more (big).
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.4] Townlands are the smallest land division in Ireland; townland names are printed on OS maps, and exams ask which townland a point lies in.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.b] A 30-mark question asks for three reasons, each named, evidenced from the map with a grid reference and explained. Choose the three for which the map gives the clearest evidence.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.0] Bridging point: the town grew where a river could be crossed, and bridges and roads converge on it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.1] Route focus: several N and R roads and a railway meet, so the town became a market and service centre.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.2] Defence: a castle, town walls, a hill or a river bend guards the site.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.3] River or coastal site: water supply, transport, power for mills, and a harbour or pier for trade and fishing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.4] Flat or gently sloping lowland: easy to build on, with widely spaced contours.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.5] Fertile surrounding farmland: dispersed farms and no marsh, so the town serves as a market town for its hinterland (the area around it that it serves).
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.6] Ecclesiastical origin: an abbey or early church site. Tourism: a coast, beaches, lakes or uplands attract visitors to the town.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.7] Illustrative: the bridging point is the bridge at R 268 441, with roads converging on it; the route focus is the N99, the R990 and the railway station at R 272 445; defence is the castle at R 269 439 and the motte at R 280 436, guarding the crossing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.b] A function is a job a town does for its people and surroundings. Name each function, give the symbol or label and its grid reference, and explain it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.0] Residential: built-up area and housing estates, because they show where people live.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.1] Market and retail: a route focus and a central square, because people gather where routes meet to buy and sell.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.2] Services: hospital, Garda station, post office, schools and courthouse, where labelled.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.3] Transport: railway station, bus routes and motorway junction, such as the Rivertown station at R 272 445.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.4] Industrial: industrial estate or factory labels, because they show where goods are made.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.5] Tourist and recreational: tourist information office, golf course, hostels, camping and caravan sites, marinas, viewpoints and walks.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.6] Port or fishing: pier, harbour and slipway, because boats land and load there.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.7] Ecclesiastical: churches and abbeys. Defensive in the past: castle.
[geo-mapwork-s1.34.b] A town's layout records its history, with the oldest parts near the crossing point and newer parts at the edge.
[geo-mapwork-s1.34.0] Old core, the first part to be built: narrow streets, a square, and a castle or abbey near the bridge.
[geo-mapwork-s1.34.1] Newer edge, where there is more space: housing estates, industrial estates, a bypass or motorway with junctions, and ribbon development.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.b] Choose a site with a six-figure grid reference and give three reasons, each supported by map evidence.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.0] Flat land: widely spaced contours, because it is cheaper and easier to build on.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.1] Access: an N road, a motorway junction, a railway or a port, so that goods and workers can reach the site.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.2] Nearness to a town for labour, services and markets; space to expand; a site off the floodplain and away from protected or scenic areas.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.3] Wind farms need exposed high ground away from houses; hotels need to be near tourist attractions.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.4] State a disadvantage when asked, e.g. visual impact, traffic, loss of farmland or conflict with residents.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.b] Economic activities are the ways people earn a living, grouped as primary, secondary and tertiary. The map shows each through symbols and labels, and tourism can be judged from what the map offers visitors.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.0] Primary activity (using natural resources): forestry plantations, farming (shown by dispersed farms and field land), quarries and fishing piers.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.1] Secondary activity (making goods): industrial estates and factories, such as the industrial estate on the N99 at the north-eastern edge of Rivertown.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.2] Tertiary activity (providing services): services, tourism and transport, such as shops, hotels and railways.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.3] Each activity is evidenced by a map symbol or label and its grid reference.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.4] Tourism potential, accessibility and services, so that visitors can get there and stay: road access, parking, accommodation and tourist information.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.5] Tourism potential, attractions that draw visitors, such as antiquities, viewpoints, walks, cycle routes, beaches, lakes and golf courses.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.6] Each factor is evidenced with grid references and explained.
[geo-mapwork-s1.37.b] Statistical maps show numbers on a map. The OS map links to them through its contours and its key.
[geo-mapwork-s1.37.0] Contours are isolines (lines joining places of equal value), so they work like the lines on a weather map, the same kind of line used on isoline statistical maps, where the value is a statistic instead of height.
[geo-mapwork-s1.37.1] Choropleth, dot, isoline, proportional-symbol and flow-line maps are taught in Graph skills; there is more on them there.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.b] Map questions fall into short questions, matching questions, sketch maps and long map-evidence questions. Short questions are worth Higher 2 marks each; Ordinary 3–5 marks, often tick-box.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.0] Short questions: a six-figure reference for a named building, road junction or exit point; the antiquity, public service or feature at a grid reference; the height of a spot height, pillar or highest point; the length of a road, motorway or railway; the straight-line distance between two points.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.1] Short questions also ask for the area of the whole map, of a lake or bay, of land above a contour, or of land east, west, north or south of a grid line or railway.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.2] More short questions ask for the direction of travel; the part of the map a feature lies in; the type of forestry; what N or R stands for; a legend symbol; a cultural area (Gaeltacht).
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.3] Matching questions give features (such as a meander, a drumlin or a headland) to grid references, and ask for two drainage patterns, the type of lake or the aspect of a slope at a grid reference; elevation profile questions and true or false profile statements (Higher).
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.4] Sketch maps: Higher 20 marks at half scale; Ordinary 30 marks; typically four or five features.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.5] 15-mark map-and-process question (Higher): name and locate two landforms with six-figure grid references and explain one; locate a very steep and a very gentle slope.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.6] 30-mark map-evidence questions: three reasons a town developed at its location; three rural settlement patterns; variations in settlement distribution; absence and high density of settlement.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.7] More 30-mark map-evidence questions: three phases of historic settlement; site suitability with a six-figure reference; economic activity and tourism potential.

CANDIDATES geo-rivers-s4: 4. Human interaction: river processes
[geo-rivers-s4.0.b] Rivers are useful, so people change them. Every change alters how the river's processes work: its velocity (speed of flow), erosion (wearing away of bed and banks), transport (carrying of material), deposition (dropping of material) and flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.0] Reasons for interfering include making electricity, supplying water, irrigation (watering crops), navigation (letting boats travel), draining farmland and protecting towns from flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.1] The three measures studied here are hydro-electric dams, canalisation and flood control, each with Irish and international examples.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.b] A hydro-electric dam turns the energy of falling water into electricity. It does this by holding the river back and then releasing it in a controlled way.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.0] The dam stores water in a reservoir (an artificial lake behind the dam), and the water is then released so that it falls through turbines (wheels that spin and drive generators).
[geo-rivers-s4.1.1] The drop in height of the water (the head) gives the power, so a greater drop means more energy from the same water.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.b] Upstream of a dam the river meets still water in the reservoir. This changes its processes completely, because a river can only carry material while it keeps moving.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.0] Velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load (the sand, silt and stones it was carrying) there.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.1] The reservoir slowly fills with sediment, a process called sedimentation, which reduces its capacity (how much water it can hold).
[geo-rivers-s4.2.2] A delta (a fan of deposited sediment) can build where the river enters the reservoir, because that is where the flow slows and drops its load.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.b] Below the dam the river has lost its sediment and its natural rhythm of floods and low flows. Both changes alter erosion and deposition.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.0] Water released from the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam. This is called clear-water erosion.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.1] The floodplain (the flat land beside the river) and delta receive no new silt, so deltas and coasts erode because nothing replaces the material lost.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.2] Discharge (the volume of water flowing past a point) is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.3] Less flooding means less deposition on the floodplain, because floodwater is what normally lays down silt there.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.4] Where water is diverted out of the old channel, the bypassed channel carries much less water.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.5] Dams block migrating salmon and eels, which cannot pass the wall. Fish passes (routes around the dam) and trapping and transporting fish around dams are responses.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.b] The Shannon Scheme is one power station at Ardnacrusha, near Limerick, fed by a weir and a canal. It is not a series of dams.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.0] It was built 1925–1929 by the German firm Siemens for the Irish Free State, and it was Ireland's first large hydro-electric station.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.1] Parteen Weir (a low barrier across the river) diverts most of the Shannon's water into a headrace canal to Ardnacrusha. A tailrace (the channel carrying used water away) returns it to the river near Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.2] Lough Derg's level is controlled by Parteen Weir, so the lough acts as the scheme's reservoir.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.3] The original channel between Parteen and Limerick, the Old River Shannon, carries much less water because most of it is diverted.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.4] Salmon and eel stocks declined. The ESB built a fish pass, and it traps silver eels (adult eels migrating downstream) upstream and transports them past the dam.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.b] Ardnacrusha is not the only Irish example. Four more dams and power stations can be named in an answer.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.0] Poulaphouca dam, River Liffey: created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, Co. Wicklow. It generates electricity and supplies drinking water to Dublin.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.1] Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid dams, River Lee, Co. Cork.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.2] Cathaleen's Fall power station, River Erne, at Ballyshannon, Co. Donegal.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.b] The Aswan High Dam is the main international case study. It holds back the Nile in a huge reservoir to give Egypt control over a river that once flooded and shrank unpredictably.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.0] Aswan High Dam, River Nile, Egypt, was completed in 1970. Its reservoir is Lake Nasser, which extends into Sudan.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.1] Benefits are flood control, year-round irrigation (which allows more than one crop a year), hydro-electricity, and protection from drought.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.b] The dam's effects follow from the same processes as any dam: trapped sediment, clear-water erosion and regulated flow. They then spread into farming, fishing, health and settlement.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.0] Silt is trapped in Lake Nasser, so farmers downstream rely on artificial fertilisers instead of the natural silt that floods once left.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.1] The river bed below the dam is scoured (worn away) by clear water, which has spare energy to erode.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.2] The Nile delta coast is eroding, e.g. at the Rosetta promontory (a headland on the delta coast), because no new sediment arrives to replace what the sea removes.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.3] The sardine fishery off the delta in the Mediterranean declined sharply.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.4] Irrigated land suffers waterlogging (soil saturated with water) and salinisation (a build-up of salt in the soil).
[geo-rivers-s4.7.5] Perennial irrigation canals (canals that hold water all year) spread the disease schistosomiasis (bilharzia).
[geo-rivers-s4.7.6] Nubian people were displaced, and the temples of Abu Simbel were moved to higher ground.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.b] The Three Gorges Dam is a second international example, built on the Yangtze to produce power and to reduce floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.0] Three Gorges Dam, Hubei province, China, is the world's largest power station by installed capacity.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.1] It was completed in 2006 and fully operational in 2012, and over one million people were relocated.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.2] It aims to reduce flood peaks downstream, because the Yangtze floods of 1998 killed thousands.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.3] It traps sediment that once reached the river's lower course and delta, so those areas receive less of the sediment they depended on.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.b] Canalisation modifies an existing river channel. It is not the same as building a canal.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.0] Methods are straightening, deepening and widening the channel, sometimes lining it with concrete, cutting through meanders (bends in the river), dredging (scooping sediment from the bed) and building locks and weirs for navigation.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.1] Purposes are navigation, faster drainage of land, and flood control.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.b] Making a river straighter and smoother makes the water move faster. Almost every other effect follows from that rise in velocity.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.0] A straighter, shorter course has a steeper gradient (slope) and less friction (drag from the bed and banks), so velocity rises.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.1] Water and floods are passed downstream faster, so flood peaks downstream are higher and arrive sooner.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.2] Faster flow has more energy, so it erodes the bed and banks.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.3] Where the channel widens or the flow slows again, deposition increases and dredging is needed to keep the channel clear.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.4] Meanders, oxbow lakes (cut-off loops of old meander), pools and riffles (deeper and shallower stretches of the bed) are lost, and the river is cut off from its floodplain.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.b] Canalisation has been used for drainage and navigation in Ireland and for shortening and straightening big rivers abroad.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.0] Arterial drainage (deepening and straightening main rivers to drain land) was carried out by the Office of Public Works (OPW) under the Arterial Drainage Act 1945, e.g. the Moy and Boyne schemes. Rivers were deepened and straightened mainly to drain farmland, not to protect towns.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.1] Arterial drainage effects were changes to fish spawning beds, bank erosion and downstream flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.2] Mississippi: cut-offs (new short channels across bends) made by the US Army Corps of Engineers shortened the river.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.3] Rhine: the Upper Rhine was straightened in the 19th century under Johann Gottfried Tulla, which sped flood waves downstream and caused the bed to erode downward.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.4] Navigation in Ireland: locks and weirs on the Shannon Navigation control water levels and slow the flow.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.b] Flood control measures respond to the causes of river flooding, so it helps to know what those causes are.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.0] Prolonged or intense rain, and saturated ground (soil that is already full of water and cannot absorb more), send water into rivers faster than they can carry it.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.1] Urban surfaces and drains speed runoff (water flowing over land into rivers). Deforestation and drainage of bogs and farmland upstream add to it.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.2] Building on floodplains puts homes in the path of floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.b] Hard engineering uses built structures to hold floodwater back or move it away. Each one changes river processes, and some pass the problem on downstream.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.0] Embankments and levees (raised banks) confine flow to the channel, so deposition raises the bed and the banks must be raised again.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.1] With embankments and levees the floodplain gets no silt, and failure gives sudden, severe floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.2] Flood walls and demountable (removable) barriers protect towns.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.3] Dams and reservoirs store floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.4] Dredging and deepening increase channel capacity, but the flow is faster and downstream flooding can increase.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.5] Relief (bypass) channels take floodwater around the town.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.b] Soft engineering works with the river instead of against it. It slows water or gives it space, so natural processes carry on.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.0] Floodplain zoning uses planning controls on building, so that homes are kept off land that floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.1] Afforestation (planting trees) upstream works through interception (leaves catching rain), which slows runoff.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.2] Wetlands and bogs can be restored, and washlands (land that is allowed to flood) give the river more room.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.3] Flood forecasting and warning give people time to prepare.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.4] Effect on processes: natural flooding and floodplain deposition continue.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.b] Irish floods show hard engineering, dam management and flood-risk planning in practice.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.0] Cork, November 2009: the River Lee flooded when the ESB released large volumes from Inniscarra dam during very heavy rain because the reservoir was full.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.1] The releases were not the sole cause, because the rain was exceptional. The University College Cork and the city water treatment works were flooded, and many homes lost their water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.2] Clonmel, Co. Tipperary: an OPW flood relief scheme on the River Suir uses demountable barriers that are put in place when floods are forecast.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.3] December 2015: Storm Desmond caused prolonged flooding on the Shannon, in towns such as Athlone.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.4] CFRAM (Catchment Flood Risk Assessment and Management) is the OPW programme that maps flood risk.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.b] The Mississippi is the international case study for levees. It shows both what they protect and how they can fail.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.0] The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 led to the US federal levee and floodway system run by the US Army Corps of Engineers.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.1] The 1993 flood breached many levees along the upper Mississippi and Missouri.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.2] In August 2005 Hurricane Katrina caused levee and floodwall failures in New Orleans, and about 80% of the city flooded.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.3] Levees stop flood silt reaching the delta, which contributes to the loss of Louisiana's coastal wetlands.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.b] The Netherlands shows a soft-engineering response to flooding: instead of building higher walls, it gives the river space.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.0] Room for the River (Ruimte voor de Rivier), Netherlands, started after near-disastrous high water on the Rhine and Maas in 1993 and 1995.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.1] Measures include moving dykes (embankments) back, lowering floodplains and digging side channels so the river can spread out safely.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.b] Exams expect a judgement, so weigh what these measures achieve against what they cost.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.0] Benefits are protection of homes, electricity, navigation and water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.1] Costs are problems moved downstream, sediment starvation (rivers and deltas losing the sediment they need), habitat loss, high cost, and a false sense of security.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.2] There is a trend towards working with natural processes.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s2: 2. Human interaction with the rock cycle
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.b] People take rock, minerals and energy out of the earth's crust, and in doing so they become part of the rock cycle. The syllabus asks you to study one of four options: mining, extraction of building materials (quarrying), oil and gas exploitation, or geothermal energy production.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.0] Extraction removes rock far faster than natural weathering (the breakdown of rock in place) and erosion (the wearing away and carrying off of rock) can, and it exposes fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.1] Extraction creates new deposits of waste, which are stored on the surface, for example spoil heaps (piles of waste rock) and tailings (fine waste left after ore is processed).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.2] People therefore act as an exogenic process (one that works on the surface of the crust from outside): removing rock is the human equivalent of erosion, and dumping waste is the human equivalent of deposition.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.b] Mining removes valuable minerals from the crust. The method depends on how deep the ore (rock containing a metal worth extracting) lies, and the ore must then be processed to separate the useful mineral from the rest.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.0] Mined materials include, for example, metal ores (zinc, lead, copper, gold), coal and salt.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.1] Ore bodies form where minerals are concentrated by igneous activity (the movement and cooling of magma) or by hot fluids moving through rock, which is why ore is found in particular places rather than everywhere.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.2] Exploration comes first, so money is not wasted on a poor site: geological surveys and test drilling show whether the ore is worth mining before a mine is developed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.3] Open-cast (open-pit) mining removes the overburden (the soil and rock lying above the ore) and works ore that lies near the surface, because digging down from above is cheapest there.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.4] Underground (shaft) mining is used because deep ore cannot be reached from the surface; it goes through shafts and tunnels; room and pillar is one underground method.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.5] Processing crushes and grinds the ore and separates the mineral from the waste rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.6] Waste rock is stored in spoil heaps, and fine waste, called tailings, is stored in tailings ponds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.b] A named case study with specific facts carries a mining answer. Learn one Irish mine, one open-pit mine abroad, and Aberfan as an example of what can go wrong with mining waste.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath: an underground zinc and lead mine, Europe's largest zinc mine, in production since 1977 and owned by the Swedish company Boliden; the ore lies in Carboniferous limestone (limestone formed in the Carboniferous period).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.1] Bingham Canyon copper mine, Utah, USA: one of the largest open-pit mines in the world.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.2] Super Pit gold mine, Kalgoorlie, Western Australia: a large open-pit gold mine.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.3] Aberfan, south Wales, 21 October 1966: a colliery (coal mine) spoil tip built over springs on a hillside collapsed into the village after heavy rain, burying Pantglas Junior School; 144 people were killed, 116 of them children.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.4] Aberfan was a flow slide of saturated waste, a mass movement process (the downhill movement of loose material under gravity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.b] Mining brings real economic benefits, but it also changes the land and water around the mine. A good answer gives both sides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.0] Landscape scars and subsidence (the sinking of the ground surface) occur above and around underground workings.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.1] Spoil heaps and tailings take up land, so they remove it from farming or wildlife.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.2] Avoca, Co. Wicklow: centuries of copper mining, now closed, left acid mine drainage (acidic water draining from old workings) that discolours and pollutes the Avoca River with heavy metals (toxic metals such as copper).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.3] Dust, noise and heavy traffic affect people living near the mine, for example on local roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.4] Positive impacts are jobs, exports and local spending, so the mine supports the local economy.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.b] Governments and mining companies try to limit the damage, both while the mine runs and after it closes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.0] Environmental licensing and monitoring control the handling of waste and the quality of water leaving the site, so pollution can be caught and stopped.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.1] Tailings ponds are lined to stop seepage (waste liquid leaking into the ground) and capped (sealed over) when they are closed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.2] Lisheen, Co. Tipperary: a zinc and lead mine that closed in 2015; the site was rehabilitated (restored to a safe, usable condition).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.b] Quarries work rock and sand close to the surface for cement, aggregate (crushed rock used in concrete), building stone and roofing. This option is called quarrying on the Ordinary Level paper, but it is the same option.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.0] Limestone is quarried, for example, for cement, aggregate and lime for agriculture.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.1] Sand and gravel come from glacial and fluvio-glacial deposits (material dropped by ice and by glacial meltwater) and are used in concrete.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.2] Granite and other dimension stone (stone cut into blocks of a set size) are cut from the rock for building.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.3] Slate is quarried for roofing, such as Valentia slate, and marble for decoration.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.b] Quarrying follows a fixed order, working from the top of the site down into the rock and then preparing the rock for sale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.0] 1. Remove the overburden: the soil and glacial till (the mixed clay and stones left by ice) above the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.1] 2. Cut benches (stepped ledges) into the rock face.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.2] 3. Drill and blast the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.3] 4. Crush and screen the rock, grading it by size.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.4] Dimension stone is cut to size rather than blasted, because blasting would shatter the blocks.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.b] Cement is one of the main uses of quarried limestone, and making it links directly back to the rock cycle through the carbon locked in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.0] Limestone is crushed and mixed with shale, then heated in a kiln (a very hot furnace) to make clinker; the clinker is ground with gypsum to make cement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.1] Heating limestone releases carbon dioxide, because calcium carbonate gives lime and carbon dioxide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.2] The carbon dioxide returns to the atmosphere carbon that had been locked in the rock for hundreds of millions of years.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.3] Irish Cement's works at Platin, near Drogheda, Co. Meath, and at Castlemungret, Co. Limerick, are both based on Carboniferous limestone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.b] Each Irish material has a place you can name. Use these in your answer in place of general statements such as a quarry in Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.0] Sand and gravel: large pits in glacial deposits around Blessington, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.1] Granite: quarried at Ballyknockan, Co. Wicklow, from the Leinster granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.2] Marble: green Connemara marble is quarried in Connemara, Co. Galway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.3] Slate: quarried on Valentia Island, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.b] A quarry sits in the countryside, so its effects fall on neighbours, farmland and water as well as on the rock itself.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.0] Permanent scars, and loss of farmland and habitat, because the ground is removed rather than just covered.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.1] Dust, noise and blasting vibration, and heavy lorries on rural roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.2] Lowering of the local water table (the level below which the ground is saturated with water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.3] Positive impacts are jobs and the materials needed for housing and roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.b] Quarries are controlled through the planning system, and the land can be given a new use once the rock is worked out.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.0] Planning permission comes with conditions on working hours, and limits on dust and noise, so neighbours are protected.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.1] Earth banks and trees screen the quarry from view, so it is less of an eyesore.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.2] Worked-out quarries are rehabilitated as lakes, wildlife habitats, landfill or amenity areas (places for public recreation).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.b] Oil and gas are fossil fuels that form from living things buried in sediment, which makes them part of the sedimentary side of the rock cycle. They only become a usable resource where the right rock layers hold them in place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.0] The remains of tiny marine organisms are buried in sea-floor sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.1] Heat and pressure over millions of years turn these remains into oil and gas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.2] Oil and gas migrate upward through porous (full of tiny holes), permeable (letting fluids pass through) reservoir rock, such as sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.3] They are trapped beneath impermeable cap rock (rock that fluids cannot pass through), such as shale, in a trap: an anticline (an upfold of rock layers), a fault (a crack along which rock has moved) or a salt dome (a rising mass of salt that pushes up the layers above).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.b] Finding and producing oil and gas takes several stages. Ireland has two gas fields to use as case studies, and Corrib shows how a project can cause conflict.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.0] Seismic surveys (which use sound waves to map rock layers) locate possible traps, and exploratory wells test them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.1] Production, which starts once a well finds oil or gas, uses production platforms or subsea wells, with pipelines to an onshore terminal.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.2] Kinsale Head gas field, off the Co. Cork coast: discovered 1971, in production from 1978; Ireland's first commercial gas field; production ended in 2020 when the field was depleted.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.3] Corrib gas field, off the coast of north-west Co. Mayo: discovered 1996, and gas first flowed in December 2015; gas is piped ashore to a processing terminal at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo, originally operated by Shell.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.4] Opposition at Corrib: the Shell to Sea campaign objected to a high-pressure raw-gas pipeline near homes, and five local men, the Rossport Five, were jailed in 2005 for refusing to obey a court order not to obstruct the pipeline work.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.b] Offshore drilling can go badly wrong. The Deepwater Horizon spill is the international example to learn, alongside the North Sea as a major producing region.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.0] The Deepwater Horizon oil rig, leased by BP in the Gulf of Mexico, exploded on 20 April 2010, killing 11 workers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.1] About 4.9 million barrels of oil spilled before the well was sealed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.2] The spill damaged, for example, fishing, tourism and wildlife along the US Gulf coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.3] The North Sea, shared by the UK and Norway, is a major oil and gas province (a region with large oil and gas reserves).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.b] Fracking is a newer way of getting gas from rock that does not flow freely, and it is controversial. Like all oil and gas use, it brings benefits and costs that you must weigh.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.0] Hydraulic fracturing (fracking) of shale (a fine-grained rock that traps gas) releases gas; concerns include groundwater pollution and induced earthquakes (quakes caused by human activity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.1] Ireland banned onshore fracking by law in 2017.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.2] Positive impacts are energy supply and security, revenue and jobs, since a country with its own gas depends less on imports.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.3] Negative impacts are oil spills, damage to marine life and fishing, and conflict with local communities.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.4] Burning fossil fuels returns stored carbon to the atmosphere, contributing to climate change.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.b] Geothermal energy is heat from inside the earth. It is available everywhere deep enough, but it is much easier to use where the heat is close to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.0] Heat comes from the earth's interior, from radioactive decay (the breakdown of unstable atoms, which gives off heat) and residual heat (heat left over from the earth's formation).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.1] Temperature rises with depth at about 25 to 30 °C per kilometre; this is the geothermal gradient.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.2] Heat is greatest near plate boundaries and hotspots (places where hot rock rises from deep in the earth), where magma (molten rock) lies close to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.b] Where magma is near the surface, the rock heats groundwater to very high temperatures. This high-temperature (high-enthalpy) system can drive power stations and heat whole towns.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.0] Groundwater is heated by hot rock near magma.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.1] Production wells bring steam and hot water to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.2] Steam drives turbines (wheels spun by the steam) to generate electricity, and hot water heats buildings through district heating (hot water piped to many buildings from one source).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.3] Cooled water is pumped back down a reinjection well, so the water supply is returned to the ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.b] Not every country has hot rock near the surface. Two other approaches use lower heat or create the conditions for heat to be collected.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.0] Ground-source heat pumps use the steady warmth of shallow ground to heat homes. This is the low-temperature type, suited to places far from plate boundaries.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.1] Enhanced geothermal systems pump water into hot dry rock (hot rock with no natural water in it) to fracture it, so that the water picks up heat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.b] Iceland is the main international case study because geothermal heat is central to its energy supply. Larderello and Wairakei add historical and international examples.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.0] Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (a plate boundary where plates move apart) above a hotspot, which gives it geothermal heat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.1] About 9 out of 10 homes in Iceland are heated by geothermal water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.2] Geothermal power provides between a quarter and a third of Iceland's electricity; most of the rest is hydro (hydroelectric power).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.3] Hellisheiði is Iceland's largest geothermal power station, and Reykjavík has a geothermal district heating system.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.4] The Blue Lagoon is a pool of warm, mineral-rich waste water from the Svartsengi power station, now a tourist attraction.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.5] Larderello, Tuscany, Italy: the world's first geothermal electricity (1904) and first commercial geothermal power station (1913).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.6] Wairakei, New Zealand: geothermal power station opened in 1958.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.b] Ireland is far from a plate boundary, where geothermal heat is greatest, so its geothermal potential is low-temperature only.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.0] Ireland is far from a plate boundary, so it has only low-temperature geothermal potential.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.1] Warm springs at Mallow, Co. Cork, show that heated groundwater exists.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.2] Ground-source heat pumps are the main use, because they need only shallow ground, for example for heating homes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.b] Geothermal energy is clean and dependable, but it can only be used in suitable places and brings some problems of its own.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.0] Advantages: renewable (it does not run out), low carbon emissions, reliable because it does not depend on the weather, and cheap to run once built.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.1] Disadvantages: it is limited to suitable locations, and drilling costs are high.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.2] Hydrogen sulphide gas is released, giving a rotten-egg smell.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.3] Minerals are deposited in pipes as the hot water cools.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.4] Basel, Switzerland: an enhanced geothermal project triggered small earthquakes in 2006 and was abandoned.
