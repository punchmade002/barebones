TARGET geo-option-global-interdependence-s1: 1. Views of development and underdevelopment
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.b] People disagree about what 'development' means, and so about what counts as progress and who gets to set the standard. Every model in this subsection starts from a different answer.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.0] Economic development means growth in a country's output and income (the goods and services it produces and the money earned), usually measured by GNP per head (gross national product, the total income of a country's people, divided by the population).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.1] Human development means health, education, freedom and choice: people's ability to live lives they value, whatever the size of the national income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.2] Sustainable development means meeting today's needs without harming the future, so growth must not use up resources or damage the environment that later generations will need.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.3] Who defines development matters: definitions are usually set by rich countries and international institutions, which decides which goals and measures count.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.4] Measures follow views: GNP per head reflects the growth view and the HDI (Human Development Index, a score built from health, education and income) reflects the human view. There is more on this in the Economic Development notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.5] 'Underdevelopment' is itself a loaded word with two meanings. It can mean 'not yet developed', the modernisation view (poor countries are behind on a common path), or 'made poor by others', the dependency view (poor countries were held back by richer ones). Both meanings must be explained.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.b] Ideas about development have not stood still. Each era produced its own model and its own goals, usually reacting to the failures of the one before.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.0] Colonial era: colonies were treated as suppliers of raw materials for the ruling country, so their wealth flowed out, and colonial rule was justified by a 'civilising mission' (the claim that Europeans were bringing progress to others).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.1] 1950s–60s: growth and modernisation. Because the aim was catching up, development meant GNP growth and industrialisation, following Rostow's model (covered below).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.2] 1960s–70s: dependency theory emerged in Latin America, arguing that poverty was caused by the structure of the world economy rather than by the poor countries themselves.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.3] 1970s: the basic needs approach, a reaction to growth that left the poor behind, put food, water, shelter, health and education ahead of GNP growth.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.4] 1980s–90s: the neoliberal Washington Consensus (neoliberal means trusting free markets over state control). The term was coined by the economist John Williamson in 1989 for market reforms promoted by the IMF, World Bank and US Treasury.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.5] These reforms were privatisation (selling state-owned firms to private owners), free trade and cuts in state spending.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.6] From 1990: human development, measured by the HDI, and the idea of development as freedom.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.7] From 1987: sustainable development, which links growth to environmental limits because resources are finite.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.8] 2000s–2010s: goal-based views (the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals, sets of targets agreed by the UN) and rights-based views of development (development as people's entitlement).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.b] Environmental determinism is the oldest idea here. It holds that the physical environment, especially climate, decides how far a society can develop.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.0] Temperate climates (mild, moderate temperatures) were said to produce energetic, advanced peoples, while tropical climates were said to produce poor and backward societies.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.1] The theory was developed by 19th- and early 20th-century geographers. Ellen Churchill Semple, in Influences of Geographic Environment (1911), and Ellsworth Huntington, in Civilization and Climate (1915), argued that climate determined how 'civilised' a people could become.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.2] It was used to justify colonialism and racial hierarchies (rankings of peoples), presenting European rule as the natural order.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.3] Critique: it ignores history, including colonialism, slavery and trade rules, as well as politics and people's choices.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.4] Critique: it is contradicted by Singapore, which is tropical yet one of the world's richest countries, and by poverty in temperate places.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.5] Critique: its assumptions about the abilities of different peoples were racist, and they are rejected today.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.6] Reply: possibilism, associated with the French geographer Paul Vidal de la Blache, holds that the environment offers possibilities and people choose which to use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.b] Few geographers now say the environment decides everything, but some still argue that physical geography shapes development. This version keeps the idea without the racism.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.0] Jeffrey Sachs, in The End of Poverty (2005), stressed malaria, poor soils and a landlocked location (no coast, so no easy access to sea trade) as obstacles that aid could help overcome.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.1] Jared Diamond, in Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997), argued that geography (crops, animals and the east–west axis of Eurasia) explains why some societies gained power first.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.2] Botswana is landlocked, yet it became independent in 1966 as one of the world's poorest countries. Diamonds and stable government made it an upper-middle-income country, so physical limits can be overcome.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.3] Physical factors raise the costs of development without fixing its outcome, which is why Botswana could succeed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.b] Modernisation theory holds that all societies pass along one path from traditional to modern, as Western Europe and North America did. Poor countries are behind and need investment, technology, industry and Western values to catch up.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.0] Walt W. Rostow, an American economist and adviser to Presidents Kennedy and Johnson, set out his five-stage model in The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-Communist Manifesto (1960).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.1] Stage 1, traditional society: because output is low, people live on subsistence farming (growing food mainly for the family's own use) and limited technology.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.2] Stage 2, preconditions for take-off: investment in transport and education, new ideas, and trade opening to outside contact.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.3] Stage 3, take-off: investment rises to over 10% of national income and a few leading industries grow fast.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.4] Stage 4, drive to maturity: growth spreads across the economy and diversifies into many industries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.5] Stage 5, age of high mass consumption: because the economy is mature, most people enjoy cars, durable goods (long-lasting items such as appliances) and services.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.6] Rostow dated Britain's take-off to the late 18th century (about 1783–1802). He is not used to date any other country's take-off.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.b] Applying Rostow to Ireland is a textbook interpretation, not Rostow's own work. The model can be applied to Irish development as follows.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.0] Traditional stage: a farming economy with heavy emigration, including net emigration (more people leaving than arriving) of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.1] Preconditions: T. K. Whitaker's Economic Development and the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958), and an opening to foreign investment, with the IDA (the Industrial Development Authority, which attracts foreign firms) set up in 1949.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.2] Take-off: industrial growth in the 1960s and 1970s, free secondary education from 1967, and EEC (the European Economic Community, now the EU) membership from 1 January 1973.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.3] Drive to maturity: growth in the 1980s and 1990s led by multinational companies (firms operating in many countries).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.4] High mass consumption: the Celtic Tiger, from the mid-1990s to 2007.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.5] Limits of the model: the crash from 2008 and the EU–IMF bailout of November 2010 show that the stages can be reversed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.b] South Korea is an East Asian success story whose industrialisation seems to fit Rostow's stages, because it climbed from poverty to wealth within decades.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.0] The Korean War (1950–53) left South Korea among the world's poorest countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.1] Export-led industrialisation (building factories to sell goods abroad) was planned under Park Chung-hee from the 1960s.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.2] Large family conglomerates (chaebol), such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG, led the growth, and heavy investment in education supplied skilled workers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.3] South Korea joined the OECD (a club of mainly wealthy countries) in 1996 and was reclassified as a developed country by UNCTAD (the UN body for trade and development) in 2021.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.4] Its rapid industrialisation seems to fit Rostow's stages, which is why it is used as evidence for the model.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.b] Modernisation has been widely criticised, because many countries did not follow the predicted path. Each criticism below is a separate point to make in an evaluation.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.0] Eurocentric (judging everything from a European viewpoint): it assumes the Western path is the only path and that Western society is the goal.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.1] Linear and stage-bound: countries can skip, stall or go backwards. Zimbabwe, with land seizures from 2000 and hyperinflation (prices rising out of control) peaking in 2008, and Syria, where civil war from 2011 reversed decades of progress, show this.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.2] It ignores colonialism and unequal trade as causes of poverty, treating poor countries as simply late starters.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.3] It assumes wealth will trickle down from growth to the poor, which often does not happen, because owners and governments keep most of the gain.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.4] It is growth-centred and environmentally unsustainable: mass consumption for everyone is impossible on current resources.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.5] It ignores culture, inequality and gender, so growth alone can leave women and minorities behind.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.6] Many African states that followed modernisation advice did not take off.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.8.b] Modernisation was not only an idea. It shaped what governments and international bodies actually did, and it was top-down: decided from above rather than by local people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.8.0] Large prestige projects, such as dams and steelworks, were built to bring industry quickly, and were often built with little local participation.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.8.1] The Washington Consensus described above applied the same top-down thinking through markets, imposing reforms from outside.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.8.2] Debt and aid followed these policies, because poor countries borrowed to pay for such projects. There is more on this in the Empowering People notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.b] Dependency theory turns modernisation upside down. It holds that poor countries are poor because of how they were drawn into the world economy, not because of their own backwardness.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.0] The rich core (metropolis) countries develop by draining wealth from the poor periphery (satellite) countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.1] Development of the core and underdevelopment of the periphery are two sides of one process, because the core's wealth and the periphery's poverty are linked.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.2] Origins: Latin American economists and thinkers. Raúl Prebisch, an Argentine economist, headed the UN Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLA), set up in 1948.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.3] With Hans Singer, Prebisch argued in 1950 that the terms of trade (the price of exports compared with the price of imports) of primary-product exporters (sellers of raw materials and farm produce) tend to decline. This is the Prebisch–Singer thesis.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.4] Andre Gunder Frank, in The Development of Underdevelopment (1966), argued that Latin America was underdeveloped by its links with the core, through metropolis–satellite chains.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.5] Dependency is structural, through trade, investment and debt. It is not the same as dependence on aid.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.b] Dependency theorists point to specific mechanisms that keep the periphery poor. Each one is a separate point you can make.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.0] Colonial extraction of raw materials, using slavery where needed, moved wealth from colonies to the ruling country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.1] Dependence on one or two primary exports (raw materials or farm produce) whose prices are low and unstable, so incomes are low and hard to plan.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.2] Tariff escalation, where rich countries tax processed goods more heavily than raw materials, which keeps poor countries exporting raw materials, and rich-country subsidies (government payments to their own producers) that undercut producers elsewhere.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.3] Profit repatriation: multinationals send profits home, so the money does not stay in the host country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.4] Debt repayments drain income, so less is left to fund development.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.5] Brain drain: skilled people leave for the core, which means the periphery loses the doctors, engineers and teachers it trained.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.6] Local elites who benefit from the link with the core and resist change.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.b] Commodities are raw materials and farm products sold on world markets. These examples show countries tied to the core through one or two of them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.0] For example, Brazil's colonial economy moved through export cycles of brazilwood, sugar, gold, coffee and rubber, each tied to demand in the core.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.1] Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grow over half of the world's cocoa, mostly exported as beans rather than processed chocolate, so the higher-value processing happens elsewhere.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.2] Zambia depends on copper, which is most of its exports, so its income rises and falls with one commodity.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.3] Sudan's Gezira Scheme, from 1925, grew cotton for the Lancashire mills, so the farmland served the core's factories.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.b] Wallerstein developed dependency ideas into a three-part picture of the world, which allows for countries that change position.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.0] Immanuel Wallerstein's The Modern World-System (first volume 1974) divides the world into core, semi-periphery and periphery.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.1] Core countries control high-value production and finance, so they keep the biggest share of the profit. Periphery countries supply cheap raw materials and labour.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.2] Semi-periphery countries sit between the two, and countries can move between zones. South Korea and China have moved up.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.3] China began reforms in 1978, joined the WTO (World Trade Organization) in 2001 and has been the world's second-largest economy since 2010.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.b] Dependency theory explains some poverty well, but later events exposed its weaknesses.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.0] It cannot explain how the Asian Tigers (South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore) and China developed through trade with the core.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.1] Countries that cut their links with the world economy through self-reliance (depending on their own resources) stayed poor.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.2] It blames outside forces, so it can excuse corruption and poor government at home.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.3] The remedy, withdrawal from world trade, has rarely worked, because countries lose markets and technology.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.b] In the 1970s some thinkers turned away from GNP growth and asked what poor people actually needed, and what technology would suit them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.0] The basic needs approach of the 1970s put food, water, shelter, health and education first, not GNP growth.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.1] The ILO World Employment Conference (1976, held by the International Labour Organization, the UN body for work) put basic needs at the centre of development policy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.2] E. F. Schumacher's Small Is Beautiful (1973) argued for small-scale, appropriate technology that local people can afford, use and repair.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.3] Schumacher founded the Intermediate Technology Development Group in 1966, now called Practical Action.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.15.b] The human view judges a country by how people live, not by how much it produces.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.15.0] Amartya Sen, an Indian economist and Nobel Prize winner in Economics (1998), argued in Development as Freedom (1999) that development means widening people's choices and capabilities (what people are able to do and be).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.15.1] The first Human Development Report (by UNDP, the UN Development Programme) appeared in 1990. The HDI was created by Mahbub ul Haq with Sen. There is more on how it works in the Economic Development notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.15.2] The human view judges progress by people's lives, not by national output alone.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.16.b] Two further alternatives change who decides and what is protected: local people take part in planning, and growth stays within environmental limits.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.16.0] Bottom-up (grassroots) development puts participation, co-operatives (businesses owned by their members), NGOs (non-governmental organisations, such as charities) and women's groups at the centre, in contrast to top-down projects. There is more on this in the Empowering People notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.16.1] The Brundtland Report, Our Common Future (1987), set out sustainable development. There is more on its application in the Sustainable Development notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.16.2] The Sustainable Development Goals are 17 goals adopted in 2015, with a target year of 2030.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.17.b] If development is more than money, then GNP is the wrong ruler. These two proposals measure or describe progress differently.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.17.0] Bhutan's Gross National Happiness was put forward in the 1970s by King Jigme Singye Wangchuck as a goal ahead of GNP.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.17.1] Hans Rosling, a Swedish doctor and statistician, with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund in Factfulness (2018), argued that dividing the world into 'developed' and 'developing' is out of date and replaced it with four income levels. Most people now live in the middle-income levels.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.b] The pictures people see shape what they believe about a place. Media and charity images of Africa have been narrow, and that has consequences.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.0] Famine, war, disease and helpless victims, especially children, dominate media and charity images of Africa. Good news and ordinary life are rarely shown.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.1] Stereotypes follow, such as the belief that 'Africa is a country', when it is a continent of many different countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.2] Donors can take a 'saviour' view of themselves, and the people shown can come to see themselves as helpless.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.3] Investors and tourists may lose confidence in countries shown only as crisis zones, so the images can cost those countries income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.4] Audiences can tire of the same images, leading to donor fatigue (people giving less because the appeals feel repetitive).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.b] Coverage of this famine shows both the power and the cost of one image of Africa. It raised huge sums but also shaped how a whole continent was seen.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.0] A BBC television report by Michael Buerk in October 1984 brought the famine to world attention.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.1] Band Aid's single 'Do They Know It's Christmas?' (1984) and the Live Aid concerts (July 1985), organised by Bob Geldof, raised huge sums.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.2] The famine came during civil war in northern Ethiopia, and government policies worsened it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.3] The powerful coverage fixed an image of Africa as helpless and dependent, and hid the political causes: civil war and government policy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.b] Aid agencies, writers and speakers have pushed back against the single story of crisis.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.0] Dóchas, the Irish association of development NGOs, drew up a Code of Conduct on Images and Messages that its member organisations sign.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.1] The code requires images to respect the dignity of the people shown, avoid stereotypes and tell the full context.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.2] NGOs use dignified images and the people's own voices in their appeals, so the people shown speak for themselves.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.3] Kenyan writer Binyavanga Wainaina's satirical essay 'How to Write About Africa' (Granta, 2005) mocked Western clichés about the continent.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.4] Nigerian novelist Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's talk 'The Danger of a Single Story' (2009) argued that a single story about a place distorts it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.21.b] Positive examples from sub-Saharan Africa (the part of the continent south of the Sahara desert) replace the image of helplessness with specific achievements.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.21.0] Rwanda: after the genocide from 7 April 1994, in which about 800,000 people, mostly Tutsi, were killed in about 100 days, the country rebuilt. Since the 2013 election women have held over 60% of the seats in the lower house of parliament, the highest share of any country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.21.1] Kenya: M-Pesa, a mobile-phone money transfer service launched in 2007 by Safaricom (part-owned by Vodafone), lets people without bank accounts send, receive and save money by text message. Most Kenyan adults use it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.21.2] Botswana: its growth from diamonds under stable government is the counter-image to the landlocked-state argument above.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.b] Words used for poorer countries carry hidden assumptions. A loaded term suggests a judgement while sounding neutral.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.0] 'Underdeveloped' and 'backward' imply that poor societies are lagging behind a normal path.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.1] 'Primitive' implies that poor societies are at an earlier stage of human history.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.2] 'Developing' implies one direction of travel, towards the Western model, so it assumes every country wants the same goal.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.3] 'Less economically developed country' (LEDC) still measures other countries against the developed norm.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.4] 'Global South' groups countries by their position in the world economy rather than by wealth.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.5] 'Majority world' is a term popularised by the Bangladeshi photographer Shahidul Alam, because most of the world's people live there.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.6] The UN created the 'least developed countries' category in 1971. Most of its members are in sub-Saharan Africa, and it is an official term.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.b] These labels began as politics, not as a ranking of wealth. Their meaning drifted after the Cold War (the rivalry between the USA and the Soviet Union) ended.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.0] 'Third World' was coined by the French demographer Alfred Sauvy in 1952, by comparison with the 'Third Estate' of the French Revolution (the ordinary people, outside the clergy and nobility).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.1] It was originally a Cold War political term, not a ranking of wealth. The First World was the USA-led capitalist West and the Second World the Soviet-led communist bloc.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.2] The Third World was the rest: mostly newly independent former colonies that did not align with either bloc.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.3] The Bandung Conference, Indonesia, in April 1955 brought together leaders of newly independent Asian and African states. The Non-Aligned Movement (countries refusing to join either bloc) was founded at Belgrade in 1961.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.4] The Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991, ending the Second World and leaving the terms without their original meaning.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.5] The terms became a ranking, with first meaning best, and a byword for poverty, because people forgot their Cold War origin. They lump together very different countries, from Singapore to the poorest states.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.b] The North–South model divides the rich, industrialised North from the poor South. It replaced the three worlds as the main way of picturing global inequality.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.0] The Brandt Report, North–South: A Programme for Survival (1980), came from a commission chaired by the former West German Chancellor Willy Brandt.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.1] The Brandt Line runs roughly along 30°N and dips south to include Australia and New Zealand in the North.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.2] The Brandt Line is a 1980 concept, not a current UN or World Bank classification.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.3] The gap in income, health and power between most of Africa and Europe remains very large, which is why the idea still has some use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.4] World Inequality Report 2022: the richest 10% of people own about 76% of global wealth, and the poorest half about 2%. Poverty and deprivation also exist within the North.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.5] Global institutions remain weighted towards wealthy states: the five permanent members of the UN Security Council (the body with power to decide on international action) are the USA, the UK, France, Russia and China.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.b] Since 1980 many countries south of the Brandt Line have grown rich or powerful, so a simple two-part split no longer fits the world.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.0] Many 'South' countries are now high-income or industrial powers, such as Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan and Hong Kong, so 'South' no longer means poor.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.1] China is the world's second-largest economy, and India is among the five largest.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.2] The BRIC term (Brazil, Russia, India, China) was coined in 2001 by Jim O'Neill of Goldman Sachs, and the first BRIC summit was held in 2009. South Africa joined in 2011, forming BRICS. Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the United Arab Emirates joined in January 2024, and Indonesia in January 2025.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.3] The G20, set up in 1999, includes China, India, Brazil, South Africa, Indonesia, Mexico and Saudi Arabia, giving the South a stronger voice.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.4] Most of the world's people now live in middle-income countries, as Hans Rosling showed in Factfulness, so a rich/poor split misdescribes them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.5] In 2016 the World Bank announced that it would stop dividing countries into 'developing' and 'developed' in its main data tables, keeping its four income groups.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.26.b] Eurocentric thinking sees the world from a European point of view and judges other societies by European standards, as if Europe's path, values and history were the norm. Maps show it clearly.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.26.0] The Mercator projection (a way of flattening the globe onto a map) was designed by the Flemish map-maker Gerardus Mercator in 1569 for navigation, because lines of constant compass bearing are straight on it. Its effect is to place Europe at the top centre of the world map.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.26.1] On the Mercator projection, Africa is about 14 times the area of Greenland, yet the two look similar in size.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.26.2] The Peters (equal-area) projection, presented by the German historian Arno Peters in 1973 (the Gall–Peters projection), shows true areas and challenges this distortion.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.26.3] Greenwich (London) was chosen as the prime meridian (the line of 0° longitude from which other places are measured) at the International Meridian Conference, Washington, in 1884.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.26.4] Regional names such as 'Middle East' and 'Far East' are measured from Europe, so they only make sense from a European position.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.27.b] Eurocentrism also shaped how history was told and how development was defined, with Western experience treated as the standard.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.27.0] Christopher Columbus reached the Caribbean in 1492. Describing the Americas as discovered treats lands that were already inhabited as if they were empty.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.27.1] At the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 European powers set the rules for claiming Africa, which they then divided among themselves. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.27.2] African and Asian achievements are ignored in European histories, so Europe appears to be the only source of progress.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.27.3] Edward Said's Orientalism (1978) showed how Western writing portrayed the East as exotic, backward and inferior.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.27.4] 'Developed' was defined as becoming like Western Europe, with industry, urbanisation (growth of towns and cities) and mass consumption.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.27.5] Traditional knowledge and ways of life were dismissed, so Western experts designed projects without local input.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.28.b] The balance of economic power is shifting, and with it the idea that Europe is the model.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.28.0] China's Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, and the growth of South–South trade and aid (between developing countries, including China in Africa), show a shift away from European-led models.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.28.1] The rise of Asia and the voices of local people now challenge Eurocentric models of development.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-globalisation-s4: 4. How the global economy is organised
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.b] A basic processing unit is a factory carrying out one stage of production: processing a raw material, making a component (a part that goes into a finished product) or assembling a product. Multinational companies (MNCs, firms operating in many countries) place each stage in whichever country suits it best, which is why production is so widely spread.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.0] The Intel and Apple chains, such as those in 'One multinational company (MNC)', are examples of stages spread across several countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.1] Cheap labour suits labour-intensive assembly (work that needs many workers rather than machines), so firms put assembly where wages are low.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.2] Units are placed close to raw materials or to markets, which cuts the distance inputs or finished goods must travel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.3] A factory inside a trade bloc (a group of countries with shared trade rules) sells to the bloc's members without the barriers that apply to imports from outside.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.4] Tax breaks (reduced tax bills) and grants (money from government) lower the cost of operating in a country, so they attract factories.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.5] Falling transport and communication costs make distant factories practical, because a plant far away can still be supplied and managed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.6] Spreading production across many countries means that one disaster or strike does not stop all production, so the firm spreads its risk.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.b] Nike shows how far a firm can spread production: it designs and sells shoes but owns almost none of the factories that make them. Do not say Nike owns its Asian factories.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.0] Founded as Blue Ribbon Sports in 1964 by Phil Knight and Bill Bowerman; renamed Nike in 1971.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.1] Headquarters, design and marketing are in Beaverton, Oregon, USA, so the high-skill, high-value work stays at home.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.2] Footwear is made by independent contract factories (outside firms paid to make the product), mainly in Vietnam (the largest), China and Indonesia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.3] Nike sells its products worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.4] Sweatshop criticism in the 1990s (attacks on factories with very low pay and poor conditions), including of factories in Indonesia, led to factory monitoring and published supplier lists (lists of the factories Nike uses).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.b] Governments attract factories by creating special areas with easy trade rules. In an export processing zone, firms import components and export finished goods free of customs duty (a tax on goods crossing a border), and they receive tax breaks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.0] Shannon Free Zone, Co. Clare, set up in 1959, was one of the world's first export processing zones and Ireland's pioneer. The duty-free shop at Shannon Airport was 1947, so do not swap the dates.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.1] Shenzhen, China, became a Special Economic Zone (a region with special business rules to attract foreign firms) in 1980.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.2] Maquiladoras are assembly plants along Mexico's border with the USA, such as those at Tijuana and Ciudad Juárez, set up from 1965.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.3] A maquiladora imports components duty-free and exports finished goods to the USA, so it is an example of an export processing zone at work.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.b] Spreading production across many countries brings risks as well as savings. The costs fall on supply chains, workers and the environment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.0] Long supply chains (the linked stages from raw material to finished product) are vulnerable to disruption, as the 2021 chip shortage, the Ever Given blockage and the Red Sea diversions showed. There is more on this in 'Patterns in world trade'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.1] Branch plants (factories owned by a firm whose headquarters is elsewhere) close when costs change, and their workers lose jobs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.2] Some plants pay low wages and have poor conditions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.3] Moving components and goods over long distances has an environmental cost.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.4] Responses include reshoring (moving production back to the home country) and friend-shoring (moving it to politically allied countries).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.b] At world scale, regions play different roles in the global economy. The core is the wealthy, powerful centre; the periphery is the poorer edge that supplies it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.0] Core regions have capital (money to invest), MNC headquarters, research and development, skilled labour, good infrastructure and political power.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.1] The core's examples are the USA, western Europe's Blue Banana (Europe's core belt of dense development) and Japan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.2] The periphery supplies raw materials, food and cheap labour, with weak infrastructure and out-migration of people (people leaving to find work elsewhere).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.3] Most of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America and parts of South Asia are periphery.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.b] Some countries sit between the core and the periphery. They are changing their role in the global economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.0] The semi-periphery is made up of newly industrialised countries (NICs, countries that have recently built large manufacturing industries) moving towards the core.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.1] South Korea did so earlier; China, India, Brazil and Mexico are doing so now.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.b] The core did not become rich by accident. An early start, overseas empires and a self-reinforcing cycle of growth built its lead.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.0] Britain industrialised first, from the late 18th century (the Industrial Revolution), followed by western Europe and the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.1] Colonial empires supplied the core with raw materials and markets, and profits were reinvested in the core, which strengthened it further.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.2] Cumulative causation, a model by Gunnar Myrdal (1957), says success attracts more investment, people and services, which increases success.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.3] Backwash effects drain capital and skilled people from the periphery to the core, so the core grows at the periphery's expense.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.4] The Blue Banana runs from south-east England and London through the Benelux countries and the Rhine valley to northern Italy. There is more on this in 'Socio-economic regions'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.b] The periphery's economy was shaped by outside rulers for their benefit, which left it badly placed after independence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.0] Colonial economies were built around exporting one or two raw materials, so they had little other industry.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.1] Railways ran from mines and plantations to ports, rather than linking regions to each other, because their purpose was export.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.2] Borders were drawn by the colonisers, not by the people who lived there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.3] After independence these economies depended on falling commodity prices (the prices of raw materials), were burdened by debt and suffered conflict.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.b] Core and periphery are not fixed. Some places have moved up the ladder, and the North-South model is a simple picture of the pattern.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.0] Spread effects (the benefits of growth passing outwards from the core) and investment can raise peripheral regions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.1] The Asian Tigers and China moved from periphery to semi-periphery or core in about two generations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.2] Ireland moved from the European periphery to a core-like economy through MNC investment. There is more on this in 'Irish Regions - Core and Peripheral'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.3] The Brandt Line, from the Brandt Report of 1980, is a simple North-South model (a line dividing richer northern from poorer southern countries) of the same pattern.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.b] Sub-Saharan Africa is the main region excluded from world manufacturing. It has a very small share of world manufacturing and exports mostly unprocessed raw materials.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.0] Nigeria: crude oil is most of its exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.1] Zambia: copper is most of its exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.2] The Democratic Republic of the Congo produces most of the world's cobalt and exports it raw despite its huge reserves, amid conflict.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.3] Sudan: civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces since April 2023 has caused one of the world's largest displacement crises, with people forced to flee their homes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.4] South Sudan became independent from Sudan in 2011, taking most of the oil fields.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.5] The 2023 war is within Sudan, not a war with South Sudan, because they have been separate countries since 2011.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.b] Many linked problems keep sub-Saharan Africa out of manufacturing. Each one raises costs or risks for firms, or limits what the countries can do for themselves.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.0] Poor infrastructure: roads and ports are inadequate and electricity is unreliable, which makes running a factory costly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.1] Landlocked countries (with no coast): Africa has 16, such as Chad, Niger, Mali and South Sudan, and they face high transport costs to reach ports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.2] Political instability, conflict and corruption scare off investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.3] Low levels of education and skills, and poor health, limit the workforce.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.4] Small, poor home markets give firms little reason to produce locally.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.5] Heavy debt absorbs money that could fund development.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.6] The colonial legacy: economies were set up to export raw materials, and that pattern persists.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.7] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials, so processing at home does not pay; coffee beans enter rich markets with low tariffs, while roasted coffee faces higher ones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.b] Several measures try to help excluded regions join world trade and industry. They target debt, trade barriers and infrastructure.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.0] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative, begun in 1996, gives debt relief (cancelling or reducing debts) through the World Bank and IMF.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.1] The African Continental Free Trade Area, which removes trade barriers between African countries, began trading on 1 January 2021.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.2] Chinese and other foreign investment is building infrastructure.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.3] Fair trade (a system that pays producers a fairer price). There is more on this in 'Patterns in world trade'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.b] Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, MNCs, technology and the movement of people and ideas. It has changed what is traded, who trades and how goods move.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.0] About 80% of world trade is linked to the international production networks of MNCs (UNCTAD, 2013).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.1] Much of it is intra-firm trade, between parts of the same company or with its suppliers in global value chains (production split into stages across countries).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.2] Trade is increasingly in components rather than finished goods, because each stage of production crosses borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.3] Trade in services and digital trade has grown rapidly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.4] New trading powers, led by China, have entered world trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.5] The WTO (World Trade Organization) and trade blocs have lowered barriers, so goods cross borders more easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.6] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard steel boxes) and huge ports have cut the cost of shipping, so distant trade pays.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.7] E-commerce (online selling) brings small sellers into world markets, so they can reach buyers abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.b] Globalisation does not benefit everyone equally. Some groups and regions gain, others lose.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.0] Consumers gain cheaper goods, because production moves to low-cost countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.1] NICs and China gain jobs and growth, because factories locate there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.2] Manufacturing regions in the core lose jobs, because production moves abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.3] Excluded regions fall further behind, because they get little of the new investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.4] Workers in low-wage assembly can face poor and unsafe conditions, as the Rana Plaza collapse showed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.5] Transporting goods over long distances has environmental costs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.b] Since 2018 some governments have pushed back against open trade, raising barriers between major economies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.0] The US-China trade war from 2018, the US tariffs of 2025 and Brexit have raised barriers between major economies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.1] Governments are reshoring strategic industries such as chips (the microchips in electronics), bringing production back home so they do not depend on distant suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.2] Supply-chain worries grew after COVID-19.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.b] India's economy was tightly controlled before 1991, and it opened to globalisation only after a crisis.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.0] Five-year plans from 1951 directed investment and production, so the state, not the market, decided what was made.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.1] Protection (tariffs and rules that shut out imports) shielded Indian industries from foreign competition.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.2] The Licence Raj: firms needed government licences to produce and expand, which limited competition and growth.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.3] A balance-of-payments crisis (when a country cannot pay for its imports and debts) in 1991 forced change.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.4] Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh removed most licences, cut tariffs and opened India to foreign investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.b] Globalisation brought rapid growth, new industries and foreign investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.0] Bengaluru became known as the 'Silicon Valley of India'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.1] IT and business-process outsourcing (hiring firms abroad to run tasks such as customer service) grew rapidly; Tata Consultancy Services (founded 1968), Infosys (founded 1981) and Wipro grew, with Bengaluru as a major hub.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.2] Texas Instruments opened a design centre in Bengaluru in 1985.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.3] Maruti Suzuki, a joint venture (a company shared between partners) with Suzuki of Japan, made its first car in 1983.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.4] India is a leading maker of generic medicines (cheaper copies of branded drugs), earning it the name 'pharmacy of the world'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.5] Make in India, launched in 2014, aimed to attract manufacturing; iPhones are assembled near Chennai and Bengaluru.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.6] Services produce over half of India's GDP (the value of everything it produces), and India is among the world's five largest economies (early 2020s). A growing middle class has emerged.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.7] Hundreds of millions of people have been moved out of extreme poverty, as growth created jobs and income.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.b] The gains from globalisation have been uneven, and it has brought costs for some people and places.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.0] Inequality exists between cities and rural areas, and between states.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.1] Small farmers are exposed to world prices, so they can lose when prices fall.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.2] Farmers' protests in 2020-21 were against three farm laws, which were repealed in November 2021.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.3] Most workers are still in the informal sector (jobs without contracts, regular pay or legal protection).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.4] Pollution and accidents at MNC plants: the Bhopal gas leak at the Union Carbide pesticide plant in December 1984 killed thousands; Union Carbide was a US MNC.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.b] China is a second developing economy, which opened to the world after 1978. It gained enormous growth but also costs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.0] Reforms under Deng Xiaoping from 1978 opened China to foreign trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.1] Special Economic Zones from 1980 attracted foreign investment, such as Shenzhen.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.2] China joined the WTO on 11 December 2001, which gave it easier access to world markets.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.3] China has been the largest goods exporter since 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.4] About 800 million people have been lifted out of extreme poverty since 1978 (World Bank).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.5] Inequality exists between the coast and the interior, because growth centred on coastal zones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.6] Pollution from rapid industrial growth and dependence on exports are costs of this model, because a fall in world demand would hit China's economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.b] Bangladesh shows both sides of globalisation: jobs from the garment industry, and the dangers of low-cost production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.0] Ready-made garments are most of Bangladesh's exports; it is the world's second-largest garment exporter after China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.1] The garment industry provides jobs, especially for women.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.2] Rana Plaza, Savar, near Dhaka, collapsed on 24 April 2013, killing over 1,100 garment workers. It was a building collapse, not a fire.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.3] The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh, signed in 2013 by clothing brands and unions, introduced factory inspections.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s2: 2. Spatial variations in development
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.b] Development (how wealthy and well-off people are, in income, health and education) is not spread evenly. It varies between continents, countries, regions within a country and districts within cities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.0] The Brandt Line, from the Brandt Report North–South: A Programme for Survival (1980), separates the rich North from the poorer South. It dates from 1980, so it is not a current UN classification.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.1] The Brandt Commission that wrote the report was chaired by the former West German Chancellor Willy Brandt. The line runs roughly along 30°N, dipping south so that Australia and New Zealand fall in the North.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.2] The North (North America, Europe, Russia, Japan, Australia and New Zealand) is richer than the South (Latin America, Africa and most of Asia).
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.3] The line is a simplification because some countries in the South are now rich: Singapore, South Korea and the Gulf states.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.4] The terms First World and Third World are outdated. The critique of these labels belongs to the Global Interdependence option.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.5] Global inequality is extreme: the richest tenth of the world's people own about 76% of global wealth, and the poorest half own about 2% (World Inequality Report 2022). The justice angle is in the Global issues and a justice perspective note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.b] The Human Development Index (HDI) combines income, health and education into one score per country. Mapping it shows the same North–South pattern as the Brandt Line, with important exceptions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.0] Very high HDI is found in places such as Europe, North America, Australia, Japan and the Gulf.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.1] High and medium HDI cover most of Latin America, East and South-East Asia and North Africa.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.2] Low HDI is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (Africa south of the Sahara), with Afghanistan, Yemen and Haiti outside Africa. Examples in Africa include Niger, Chad, South Sudan, Somalia and the Central African Republic. Haiti is the poorest country in the Americas.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.3] The named rankings and categories are in the Measuring economic development note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.b] Even inside one rich trading bloc, development differs sharply. GDP per head (the value of a country's output divided by its population) shows a richer core and a poorer edge.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.0] Luxembourg has the highest GDP per head in the EU, Ireland the second-highest, and Bulgaria the lowest (Eurostat, early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.1] Luxembourg's GDP per head is inflated by cross-border commuters, who add to its output but live elsewhere, so it overstates living standards there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.2] The EU's core is a richer north-west belt, the Blue Banana, and its periphery (the edge, far from the main centres) lies in the east and south. There is more on core and periphery in the Socio-economic regions note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.3] Within member states, Italy's Mezzogiorno (the poorer south of Italy) is about 22% of Italy's GDP, with GDP per head a little over half that of the Centre-North in recent years.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.4] Romania and Bulgaria contain many of the EU's less developed regions, which fits Bulgaria's lowest GDP per head.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.5] An EU less developed region has GDP per head under 75% of the EU average. Such regions receive most of the EU's cohesion funding (money paid to narrow the gap between richer and poorer regions).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.b] Ireland is rich overall, but incomes differ by region. Dublin pulls ahead because of the jobs and functions concentrated there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.0] The Dublin region has the highest disposable income per person (income left after tax, available to spend) in the State. The Border and Midland regions are consistently among the lowest (CSO county income figures, 2010s to 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.1] County Dublin has about 1.45 million of the State's about 5.1 million people (Census 2022), so it holds about 28% of the population.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.2] Dublin's advantages are its capital and port functions, multinational and financial-services investment, universities and the airport. Together these provide most of the high-value jobs, which is why incomes are highest there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.3] The West and North-West are peripheral, with remoteness, weaker infrastructure, dependence on farming and small firms, and out-migration (people leaving to find work elsewhere).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.4] Peripheral location does not by itself mean poverty: the West is peripheral within a very rich country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.b] The State has tried to narrow the gap between Dublin and the rest. Policy combines regional bodies, planning targets and spending on transport and broadband.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.0] The BMW region (Border, Midland and Western) held Objective 1 status, the EU's top category for regions needing extra funding, for 2000 to 2006.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.1] Three regional assemblies (regional bodies of local councillors) have operated since 2015, so regional planning has its own bodies: Northern and Western; Southern; and Eastern and Midland.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.2] Project Ireland 2040, launched in February 2018, combines the National Planning Framework (the planning strategy) and the National Development Plan (the investment plan).
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.3] The National Planning Framework aims for the Northern and Western and Southern regions together to grow as much as the Eastern and Midland region, so that growth is balanced.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.4] It also aims for half of future population growth to go to the five cities: Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.5] Metrolink is a planned metro line from Swords, via Dublin Airport, to Dublin city centre. The National Broadband Plan (contract signed 2019) aims to bring high-speed broadband to rural homes.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.6] Transport investment can reduce regional income differences: better access attracts firms to regional towns, cuts costs and commuting times, and widens the job market. It also reduces congestion, shortens journeys and lowers emissions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.b] National averages hide local differences. Rich countries contain poor districts, and large countries contain regions at very different levels.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.0] Even rich places contain pockets of deprivation, and poor regions exist within richer countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.1] India: Census 2011 literacy was about 94% in Kerala and about 62% in Bihar. Fertility (children per woman) was about 1.8 in Kerala and about 3.0 in Bihar (NFHS-5, 2019 to 2021).
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.2] Brazil: the poor North-East contrasts with the rich South-East, an example of a poor region inside one country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.b] Physical factors affect development by limiting or supporting farming, health and transport. They influence development but do not determine it.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.0] The Sahel, the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara (Mali, Niger, Chad and Burkina Faso), has suffered repeated droughts since the 1970s, which limit farming and cause famine, because crops fail without reliable rain.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.1] In Ethiopia, outside the Sahel, drought worsened by civil war led to the 1984 to 1985 famine, followed by Live Aid (the 1985 charity appeal) in 1985.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.2] Extreme heat and tropical storms add difficulty, while temperate climates (mild, without extremes) suit productive farming.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.3] Climate does not decide development: Singapore is tropical, yet it is one of the world's richest countries. Botswana shows that a poor start can be overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.4] Malaria and other tropical diseases reduce life expectancy, the productivity of workers and children's schooling, because ill people cannot work or attend school. The malaria figure is in the Measuring economic development note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.5] Mountains, deserts and poor soils limit farming and make transport costly, while fertile lowlands and river plains support dense populations and farming surpluses.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.b] Where a country lies, what lies under it and what hazards strike it all shape its wealth. These can help or hold back development.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.0] Landlocked countries (with no coast) depend on neighbours' roads and ports, which raises trade costs. Landlocked developing countries include Chad, Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, Malawi, Uganda, Afghanistan and Bolivia.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.1] Coastal countries with good harbours trade more easily, because ships can reach them directly. Peripheral location adds to transport costs for Ireland and for the EU's outlying regions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.2] Natural resources can fund development: Botswana's diamonds were discovered at Orapa in 1967 and are mined by Debswana, a joint venture of the government and De Beers.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.3] Norway's oil has been extracted from the North Sea since the early 1970s, and its sovereign wealth fund (a state-owned investment fund), the Government Pension Fund Global, is one of the world's largest.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.4] Resources can also bring the resource curse of corruption, conflict and dependence on one export. Nigeria's oil from the Niger Delta dominates its exports, and oil spills and conflict have affected the Delta.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.5] The DR Congo produces about 70% of the world's cobalt (early 2020s), yet it is one of the poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.6] Haiti's earthquake of 12 January 2010, magnitude 7.0, struck near Port-au-Prince and killed over 100,000 people (estimates vary).
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.7] Floods, cyclones and droughts destroy infrastructure and set poor countries back for years, while rich countries recover faster.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.b] Social factors concern the people themselves: how many there are, how educated and healthy they are, and how women are treated. Each affects how productive a country can become.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.0] Rapid population growth spreads spending on schools and health thinly and creates more young dependants (children supported by workers). Niger has the world's highest fertility rate, about 6 to 7 children per woman (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.1] A falling birth rate can give a demographic dividend, a larger share of working-age people to drive growth.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.2] An educated workforce raises productivity and attracts investment. Low literacy, especially among women, holds development back, as the Kerala and Bihar contrast shows.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.3] Disease, malnutrition and HIV/AIDS reduce the workforce and life expectancy, and health spending raises productivity.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.4] Where girls are educated and women work outside the home, families are smaller, children are healthier and household incomes are higher.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.5] Brain drain: doctors, nurses and graduates leave poor countries, which loses skills. Remittances (money sent home by migrants) help offset this; India is the world's largest recipient of remittances (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.6] Water, sanitation, electricity, roads and internet are both a cause and a result of development: richer places can afford them, and having them makes growth easier.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.b] Cultural factors are the beliefs, customs and social divisions of a society. They can widen or narrow people's opportunities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.0] Attitudes to women's roles and girls' education matter because they shape schooling: early marriage reduces schooling and raises fertility.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.1] Social structures and inequality limit opportunity for some groups: caste in India, land concentration in Latin America (a few owners holding most land) and ethnic divisions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.2] Many languages within borders drawn in the colonial period can make national unity and education harder. A world language such as English can help, as in India's IT sector.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.3] The colonial cultural legacy, which still shapes society, includes European languages, education aimed at a small elite and a culture of exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.4] Attitudes to enterprise, saving and education differ between societies: South Korea places a high value on education. Traditional farming practices and land tenure (how land is owned and held) can slow change.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.b] Governments set the conditions for growth: peace, honest institutions and sensible policy attract investment, while conflict and mismanagement drive it away.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.0] Political stability and good governance attract investment and allow long-term planning. Botswana became independent in 1966 and has held regular elections as a multi-party democracy since independence.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.1] War destroys infrastructure, displaces people and scares off investors, so growth stops. Syria's civil war from 2011 displaced millions. South Sudan became independent in July 2011 and entered civil war in December 2013; There is more on Sudan in the Case study of a developing economy note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.2] Corruption (misuse of office for private gain) diverts public money and aid. Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index ranks Denmark at or near the top and Somalia and South Sudan at or near the bottom (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.3] Colonial history left borders, export economies and weak institutions; the detail is in the Case study of a developing economy note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.4] Government economic policy matters: education, infrastructure, openness to trade and investment, and industrial policy (state support for chosen industries), as in South Korea, China and Ireland, contrast with mismanagement, as in Zimbabwe and Venezuela.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.5] Debt repayments take money from schools and hospitals, and rich countries' farm subsidies and tariffs (taxes on imports) disadvantage poor exporters. The detail is in the Global issues and a justice perspective note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.6] EU membership and funds helped Ireland, Spain and Portugal, all major recipients of EU regional and cohesion funds, to converge with richer states (catch up with them).
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.b] Levels of development are not permanent. Countries can move up or down, and theory suggests why gaps can persist or close.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.0] Development is not fixed: countries can rise, as Ireland, South Korea, China and Botswana have, or fall, as Zimbabwe, Venezuela and Syria have.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.1] A falling HDI is usually caused by war, economic collapse or disease, because each destroys incomes, health or schooling.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.2] Cumulative causation: core regions keep growing because they attract capital and skilled migrants, so success feeds further success.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.3] Spread effects: growth can sometimes spread from the core to the periphery. Full core–periphery theory is in The Global Economy: Multinationals and Globalisation chapter; models of development are in the Global Interdependence option.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.b] South Korea went from war-torn poverty to a high-income industrial economy. Its growth came from planning, industry and education rather than resources.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.0] The Korean War (1950 to 1953) destroyed so much that it left the South among the world's poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.1] From the 1960s, under Park Chung-hee, the government planned export-led industrialisation (growth by making goods to sell abroad): textiles first, then steel, shipbuilding, cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.2] Chaebol, large family-controlled conglomerates (groups of many firms) such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG, drove the industrial growth.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.3] The government invested heavily in education. South Korea joined the OECD (a club of rich countries) in 1996.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.4] The 1997 Asian financial crisis hit South Korea hard, but it recovered. UNCTAD (the UN trade and development body) reclassified it as a developed country in 2021, and it is now a very high HDI country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.5] Its growth came from industry and education, not from natural resources.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.b] China's rise began with deliberate reform. It has lifted huge numbers out of poverty, but the gains are uneven.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.0] Economic reforms began in 1978 under Deng Xiaoping, known as opening up (allowing trade and foreign investment).
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.1] Special Economic Zones (areas with special rules to attract foreign business) were set up from 1980, the first at Shenzhen, beside Hong Kong.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.2] China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, which gave its exporters easier access to world markets.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.3] The World Bank estimates that close to 800 million Chinese were lifted out of extreme poverty since 1978 (World Bank, 2022).
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.4] China has been the world's second-largest economy since 2010, overtaking Japan, but it is upper-middle income, not a developed country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.5] Coastal provinces are much richer than the western interior, and hundreds of millions of rural people migrated to coastal cities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.b] Botswana began as one of the world's poorest countries and put its diamond revenue into roads, schools and clinics. It shows that a poor start can be overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.0] Botswana became independent in 1966 as one of the world's poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.1] Diamond revenue funded roads, schools and clinics, so the money was turned into development, and stable democratic government and comparatively low corruption helped.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.2] Botswana is now an upper-middle-income country, so its start as one of the poorest was overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.3] HIV/AIDS sharply reduced life expectancy in the 1990s and 2000s; it recovered with free antiretroviral treatment.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.4] Dependence on diamonds is a weakness of the economy, because the country relies on one resource.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.b] Ireland moved from stagnation and emigration in the 1950s to one of the richest EU states. Policy change, foreign investment and EU membership drove it, with a serious setback in 2008.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.0] Heavy emigration in the 1950s, a sign of stagnation: net emigration was about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.1] T. K. Whitaker's Economic Development (1958) led to the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958), which offered free trade, grants and tax breaks for foreign firms.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.2] Free secondary education came in 1967, and Ireland joined the EEC (the forerunner of the EU) on 1 January 1973.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.3] The Celtic Tiger boom ran from the mid-1990s to 2007. Free secondary education, free trade and foreign investment helped drive it.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.4] A property crash and banking crisis from 2008 led to an EU–IMF bailout of €85 billion, agreed in November 2010; Ireland exited the programme in December 2013.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.b] Development can also go into reverse. These three countries show economic mismanagement, dependence on one export and war pulling living standards down.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.0] Zimbabwe: land seizures from 2000 cut farm output, so the economy suffered; hyperinflation (prices rising out of control) peaked in 2008, and the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned in 2009.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.1] Venezuela: the economy depended on oil. After oil prices collapsed in 2014, the economy shrank by well over half, and more than 7 million people emigrated (2024).
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.2] Syria: its HDI fell because of the civil war that began in 2011, reversing decades of progress.
