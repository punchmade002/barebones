TARGET geo-option-geoecology-s1: 1. Soil composition and characteristics
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.b] Soil is the thin layer of loose material on the earth's surface in which plants grow. Its formation is called pedogenesis, and it starts with the rock or deposit the soil forms from, known as the parent material.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.0] Soil is made of four components: mineral matter (particles of weathered rock), organic matter (living and dead material), water and air. Every later block looks at how one of these shapes the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.1] Residual soils form from rock weathered in situ, meaning in place, directly beneath them, so the soil and the rock below are closely related.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.2] Transported soils form on redeposited weathered material that has been carried and laid down by ice, water, wind or volcanic activity, so they can differ from the rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.3] Transported parent materials include glacial till (boulder clay), fluvio-glacial sands and gravels (laid down by meltwater from ice), alluvium (river deposits), loess (wind-blown silt), wind-blown sand and volcanic ash.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.0.4] Weathering (the breakdown of rock at the surface) produces regolith, the loose layer that soil develops from. Freeze-thaw, carbonation and hydrolysis are among the processes involved. There is more on this in Weathering processes, in Rocks, Weathering and Mass Movement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.b] Named places make the residual and transported types concrete. Remember that the same area can look residual on a map of rock types but be transported in fact.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.0] Residual examples: the limestone of the Burren and the granite of the Wicklow Mountains, which gives acidic, sandy soils. Most Midland soils formed on boulder clay over the limestone, so they are transported, not residual.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.1] Transported examples: the boulder clay (till) plains of the Irish Midlands, and alluvium on the floodplain of the Shannon Callows, meadows beside the River Shannon that flood in winter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.1.2] Loess (wind-blown silt), called limon in France, covers plateaux of the Paris Basin, such as the Beauce. There is more on this in A Core European Region: the Paris Basin.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.b] Soils take time to develop, and in Ireland the clock was reset by the ice. This is why Irish soils are young and why they often do not match the rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.0] Most Irish soils formed on glacial deposits laid down during the last (Midlandian) glaciation, which ended about 10,000 years ago. There is more on this in Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.2.1] So most Irish soils have developed in about 10,000 years or less, since the ice melted, and they often differ from the solid rock beneath them because the glacial deposits came from elsewhere.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.b] A soil profile is a vertical section from the surface down to bedrock, divided into layers called horizons. A soil pit or road cutting shows it, and the horizons are named by letters.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.0] O horizon: organic litter (undecomposed plant and animal remains) and humus (the dark, decomposed organic material) on the surface.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.1] A horizon: topsoil, with mineral matter mixed with humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.2] B horizon: subsoil, which receives material washed down from above.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.3] C horizon: weathered parent material, the rock or deposit the soil forms from.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.4] R horizon: bedrock, the solid rock beneath.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.3.5] There is more on the full profiles of named soils in Soil processes and the global pattern of soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.b] Soil is mostly mineral matter, with small shares of organic matter, water and air. The figures below are the textbook values for a good loam, a balanced, fertile soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.0] A typical good loam has about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water and 25% air, by volume.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.1] These proportions describe a well-structured loam. They are not fixed values for every soil, so don't present them as true of all soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.2] Water and air share the pore spaces (the gaps between particles), so when one rises the other falls. A wet soil is poorly aerated for this reason.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.4.3] Diagram: a pie chart of the four components.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.b] Mineral matter is the largest component, and the parent rock decides what kind of mineral matter a soil gets. It is the first of the four components to know for an essay on soil development.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.0] Mineral matter is particles of weathered rock, from stones down to clay. It forms the soil's skeleton and decides its texture (the mix of particle sizes).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.1] As it weathers, mineral matter releases nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium, which plants then take up through their roots.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.2] Parent rock controls the result: limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils (bases are the chemicals that neutralise acid).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.3] Granite gives sandy, acidic soils with quartz grains and clay from weathered feldspar.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.4] Sandstone gives sandy, acidic, free-draining soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.5.5] Clay particles are the chemically active part of the mineral matter, because they hold on to nutrients and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.b] Organic matter is a small share of the soil by volume, but it gives soil its dark colour, its crumbly structure and much of its fertility. The key is the difference between litter and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.0] Organic matter is living organisms (bacteria, fungi, earthworms, insects and roots) plus dead plant and animal remains.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.1] Litter is undecomposed material. Decomposers (organisms that feed on dead material) break it down into humus, a dark, sticky, jelly-like substance, which is the end product of decomposition.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.2] Humus darkens the soil, binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients, releases nutrients slowly, and is the main store of nitrogen in the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.3] Earthworms mix and aerate the soil, drag litter down into it, and their casts (droppings) improve structure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.4] Charles Darwin's last book (1881), The Formation of Vegetable Mould through the Action of Worms, showed how earthworms mix and enrich soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.6.5] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (bacteria that turn nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use), such as those in the root nodules of legumes, add nitrogen. Clover is the standard example of a legume in Irish grassland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.b] Humus comes in different forms depending on the vegetation above and the organisms below. The forms are named mull, moder and mor, and each is linked to a soil type.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.0] Mull humus is mild, crumbly, well decomposed and well mixed by earthworms. It is slightly acidic to neutral and forms under deciduous woodland and grassland, giving brown earths.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.1] Mor humus is acidic, fibrous and poorly decomposed, with few earthworms because the acidity does not suit them. It forms under coniferous forest and heath, giving podzols.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.7.2] Moder is intermediate between mull and mor.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.b] Water enters the soil as precipitation and is held in its pores. It exists in three forms, and only some of it is any use to plants.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.0] Water dissolves nutrients to form the soil solution, from which roots absorb them.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.1] Gravitational water drains down through large pores after rain and carries nutrients away. This is the cause of leaching (the washing of nutrients down out of the soil).
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.2] Capillary water is held in small pores by surface tension. It is available to plants and can move up as well as down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.3] Hygroscopic water is a thin film bound tightly to particles, so plants cannot use it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.4] Field capacity is the water a soil holds after gravitational water has drained away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.8.5] Wilting point is reached when only water that plants cannot extract is left, so the plants wilt.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.b] Which way water moves depends on whether rain or evaporation wins. This decides what the soil loses or collects, and it leads into the soil processes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.0] Where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration (water lost by evaporation and plant transpiration), water moves down and leaches the soil. This is the situation in Ireland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.1] Where evaporation exceeds precipitation, capillary action raises water to the surface and leaves salts and lime there, as in deserts and grasslands.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.9.2] Too much water fills all the pores and causes waterlogging, which leads to gleying (the grey, rusty-mottled soil of waterlogged ground). There is more on this in Soil processes and the global pattern of soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.b] Air fills whatever pore space water does not. Living things in the soil need it, and whether it is plentiful or scarce changes how the soil forms.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.0] Air fills the pores not occupied by water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.1] Soil air supplies oxygen for root respiration and for the aerobic organisms (those that need oxygen) that decompose litter, and nitrogen for nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.2] Soil air is richer in carbon dioxide than the atmosphere. This forms weak carbonic acid in soil water, which aids chemical weathering.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.3] Poorly aerated (anaerobic, meaning without oxygen) soils decompose slowly, so organic matter builds up as peat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.10.4] In poorly aerated soils iron is reduced (it gains electrons and changes chemical form), giving grey colours.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.b] The four components do not act separately: each changes how the others behave. Showing this interaction is part of a full answer on soil development.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.0] Humus improves crumb structure, which opens pores for both air and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.1] Clay holds water but reduces air, because its tiny pores fill with water and leave little room for air.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.11.2] Organisms need both air and water to work, so a soil that is too wet or too dry supports less life and less decomposition.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.b] Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay in a soil. It is set by the size of the mineral particles, so it is one of the most basic characteristics of a soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.0] Sand has particles 2 to 0.05 mm across; silt 0.05 to 0.002 mm; clay under 0.002 mm. Particles over 2 mm are gravel or stones. Some textbooks put the sand and silt limit at 0.06 mm, which is also acceptable.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.1] Clay is the smallest particle and sand the largest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.2] Field test by feel: sand is gritty, silt is smooth and silky, and clay is sticky and can be rolled into a ribbon when moist.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.12.3] Texture is shown on a soil texture triangle, a triangular graph. There is more on this method in Figure interpretation, in Tables, Graphs and Data Skills.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.b] Particle size decides how water, air and nutrients move through a soil and how easy it is to farm. Each texture has strengths and weaknesses, and loam balances them.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.0] Sandy soils are light, free-draining, well aerated, warm quickly in spring and are easy to plough. They hold little water and few nutrients, and they are easily leached.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.1] Clay soils are heavy and hold water and nutrients. They drain slowly, become waterlogged and cold, crack and harden when dry, and are hard to work.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.2] A loam is a balanced mix, roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay. It combines good drainage with good water and nutrient retention, and is the best for farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.13.3] Silty soils are fertile but form a surface crust and erode easily.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.b] Structure is the way particles are grouped into clumps called peds. Two soils with the same texture can behave very differently if their peds differ.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.0] Crumb: small rounded peds, typical of humus-rich topsoil, and the best structure for farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.1] Granular: a further type of ped structure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.2] Platy: flat, horizontal plates that hinder drainage and root growth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.3] Blocky: angular cubes, common in clay subsoils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.4] Prismatic or columnar: vertical columns, found in some clay subsoils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.14.5] Structureless soils are single-grain (loose sand) or massive (solid clay), because their particles are not grouped into peds.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.b] Structure matters because it sets the pore spaces that water, air and roots use. It can be built up by natural agents and destroyed by pressure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.0] Structure controls the size and number of pores, so it decides drainage, aeration and how easily roots penetrate.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.1] Humus, clay, earthworms, roots and freeze-thaw build peds.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.15.2] Compaction (squashing the soil together) by heavy machinery, and trampling by livestock on wet ground (called poaching in Ireland), destroy peds and so close up the pores.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.b] Colour is a clue to a soil's composition and conditions, so reading it tells you what the soil contains and how well drained it is. Each colour below is linked to a cause and a named soil type.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.0] Black or dark brown: rich in humus, as in chernozems and peat.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.1] Brown: humus plus iron compounds, as in brown earths.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.2] Red, orange or yellow: iron oxides in a well-aerated soil, as in tropical latosols and terra rossa.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.3] Ash-grey or bleached: heavily leached (washed of its nutrients and colouring), as in the E horizon of a podzol.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.4] Grey or blue-grey with orange (rusty) mottles: waterlogged, with reduced iron, as in gley.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.5] White: lime or salt crusts, as in desert soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.16.6] Dark soils absorb more heat, so colour also affects soil temperature.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.b] How much water a soil can hold, and how fast it passes through, depends on its other characteristics. Two similar-sounding terms, porosity and permeability, must be kept apart.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.0] Water retention depends on texture (clay retains water, sand drains), structure and organic content. Humus holds several times its own weight of water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.1] Porosity is how much pore space a soil has. Permeability is how easily water passes through it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.17.2] Clay soils have high porosity but low permeability, because their pores are tiny, so they drain slowly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.b] Organic content is the amount of humus in a soil. It varies a lot between soil types and affects several other characteristics.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.0] It is high in chernozems and peat, moderate in brown earths, and low in desert soils, which have little vegetation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.1] Tropical latosols are surprisingly low in humus, because litter decays so fast that little humus builds up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.18.2] Organic content affects colour, structure, water retention and fertility.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.b] pH tells you how acidic or alkaline a soil is, which affects which organisms and crops can live in it. It is shaped by climate, parent rock and vegetation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.0] pH is the measure of acidity or alkalinity, on a scale of 0 to 14, with 7 neutral.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.1] Soils become acidic where heavy rain leaches out bases, and where acid litter such as conifer needles or heather builds up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.2] Soils are alkaline on limestone and chalk and in dry climates.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.3] Most crops and earthworms prefer slightly acidic to neutral soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.4] Teagasc, the Irish agriculture and food development authority, advises a soil pH of about 6.3 for grassland and about 6.5 for tillage crops. Liming corrects lower values.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.19.5] Farmers spread lime, which is ground limestone, to raise pH.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.b] A fertile soil holds nutrients in a form that roots can reach and rain cannot wash away. This depends on tiny charged particles in the clay and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.0] Clay particles and humus form the clay-humus complex. Its tiny, negatively charged particles, called colloids, hold positively charged nutrient ions (charged atoms) such as calcium, magnesium and potassium.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.1] Held nutrients are not washed away, and they are exchanged with roots, so plants can take them up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.2] Base-rich soils are fertile; base-poor, leached soils are not.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.20.3] NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium) are the main nutrients farmers add.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.b] The characteristics above work together to decide which soil processes dominate and which soil forms. Each link below is one step of the argument an essay needs.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.0] Texture and structure control drainage, so they decide whether leaching, gleying or capillary rise dominates.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.1] pH controls which organisms live in the soil, so it decides whether mull or mor humus forms and how fast humification (the breakdown of litter into humus) happens.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.2] Organic content controls structure and fertility, so it decides how well the soil supports the vegetation that feeds it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.3] Water retention and aeration together decide whether decomposition is aerobic, as in fertile brown soils, or anaerobic, as in peat and gley.
[geo-option-geoecology-s1.21.4] Use brown earth, podzol, gley, latosol and chernozem as illustrations by name.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s3: 3. Weathering processes
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.b] Rock at the surface is attacked by weather, water, plants and animals. Weathering is the first step in wearing landscapes down, and it must be kept separate from erosion.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.0] Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place (in situ) at or near the earth's surface, by weather, water, plants and animals. Erosion is the wearing away and removal of material by a moving agent such as a river, the sea, ice or wind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.1] Weathering involves no transport: the rock breaks down where it lies, so no moving agent is involved. This prepares the rock for erosion and for mass movement (the downhill movement of loose material under gravity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.2] Regolith is the layer of loose, weathered rock fragments lying on top of solid bedrock (the unbroken rock beneath).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.3] Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals (the chemical substances the rock is made of).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.4] Chemical weathering changes or dissolves the minerals in the rock, so the rock itself is altered, not just broken.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.5] Biological weathering by plants and animals works through both physical and chemical means.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.b] Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process. It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock (rock with natural cracks called joints).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.1] 2. At night the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.2] 3. By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.3] 4. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular (sharp-edged) fragments.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.4] It is most effective on mountains in upland Ireland, on high mountains and in periglacial areas (cold areas where the ground is frozen for much of the year). Freeze-thaw is active on Irish mountain summits such as Carrauntoohil, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.5] Result: scree (talus) slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields (areas covered in broken angular rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.b] In hot deserts the rock surface swings between hot days and cold nights. Both processes here are physical weathering driven by that heating and cooling, called insolation (heating by the sun).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.0] Exfoliation, or onion weathering, is an insolation process in hot deserts such as the Sahara, where the daily temperature range (the gap between day and night temperature) is large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.1] The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day, and cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.2] Stress builds up, and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion, leaving rounded boulders.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.3] Many geographers stress that some moisture, such as dew, must be present for exfoliation to work effectively.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.4] Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.b] Rock can also break because the forces on it change, or because growing crystals push it apart. Neither process changes the minerals, so both are physical.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.0] Pressure release (also called unloading or sheeting): when the overlying rock is eroded, the weight pressing on buried rock such as granite is removed, so it expands upward and cracks into sheets parallel to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.1] The sheets peel off to leave domes, such as Half Dome, Yosemite, California, USA, a granite dome shaped by sheeting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.2] Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores (tiny holes) and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.3] Salt crystallisation is common in hot deserts and on coasts, where salt water is available and evaporates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.b] Plants and animals break rock down in two ways. Their physical action is a form of physical weathering, and the acids they produce cause chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.0] Physical: plant roots grow into cracks and widen them, and burrowing animals expose fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.1] Chemical: decaying plants produce humic acids, and lichens and mosses release acids that attack rock surfaces.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.b] Carbonation is a chemical weathering process. It dissolves limestone, and it works along joints and bedding planes (the flat surfaces between layers of rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.0] Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.1] The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble, so it is carried away in solution (dissolved in water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.2] Word equation: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.0] Hydrolysis: water reacts with feldspar (a mineral in granite) and turns it into clay (kaolin, or china clay); the quartz grains (another mineral in granite) are left as sand, and the granite crumbles.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.1] China clay from the hydrolysis of granite is quarried around St Austell, Cornwall, England, and on the edge of Dartmoor.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.2] Oxidation: oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron in the rock to form iron oxide (rust), which gives red, yellow or brown colours and weakens the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.3] Hydration: some minerals absorb water and swell, setting up stress in the rock; anhydrite, for example, becomes gypsum.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.b] Weathering does not act at the same speed or in the same way everywhere. Climate decides which type of weathering dominates, and rock type decides how vulnerable the rock is.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.0] Climate: temperature and rainfall decide which type of weathering dominates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.1] Warm, wet climates favour fast chemical weathering because, by Van 't Hoff's rule, the rate of chemical reactions roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.2] Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, and hot deserts favour exfoliation and salt crystallisation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.3] Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.4] Rock type (its mineral composition): limestone is vulnerable to carbonation, granite to hydrolysis, and quartzite (a very hard rock) resists both.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.b] Beyond climate and rock type, four more factors affect how quickly rock weathers. Most work by letting water reach the rock or by exposing fresh rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.0] Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults (breaks in the rock where blocks have moved) let water in, so heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.1] Vegetation and soil cover: roots and humic acids speed weathering, and soil holds moisture against the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.2] Time: older surfaces are more weathered, because weathering has had longer to act.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.3] Relief (the steepness and shape of the land): steep slopes remove debris and expose fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.4] Aspect (the direction a slope faces): shady slopes stay frozen longer, so freeze-thaw is stronger on them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.b] Temperature links the processes above. It matters in two different ways, and the type of weathering that results depends on the temperature pattern of the place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.0] Temperature acts in two ways: as a trigger, through the expansion and contraction that cause stress, and as a control on the rate of chemical reactions.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.1] Freeze-thaw depends on temperature crossing the freezing point, and exfoliation and granular disintegration depend on a large daily range; both need changes in temperature, not just warmth.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.3] Method: name the process and its type, state the temperature condition it needs, explain the stress or rate change it produces, and link this to the result, such as scree from freeze-thaw.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s1: 1. The rock cycle and rock formation
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.b] Rock is never permanent. It is continually formed, modified, destroyed and reconstituted (built again from the broken pieces), so the three rock types are linked in one cycle. Two sets of forces drive it, one working from inside the earth and one at its surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.0] Endogenic (internal) forces, driven from within the earth, form and modify rock through magma (molten rock below the surface), heat and pressure.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.1] Exogenic (external) forces, working at the surface, destroy rock through weathering (breakdown of rock in place by weather) and erosion (wearing away and carrying off of rock fragments).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.2] Deposition (dropping of carried sediment, the loose fragments of rock) reconstitutes rock, because the sediment is later turned into new sedimentary rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.b] The cycle is easiest to learn as a circular flow chart in which each rock type is reached from another by a named process. Short-cuts across the circle show that rock does not have to follow the full loop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.0] 1. Magma cools and crystallises (forms crystals as it hardens) to make igneous rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.1] 2. Weathering and erosion break igneous rock down into sediment, because rock at the surface is attacked by weather.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.2] 3. Deposition, compaction and cementation turn the sediment into sedimentary rock, because the layers are squeezed and glued together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.3] 4. Heat and pressure change sedimentary rock into metamorphic rock, because buried rock is squeezed and heated without melting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.4] 5. Melting turns the metamorphic rock back into magma, and the cycle starts again.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.5] Short-cuts: any rock can be weathered into sediment, and any rock can be metamorphosed (changed by heat and pressure) or melted, so the arrows do not all follow one loop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.6] Diagram: draw a circular flow chart with magma and the three rock types as the nodes, and label each arrow with its process, for example crystallisation or compaction and cementation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.b] The rock cycle does not run at random. It is tied to the moving plates (the large slabs that make up the earth's crust), which decide where each stage happens.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.0] Rock is created at plate boundaries and at hotspots (fixed points of rising magma), because magma reaches the crust there.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.1] Rock is metamorphosed at collision zones, where plates push together and the heat and pressure are great.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.2] Rock is destroyed or recycled at subduction zones, where one plate sinks beneath another and the rock melts back into magma.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.b] Igneous rock forms when molten rock cools and crystallises. Magma is molten rock below the surface; lava is molten rock at the surface. Where the rock cools decides how big its crystals grow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.0] Crystal size depends on cooling rate: slow cooling gives large crystals because they have time to grow, and fast cooling gives small ones.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.1] Plutonic (intrusive) rock is igneous rock that cools slowly deep in the crust, so its crystals are large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.2] Volcanic (extrusive) rock is lava that cools quickly at the surface, so its crystals are small.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.3] Uses: granite is used as building stone, kerbs, worktops and headstones; basalt is crushed for road chippings and aggregate (crushed stone used in construction).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.b] Granite is a plutonic rock. When magma pushes into surrounding rock and cools there, it forms masses called intrusions, and their shape gives them different names.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.0] Granite is a plutonic rock built from quartz, feldspar and mica crystals, which are large enough to see because the magma cooled slowly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.1] Batholiths are huge dome-shaped masses of plutonic rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.2] Dykes are vertical intrusions that cut across the beds (layers) of the rock around them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.3] Sills are horizontal intrusions that lie along bedding planes (the horizontal surfaces between layers).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.4] Plutonic rock reaches the surface only after the rock above it has been eroded away, because it formed deep underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.b] Volcanic rock forms at the surface, so it cools fast. Which kind forms depends on the plate setting, the tectonic setting, in which the magma reaches the crust.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.0] Basalt is the commonest volcanic rock and forms from runny lava, which flows easily before it sets.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.1] Basalt forms at constructive (divergent) boundaries, where plates move apart, and at hotspots.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.2] Andesite and granite batholiths form above subduction zones at destructive boundaries, where one plate sinks beneath another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.b] Sedimentary rock is formed from sediment laid down in layers (strata), usually in seas, lakes, river basins and deserts. Loose sediment then has to be turned into solid rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.0] Sediment comes from weathered and eroded fragments of older rock, from the remains of plants and animals, or from minerals precipitated from water (that is, coming out of solution as solids).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.1] Sediment builds up in subsiding basins (areas of sinking land), on continental shelves (the shallow sea floor around a continent) and along trailing (passive) margins, where no plate boundary lies at the coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.2] Lithification is the conversion of loose sediment into solid rock by compaction and cementation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.3] Compaction: the weight of overlying layers squeezes out water and air, packing the grains together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.4] Cementation: minerals such as silica, calcite and iron oxide, carried by groundwater, bind the grains together like glue.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.b] Because sedimentary rock is laid down in layers from material at the earth's surface, it often contains fossils and shows bedding planes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.0] Many sedimentary rocks contain fossils, the remains or traces of past life, because living things were buried in the sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.1] Bedding planes are horizontal surfaces separating one layer from the next.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.2] Joints are vertical cracks that cut through the layers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.3] Many sedimentary rocks are porous, holding water in the spaces between grains. Porous is not the same as permeable (letting water pass through).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.b] Sedimentary rocks are sorted into three groups by the material they are made from.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.0] Clastic rocks are made of broken fragments: sandstone from sand, and shale from mud and clay.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.1] Organic rocks are made of the remains of living things: limestone from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures, and coal from plant remains that accumulated in swamps.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.2] Chemical rocks form from minerals that come out of solution: rock salt and other evaporites form as seawater evaporates and leaves its dissolved minerals behind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.b] Each Irish sedimentary rock records the conditions in which it formed, such as the climate and the setting at that time. For a longer answer, give the conditions as well as the rock and its place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.0] Carboniferous limestone (Carboniferous period, about 360 to 300 million years ago) formed when Ireland lay near the equator under a warm, shallow, clear sea, where shells and skeletons built up the deposit. It covers most of the central lowland and forms the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.1] Old Red Sandstone (Devonian period) formed in hot, semi-arid (very dry) conditions from sand and pebbles laid down by rivers; its red colour comes from iron oxide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.2] Old Red Sandstone forms the Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtees and the Comeraghs.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.3] Shale and sandstone of the Cliffs of Moher, Co. Clare, are layers of shale, siltstone and sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.4] Chalk lies beneath the basalt along the Antrim coast, so the chalk is the older rock and the lava flowed over it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.5] Coal at Arigna, Co. Roscommon, and Castlecomer, Co. Kilkenny, formed from plant remains in swamps; both are former coal-mining areas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.6] Rock salt is mined at the Kilroot salt mine, near Carrickfergus, Co. Antrim.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.b] Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by great heat and/or pressure without melting. The original rock is called the parent rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.0] The two factors that cause metamorphism are heat and pressure.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.1] Minerals recrystallise (re-form as new crystals) while the rock stays solid, so the rock usually becomes harder and denser.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.2] The rock may develop banding or cleavage (a tendency to split along flat surfaces).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.b] Metamorphism happens on two scales, depending on whether the heat and pressure come from a single intrusion or from the movement of plates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.0] Thermal (contact) metamorphism: rock beside an igneous intrusion is baked by its heat, forming a ring of altered rock called a metamorphic aureole; it is small in scale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.1] Regional metamorphism: heat and pressure act over huge areas during plate collision and mountain building; it is large in scale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.b] Each metamorphic rock comes from a particular parent rock, and the pairs can be asked either way round.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.0] Limestone becomes marble; Connemara marble is green and is quarried in Connemara, Co. Galway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.1] Sandstone becomes quartzite, so the parent of quartzite is a sedimentary rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.2] Shale (mudstone) becomes slate, and with more heat and pressure becomes schist, so schist is the more strongly altered rock. Slate is quarried on Valentia Island, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.3] Granite becomes gneiss, so gneiss is a metamorphic rock formed from an igneous parent.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.4] Quartzite forms Errigal, Co. Donegal; Croagh Patrick, Co. Mayo; the Great Sugar Loaf, Co. Wicklow; and the Twelve Bens, Connemara.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.5] Mica schist is found in Donegal and Connemara, and a metamorphic aureole of mica schist surrounds the Leinster granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.6] Gneiss at Inishtrahull, off Co. Donegal, is about 1.7 billion years old and is the oldest rock in Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.b] Ireland's rock types form a recognisable pattern across the island, and each reflects an event in its geological past.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.0] Limestone floors the central lowland, and Old Red Sandstone forms the ridges of Munster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.1] Granite outcrops (shows at the surface) in Wicklow, Donegal and the Mournes; basalt covers Co. Antrim; metamorphic rocks lie in Donegal, Connemara and Mayo.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.2] Plutonic example: the Leinster batholith, the granite core of the Wicklow Mountains, is about 400 million years old and is the largest granite mass in Ireland and Britain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.3] The Mourne granite, in the Mourne Mountains, Co. Down, is much younger, about 56 million years old.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.4] Volcanic example: the Antrim plateau was built by lava flows about 60 million years ago, as the Atlantic began to open. The Giant's Causeway, Co. Antrim, is basalt columns.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.5] The closing of the Iapetus Ocean during Caledonian mountain building produced the Leinster and Donegal granites and regional metamorphism.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.b] North America has two very different edges. The western edge is an active plate margin, where plates are moving and rock is made and changed. The eastern edge is a trailing (passive) margin, where no plate boundary lies at the coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.0] Active western margin: Pacific plates subduct (sink) beneath or slide past North America.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.1] Volcanic rock: the Cascade Range volcanoes, including Mount St Helens in Washington state (erupted 1980), lie above a subducting oceanic plate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.2] Plutonic rock: the Sierra Nevada granite batholith, California.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.3] Regional metamorphism affects the western mountain belts.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.4] The San Andreas Fault, California, is a transform boundary where plates slide past each other.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.5] Trailing eastern margin: there is no plate boundary at the coast, so there are no active volcanoes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.6] Because the margin is quiet, thick sediments accumulate on the Atlantic coastal plain, on the continental shelf and in the Mississippi delta.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.7] The Appalachians are old, worn-down fold mountains of sedimentary and metamorphic rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.b] Rocks can be told apart by what they look like, and photo questions rely on this. Each rock below has one or two clues that separate it from the others.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.0] Granite: speckled, with large interlocking crystals, because it cooled slowly underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.1] Basalt: dark and fine-grained because it cooled quickly; it may form columns, as at the Giant's Causeway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.2] Sandstone: gritty grains, often reddish or buff, and layered, because it is made of cemented sand laid down in beds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.3] Limestone: pale grey, and it may show fossils because it is made from shells and skeletons.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.4] Marble: white or coloured, with sparkling crystals and no fossils, which separates it from limestone, its parent rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.5] Quartzite: pale, very hard and glassy.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.6] Slate: dark grey, and it splits into thin sheets, which is the cleavage (splitting) that metamorphism can produce.

CANDIDATES geo-glaciation-s1: 1. Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.b] Ireland was glaciated (covered by moving ice) during the Pleistocene, the geological period of repeated ice ages. The landforms in this subsection were shaped by ice in its two main cold stages, and the type of ice involved decides what it can shape.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.0] The older cold stage is the Munsterian; the more recent is the Midlandian. Their names are the ones to use when you are asked to date Irish glaciation.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.1] Munsterian ice covered almost all of the island, so it was more extensive than the Midlandian ice, which left the far south free.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.2] Midlandian glaciation ended about 10,000 years ago, and its ice did not cover the far south of Ireland, so the south kept land the Midlandian ice never reached.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.3] An ice sheet is a continental mass of ice covering whole landscapes, buried hills and all, as in Antarctica and Greenland today.
[geo-glaciation-s1.0.4] A valley glacier is confined to a valley, so its path is set by the landscape. It is fed by snow and ice from corries (armchair-shaped hollows high on the mountainside).
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.b] Glacier ice is not frozen water poured into a valley. It is built up slowly from snow that never melts away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.0] Snow that survives the summer is compacted into firn (granular snow, also called nevé), because each year's new snow presses down on the older snow beneath.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.1] Firn becomes glacier ice as the weight of overlying snow squeezes out the air and the ice crystals grow larger and interlock, producing dense solid ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.b] A glacier gains ice at its head and loses it at its snout (its lower end), and the balance between these determines its behaviour. Geographers compare this to a budget of income against spending.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.0] Accumulation zone: the upper part of the glacier, where snow and ice are gained, because more snow falls there than melts.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.1] Ablation zone: the lower part, including the snout, where ice is lost by melting. Ablation simply means loss of ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.2] Equilibrium line: the boundary between the two zones, where gain and loss are equal over a year, so above it the glacier gains and below it the glacier loses.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.b] A glacier is not frozen in place. Gravity and the weight of the ice make it creep downhill by three different mechanisms, and friction makes some parts move faster than others.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.0] Basal slip: meltwater at the base lubricates the bed (the rock floor beneath the ice), so the whole glacier slides over the rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.1] Internal deformation (plastic flow): ice crystals slip past one another under the weight of the ice, so the glacier flows downhill.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.2] Rotational movement: ice in a corrie (the hollow where a glacier begins) rotates within the hollow instead of moving straight down the valley.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.3] Ice moves fastest at the centre of the surface and slowest at the sides and base, where friction with the valley walls and bed is greatest.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.b] The snout's position depends on whether more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts at the snout. This balance decides whether the glacier is getting longer or shorter.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.0] Advance: when more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts, the snout moves forward.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.1] Retreat: when melting exceeds the ice arriving, the snout moves back up the valley, although the ice is still flowing forward. The snout retreats because it melts back faster than the ice feeds it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.2] Heavier snowfall or cooler summers, which reduce melting, cause the glacier to advance.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.3] Less snowfall or warmer summers, which increase melting, cause the glacier to retreat.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.b] Average rate of movement is found by dividing the distance moved by the number of years. The figures below are illustrative, invented for practice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.0] Average rate = distance moved ÷ number of years, given in metres per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.1] Illustrative: a snout that retreats 600 m over 20 years has moved an average of 600 ÷ 20 = 30 m per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.5.2] Illustrative: a snout that advances 450 m over 9 years has moved an average of 450 ÷ 9 = 50 m per year.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.b] Glaciers erode mainly by plucking and abrasion, while freeze-thaw supplies rock debris at the edges of the ice. Together these processes carve the corries, valleys and lakes of glaciated uplands.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.0] Plucking (quarrying): meltwater enters joints (cracks) in the bedrock and freezes, bonding the ice to the rock; as the glacier moves on, it tears out blocks, mainly on the downhill side of rock obstacles.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.1] Abrasion: rock fragments frozen into the base of the ice scrape and polish the bedrock, like sandpaper on wood. It produces striations (scratches), polished rock and rock flour.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.2] Rock flour: fine powder ground from the bedrock by abrasion, which meltwater carries away, giving glacial lakes a milky colour.
[geo-glaciation-s1.6.3] Freeze-thaw on the rock above and beside the ice: water freezing in cracks expands and breaks off fragments, which fall onto the ice and are carried away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.b] A corrie (also called a cirque) is the armchair-shaped hollow where a mountain glacier begins. Corrie ice feeds valley glaciers, so corries are linked to the valleys below them.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.0] 1. Snow collects in a hollow on the mountainside; nivation (erosion by snow patches) and freeze-thaw enlarge the hollow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.1] 2. The snow turns to ice, and the rotating glacier plucks and abrades the back wall and floor, deepening the hollow and steepening the back wall.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.2] 3. The result is a bowl with a steep back wall and a rock lip, called the threshold, at its lower edge, because the ice erodes the floor more deeply than the rim.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.3] 4. When the ice melts, water collects behind the lip, because the lip dams the hollow, and forms a lake called a tarn.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.4] Examples: Coumshingaun, Comeragh Mountains, Co. Waterford; the Devil's Punchbowl, Mangerton, Co. Kerry.
[geo-glaciation-s1.7.5] Diagram: cross-section of a corrie with steep back wall, rock lip and tarn; plan view of the bowl-shaped hollow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.b] Corries do not only deepen hollows; they also eat into the mountain around them. Where corries meet, they leave sharp ridges and peaks standing between them.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.0] 1. Two corries cut back into the mountainside from opposite sides of a ridge.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.1] 2. Their back walls retreat, so the ridge between them is sharpened into a thin, knife-edged arête.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.2] 3. Three or more corries erode back around one summit from different directions.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.3] 4. The summit is left as a sharp, pointed pyramidal peak, also called a horn, because erosion has cut away every side of it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.4] Arête example: Striding Edge, Helvellyn, Lake District, England.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.5] Pyramidal peak examples: Carrauntoohil, Macgillycuddy's Reeks, Co. Kerry, Ireland's highest mountain; the Matterhorn, Swiss-Italian Alps.
[geo-glaciation-s1.8.6] Diagram: plan view of two corries backing onto an arête; plan view of a horn ringed by three corries.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.b] A glacier reshapes the river valley it occupies. It turns a narrow V-shaped valley into a wide trough, which is why glaciated valleys look so different from river valleys.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.0] 1. Before glaciation the valley is V-shaped, cut by a river.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.1] 2. A glacier fills the valley and deepens and widens its floor by plucking and abrasion.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.2] 3. The glacier cuts off the river's interlocking spurs (ridges that overlap from alternate sides of a river valley), leaving blunt cliff faces called truncated spurs.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.3] 4. The result is a broad, flat-floored trough with steep sides, which gives the valley its U-shape because the ice cuts down and sideways at once. It is also called a glacial trough.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.4] Examples: Glendalough and Glenmalure, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.9.5] Diagram: cross-sections of a V-shaped valley beside a U-shaped valley, with truncated spurs labelled.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.b] When a small glacier joins a large one, the two do not cut to the same depth. This leaves the smaller valley stranded high above the main one.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.0] 1. A smaller tributary glacier joins the larger glacier that is filling the main valley.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.1] 2. Because the tributary glacier is smaller, it erodes less deeply, so its valley is left hanging above the main valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.2] 3. When the ice melts, the tributary stream falls over the edge of the hanging valley as a waterfall.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.3] Example: Poulanass Waterfall, falling from a hanging valley into the Glendalough valley, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.10.4] Diagram: long profile (side view along the valley) of a hanging valley and its waterfall above the main valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.b] A glacier does not erode its valley floor evenly. Where it cuts deeper, it leaves a rock basin (a hollow in the bedrock) that fills with water as a lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.0] 1. Plucking and abrasion overdeepen a section of the valley floor more than the rest, leaving a rock basin.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.1] 2. When the ice melts, the basin fills with water, forming a long, narrow ribbon lake, because the lake takes the shape of the valley floor.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.2] Example: Upper Lake, Glendalough, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.3] Paternoster lakes: a chain of rock-basin lakes along a valley floor, separated by bars of harder rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.4] Tarns, ribbon lakes and kettle lakes are all glacial lakes.
[geo-glaciation-s1.11.5] Diagram: long profile of an overdeepened valley with a ribbon lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.b] A fjord is a glaciated valley on the coast that the sea has flooded after the ice melted. It forms only where a glacier cut the valley floor below sea level.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.0] 1. A glacier overdeepens a coastal valley so that its floor lies well below sea level.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.1] 2. When the ice melts, the sea floods the valley, leaving a fjord with steep sides.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.2] Examples: Killary Harbour, on the Co. Galway-Co. Mayo border; Sognefjord, Norway.
[geo-glaciation-s1.12.3] Diagram: plan view of a fjord with steep sides and the sea at its mouth.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.b] Where ice flows over a bump of hard bedrock, it treats the two sides differently. Abrasion smooths the side it climbs and plucking roughens the side it leaves.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.0] 1. Ice flows over a bedrock outcrop (rock exposed at the surface), and abrasion smooths and polishes its upstream (stoss) side.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.1] 2. On the downstream (lee) side, plucking tears blocks from the rock, leaving a steep, rough face.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.2] 3. The result is a rounded hill with a smooth stoss side and a rough lee side.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.3] Example: Lembert Dome, Yosemite, California, USA.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.4] Striations: fine parallel scratches cut into bedrock by rock fragments held in the base of the ice; they are carved by the ice and show the direction of ice flow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.13.5] Diagram: cross-section of a roche moutonnee showing the stoss side, lee side and direction of ice flow; plan view of striations on bedrock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.b] A glacier carries rock debris, called its load, in three positions. Where the load sits decides how much work it does and what it looks like when dropped.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.0] Supraglacial load: debris resting on the surface of the glacier, such as rock falling from the valley sides.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.1] Englacial load: debris trapped within the ice, so it is carried inside the glacier, away from the surface and base.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.2] Subglacial load: debris dragged along the base; it is the most abrasive part of the load and does most of the erosion, because it is pressed against the bedrock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.14.3] Glacial load is angular and unsorted: ice does not sort it by size, and it is not rounded by water as river load is, so sharp edges and mixed sizes survive.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.b] Deposition means dropping the load. Ice that deposits material directly leaves a jumble, and this jumble also affects how water drains across the land afterwards.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.0] Till (boulder clay) is unsorted and unstratified (not laid down in layers), angular material ranging from clay to boulders, deposited directly by ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.1] Boulder clay plains of the Irish Midlands are formed where till is left across the land, for example when ice melts and drops its load over a wide area.
[geo-glaciation-s1.15.2] Deranged drainage: no clear pattern, with many lakes and marshes, forms on poorly drained glacial deposits such as till.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.b] A moraine is a ridge or sheet of till. Its name depends on where in or around the glacier it was left.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.0] Lateral moraine: a ridge of till along the side of a glacier, built from debris falling from the valley walls, such as frost-shattered rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.1] Medial moraine: formed where two glaciers join, as their lateral moraines merge and run down the middle of the combined ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.2] Terminal (end) moraine: an arc-shaped ridge of till at the snout, marking the furthest advance or a long pause in retreat, because the snout dumps its load where it stays still.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.3] Recessional moraine: a ridge of till built at the snout during a pause while the glacier retreats.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.4] Ground moraine: a sheet of till left on the bed as the glacier melts back.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.5] Southern Irish End Moraine: marks the southern limit of the Midlandian ice sheet.
[geo-glaciation-s1.16.6] Diagram: cross-section of a valley glacier with lateral, medial and terminal moraines labelled; plan view of an arc-shaped terminal moraine.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.b] Drumlins are smooth, oval hills of till, moulded by the ice that flowed over them. Their shape records which way the ice was moving.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.0] 1. Ice deposits or overrides an accumulation of till on the bed, building an irregular mass of material.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.1] 2. Ice flowing over the mass moulds it into an elongated, streamlined hill lying parallel to the direction of ice flow.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.2] 3. The blunt, steep end faces up-ice, and the tapered end points down-ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.3] 4. Drumlins occur in swarms, all aligned in one direction, forming a basket of eggs landscape.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.4] 5. Where the sea floods low ground, drumlins become islands, forming drowned drumlins, as in Clew Bay, Co. Mayo, and Strangford Lough, Co. Down.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.5] Example: the drumlin belt across Co. Cavan, Co. Monaghan and Co. Leitrim.
[geo-glaciation-s1.17.6] Diagram: plan view of a drumlin swarm with an ice-flow arrow; side profile showing the blunt up-ice end and tapered down-ice end.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.b] Ice can carry very large rocks over long distances. When it melts it drops them, so they end up in places where they do not belong geologically.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.0] Erratic: a boulder carried by ice and dropped far from its source, with a rock type unlike the bedrock beneath it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.18.1] Example: granite erratics from Connemara resting on the limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.b] Fluvio-glacial material is laid down by meltwater rather than directly by ice, so it differs from till. Exams ask you to sort landforms into three groups, listed here.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.0] Meltwater sorts material by size, so fluvio-glacial deposits are sorted, and layers are stratified (laid down in layers); till is neither.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.1] Exam classification, glacial erosion: corrie, arete, pyramidal peak, U-shaped valley, hanging valley, ribbon lake, fjord, roche moutonnee, striations.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.2] Exam classification, glacial deposition: till, moraines, drumlins, erratics.
[geo-glaciation-s1.19.3] Exam classification, fluvio-glacial: eskers, outwash plains, kames, kettle holes.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.b] An esker is a long ridge built by a meltwater stream that once flowed through a tunnel in the ice. The ridge is what is left once the ice around it has gone.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.0] 1. Meltwater flows in a tunnel within or beneath the glacier.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.1] 2. The stream deposits sorted sand and gravel on the tunnel floor as its velocity falls, because slower water can no longer carry its load.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.2] 3. When the ice melts, the deposit is left as a long, winding ridge called an esker.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.3] Example: Esker Riada, a series of eskers across the Midlands from roughly Dublin to Galway, used as an ancient routeway (the Slí Mhór); Clonmacnoise, Co. Offaly, is built beside it.
[geo-glaciation-s1.20.4] Diagram: plan view of a winding esker ridge; cross-section of a ridge of sorted gravel.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.b] Meltwater leaves the glacier carrying sand and gravel and drops it beyond the ice limit. It builds a broad, flat plain, also called a sandur.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.0] 1. Meltwater spreads sand and gravel beyond the terminal moraine (the ridge of till marking the furthest ice advance) onto flat ground.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.1] 2. The deposit forms a flat, sorted outwash plain.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.2] Example: The Curragh, Co. Kildare, a plain of sand and gravel; it is well-drained grassland used for horse training and an army camp.
[geo-glaciation-s1.21.3] Diagram: plan view of an outwash plain lying beyond a terminal moraine.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.b] Meltwater also dumps sand and gravel on and around the ice itself. When the ice is gone, mounds and hollows are left behind.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.0] 1. Meltwater deposits sand and gravel in a hollow on the ice surface or at its margin.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.1] 2. When the ice melts, the deposit is left as a mound called a kame; where it lies against a valley side it forms a kame terrace.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.2] 3. A block of ice buried in outwash melts, leaving a hollow called a kettle hole. When water fills the hollow it becomes a kettle lake.
[geo-glaciation-s1.22.3] Diagram: cross-section of a kame beside a kettle hole.
