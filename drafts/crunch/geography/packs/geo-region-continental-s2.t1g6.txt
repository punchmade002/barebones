[geo-region-continental-s2.0.b] The region studied is the Federative Republic of Brazil, a country so large that it fills about half of a continent. Its position across the tropics shapes almost everything else about it.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.0] Brazil is in eastern South America and covers about 8.5 million km², the world's fifth-largest country by area and about half of South America.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.1] The equator crosses the far north, near the mouth of the Amazon, and the Tropic of Capricorn (the line of latitude south of the equator marking the edge of the tropics) passes close to São Paulo, so most of Brazil is tropical.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.2] It has an Atlantic coastline of about 7,400 km, which is why early settlement and today's biggest cities cluster near the sea.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.3] Brazil borders every South American country except Chile and Ecuador, but those neighbours are only neighbours, not part of the region studied.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.4] The country has 26 states and the Federal District, and its capital is Brasília.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.b] A region is an area with something that unites it. Brazil is big enough to be treated like a continent in its own right, and it holds together as one unit.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.0] Its continental size: it is larger than most continents' individual countries and contains very different climates, landscapes and economies.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.1] It is a single federal state (a country made of states that share a central government) with one language, Portuguese, surrounded by Spanish-speaking countries, which sets it apart culturally.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.2] It has its own culture and economy, shaped by Portuguese colonisation, African and Indigenous peoples and later immigrants.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.3] It has clear internal regions, the five macro-regions, which let you study how physical, economic and human processes interact inside one country.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.b] Brazil is divided officially into five macro-regions (large groupings of states). You will use these names in almost every answer, so learn what each is known for.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.0] North: the Amazon, the largest region by area and the least populated.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.1] Northeast: the sugar coast and the semi-arid interior called the sertão; it is the poorest region.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.2] Centre-West: the Cerrado (tropical savanna), the soya frontier and the capital, Brasília.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.3] Southeast: São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais; this is the economic core of the country.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.4] South: a cooler region of European immigrant settlement with prosperous farming and industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.b] A sketch map earns marks for an accurate outline and for showing and naming each feature asked for. Learn one example of each feature and exactly where it goes. Names may go on the map or in a key.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.0] Outline: a broad northern bulge across the Amazon, an eastward shoulder at the Northeast, then the coast running south-west and the country tapering towards the south.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.1] Relief: outline the Brazilian Highlands (the east-centre, inland from the coast, from the Northeast down to the south) or the Amazon Basin lowland (the north-west and north).
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.2] River: draw the Amazon entering in the far west, flowing east past Manaus, where the Rio Negro joins from the north-west, and ending at a wide estuary close to the equator. Mark Marajó Island at the mouth.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.3] Urban centres as dots: São Paulo just inland behind the south-east coast near the Tropic of Capricorn, and Rio de Janeiro on the coast east-north-east of São Paulo.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.4] Agricultural region as an outlined area: the soya region of Mato Grosso in the centre-west, south of the Amazon lowland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.5] Industrial region as an outlined area: the triangle joining São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and Belo Horizonte. Other placing cues: Manaus in the centre-north, Brasília in the centre and Salvador on the Northeast coast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.b] Six controls explain why Brazil has so many climates inside one country. Each one pushes temperature or rainfall up or down in a particular area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.0] Latitude: most of Brazil lies between the equator and the Tropic of Capricorn, so it gets strong sun and is mostly hot.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.1] The ITCZ (Intertropical Convergence Zone, the belt where winds from north and south meet and air rises to give heavy rain) moves north and south with the overhead sun, so it brings rain to different areas in different seasons.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.2] The south-east trade winds blow in off the warm Atlantic, carrying moist air onto the east coast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.3] Altitude: the plateau is cooler than the coast because air cools with height.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.4] Relief: the coastal escarpment (a steep slope) forces moist air to rise, cool and drop rain on the seaward side.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.5] Cold fronts from the south reach the South and parts of the Southeast, bringing sudden falls in temperature and sometimes frost.
[geo-region-continental-s2.5.b] The North has an equatorial climate (found on or near the equator): hot, wet and with almost no seasons. This is the climate that supports the rainforest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.5.0] It is hot all year, with Manaus monthly means of about 26-28 degrees C, so the annual temperature range is small.
[geo-region-continental-s2.5.1] Manaus gets about 2,300 mm of rain a year, with heavy rain in every month, because strong heating makes warm moist air rise and form storm clouds (convectional rain).
[geo-region-continental-s2.5.2] The climate sustains the Amazon rainforest biome, which is dealt with in the Geoecology option.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.b] The Cerrado has a tropical wet-and-dry (savanna) climate, with a clear rainy summer and a long dry winter. The change comes from the movement of the ITCZ and the overhead sun.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.0] The rainy season runs from about October to April, when the ITCZ and the overhead sun move south, so warm moist air rises over the Centre-West.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.1] The dry season runs from about May to September, when the ITCZ has moved north, so the winter is long and dry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.2] Brasília is the example: it follows this wet and dry pattern, and its altitude of about 1,100 m keeps it milder than the lowlands.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.3] This clear wet season matters for farming, because crops such as soya are planted with the first rains.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.b] The interior of the Northeast is the driest part of Brazil even though it lies in the tropics. The sertão (the semi-arid Northeast interior) has low and unreliable rain, which is behind many of the region's problems.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.0] The sertão gets roughly 250-800 mm a year, falling in a short rainy season and varying greatly from year to year.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.1] It lies in the rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of high ground) of the coastal plateau, so moist air from the Atlantic loses its rain before it arrives.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.2] Sinking air limits convection, and the ITCZ reaches the area only briefly, so there are few chances for heavy rain.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.3] El Niño years (when the Pacific Ocean is unusually warm) bring drought, so the rains can fail altogether.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.4] The officially defined drought-prone area is the drought polygon (Polígono das Secas). A severe drought hit the Northeast from 2012 to 2017.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.b] The east coast is wet while the interior behind it is dry. Winds off the ocean and the shape of the land explain the difference.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.0] The south-east trade winds bring moisture off the warm Atlantic, so the air arriving at the coast is already full of water vapour.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.1] Where the air meets the coastal escarpment, such as the Serra do Mar behind the coast of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro states, it is forced to rise and cool, giving relief rain.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.2] This creates a wet coastal strip that once carried the Atlantic Forest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.3] The wet coast contrasts with the semi-arid sertão further inland, which lies beyond the rain-giving slopes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.9.b] In the Southeast, height above sea level matters as much as latitude. The plateau is noticeably cooler than the coast at the same latitude.
[geo-region-continental-s2.9.0] Temperatures fall with altitude, so the plateau moderates the heat of the tropics.
[geo-region-continental-s2.9.1] São Paulo, on the plateau, is cooler than Rio de Janeiro on the coast, which is why the highlands are comfortable for people and suit crops such as coffee.
[geo-region-continental-s2.9.2] This cooler climate is one reason the plateau became the centre of settlement, farming and industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.b] The far south lies furthest from the equator, and it is the only part of Brazil with a real winter. This affects what can be grown.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.0] It has four seasons and rain all year, because cold fronts from the south bring rain in every season.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.1] Winter frosts occur, and there is occasional snow in the highlands of Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.2] Porto Alegre is the main city of the far south.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.3] The frost risk shaped where coffee is grown, because frost kills the trees. This is why coffee moved north after the frost of 1975, which the coffee notes deal with in full.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.b] Brazil's climate is not safe or steady. Droughts and floods both cause deaths and damage, and some hazards are linked to El Niño and to forest loss.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.0] Droughts in the Northeast are repeated and severe, as in 2012 to 2017.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.1] In 2023 a severe Amazon drought brought the Rio Negro at Manaus to its lowest level on record, in October 2023, stranding river communities.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.2] In May 2024 floods in Rio Grande do Sul killed more than 170 people and displaced hundreds of thousands, and Porto Alegre was flooded.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.3] In January 2011 floods and landslides in the mountain region of Rio de Janeiro state killed more than 900 people, Brazil's worst natural disaster. Landslides also hit Petrópolis in February 2022.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.4] El Niño links droughts in the north and east to Pacific warming, and deforestation reduces rainfall recycling (the forest returning water to the air), which may make drought worse.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.b] Climate decides what can be grown, where people live and how energy is produced. Four effects are worth learning.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.0] Warm temperatures in the tropics allow two or three crops a year, which raises farm output.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.1] Drought drives migration out of the sertão, because people cannot farm when the rains fail.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.2] Heavy rainfall over much of the country feeds the rivers that power hydroelectricity.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.3] Beaches, sun and warm weather attract tourists, so climate supports the tourist industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.b] Many people expect tropical soils to be rich because of the lush forest. In fact most Amazon and Cerrado soils are poor, and the reason is climate.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.0] Latosols (also called ferralsols or oxisols) are deep, red-yellow soils that cover much of the Amazon and Cerrado.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.1] Heat and heavy rain leach them (wash nutrients out of them), so they are acidic and low in nutrients.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.2] The fertility is held in the living vegetation and its litter, not in the soil, so cleared land loses its fertility fast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.3] The process that produces these soils, laterisation, is explained in the Geoecology option.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.b] The Cerrado soils were once thought useless for crops. Science changed that, and now they are a major farming area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.0] Cerrado soils are acidic and high in aluminium, which damages plant roots.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.1] Liming (adding crushed limestone) cuts the acidity, and fertiliser adds nutrients.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.2] EMBRAPA (Brazilian Agricultural Research Corporation, founded 1973) developed these methods.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.3] The result was that infertile savanna became productive farmland, a change that underlies the soya belt covered in the agriculture notes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.b] Two soils explain much of Brazil's farming history. Both are deep and fertile, and each was the base of a famous crop.
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.0] Terra roxa means purple earth in Portuguese, from its dark red colour. It is deep and fertile.
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.1] It weathers from the basalt of the Paraná Plateau in Paraná and São Paulo states, and it was the soil of the coffee boom.
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.2] Massapê is a dark, clay-rich, fertile soil found on the humid Northeast coast, in the Zona da Mata (the wet coastal strip around Recife and Salvador).
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.3] Massapê was the base of the colonial sugar plantations, which is why sugar grew there first.
[geo-region-continental-s2.16.b] Two soils break the rule that Amazon soils are poor. One is natural and one is made by people.
[geo-region-continental-s2.16.0] Várzea (floodplain) soils are renewed every year by silt left by floods, so they are among the few fertile Amazon soils.
[geo-region-continental-s2.16.1] Terra preta (black earth) is dark, fertile soil made by pre-Columbian Amazon peoples (those living there before European contact) by adding charcoal, food waste and pottery.
[geo-region-continental-s2.16.2] Terra preta shows that people can improve tropical soils, and not only damage them.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.b] Soils can be lost or damaged as well as improved. Three main problems affect Brazilian farmland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.0] Sertão soils are thin and stony, and easily eroded because there is little vegetation to hold them.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.1] Erosion follows forest clearance, because rain then washes away the exposed topsoil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.2] Old pasture suffers nutrient loss and compaction (being pressed hard by cattle) so grass grows poorly.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.3] Salinisation (a build-up of salt) occurs in irrigated areas where the water evaporates and leaves the salt behind.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.b] Brazil has six biomes (large natural vegetation regions). They show that Brazil is not all rainforest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.0] Amazon rainforest covers about 49% of Brazil's area, the largest biome.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.1] Cerrado (tropical savanna) covers about 24%, and is often called the world's most biodiverse savanna.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.2] Atlantic Forest covers about 13%, but only about an eighth of the original forest remains because it grew where most Brazilians live.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.3] Caatinga (dry thorn scrub of the sertão) covers about 10% and is found only in Brazil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.4] Pampa (grassland) covers about 2% and so does the Pantanal (wetland).
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.b] Each biome matches a climate and soil. Link the vegetation to what causes it.
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.0] The Cerrado has a wet and dry climate and acidic soils, so it supports tropical grassland with scattered trees.
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.1] The Caatinga's dry thorn scrub matches the semi-arid climate of the sertão, because only drought-tolerant plants survive there.
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.2] The Atlantic Forest grew on the wet east coast fed by the trade winds, and its heavy loss came from settlement and farming along the coast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.3] The Pantanal is a seasonally flooded wetland, and the Pampa is a grassland in the south where gaúchos herd cattle in Rio Grande do Sul.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.b] Relief is the shape of the land. Brazil is mostly old, low or gently rising ground, with five main divisions to learn.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.0] The Brazilian Highlands (Planalto) cover the centre, east and south.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.1] The Amazon lowland (basin) lies across the north.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.2] The Guiana Highlands lie on the northern border.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.3] The Pantanal lowland is in the Centre-West, and a narrow coastal plain runs along the Atlantic.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.4] These divisions are the starting point for the sketch map relief feature.
[geo-region-continental-s2.21.b] Brazil sits in the middle of a tectonic plate, which explains why its landscapes are old and quiet.
[geo-region-continental-s2.21.0] Brazil lies in the centre of the South American plate, far from plate boundaries, so it has no active volcanoes and few strong earthquakes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.21.1] The highlands are part of the ancient Brazilian Shield (an old crystalline block of hard rock), once part of Gondwanaland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.21.2] Mesosaurus fossils are found in Brazil and southern Africa, evidence that the two were joined.
[geo-region-continental-s2.22.b] The Highlands are the main block of high ground. They are old, worn-down and tilted, and this shapes where rivers go.
[geo-region-continental-s2.22.0] They are old, worn-down plateaus and tablelands, mostly 300-900 m high, shaped over a very long time by weathering and erosion.
[geo-region-continental-s2.22.1] They tilt from a high eastern edge towards the interior, so many rivers rise near the coast but flow inland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.22.2] This tilt is one reason the Paraná and São Francisco flow away from the nearest sea.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.b] Behind the coast of the Southeast the land rises steeply. This steep edge has had a big effect on settlement and transport.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.0] The Serra do Mar and Serra da Mantiqueira rise steeply behind the coast, so the coastal plain is very narrow.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.1] São Paulo sits on the plateau about 800 m above sea level and about 70 km from the port of Santos.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.2] The slope is a barrier to transport between ports and the plateau, so roads and railways were costly to build.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.3] It also forces moist air to rise, giving the relief rain described under the east coast climate.
[geo-region-continental-s2.24.b] In the south the Highlands are covered by lava. This basalt created the best coffee soil and one of the world's great waterfalls.
[geo-region-continental-s2.24.0] Thick layers of basalt (a dark volcanic rock) formed from ancient fissure eruptions, flood basalts about 130 million years old.
[geo-region-continental-s2.24.1] The basalt weathers into terra roxa, the soil of the coffee boom.
[geo-region-continental-s2.24.2] The Iguaçu Falls drop over its basalt edge on the Iguaçu River on the Brazil-Argentina border, about 275 separate falls and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.b] The north has a very different relief from the Highlands. One block is the lowest, flattest part of Brazil, and the other holds its highest peak.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.0] The Amazon lowland is a vast, low, flat basin of sedimentary rocks and alluvium (river-deposited sediment), between the Guiana and Brazilian Highlands.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.1] It widens to the west.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.2] The Guiana Highlands lie on the northern border with Venezuela.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.3] Pico da Neblina, about 2,995 m, in Amazonas state, is Brazil's highest peak.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.b] The Pantanal is a lowland that floods and drains with the seasons. It is a special type of landscape in Brazil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.0] It is a huge, flat, seasonally flooded depression in the Centre-West, mainly in Mato Grosso and Mato Grosso do Sul.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.1] It is flooded for several months each wet season, because rivers spill over the flat land.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.2] It is the world's largest tropical wetland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.3] It extends into Bolivia and Paraguay, so it is a shared feature.
[geo-region-continental-s2.27.b] The coast has two very different kinds of scenery, hard rock in the Southeast and sand in the Northeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.27.0] At Rio de Janeiro, granite domes and steep hills rise by the sea. Sugarloaf Mountain (Pão de Açúcar) is a granite-gneiss dome about 396 m high.
[geo-region-continental-s2.27.1] In the Northeast, Lençóis Maranhenses in Maranhão has white sand dunes with rain-filled lagoons.
[geo-region-continental-s2.27.2] Both are tourist attractions, which links relief to the tertiary sector.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.b] Relief decides where people farm, build and travel. Four patterns stand out.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.0] The plateau carries most farming, industry and cities, because the land is gently sloping and the climate is cooler.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.1] The escarpment limited early inland settlement and needs costly roads and railways.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.2] The lowland Amazon is sparsely settled, because of dense forest, flooding and difficult access.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.3] Steep coastal hills host favelas (informal housing areas) that are prone to landslides.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.b] A river basin is the area drained by a river and its tributaries. Brazil has several enormous ones, and the pattern is shaped by the tilt of the Highlands.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.0] The Amazon basin drains the north.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.1] The Paraná-Paraguay basin drains the south and centre.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.2] The São Francisco basin drains the east.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.3] The Tocantins-Araguaia basin is a fourth, flowing north through the Centre-West.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.b] The Amazon is the world's largest river by discharge and basin area. Do not call it the longest, because that is disputed with the Nile.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.0] It rises in the Andes of Peru, is about 6,400 km long and has a basin of about 7 million km², the world's largest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.1] It carries about a fifth of all the fresh water that rivers deliver to the oceans, and it has over a thousand tributaries.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.2] In Brazil it is called the Solimões until it meets the Rio Negro at Manaus, the Meeting of the Waters, where dark and pale water flow side by side for several kilometres before mixing.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.3] It flows east to the Atlantic near the equator, ending in a wide estuary around Marajó Island rather than a large delta.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.4] Ocean-going ships can reach Manaus, about 1,500 km inland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.b] The Amazon is not a simple channel. It floods, it has two kinds of water and it is shaped by the tide at its mouth.
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.0] The annual flood rises many metres and spreads into the várzea (forest and land flooded by silt-laden white-water rivers).
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.1] It also spreads into the igapó (flooded black-water forest).
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.2] Black-water rivers such as the Rio Negro are acidic and low in silt, while white-water rivers such as the Solimões are silt-laden from the Andes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.3] The pororoca (a tidal bore, a wave that travels upstream) occurs at the mouth.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.b] The Paraná is the main river of the south and centre. Its flow is controlled by dams that supply the Southeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.0] It rises on the Brazilian Highlands and flows south-west into Argentina and the Río de la Plata.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.1] It is heavily dammed for hydroelectricity.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.2] Itaipu Dam, on the Brazil-Paraguay border and shared by the two countries, began generating in 1984 and is one of the world's largest hydroelectric plants by output.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.3] The Iguaçu Falls are on a tributary.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.b] The São Francisco brings water to the dry Northeast, which is why it is called the river of national integration.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.0] It rises in the Serra da Canastra, Minas Gerais, is about 2,800 km long, flows north through the semi-arid Northeast and turns east to reach the Atlantic between Alagoas and Sergipe.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.1] It is a lifeline for the sertão, because it is one of few permanent water sources in a dry area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.2] Dams give power, and the Sobradinho Dam forms one of the world's largest artificial lakes by area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.3] Petrolina (Pernambuco) and Juazeiro (Bahia) face each other across the river and export irrigated grapes and mangoes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.4] The transposition project, begun in 2007, carries water by canals to the drier states of Ceará, Paraíba, Pernambuco and Rio Grande do Norte.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.b] Dams supply cheap power, but they also remove land and people from their homes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.0] Itaipu on the Paraná supplies power to the industrial Southeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.1] Tucuruí Dam on the Tocantins, Pará (1984), powers aluminium smelting.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.2] Belo Monte Dam on the Xingu, Pará, was completed in 2019.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.3] Belo Monte was opposed by the Kayapó, displaced thousands of people and flooded forest, so the controversy is about the cost to people and nature.
[geo-region-continental-s2.35.b] Rivers are under pressure from mining and cities. Three examples show the problem.
[geo-region-continental-s2.35.0] Mercury from illegal gold mining pollutes rivers in Yanomami territory in Roraima, which harms the health of people who eat the fish.
[geo-region-continental-s2.35.1] The Rio Doce was polluted by mine waste after the Mariana disaster, which the mining notes explain.
[geo-region-continental-s2.35.2] The Tietê at São Paulo is heavily polluted by sewage from the city.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.b] Rivers provide transport, food and water, but both floods and droughts can disrupt them.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.0] The Amazon is the main highway of the North, because there are few roads in the forest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.1] Rivers supply fish, and water for irrigation and cities.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.2] Hydroelectricity comes from rivers with a steep fall and a large flow.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.3] Floods and droughts both disrupt river transport, as in the 2023 drought when the Rio Negro fell to a record low.
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.b] Brazil is an agricultural superpower. Its farms feed the world market far more than they employ its own people, which shows how mechanised and commercial the leading sector is.
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.0] Brazil is the world's largest producer and exporter of soya beans, and the largest producer and exporter of coffee, which is about a third of world output (2020s).
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.1] It is also the largest producer of sugar cane and orange juice, and the largest exporter of beef and chicken, so it leads the world in several different foods.
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.2] Agriculture itself is under a tenth of GDP and of jobs (early 2020s). This is small because a few huge, machine-run farms produce the crops with few workers.
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.3] Agribusiness as a whole (farming plus processing and trade) is much larger than farming alone, and it makes up a large share of Brazil's exports.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.b] Brazilian farming ranges from giant mechanised farms to tiny family plots. Exams ask for two types, so learn each with a crop and a place.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.0] Large commercial mechanised farms grow soya, maize and cotton in the Cerrado (the tropical savanna of the Centre-West). Machines suit the flat land, so a few workers farm huge areas.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.1] Plantations grow one crop on a large estate for sale abroad, such as sugar cane, coffee and oranges.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.2] Extensive cattle ranching uses large areas of land with few animals per hectare and little labour, on the Cerrado, the Pantanal and cleared Amazon land.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.3] Subsistence and family farming grows food for the household and local sale: cassava (manioc), beans and maize. It includes shifting cultivation in the Amazon and small farms in the sertão (the dry Northeast interior).
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.4] Irrigated fruit farming uses river water to grow export fruit in the dry Northeast, for example grapes and mangoes.

CANDIDATES geo-urban-s7: 7. Developing-world cities
[geo-urban-s7.0.b] Cities in the developing world (poorer countries, mostly in Asia, Africa and Latin America) now account for most of the world's urban growth. The UN figures below show how big the shift is.
[geo-urban-s7.0.0] Most of the world's urban growth now takes place in developing countries in Asia, Africa and Latin America, so the fastest-growing cities are no longer in Europe or North America.
[geo-urban-s7.0.1] About 30% of the world's population was urban (living in towns and cities) in 1950 and about 55% in 2018 (UN, World Urbanization Prospects 2018).
[geo-urban-s7.0.2] Over half of the world's population has been urban since about 2007, so the world passed the point where city dwellers outnumber rural dwellers.
[geo-urban-s7.0.3] The UN projects that about 68% of the world's population will be urban by 2050.
[geo-urban-s7.0.4] Close to 90% of the increase to 2050 is expected in Asia and Africa; India, China and Nigeria together account for over a third of it.
[geo-urban-s7.0.5] Megacities, with over 10 million people, are a growing feature of urbanisation in the developing world.
[geo-urban-s7.0.6] UN-Habitat (the UN agency for human settlements) estimates that over 1 billion people live in slums or informal settlements, meaning crowded housing built without legal permission or services.
[geo-urban-s7.1.b] Cities grow in two ways: more babies than deaths, and more people arriving than leaving. In developing countries both are strong at once. There is more on migration theory in the Rural-to-urban migration in developed and developing regions note.
[geo-urban-s7.1.0] Natural increase (births minus deaths): the urban population is young, birth rates are high and death rates have fallen, so the city grows even without newcomers.
[geo-urban-s7.1.1] Push factors (things that drive people out of the countryside): rural poverty, landlessness, small farms, mechanisation (machines replacing farm workers), drought and crop failure, lack of services, conflict and natural disasters.
[geo-urban-s7.1.2] Pull factors (things that draw people to the city): jobs in the formal and informal sectors, higher wages, schools and hospitals, perceived opportunities, and family already living in the city.
[geo-urban-s7.1.3] Primate cities (a country's largest city, far bigger than any other) attract investment and government spending, which reinforces their pull on migrants.
[geo-urban-s7.2.b] Migrants arrive faster than the city can build homes, and most have little money. They build their own shelter wherever they can, which creates the first and most visible problem of rapid growth.
[geo-urban-s7.2.0] Migrants with little money and no legal land settle in shanty towns, slums and informal settlements, called bustees, favelas, chawls or jhopadpattis depending on the country.
[geo-urban-s7.2.1] These settlements are built on marginal land (land nobody else wants because it is unsafe or unsuitable): river banks, steep slopes, beside railways, rubbish dumps and floodplains.
[geo-urban-s7.2.2] Tenure (the legal right to occupy land) is insecure, so residents can be evicted and have no legal right to the land they occupy.
[geo-urban-s7.2.3] Overcrowding is severe, and some people have no shelter at all, living as homeless pavement dwellers.
[geo-urban-s7.3.b] Informal settlements are built without services, and this directly harms health. Sanitation means the safe disposal of human waste and dirty water.
[geo-urban-s7.3.0] Without piped water or sewers, settlements rely on open drains and shared toilets, so waste stays close to where people live and cook.
[geo-urban-s7.3.1] Contaminated water and waste spread water-borne disease (illness carried in dirty water): cholera, typhoid and dysentery.
[geo-urban-s7.4.b] There are not enough regular jobs for all the migrants, so many make a living outside the formal economy. The informal sector means work that is unregistered, untaxed and unprotected.
[geo-urban-s7.4.0] Many residents work in the informal sector as street vendors, rickshaw pullers, waste pickers and home workers.
[geo-urban-s7.4.1] Informal work offers no job security and low pay; child labour is part of informal employment.
[geo-urban-s7.4.2] Underemployment means that people have work but not enough of it to earn a living, so they are counted as working yet remain poor.
[geo-urban-s7.5.b] Road and rail systems were built for much smaller populations. When the city expands faster than its transport, movement slows for everyone.
[geo-urban-s7.5.0] Road networks cannot cope with the growth in traffic, so journeys take longer and congestion (traffic jams) becomes permanent.
[geo-urban-s7.5.1] Trains and buses are overcrowded at peak times, when most people travel to and from work.
[geo-urban-s7.5.2] Road deaths are a serious problem in the city, because crowded roads mix heavy traffic with pedestrians.
[geo-urban-s7.6.b] Crowded cities with weak services release waste into the air, rivers and streets faster than it can be removed.
[geo-urban-s7.6.0] Air pollution comes from traffic, industry and cooking fuels burned in crowded homes.
[geo-urban-s7.6.1] Untreated sewage and industrial waste are discharged into rivers, because there are no treatment plants to clean them first.
[geo-urban-s7.6.2] Rubbish is not collected in many areas, so it builds up in streets and drains.
[geo-urban-s7.7.b] Poor people settle on the land most exposed to danger, so natural hazards hit informal settlements hardest.
[geo-urban-s7.7.0] Monsoon (seasonal heavy rain) or tropical downpours flood low-lying, paved and badly drained land, where settlements are often built; paving stops rain soaking into the ground and poor drains cannot carry it away.
[geo-urban-s7.7.1] Hillside slums are at risk of landslides, and crowded, poorly built homes are at risk of fire.
[geo-urban-s7.8.b] Rapid growth also produces social strains, which weak city government is too stretched to manage.
[geo-urban-s7.8.0] Crime and drugs are social problems, and street children live and work on the streets.
[geo-urban-s7.8.1] Rich and poor districts sit side by side, so inequality is visible within the city.
[geo-urban-s7.8.2] Local government is weak, so it struggles to provide services.
[geo-urban-s7.9.b] The first response to slums is often to remove them. This is the least successful of the solutions.
[geo-urban-s7.9.0] Slum clearance removes informal settlements by demolition and eviction (forcing residents out).
[geo-urban-s7.9.1] It usually fails, because residents are moved further out or into new slums and the underlying problems (poverty and lack of housing) remain.
[geo-urban-s7.10.b] Instead of building homes for people, the authorities prepare land and let residents do the building. No named scheme is needed for this general solution.
[geo-urban-s7.10.0] In a site-and-service scheme the authorities provide serviced plots with water, drains and power.
[geo-urban-s7.10.1] Residents build their own homes on these plots, which is known as aided self-help (the authorities help with the services, the residents do the building).
[geo-urban-s7.11.b] Upgrading improves a slum where it stands rather than removing it, so nobody has to move.
[geo-urban-s7.11.0] Lanes are paved, water taps, toilets, drains and electricity are installed, and residents are given secure tenure (a legal right to stay).
[geo-urban-s7.11.1] Because the community stays in place, people can improve their own homes over time, as they no longer fear eviction.
[geo-urban-s7.12.b] Another approach is to build new towns beside the city so that growth goes there instead of into the crowded centre.
[geo-urban-s7.12.0] Planned satellite towns and new towns (towns built beside a large city, with planned housing, jobs and services) divert growth away from the overcrowded core.
[geo-urban-s7.12.1] Examples are given in the Mumbai case study (Navi Mumbai) and the Kolkata case study (Salt Lake and New Town).
[geo-urban-s7.13.b] Cities cannot solve congestion just by building more roads, so they invest in public transport that carries many people at once.
[geo-urban-s7.13.0] Metros (underground or elevated urban railways), suburban rail and bus rapid transit (buses with their own lanes and fast boarding, working like a train system) move large numbers of people without adding more cars to the roads.
[geo-urban-s7.13.1] Curitiba in Brazil is the leading example of bus rapid transit, described below.
[geo-urban-s7.14.b] Curitiba, in southern Brazil, shows how a cheap, well-planned bus system can ease traffic in a developing-world city.
[geo-urban-s7.14.0] Bus rapid transit began in Curitiba in 1974 under architect-mayor Jaime Lerner.
[geo-urban-s7.14.1] Dedicated bus lanes and raised tube stations allow fast boarding, which reduces car use because the bus is quicker than sitting in traffic.
[geo-urban-s7.14.2] Recyclable waste could be exchanged for food or bus tokens, which tackled rubbish and helped poorer residents at the same time.
[geo-urban-s7.14.3] The model has been copied worldwide, for example in Bogotá's TransMilenio (2000).
[geo-urban-s7.15.b] Some solutions act on the push factors in the countryside, so fewer people leave for the city in the first place.
[geo-urban-s7.15.0] Rural development, land reform (giving landless farmers land) and jobs in smaller cities give people reasons to stay in rural areas.
[geo-urban-s7.15.1] NGOs (non-governmental organisations, i.e. charities) and community groups also act on these problems, as the Missionaries of Charity do in Kolkata.
[geo-urban-s7.16.b] Kolkata (called Calcutta until 2001) is the main developing-world case study. Its site and history explain why it became one of India's largest cities.
[geo-urban-s7.16.0] Kolkata lies on the Hooghly river near the Bay of Bengal, on low-lying delta land (flat land built up by river deposits), which is why it floods easily.
[geo-urban-s7.16.1] It was founded in 1690 by the British East India Company (Job Charnock).
[geo-urban-s7.16.2] It developed as a port on the Hooghly, with jute mills (factories making cloth and sacks from the jute plant); Howrah Bridge across the river opened in 1943.
[geo-urban-s7.16.3] It was the capital of British India until 1911, so government jobs and trade drew people to it.
[geo-urban-s7.16.4] The city was renamed from Calcutta to Kolkata in 2001.
[geo-urban-s7.17.b] Kolkata grew because colonial rule built an economy that drew people in, and because disasters and wars then pushed more people towards it.
[geo-urban-s7.17.0] It was a colonial port and administrative capital, and its jute and engineering industries attracted migrants from Bihar, Odisha and rural Bengal.
[geo-urban-s7.17.1] The Bengal famine of 1943 drove millions of rural people towards the city.
[geo-urban-s7.17.2] Partition in 1947 (the division of British India into India and Pakistan) brought refugees, and the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War brought a further wave.
[geo-urban-s7.17.3] Natural increase adds to the population, and the city has regional primacy in eastern India, meaning it dominates the region's economy and services.
[geo-urban-s7.17.4] Census 2011 gave an urban agglomeration (the built-up city and its suburbs) of about 14.1 million people.
[geo-urban-s7.18.b] Migrants arriving faster than housing could be built ended up in slums or on the streets.
[geo-urban-s7.18.0] Bustees (slums) house many of the city's poorest people, in overcrowded conditions, because they cannot afford other housing.
[geo-urban-s7.18.1] Pavement dwellers have no shelter and live on the streets.
[geo-urban-s7.18.2] Census 2011 counted about 65 million people in slums across India; this is an India-wide figure, not a Kolkata figure.
[geo-urban-s7.19.b] Kolkata's economy weakened just as migrants kept arriving, so there were too few jobs.
[geo-urban-s7.19.0] Partition cut the city off from the jute-growing lands that became East Pakistan, so the economy declined because the jute mills lost their raw material.
[geo-urban-s7.19.1] Jobs did not keep pace with migrants, so many people depend on informal work.
[geo-urban-s7.19.2] Informal work includes hand-pulled rickshaws; Kolkata is one of the few cities in the world where these still operate.
[geo-urban-s7.20.b] Services and infrastructure (the basic systems a city runs on) were never built for so many people, and the city's low-lying site adds to the problems.
[geo-urban-s7.20.0] Water supply and sewerage (the system of pipes carrying away waste water) are poor, so many residents lack safe water and proper sanitation.
[geo-urban-s7.20.1] Monsoon rain floods the low-lying streets of the city.
[geo-urban-s7.20.2] Air pollution and traffic congestion add to the city's environmental problems.
[geo-urban-s7.21.b] Kolkata's planners, governments and charities have tried housing, transport, new towns and environmental solutions. Together these form the solutions part of the case study.
[geo-urban-s7.21.0] The Calcutta Metropolitan Development Authority, the city's planning body, was set up in 1970.
[geo-urban-s7.21.1] From the 1970s its bustee improvement programme provided water taps, toilets, paved lanes and drains.
[geo-urban-s7.21.2] Kolkata Metro, India's first, opened in 1984, which eases congestion by moving people underground.
[geo-urban-s7.21.3] Salt Lake City (Bidhannagar) is a planned township built from the 1960s on reclaimed wetland (wetland drained and filled to make building land) east of the city; its Sector V is an IT hub.
[geo-urban-s7.21.4] New Town (Rajarhat) was planned from the late 1990s, with IT parks (business parks for information technology firms).
[geo-urban-s7.21.5] The East Kolkata Wetlands were designated a Ramsar wetland of international importance in 2002; their fish ponds and fields use and naturally treat much of the city's sewage.
[geo-urban-s7.21.6] Mother Teresa founded the Missionaries of Charity in the city in 1950 and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1979.
[geo-urban-s7.22.b] To judge the responses, ask who benefited and what is left unsolved.
[geo-urban-s7.22.0] Bustee upgrading improved conditions for many residents, but slums remain.
[geo-urban-s7.22.1] The new towns serve the middle classes more than the poor.

[geo-urban-s7.23.0] Mumbai was originally seven islands, joined together by land reclamation (building up land from the sea), with a deep, sheltered natural harbour.
[geo-urban-s7.23.1] It was a British East India Company port, and cotton mills were built there from the 19th century.
[geo-urban-s7.23.2] Mumbai is India's financial capital, not its political capital; it houses the Reserve Bank of India headquarters and the Bombay Stock Exchange, founded in 1875, Asia's oldest.

[geo-urban-s7.23.6] Census 2011 gave a Greater Mumbai urban agglomeration (the city plus its built-up suburbs) of about 18.4 million people.

[geo-urban-s7.24.0] Dharavi is one of Asia's largest slums, in central Mumbai, with many small home-based industries such as recycling, leather and pottery.
[geo-urban-s7.24.1] Land and housing costs are very high on the narrow peninsula (a strip of land almost surrounded by water), which limits the space available for housing.
[geo-urban-s7.25.b] Dense population puts pressure on transport and services, and heavy monsoon rain can overwhelm the city.
[geo-urban-s7.25.0] The suburban railway carries several million passengers a day, and its trains are overcrowded.
[geo-urban-s7.25.1] Road congestion adds to the pressure on the transport network.
[geo-urban-s7.25.2] On 26 July 2005 about 944 mm of rain fell in one day at Santacruz, flooding the city.
[geo-urban-s7.25.3] Water supply and sanitation cannot keep pace with the dense population.
[geo-urban-s7.26.b] Mumbai's old industrial base collapsed in the 1980s, which changed where people worked.
[geo-urban-s7.26.0] The Great Bombay textile strike of 1982 led to the closure of most cotton mills.
[geo-urban-s7.26.1] Mill land was later redeveloped, so the city's industrial base changed, for example away from cotton.
[geo-urban-s7.27.b] Mumbai's planners tried to relieve the crowded island city by building a second city across the water.
[geo-urban-s7.27.0] Navi Mumbai is a planned twin city across the harbour, begun in the 1970s by CIDCO (City and Industrial Development Corporation of Maharashtra, set up 1970).
[geo-urban-s7.27.1] It is designed to take population and industry away from the crowded core of Mumbai.
[geo-urban-s7.28.b] Rehabilitation means rehousing slum dwellers in new buildings, with private developers paying for it.
[geo-urban-s7.28.0] The Slum Rehabilitation Authority, set up in 1995, lets developers rehouse slum dwellers free in new blocks and sell the remaining flats, so the developers pay for it from those sales.
[geo-urban-s7.28.1] The Dharavi Redevelopment Project was awarded to the Adani Group in 2022, as a plan to rebuild the slum, for example with new housing.

[geo-urban-s7.29.0] Mumbai Metro Line 1 opened in 2014, so some commuters have an alternative to the overcrowded roads and trains.

CANDIDATES geo-population-migration-s6: 6. Rural-to-urban migration in developed and developing regions
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.b] Rural-to-urban migration means people leaving the countryside (the donor region, which gives up people) for cities (the receiver region, which takes them in). It is one of the biggest forces reshaping where the world's people live.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.0] Urbanisation is the growing share of people living in towns, while urban growth is the rise in the number of people living in towns. The two are different measures, so keep them apart.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.1] Rural-to-urban migration is the main driver of urbanisation in developing countries (poorer countries with fast-growing populations). Natural increase in the cities, meaning births outnumbering deaths, is the other driver.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.2] Over half the world's people have lived in urban areas since about 2007 (UN), so the world passed the point where towns held more people than the countryside.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.3] About 68% of the world's people are expected to be urban by 2050 (UN, 2018 projection), which shows the trend is still rising.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.4] The fastest urban growth is now in Asia and Africa, where megacities (cities with very large populations) are forming.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.b] Push factors are the conditions that make people want to leave the countryside. Most come down to too little work, income or service in the place they live.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.0] Lack of jobs and low farm incomes leave rural people with too little money to live on, so they look for work elsewhere.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.1] Mechanisation of farming (using machines instead of workers) reduces the labour needed on the land, so fewer people are employed in farming.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.2] Small, subdivided holdings (farms split into ever smaller pieces) cannot support a family, so family members move away.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.3] Poor services, including schools, hospitals, broadband and transport, make rural life harder and push people toward places that have them.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.4] Drought, famine and land degradation (the land losing its ability to grow crops) push people out in the developing world.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.5] Isolation and lack of social life push people out, because rural life offers little to do and few people to meet.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.b] Pull factors are the attractions that draw people to cities. They are largely the opposite of the push factors, which is why movement from the countryside is so steady.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.0] Jobs in industry and services (work such as shops, offices and banks) give people a way to earn a living.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.1] Higher wages in the city make the move worth the cost.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.2] Third-level education (university and college) is found in cities, so young people go there to study.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.3] Better health care and services are available in cities, where there are more hospitals and facilities.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.4] Entertainment and the 'bright lights' of the city attract young people looking for a more exciting social life.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.5] Existing family networks create chain migration, which means one person moves and relatives and neighbours follow because someone is already there to help them settle.
[geo-population-migration-s6.3.b] Not all movement is toward cities. In developed countries some people now move the other way.
[geo-population-migration-s6.3.0] Counter-urbanisation is people moving out of cities to towns and the countryside, and it is found in developed countries (richer countries).
[geo-population-migration-s6.3.1] Remote working since COVID-19 lets people live outside cities while working in them, because they no longer need to be near the office every day.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.b] Ireland was a rural country until the later 20th century, and its population then concentrated in cities. This is the main developed-world case study.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.0] Census 1971 was the first census in which more people lived in towns than in rural areas, so the urban share crossed half in that year.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.1] Census 2016: about 63% of the population was urban.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.2] Flight from the land happened because farming mechanised and small farms became unviable (could no longer earn enough to survive), so people moved to cities.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.3] Industrial and service jobs concentrated in Dublin and the cities, which pulled the people who left farming toward them.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.b] Growth is concentrated in Dublin, with the commuter counties (counties near Dublin where people live and travel to work) and the regional cities also growing. Be careful with which Dublin figure you use.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.0] Census 2022: the State had about 5.15 million people.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.1] Co. Dublin had about 1.46 million; Dublin city and suburbs had about 1.26 million; the Greater Dublin Area (Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow) had about 2.1 million, about 40% of the State's population.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.2] Dublin is the primate city, a city much larger than any other in its country, so it dominates Irish life.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.3] Commuter towns, where many residents travel daily to work in Dublin, include Navan, Drogheda, Naas and Newbridge.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.4] Regional cities (city and suburbs): Cork about 220,000; Limerick about 100,000; Galway city about 85,000; Waterford about 60,000.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.5] Dublin's earlier planned growth created the new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown under the Myles Wright plan of 1967.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.b] Receiving large numbers of migrants brings Dublin both benefits and strains. The positives come from having many people in one place, and the negatives from fitting them all in.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.0] A large labour pool (many workers available to hire) attracts multinationals and service firms.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.1] Economies of scale support transport, universities and hospitals, because a large population makes these services cheaper per person to provide.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.2] A rich cultural life develops where so many people live.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.3] Housing shortages, high rents and prices, and homelessness occur because homes cannot be built as fast as people arrive.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.4] Urban sprawl (the city spreading outward over farmland) reaches into Meath, Kildare and Wicklow.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.5] Long commutes and traffic congestion result when workers live far out and travel in.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.6] Pressure on water supply and other services builds as the population grows.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.7] High-rise and infill development pressure (building taller, and building on gaps within the city) grows because land is scarce.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.b] Rural decline in Ireland is now local, in remote parishes and townlands (the smallest rural land divisions), rather than county-wide. The figures below show how uneven population is across the country.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.0] Census 2022: the population of every county grew between 2016 and 2022, so decline is within counties rather than whole counties.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.1] Leitrim had about 155,000 people in 1841 and about 35,000 in Census 2022, the least populous county.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.2] Mayo had about 138,000 people and about 25 people per km² in Census 2022; Co. Dublin had about 1,600 per km², so the contrast in density is very large.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.3] An ageing population and higher dependency ratios (more people too old or too young to work for each worker) follow because young adults leave.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.4] Fewer young adults and lower birth rates follow, since the people most likely to have children are the ones who move away.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.b] When young people leave, the services and community life that depended on them go too. Migration is not all loss for the donor region, though.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.0] Closure of schools, post offices, Garda stations, shops and pubs, and derelict houses, happen because too few customers and users remain to keep them open.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.1] Loss of community and sporting clubs, with GAA teams amalgamating (joining with neighbouring clubs to field a team), and farms with no successor, follow as young people leave.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.2] Cultural loss, including the decline of the Gaeltacht (the Irish-speaking regions), occurs as Irish speakers move away.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.3] Positive effects: less pressure on land, remittances (money sent home by migrants) and returning migrants, and some farm consolidation (neighbouring farms joined into larger, more viable ones).
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.b] The State has tried to slow decline in the regions and to spread growth more evenly, with plans and agencies set up between 1980 and 2018.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.0] The National Spatial Strategy (2002) set out gateways and hubs (larger and smaller centres chosen to attract growth) to spread growth.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.1] The decentralisation programme, announced in 2003, moves public service jobs out of Dublin.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.2] The Western Development Commission has been a statutory body (set up by law) since 1999.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.3] CLÁR, a programme of investment in depopulated rural areas, was launched in 2001.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.4] Údarás na Gaeltachta, founded in 1980, creates jobs in the Gaeltacht.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.5] The National Planning Framework (Project Ireland 2040), published in February 2018, plans for about 1 million more people by 2040, with about half of growth in the five cities (Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford) and their suburbs, and with the four cities other than Dublin growing faster than Dublin.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.6] The Town and Village Renewal Scheme and rural broadband with remote working hubs support rural areas, since remote work lets people stay in the countryside.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.7] Limitation: rural areas and regional cities still depend on the jobs and services that drew people to Dublin, so the pull of Dublin remains.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.b] Two European countries show the same donor and receiver pattern: a poorer region loses people to richer cities, and one capital dominates its country.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.0] Italy: after the Second World War, people moved from the Mezzogiorno (southern Italy) to the industrial cities of the north, Turin and Milan, and abroad to Germany, Switzerland and Belgium.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.1] The Mezzogiorno has about 20 million people (2020s), about one-third of Italy's population.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.2] Depopulating southern villages responded with €1 house schemes (selling empty houses very cheaply to attract new residents), such as Sambuca di Sicilia.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.3] France: Paris dominates the country; Jean-François Gravier's Paris et le désert français (1947) described the regions outside Paris as a French desert.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.4] Île-de-France (the region around Paris) has about 12 million people (2020s), about 18% of France's population.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.b] Migration from poor rural states to the big cities fills slums and sends remittances back to the villages.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.0] Migrants come from poorer rural states, Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, to Mumbai, Delhi and Kolkata.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.1] Push factors: small holdings, debt, landlessness (owning no land to farm) and drought.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.2] Pull factors: jobs in construction, factories and the informal sector (work without contracts or regular pay, such as street trading).
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.3] Census 2011: about 31% of Indians were urban, up from about 17% in 1951; about 833 million people (about 69%) were rural.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.4] Census 2011 urban agglomerations (a city with its built-up surroundings): Greater Mumbai about 18.4 million; Delhi about 16.3 million; Kolkata about 14.1 million.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.5] Bihar is India's densest state, at about 1,100 per km², which adds to the pressure to leave.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.b] The cities cannot house everyone who arrives, so slums grow. Villages left behind lose workers but gain money sent home.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.0] About 65 million people lived in slums (crowded areas of poor housing).
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.1] Dharavi, Mumbai, is one of Asia's largest slums, about 2 km² near the city centre, with hundreds of thousands of residents and many small industries such as recycling, leather, pottery and garments. Kolkata's slums are called bustees.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.2] Receiver impacts: pressure on water, sanitation and transport; informal work; and a large supply of cheap labour.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.3] Donor impacts: male out-migration leaves women, children and the old to farm, and working-age labour is lost.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.4] Remittances (money sent home) fund houses, education and farm inputs in donor villages.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.5] India is the world's largest recipient of remittances, mostly from international migrants, so the figure does not measure rural-to-urban flows directly.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.6] In the March 2020 COVID-19 lockdown, millions of migrant workers walked or travelled home from the cities.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.b] Brazil's migration runs from the drought-prone North-East to the south-east, and Brazil is now highly urbanised.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.0] Migrants from the sertão, the drought-prone semi-arid interior of the North-East (nordestinos), moved to São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro; some also helped build Brasília, the capital from 1960.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.1] Census 2022: Brazil had about 203 million people, about 87% of them urban (2020s).
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.2] São Paulo city had about 11.5 million people (Census 2022), and over 20 million in its metropolitan region; it is Brazil's largest city and industrial centre.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.3] Favelas (shanty towns) grow on hillsides and at the urban edge; Rocinha in Rio de Janeiro is one of Brazil's largest.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.4] Informal work is common in the favelas, and some favelas have crime and gang violence.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.5] The North-East, the donor region, loses young people.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.b] In China the government's household registration rules shape who can settle in a city, so migrants often work in cities without the rights of residents.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.0] The hukou (household registration) system, introduced in 1958, ties public services to a person's registered home, so a migrant is not fully entitled to services in the city.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.1] Nearly 300 million rural migrant workers (2023), many of them in coastal cities such as Shenzhen, Guangzhou and Shanghai.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.2] Shenzhen grew from a small town to a city of over 17 million after becoming China's first Special Economic Zone (an area with special rules to attract business) in 1980.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.3] China's urban share passed 50% in 2011.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.4] Rural villages are left with 'left-behind' children and elderly people, because working-age adults have gone to the cities.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.5] Migrants lack full access to urban schools, health care and housing, because their services are tied to their registered home.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.6] Hukou reform is gradual.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.b] The scale, speed and capacity of cities differ between the two kinds of region, so the impacts differ.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.0] In developed regions migration is slower, and cities can largely absorb it with planned housing and services.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.1] In developed regions the donor problem is ageing rural decline.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.2] In developing regions migration is fast and very large; cities cannot provide housing, jobs or services, so slums and the informal sector grow.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.3] In developing regions donor villages depend on remittances and lose working-age men.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.4] Urban growth in developing cities comes from both migration and high natural increase among young migrants, because young adults have many children.
