TARGET geo-option-global-interdependence-s2 (part 1 of 2): 2. An interdependent global economy
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.0.b] No country makes or grows everything it needs, so countries depend on one another. The key idea is the effects that follow when a decision is taken in one place and the results land in another.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.0.0] Countries and regions rely on each other for raw materials, components (parts used to make a product), markets (places to sell), investment, labour, food and a shared environment, so a decision in one place has effects elsewhere.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.0.1] Globalisation (the growing links between countries), through trade, multinational companies (firms operating in many countries), communications and migration, has deepened this interdependence.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.0.2] It has an economic form (trading patterns), an environmental form (shared global issues) and a political and social form (decisions on refugees, migration and human rights).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.1.b] A global product is made in some places and bought in others. Geographers call these producer regions and consumer regions, and the gap between them explains who gains most from trade.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.1.0] Producer regions supply raw materials, components and assembly (putting the parts together). These are often developing countries with low wages, which is why firms locate this work there.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.1.1] Consumer regions buy the finished product. These are mostly rich countries, and increasingly China and India, because their people have the income to buy it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.1.2] Value added (the extra worth created at each stage of making and selling a product) is shared very unequally along the chain: most of it is captured by design, branding and retail in consumer regions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.2.b] Apple is the main case study of a multinational whose product is designed in one country, built from parts made in several others, and sold worldwide. Following the iPhone shows how many regions one product links.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.2.0] Apple was founded in 1976 by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak, with headquarters in Cupertino, California (Apple Park, 2017). The iPhone launched in 2007.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.2.1] Apple designs in California, where its headquarters are. Its packaging states: 'Designed by Apple in California. Assembled in China.'
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.2.2] TSMC in Taiwan makes Apple's A-series and M-series chips (the processors inside its devices). Samsung and LG in South Korea supply screens, so no single country makes the whole phone.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.2.3] Other components come from further suppliers around the world. Minerals include cobalt (a metal used in batteries), most of which is mined in the DR Congo.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.2.4] Foxconn (Hon Hai Precision Industry), a Taiwanese contract manufacturer (a firm that builds products for other companies), assembles most iPhones in China, at Zhengzhou and Shenzhen.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.2.5] Foxconn and the Indian Tata group assemble iPhones in India near Chennai and Bengaluru, so assembly is spreading beyond China.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.2.6] Apple's largest sales regions are the Americas, Europe and Greater China, so its customers are spread across three continents.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.3.b] For the regions that make and supply the parts, Apple's orders bring real benefits but also costs. A good answer gives both sides, with named places.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.3.0] Positive: assembly and supply firms provide jobs for millions of people, bringing skills, industrial growth and export earnings to China.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.3.1] Positive: supplier firms grow because of Apple's orders, as TSMC and Foxconn show.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.3.2] Negative: a 2011 study by Kenneth Kraemer, Greg Linden and Jason Dedrick estimated that Apple kept about 58% of the sale price of an iPhone (2010 model) as profit, while Chinese assembly labour received under 2%. This shows how unequally value is shared.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.3.3] Negative: a cluster of worker suicides at Foxconn's Shenzhen factories in 2010 drew attention to long hours, strict discipline and pressure. Foxconn raised wages and put up safety nets (nets fixed to buildings to stop people falling), a response to the attention.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.3.4] Negative: in November 2022 workers at Foxconn's Zhengzhou plant protested over pay and COVID-19 lockdown conditions, delaying iPhone supplies worldwide.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.3.5] Negative: producer regions depend on Apple's orders, which can move. Some assembly has shifted to India, which shows that the orders can move.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.3.6] Negative: Amnesty International (2016) documented child labour in the DR Congo's hand-dug cobalt mines, which supply the metal for batteries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.4.b] Rich consumer regions gain the product and keep the most valuable parts of the chain, but they also send problems back to poorer countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.4.0] Consumers in rich countries gain access to new technology and services, paying high prices for products whose assembly is cheap.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.4.1] Most profit and the high-paid design and marketing jobs stay in the USA, because the company is based there.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.4.2] Electronic waste from discarded devices is exported to poorer countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.5.b] Ireland is both a place where Apple employs people and a country affected by decisions taken elsewhere about Apple. This is a good example of how one firm links a small economy to the EU and the USA.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.5.0] Apple opened at Hollyhill, Cork, in 1980, its first manufacturing plant in Europe. It now has about 6,000 employees in Cork and its European headquarters there.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.5.1] Apple's Cork campus does not assemble iPhones. Its role is European headquarters and support, not the Chinese assembly chain.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.5.2] In 2016 the European Commission ruled that Ireland had given Apple illegal tax benefits worth €13 billion. This was the recovery of illegal State aid (help from a government that breaks EU competition rules), not a fine, and Ireland argued against collecting it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.5.3] The General Court overturned the decision in 2020. In September 2024 the EU Court of Justice reinstated it, and Ireland received about €14 billion, including interest.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.5.4] A decision in Brussels therefore affected both Ireland and the USA, where the company is based.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.5.5] US tariffs (taxes on imports) on many Chinese goods from 2018, and higher tariffs on most countries' imports in 2025, have pushed Apple to move some assembly to India.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.6.b] Cadbury shows how a long-established British brand became part of an American multinational, and how Irish plants fit into its chain. It is a food company, so its chain begins on farms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.6.0] Cadbury was founded by John Cadbury in Birmingham, England, in 1824, so its origins are British. Its Bournville factory and model village for workers opened in 1879.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.6.1] The American firm Kraft Foods took over Cadbury in 2010. In 2012 Kraft's snacks business, including Cadbury, became Mondelēz International, headquartered in Chicago.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.6.2] Because Mondelēz is American, Cadbury is no longer a British-owned company.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.6.3] Cadbury Ireland has a chocolate factory at Coolock, Dublin, and a chocolate crumb plant at Rathmore, Co. Kerry.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.6.4] Rathmore uses milk from local dairy farms to make chocolate crumb (milk, sugar and cocoa), which is sent to Cadbury factories in Britain. It makes crumb, not finished bars.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.7.b] The cocoa in chocolate comes mainly from West Africa, where small farmers earn little and face serious problems. These points cover who grows cocoa, how their incomes are supported, and the child labour in the trade.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.7.0] Côte d'Ivoire is the world's largest cocoa producer, with about two-fifths of world output. Ghana is second. Together they grow over half of the world's cocoa, mostly exported as beans, so these two countries depend on one crop.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.7.1] Most cocoa is grown on small family farms of a few hectares, so individual farmers have little power over the price they are paid.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.7.2] From the 2020–21 season Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana added, because farm incomes were low, a Living Income Differential of 400 US dollars a tonne to cocoa sales to raise farmers' incomes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.7.3] A study for the US Department of Labor by NORC at the University of Chicago, published in 2020, found about 1.56 million children in child labour in cocoa growing in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana (2018–19).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.7.4] The Harkin–Engel Protocol (2001) committed the chocolate industry to end the worst forms of child labour in cocoa. Deadlines have been missed repeatedly.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.7.5] Irish dairy farms supplying Rathmore are also a producer region for the company.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.8.b] Cocoa growing also changes the land, and a country that depends on one export is exposed to price swings. Most of the profit from processing is made far from the farms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.8.0] Côte d'Ivoire has lost most of its forest since independence (1960), much of it to cocoa farms, because farmers clear forest to plant more cocoa.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.8.1] In 2024 cocoa prices rose to a record above 10,000 US dollars a tonne after poor West African harvests, caused by bad weather and the cocoa swollen shoot virus.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.8.2] Farmers gained far less than the world price, because their governments fix farm-gate prices (the price paid to the farmer) in advance.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.8.3] Value is mostly added outside Africa. The Netherlands, through the port of Amsterdam, is the world's largest cocoa-processing centre (where beans are turned into cocoa products).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.9.b] The company has made several choices about how it buys cocoa. One of them has been disputed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.9.0] Cadbury Dairy Milk carried the Fairtrade Mark (a label guaranteeing farmers a fairer deal) from 2009.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.9.1] Mondelēz launched its own sustainability programme, Cocoa Life, in 2012, to improve cocoa farming, with about 400 million US dollars committed over ten years.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.9.2] In 2017 Cadbury Dairy Milk in Britain and Ireland replaced the Fairtrade Mark with the Cocoa Life logo. Fair-trade campaigners criticised the decision.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.10.b] Chocolate is bought mostly by richer countries, which also keep most of its price. Their shoppers and governments can in turn pressure the producers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.10.0] Europe and North America consume most of the world's chocolate. Brands, marketing and retail take most of the price, because they add the most value.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.10.1] Consumer campaigns and EU rules pass pressure back to producers, so a decision in a consumer region changes how farms are run.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.10.2] The EU Deforestation Regulation (adopted 2023) will require firms selling cocoa, coffee, beef, soya, palm oil, rubber and timber in the EU to prove the goods were not grown on land deforested after 2020. Its application has been delayed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.11.b] Intel is a shorter alternative case study of a multinational, useful if you studied it in the economic option. Its chain runs from design in the USA to factories and customers around the world.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.11.0] Intel was founded in 1968 in Mountain View, California, and is headquartered in Santa Clara. Research and design are based in Oregon and California.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.11.1] Wafers (thin discs of silicon from which chips are cut) are made in fabs (chip factories) at Hillsboro (Oregon), Chandler (Arizona), Leixlip and Kiryat Gat (Israel). Leixlip makes processor wafers, not computers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.11.2] Assembly and test (the final stages, away from the wafer fabs) take place in Penang and Kulim (Malaysia), Ho Chi Minh City (Vietnam) and Chengdu (China).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.11.3] Customers are computer makers, such as Lenovo, HP and Dell, which sell worldwide.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.11.4] Intel's Leixlip fab has operated since 1989, and over €30 billion had been invested by 2023. Fab 34 opened in 2023.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.11.5] 49% of the Fab 34 joint venture (a business shared between two firms) was sold to Apollo Global Management for about $11 billion (2024).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.11.6] Decisions elsewhere affect Intel: the US CHIPS and Science Act 2022 (about $50 billion, US support for chip making), about 15,000 global job cuts announced in August 2024, and a US government stake of about 10% (2025).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.12.b] Ireland is a small, open economy that acts as a producer region for American multinationals. This makes it successful in trade but exposed to decisions taken in Washington, Brussels and London.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.12.0] Goods exports passed €200 billion in 2024 (CSO), and about a third went to the USA. Pharmaceutical and chemical products are the largest category.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.12.1] Ireland has a large goods trade surplus (exports worth more than imports), the largest of it with the USA, because so much is exported there.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.12.2] Ireland is a producer region for US multinationals: Pfizer at Ringaskiddy, Co. Cork, in pharmaceuticals; a medical-devices cluster in Galway, including Medtronic and Boston Scientific; and computer services, where Ireland is one of the world's largest exporters.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.12.3] IDA Ireland, set up in 1949 to attract foreign firms, has client firms employing about 300,000 people (2023–24).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.12.4] Dependence on US firms and markets makes Ireland vulnerable to US tax and tariff decisions. In early 2025 Irish exports to the USA rose sharply as US buyers stockpiled medicines before new tariffs.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.12.5] Brexit, a political decision, changed Irish trade routes and agri-food exports. The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020, and the transition ended on 31 December 2020.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.12.6] Corporation tax (the tax on company profits) is 12.5%, rising to 15% for very large groups from 2024 under the OECD agreement.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.13.b] Deforestation means clearing forest permanently, usually for farming or mining. It is a global issue because the land is cleared where trees grow but the demand comes from other regions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.13.0] Forests cover about 31% of the world's land, so clearing them changes the planet at scale, about 4.06 billion hectares (FAO Global Forest Resources Assessment 2020).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.13.1] About 10 million hectares a year were deforested in 2015–2020, so the loss continues every year.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.13.2] Most loss is in the tropics (the warm regions near the equator), in regions such as South America, Africa and South-East Asia.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.13.3] Loss is driven largely by demand in other regions for beef, soya, palm oil, timber, cocoa and minerals, so consumers far away help cause clearance.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.14.b] The Amazon shows how demand for food and animal feed in Europe and China helps drive clearance in Brazil.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.14.0] The Amazon is the world's largest tropical rainforest, with about 60% of it in Brazil.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.14.1] Close to a fifth of the original forest has been cleared. Cattle ranching is the largest cause, because forest is cut down to make pasture.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.14.2] Soya, much of it exported as animal feed to Europe and China, has spread north from Mato Grosso. Brazil is the world's largest exporter of soya beans.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.14.3] The Trans-Amazonian Highway was begun in the early 1970s, and roads, mining and dams add to clearance, because roads open forest to settlers and farmers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.14.4] PRODES (Brazil's satellite monitoring of clearance) recorded annual deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon of about 27,800 km² in 2004, about 4,600 km² in 2012 and about 13,000 km² in 2021, with a sharp fall from 2023.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.15.b] Clearing the Amazon harms people and climates far from Brazil, and the responses involve other countries too.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.15.0] Effects elsewhere: the carbon released adds to global warming, reduced rainfall recycling (the forest returning moisture to the air) affects farming beyond the forest, biodiversity is lost, and indigenous peoples are threatened.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.15.1] Scientists warn that the loss of about 20–25% of the forest could push parts of it towards degraded savanna (open grassland with few trees).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.15.2] Brazil's Forest Code requires 80% of Amazon land to be kept as a legal reserve, so only 20% of a holding may be cleared.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.15.3] Chico Mendes, a campaigner for the forest, was murdered on 22 December 1988.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.15.4] Responses involving other countries: the Amazon Soy Moratorium (2006), agreed by traders; the Amazon Fund (2008), with Norway as the largest donor; and the EU Deforestation Regulation, which sets rules on goods sold in the EU.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.16.b] In South-East Asia the driver is palm oil, an ingredient in many everyday goods. Clearance in one country has even affected its neighbours.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.16.0] Indonesia and Malaysia produce about 85% of the world's palm oil, used in foods, cosmetics and biofuels sold worldwide.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.16.1] Peat swamp forest (waterlogged forest on thick layers of partly rotted plant matter) in Borneo and Sumatra is cleared and burned to plant oil palms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.16.2] Regional haze (smoke pollution) in 1997 and 2015 affected Singapore and Malaysia, a transboundary effect (one that crosses a border) of clearance in another country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.16.3] The Bornean orangutan is listed as Critically Endangered (IUCN, 2016), because its forest home is being lost.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.17.b] Not all forest use is destructive. Sweden and Finland, Europe's most forested countries, use forests for a major export industry while keeping the stock of trees growing.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.17.0] About two-thirds of Sweden's land is forest. About three-quarters of Finland's land is forest, the highest share in the EU.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.17.1] Sweden's first Forestry Act (1903) required forest owners to replant after felling. The Forestry Act of 1993 gave equal weight to timber production and environmental goals.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.17.2] Sweden's standing timber volume has roughly doubled since its National Forest Inventory began in the 1920s, because growth exceeds felling. This is sustainable yield (cutting no more than grows back).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.17.3] Sweden and Finland are among the world's leading exporters of pulp, paper and sawn timber. IKEA, a Swedish company, is a major buyer of wood, so foreign demand supports the industry. UPM and Stora Enso are Finnish–Swedish forest companies.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.18.b] Buyers abroad can pressure producers to manage forests well, but the methods used are still criticised.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.18.0] Buyers in other countries demand certification (independent proof that wood comes from well-managed forests): the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC), founded in 1993, and PEFC (Programme for the Endorsement of Forest Certification), from 1999.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.18.1] Clear-felling (cutting all trees in an area at once) followed by replanting, mostly with Norway spruce and Scots pine, is the main method.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.18.2] Critics say clear-felling creates even-aged monocultures (stands of one kind of tree, all the same age), removes old-growth forest and dead wood, and threatens forest species.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.18.3] Clear-felling destroys the lichen that reindeer graze on in winter, causing conflict with Sámi reindeer herders in northern Sweden. Their grievance is about grazing and land use, not forestry as a whole.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.18.4] The Scandinavian case shows, in short, that that a forest economy can be managed sustainably when the state, owners and consumers act together.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.19.b] Ireland has little forest, so it relies on imports, which links Irish consumers to forest loss elsewhere.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.19.0] Ireland's forest cover is about 11%, mostly conifer plantations. Sitka spruce, from western North America, is the main tree.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.19.1] Coillte, the State forestry company, was set up in 1988. Private owners also hold forest.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.19.2] About 1% of Ireland was forested around 1900, so today's cover is a large rise from a very low base.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.19.3] Ireland imports products linked to tropical deforestation, such as palm oil and feed for beef, so Irish consumers are part of the chain.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.20.b] Desertification is a common exam topic, and it is easy to define it wrongly. It is about damage to the land, not about sand spreading.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.20.0] Desertification is land degradation (loss of the land's ability to support plants and crops) in drylands (areas with little rain). It is not the desert advancing a set distance each year.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.20.1] Drylands cover about 41% of the earth's land surface and are home to about 2 billion people (Millennium Ecosystem Assessment, 2005).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.20.2] The UN Convention to Combat Desertification was adopted in 1994. 17 June is World Day to Combat Desertification.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.b] The Sahel is the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, from Senegal and Mauritania through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad to Sudan. Natural and human causes combine there, and some of the causes lie in other regions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.0] Rainfall is about 200–600 mm a year, falling in one short wet season, so crops and pasture are easily damaged by drought.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.1] Physical causes: unreliable rainfall and drought, notably in the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.2] Human causes: population growth, overgrazing, overcultivation and fuelwood collection. Cash crops (crops grown for sale) such as groundnuts in Senegal and cotton take the best land for export, and nomads have been settled, which concentrates grazing on smaller areas.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.3] Global warming, linked to emissions in other regions, adds to drought.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.4] Effects: famine, conflict over land and water, and migration to cities and to Europe. Lake Chad has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s; a widely quoted UN figure puts the shrinkage at about 90%.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.5] The Great Green Wall (African Union, 2007) is a planned band about 8,000 km long from Senegal to Djibouti, aiming to restore 100 million hectares by 2030. Progress has been slow.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.6] Zaï pits (small planting holes) and stone lines, used in Yatenga, Burkina Faso, by farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo, trap water and soil.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.21.7] Farmer-managed natural regeneration (farmers protecting trees that regrow on their land) in Niger has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, in the Maradi and Zinder regions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.22.b] The Aral Sea shows how a decision made in one capital destroyed an environment and a fishing economy in other countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.22.0] In 1960 the Aral Sea, between Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, was the world's fourth-largest lake.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.22.1] From the 1960s Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to irrigate cotton. The sea shrank to a small fraction of its size and split.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.22.2] Fishing collapsed because the water shrank, and the port of Moynaq was left far from the shore.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.22.3] Salt and pesticide dust storms now blow from the exposed seabed, because the sea no longer covers it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.22.4] The loss was caused by irrigation, not by climate change.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.22.5] The Kok-Aral Dam (2005, Kazakhstan, with World Bank funding) raised the North Aral Sea, and fishing has partly recovered.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.23.b] Dryland damage is not only an African problem. In Europe it is linked to what supermarkets sell, and in China to a huge tree-planting scheme.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.23.0] Almería, in south-east Spain, is one of the driest parts of Europe. Tens of thousands of hectares of plastic greenhouses grow vegetables in winter for northern European supermarkets, including those in Ireland.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.23.1] The greenhouses are irrigated largely from groundwater, which is being over-pumped, so the water supply of a dry region is being used up.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.23.2] China's Three-North Shelter Forest Programme, begun in 1978 and due to run to 2050, plants belts of trees across northern China to hold back the Gobi and reduce dust storms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.23.3] Many trees have died where they were planted in unsuitable dry areas, so the scheme has had mixed results.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.24.b] Global warming is the rise in the planet's average temperature caused by greenhouse gases (gases that trap heat in the atmosphere). Emissions in some countries cause effects for everyone.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.24.0] Greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels in industrialised and emerging economies, from deforestation and from agriculture cause global warming.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.24.1] Atmospheric CO2 was about 280 ppm (parts per million) before the Industrial Revolution and was over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s (Mauna Loa).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.24.2] Global average surface temperature, a key measure of warming, has risen about 1.1–1.2 °C since the late 1800s (IPCC). 2024 was the warmest year on record and the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels (Copernicus).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.24.3] China has been the largest CO2 emitter since the mid-2000s, because of its industry, but the USA emits far more per person.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.24.4] Rich countries bear historical responsibility for much of the emissions that have built up in the atmosphere, because they industrialised first.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.25.b] This is the clearest interdependence point. Countries that emitted least often suffer most, because they are low-lying, dry or poor.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.25.0] Countries that emitted least suffer most, for example Bangladesh. Bangladesh, in the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta, faces floods, cyclones and sea-level rise.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.25.1] Small island states, such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives, are threatened by sea-level rise.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.25.2] Global sea level, which rises as ice melts and seawater warms, rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.25.3] Arctic summer sea ice, which melts as the air warms, has shrunk by about 13% per decade since 1979.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.25.4] The Great Barrier Reef has suffered repeated mass bleaching (corals losing their colour when stressed by warm water) since the late 1990s.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.25.5] Pakistan's floods of 2022 followed extreme monsoon rains and glacier melt. About a third of the country was flooded, over 1,700 people died and about 33 million were affected. Pakistan produces under 1% of global greenhouse gas emissions, so it caused very little of the warming that worsened the floods.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.25.6] Drought in the Sahel, rising food prices when harvests fail, and climate-driven migration spread the effects across regions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.26.b] Ireland both contributes to warming and feels its effects.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.26.0] Agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions. Transport produces about a fifth (EPA, early 2020s).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.26.1] Ireland's average temperature has risen about 0.9 °C since 1900.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.26.2] Storm Éowyn, in January 2025, is an example of the more extreme weather that warming brings.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.26.3] The Climate Act 2021 sets a 51% cut in emissions by 2030 (from 2018) and net zero (cutting emissions until what remains is balanced by removals) by 2050.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.27.b] No country can solve warming alone, so responses are agreements between countries. The key ones are listed by date, with an evaluation at the end.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.27.0] The UNFCCC (the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change), agreed at Rio de Janeiro in 1992, set up the framework for international co-operation.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.27.1] The Kyoto Protocol (1997, in force 2005) was never ratified (formally approved) by the USA, so the largest economy was outside it. The Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue 1.5 °C.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.27.2] The EU Emissions Trading System (a market in permits to emit carbon) began in 2005. The European Green Deal (2019) aims for climate neutrality by 2050 and at least a 55% cut by 2030 compared with 1990.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.27.3] COP30 (the annual UN climate conference) was held in Belém, Brazil, in November 2025.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.27.4] The Loss and Damage Fund was agreed at COP27 in Sharm el-Sheikh (2022) and became operational at COP28 in Dubai (2023), to help countries hit by climate damage, such as poor countries that emitted little.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.27.5] The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) required reporting from October 2023 for imports such as steel, cement, aluminium and fertilisers. Importers will pay for the carbon in these goods from 2026, so EU climate decisions affect exporters such as China and India.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.27.6] Evaluation: national pledges still fall short of the 1.5 °C goal, so co-operation between countries is essential.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.28.b] Different kinds of movers have different legal status, and examiners expect them to be kept apart.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.28.0] An economic migrant moves voluntarily for work and a better life.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.28.1] Under the UN Refugee Convention (1951), a refugee has a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. Its 1967 Protocol removed the time and place limits.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.28.2] An asylum seeker has applied for refugee status and is awaiting a decision.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.28.3] An internally displaced person has been forced to flee but remains inside their own country, so, unlike a refugee, has not crossed a border.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.28.4] A stateless person is not recognised as a national by any state, so no government is responsible for protecting them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.28.5] UNHCR is the UN refugee agency.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.28.6] Political refugees flee persecution or war that results from political decisions. Economic 'refugees' flee poverty, often caused by decisions elsewhere in trade, debt or conflict. The line between them is often blurred.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.29.b] Displacement is large, but the burden falls mainly on countries close to the conflict rather than on Europe.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.29.0] More than 120 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide in 2024 (UNHCR), including refugees, asylum seekers and internally displaced people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.29.1] Most refugees stay in neighbouring countries, because they can reach them most easily, mostly low- and middle-income ones, not in Europe.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.29.2] Turkey hosted the largest refugee population in the world for most of the 2010s and early 2020s, mostly Syrians.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.b] Syria is the main case of political refugees: a government's violent response to protest led to war and to a movement of people across several countries and into Europe. Each step is a decision in one place with effects in another.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.0] Protests in March 2011 were met with force by President Bashar al-Assad's government, leading to civil war.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.1] Syria's pre-war population was about 22 million. At the height of the war over half were displaced, including over 6 million refugees abroad and a similar number displaced inside Syria.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.2] Turkey hosted over 3 million Syrian refugees. Lebanon has the most refugees per head of population of any country. Zaatari camp in Jordan opened in 2012, to house refugees arriving from Syria.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.3] Alan Kurdi, a three-year-old Syrian boy, drowned and was photographed on a beach near Bodrum, Turkey, on 2 September 2015. The image changed public opinion in Europe.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.4] About 1.3 million asylum applications were made in the EU in 2015.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.5] Germany's decision to admit refugees, with Merkel's 'Wir schaffen das' (August 2015). Hungary built a border fence with Serbia in 2015, to block people crossing.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.6] EU–Turkey statement (March 2016): irregular migrants were returned from the Greek islands to Turkey, and the EU was to provide €6 billion for refugees in Turkey.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.30.7] The Assad government fell on 8 December 2024. Many refugees began to consider return, but homes, services and the economy were destroyed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.31.b] The Rohingya case shows what happens when a state refuses to recognise a people as citizens, so that no state protects them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.31.0] The Rohingya are a mainly Muslim minority from Rakhine State, Myanmar.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.31.1] Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law did not recognise them as one of the country's national groups, leaving most of them stateless, which means no state owes them protection.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.31.2] From August 2017 a military campaign of killings and village burning drove about 740,000 Rohingya into Bangladesh.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.31.3] About 1 million Rohingya live in camps around Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, including Kutupalong, the world's largest refugee camp. Restrictions limit their work and movement, so they depend on aid.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.31.4] From 2020 Bangladesh moved some refugees to Bhasan Char, a flood-prone island, which is a risk for the people moved there, in the Bay of Bengal.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.31.5] A UN fact-finding mission (2018) called for Myanmar's generals to be investigated for genocide. The Gambia brought a genocide case against Myanmar at the International Court of Justice in 2019.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.31.6] The military coup in Myanmar in February 2021 ended prospects of a safe return.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.32.b] Ukraine shows how a quick EU decision gave millions of people rights at once. It also raises a question about how other asylum seekers are treated.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.32.0] Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine began on 24 February 2022. It is a political decision that displaced millions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.32.1] The EU Temporary Protection Directive (2001) was activated for the first time on 4 March 2022, giving immediate rights to live and work, so people did not have to wait for an asylum decision.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s2.32.2] Temporary protection is not asylum. Over 4 million people held temporary protection in the EU in 2024, and Ireland had received over 100,000 Ukrainians by 2024.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-globalisation-s2: 2. The MNC and change
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.b] Modern manufacturing and services can move between regions and countries far more easily than older heavy industry. They are described as footloose, which means they are not tied to a raw material, a coalfield or a port. A multinational company (MNC, a firm that operates in several countries) can therefore choose where to locate.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.0] Light, high-value products such as chips, medicines and software are cheap to move by air, so the factory does not need to be near its customers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.1] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard sealed boxes) and cheaper transport lower the cost of moving goods between plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.2] Information and communications technology lets headquarters, the central office that controls the company, direct branches on any continent.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.3] Free movement of capital (money for investment) and lower trade barriers, through the World Trade Organization (WTO) and the EU single market, let companies serve markets across borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.4] Governments compete with grants and low taxes to attract investment, which gives companies a choice of locations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.0.5] MNCs can switch production between their own plants, so a plant that becomes less attractive can lose work to another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.b] Companies move production when another location offers a better return. The reasons are mostly about cost, markets and risk, and the moves go in both directions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.0] Lower labour and land costs, because wages and rents are a large part of what it costs to make a product.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.1] Tax advantages and grants, which lower the cost of setting up and running a plant.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.2] Being inside a trade bloc (a group of countries with shared trade rules), which avoids tariffs (taxes on imported goods).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.3] Being close to growing markets, so goods reach customers more cheaply.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.4] Access to skills, and political and economic stability, which make an investment safer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.5] Investment moved into Ireland, for example Intel in Leixlip in 1989 and Dell in Limerick in 1990, from the 1970s to the 1990s.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.1.6] Investment also moved out of Ireland when costs rose, for example Dell's manufacturing in 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.b] Mobility is not limited to the case study. These examples show the same forces at work for other companies and places, and they can support an answer on mobility.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.0] Nissan opened a car plant at Sunderland, north-east England, in 1986. It is a Japanese company locating inside the EU (then EEC) market, so its cars avoid tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.1] Bengaluru (Bangalore), India, is an IT and business-process outsourcing hub (a centre for work contracted out to another firm). Tata Consultancy Services, Infosys and Wipro are Indian IT firms, and call centres and IT services have moved there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.2] Apple is growing its iPhone assembly in India, with Foxconn and Tata near Chennai and Bengaluru, to reduce dependence on China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.2.3] Intel opened Fab 34 (a fab is a chip-making factory) at Leixlip in 2023. It cancelled a planned fab at Magdeburg, Germany, which had been announced in 2022 and was cancelled in 2025.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.3.b] When a plant moves, one region gains what another loses. The effects on the region matter because many plants are controlled from outside it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.3.0] The receiving region gains jobs, skills and exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.3.1] The losing region suffers unemployment, the loss of spin-off jobs (jobs in local suppliers and services that depended on the plant) and emigration of workers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.3.2] Branch-plant economies (regions whose factories are owned by companies based elsewhere) are vulnerable because the decisions about their plants are taken abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.b] A branch plant is a factory or office owned and controlled by a company headquartered elsewhere. Headquarters decides whether it opens or closes, to suit the whole company rather than the host region.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.0] Strategies that open branch plants: expansion into new markets; locating inside a trade bloc; following tax incentives and grants; acquiring other companies through mergers and takeovers; joint ventures that share the cost of huge investments; and government subsidy schemes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.1] The US CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 is a subsidy scheme: it provided about 50 billion US dollars of US government support for chip making and research in the USA. The EU Chips Act followed in 2023.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.2] Strategies that close branch plants: cost-cutting and rationalisation, which means concentrating production in fewer, larger plants; relocation to cheaper countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.3] Other closing strategies: outsourcing to contract manufacturers (other firms paid to make the product); falling demand for a mature product; and mergers that leave duplicate plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.4.4] Reshoring, which means bringing production back home for security of supply, so that the company is not dependent on distant plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.b] Dell is the clearest Irish example of an MNC moving production. Its story begins with a company built on a new way of selling computers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.0] Dell was founded in 1984 by Michael Dell in Austin, Texas. Its headquarters are in Round Rock, Texas.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.1] Dell sold direct: it built computers to order and sold them by phone and, from the mid-1990s, online, with no shops in between.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.2] Dell set up in Limerick (Raheen) in 1990. Limerick became its European manufacturing base, and Dell was one of Ireland's largest exporters.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.5.3] Dell went private in 2013 and bought EMC in 2016, which gave it EMC's large operation at Ovens, Co. Cork.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.b] In 2009 Dell closed its Limerick manufacturing and moved it to Poland. The move shows headquarters choosing what suits the whole company, with serious effects on the host region.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.0] In January 2009 Dell announced that it would move its Limerick manufacturing to Łódź, Poland, with the loss of about 1,900 jobs, plus job losses at local suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.1] Reasons: lower wages and costs in Poland; Polish government aid, which the EU approved; nearness to growing markets in Central and Eastern Europe.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.2] More reasons: falling PC prices, which squeezed profits, and the 2008-09 recession, which cut demand and made cost savings urgent.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.3] Effects on Limerick: the loss of about 1,900 direct jobs and of jobs at local suppliers, and the loss of one of Ireland's largest exporters.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.4] The EU Globalisation Adjustment Fund paid for retraining and support for the former Dell workers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.6.5] Dell kept its sales, support and other offices in Ireland. Manufacturing closed; the company did not leave Ireland.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.b] Intel, the chip maker studied in the multinational company note, shows corporate strategy at work: heavy investment, raising money by selling a share, cutting jobs when profits fall, and relying on government support.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.0] Intel invested billions of euro in its Leixlip campus, with over €30 billion invested by 2023.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.1] In 2024 Intel sold 49% of the Fab 34 joint venture to Apollo Global Management, an American investment firm, for about 11 billion US dollars, to raise money for its other projects.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.2] Intel announced about 15,000 global job cuts in August 2024, when its profits fell, with further cuts in 2025.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.7.3] Government support includes the US CHIPS and Science Act and a US government stake of about 10% in Intel in 2025.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.b] Product life cycle (PLC) theory, put forward by Raymond Vernon in 1966, says a product passes through introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Its best location changes at each stage, which is why MNCs move production over time.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.0] Stages: introduction (development), growth, maturity and decline. Sales are drawn as a curve against time.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.1] Introduction and growth happen in the core (the rich, developed countries), close to headquarters, research and development, skilled workers and wealthy early customers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.2] At maturity the product is standardised and competition cuts prices, so production moves to cheaper locations to keep costs down: first semi-peripheral countries (middle-income countries) such as Ireland in the past, then Eastern Europe and Asia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.8.3] In decline the plant closes, or it is upgraded to make a new product.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.9.b] Dell's move to Poland is a textbook mature-stage relocation.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.9.0] The personal computer became a mature, standardised product with falling prices, so competing on cost became essential.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.9.1] Dell therefore moved its assembly from Limerick to Łódź in Poland, a cheaper location, in 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.9.2] The Dell case shows the mature-stage pattern: production is moved to cut costs while the company keeps sales and support in Ireland.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.b] Intel's products have short lives, so its fabs are always being renewed. The cycle shapes where new technology is made and which plants survive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.0] Each chip generation has a short life of a few years.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.1] New processes are developed at Hillsboro, Oregon, then copied exactly into fabs such as Leixlip. This is Intel's 'Copy Exactly!' policy, which copies equipment and procedures exactly from the development fab to the others.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.2] Older fabs are upgraded or closed as the cycle moves on.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.3] Ireland keeps its place by winning each new generation, as Fab 34 does for the Intel 4 and Intel 3 generations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.10.4] Intel missed the start of the smartphone and AI chip cycles, which helped rivals. Nvidia leads in chips for artificial intelligence, and Apple dropped Intel chips from its Macs from 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.b] Information technology (IT) changes how an MNC makes, controls and sells its products, and it changes what customers want. Exams pair this with the product life cycle.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.0] Computer-aided design (designing on computer) and automation (machines doing tasks) are used in the fab.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.1] Global communication lets one headquarters run plants on several continents.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.2] Real-time stock control and just-in-time supply (parts arrive just when needed) reduce stock held at each plant.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.3] Online sales: Dell's direct model moved online from the mid-1990s.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.4] New technology changes demand, moving from PCs to smartphones and then to cloud computing (using remote servers to store and process data) and AI. This creates new markets and destroys older ones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.11.5] Moore's Law, from Gordon Moore in 1965, states that the number of transistors (tiny switches that make up a chip) on a chip doubles about every two years.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.b] Teleservices are services delivered at a distance using telecommunications, such as call centres, customer support, technical help desks, and shared services such as payroll and accounts. They can be located almost anywhere, so countries compete for them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.0] Why Ireland attracted them: an English-speaking and multilingual workforce, a time zone between the USA and Europe, good broadband, and IDA support (help from the State agency that attracts foreign investment).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.1] Apple's Cork campus provides AppleCare customer support in many European languages.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.2] Microsoft has been in Ireland since 1985.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.12.3] Threat: routine teleservices are moving to cheaper destinations such as India (Bengaluru) and the Philippines (Manila), and automation and AI chatbots are reducing routine jobs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.13.b] Ireland hosts the European or international headquarters of major internet companies, which makes the IT sector an important part of the economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.13.0] Google's European headquarters are at Barrow Street, Grand Canal Dock, Dublin, in the area known as Silicon Docks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.13.1] Meta (Facebook) has its international headquarters in Dublin.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.13.2] Ireland is one of the world's largest exporters of computer services.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.b] Data centres are large buildings filled with servers (powerful computers) that run cloud computing. Ireland has attracted many, but they bring a pressure on the electricity grid.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.0] Ireland attracts them because of its cool climate, which reduces cooling costs, its connectivity, and the technology companies already here.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.1] Data centres used about 21% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO), up from 5% in 2015. Most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.2] Operators include Amazon Web Services, Microsoft and Google.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.14.3] EirGrid (the company that runs the electricity grid) restricted new data-centre connections in the Dublin area from 2022, because they use such a large share of the electricity.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.b] E-commerce is buying and selling goods and services online. It changes where goods are stored, how they are delivered and which shops survive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.0] Amazon was founded in 1994 by Jeff Bezos in Seattle, USA. Alibaba was founded in 1999 in Hangzhou, China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.1] Effects: online-only companies appear, some high-street shops decline, and warehouses and delivery services grow.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.2] Cross-border parcels from Asia increase.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.3] Dell was an early large online seller.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.15.4] COVID-19 lockdowns, which began in Ireland in March 2020, accelerated online buying.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.b] The future of economic activity is being shaped by new ways of working, new technology and changing politics. Each trend brings opportunities and risks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.0] Remote and hybrid working (partly at home, partly in the office) has continued after COVID-19.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.1] Artificial intelligence and automation are reducing routine jobs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.2] Reshoring and friend-shoring (producing in allied countries): a move to secure supply of strategic industries such as chips.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s2.16.3] Risks for Ireland come from the global minimum tax, under which the rate is 15% for groups with turnover over €750 million, which reduces the tax advantage, and from US tariffs.
