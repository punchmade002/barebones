[geo-population-migration-s5.0.b] When people move, they carry their culture, appearance and beliefs with them. These three categories overlap but are not the same, and migration can bring all three into contact in one place.
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.0] An ethnic group is a group of people who share culture, language, ancestry, traditions or religion, which is why members often feel they belong to the same community.
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.1] A race is a group classed by physical traits such as skin colour. Race is a social idea with no biological basis for ranking people, so no race is naturally better than another.
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.2] Religion is a shared system of belief and practice. It can cut across ethnic and racial groups, so people of different ethnic groups or races may share one religion.
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.3] A multi-ethnic society contains several ethnic groups living together. A multicultural society goes further: it is one in which those groups keep distinct cultural practices.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.b] When migrants and the host population meet, the result can be hostility or acceptance. These terms name the attitudes, the settlement patterns and the policies that governments use in response.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.0] Racism is prejudice (an unfair opinion formed in advance) or discrimination (unfair treatment) based on race or ethnicity.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.1] Xenophobia is fear or hostility towards foreigners. Islamophobia is prejudice against Muslims, so it targets people because of their religion.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.2] Segregation is the clustering of migrants in particular areas. It happens either by choice, for support, cheap housing and chain migration (earlier migrants helping relatives and neighbours to follow them), or because discrimination forces migrants there.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.3] Assimilation is the adoption of the majority culture by migrants, so their own culture is gradually given up.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.4] Integration is the joining of migrants to society while they keep some of their own culture.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.5] Multiculturalism is the acceptance of several cultures as equal within one society, so no single culture is treated as the norm.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.b] Tensions rarely come from difference alone. They arise from circumstances, discrimination and politics, and the same general mechanisms appear in each of the case studies below.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.0] Competition for jobs, housing and services, especially in recessions (periods when the economy shrinks and jobs are scarce), and fear that migrants will undercut wages by working for less.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.1] Visible difference, such as skin colour or dress like the hijab (the headscarf worn by some Muslim women), makes minorities easy targets of prejudice.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.2] Religious practice in public space can clash with secular (non-religious) or majority-religion norms. Examples are places of worship, dress, halal food (food prepared according to Islamic rules), holy days and faith schools.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.3] Residential segregation and concentration in deprived areas reinforce poverty and separation, because people who live apart have fewer chances to mix at school or work.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.4] Language barriers in schools, work and services make it harder for migrants to take part fully.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.5] Political exploitation: far-right and anti-immigration parties and movements, and misinformation on social media, turn worries into hostility.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.6] Second-generation issues: children born in the host country may face discrimination and conflict over identity, since they belong to two cultures.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.7] Positive outcomes include cultural enrichment through food, music, sport and festivals, new places of worship, intercultural schools and mixed marriages.

CANDIDATES geo-population-migration-s3: 3. Changing migration patterns in Ireland
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.b] Migration has its own vocabulary, and examiners expect you to use it precisely. These terms let you describe who moves, where, and why.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.0] Migration is a permanent or long-term change of residence, so a holiday or a short work trip does not count.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.1] Emigration is leaving a country; immigration is arriving in one. Net migration is immigrants minus emigrants, so it is positive when more people arrive than leave and negative when more leave than arrive.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.2] Internal migration moves people within a country; international migration crosses borders, so it is the kind that meets immigration laws.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.3] Voluntary migration is a free choice; forced migration is driven by conflict or persecution (being treated badly or harmed because of who you are or what you believe), so people have little or no choice.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.4] Economic migrants move for work; refugees and asylum seekers (people who ask another country for protection) flee persecution. Asylum policy is in Migration policy in Ireland and the EU.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.5] Return migration is emigrants coming back to their home country, such as Irish emigrants returning in the 1970s.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.6] Chain migration is when migrants follow family or friends who have already moved, because those contacts offer information, a place to stay and help finding work.
[geo-population-migration-s3.1.b] People move because something drives them away from home (a push factor) or something attracts them to another place (a pull factor). Ireland has examples of both.
[geo-population-migration-s3.1.0] Push factors in Ireland: unemployment, low wages, emigration of young people in recession (a period when the economy shrinks and jobs are lost) and the decline of farm labouring (paid work on other people's farms), which left rural workers without jobs.
[geo-population-migration-s3.1.1] Pull factors in Ireland: jobs in the Celtic Tiger economy (the period of rapid growth from the mid-1990s), high wages in the UK, the USA and Australia, and the availability of work permits (official permission to take a job in a country).
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.b] Even when push and pull factors favour a move, obstacles can stop or reduce it. These barriers are also called intervening obstacles.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.0] Distance and the cost of travel, because a longer journey costs more money and time.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.1] Immigration laws, visas (permits to enter or stay) and quotas (limits on how many people may enter).
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.2] Language, because it is hard to work or settle where you cannot communicate.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.3] Family ties and age: people with family at home, or older people, are less willing or able to move.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.4] Lack of information, because people will not move to a place they know little about.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.5] Cultural difference, such as unfamiliar customs, which makes settling in harder.
[geo-population-migration-s3.3.b] Geographers have turned patterns in migration into simple models. Each gives a short rule you can use to explain why and how people move.
[geo-population-migration-s3.3.0] Ravenstein's laws of migration say that most migrants move short distances, in steps (for example from a village to a town and then to a city).
[geo-population-migration-s3.3.1] Ravenstein also said that each migration stream has a counter-stream (people moving the opposite way), that young adults are most likely to migrate, and that cities attract migrants.
[geo-population-migration-s3.3.2] Lee's push-pull model says migration is driven by push and pull factors and reduced by intervening obstacles (the barriers to migration listed above).
[geo-population-migration-s3.4.b] Migrants are not a random sample of the population. Certain groups move far more than others, which shapes both the place they leave and the place they reach.
[geo-population-migration-s3.4.0] Migration is age-selective: mostly young adults move, because they are free to take jobs and have fewest ties.
[geo-population-migration-s3.4.1] In the 1950s more women than men left some rural areas.
[geo-population-migration-s3.5.b] Emigration has shaped Irish population for well over a century. The Great Famine (a disaster in the 1840s when the potato crop failed and many starved) began a long decline in numbers.
[geo-population-migration-s3.5.0] The Great Famine (1845-49) and continuous emigration from the 1840s: about 1 million died and about 1 million emigrated across the whole island.
[geo-population-migration-s3.5.1] The State's population fell at almost every census (the official count of the population, taken regularly) from 1841 to 1961.
[geo-population-migration-s3.5.2] State population was about 6.5 million (Census 1841), about 3.0 million (Census 1926) and about 2.8 million (Census 1961), the lowest ever recorded.
[geo-population-migration-s3.5.3] The only break in the decline was a very slight rise between Census 1946 and Census 1951.
[geo-population-migration-s3.5.4] Over 30 million Americans report Irish ancestry (US Census Bureau surveys, 2020s), a sign of how many people left.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.b] The 1950s saw the highest emigration since the 1880s. Ireland was a donor region (a place people leave), and most emigrants went to Britain.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.0] Causes at home: economic stagnation (no growth) and protectionism (taxing imports to shield home industry), unemployment, and the decline of small farms and farm labouring as farming mechanised and needed fewer workers.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.1] Britain pulled workers because its post-war economy needed labour in building, factories, nursing and transport.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.2] Low fares and the Common Travel Area made the move easy. The Common Travel Area dates from the 1920s and lets Irish citizens live and work in the UK without permits.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.3] Chain migration also helped, as people followed relatives who had already gone.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.4] Net emigration (people leaving minus people arriving) was about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.5] Most went to Britain: London, Birmingham, Manchester, Coventry and Liverpool.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.6] The Commission on Emigration and Other Population Problems reported in 1954.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.7] Impact: Ireland, as donor region, lost young workers and its rural population declined.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.b] Government policy changed direction around 1958, turning towards economic growth and foreign investment. As jobs appeared, emigration fell and, for the first time since the Famine, more people arrived than left.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.0] Economic Development by T. K. Whitaker and the First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958, cut emigration.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.1] The IDA (Industrial Development Authority), set up in 1949, was given a central role in attracting foreign investment from 1969.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.2] The 1970s were the first decade of net immigration since the Famine: returning emigrants and their children came back, and jobs were available in new factories.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.3] Ireland joined the EEC (the European Economic Community, forerunner of the EU) on 1 January 1973.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.4] State population rose from about 3.0 million (Census 1971) to about 3.4 million (Census 1981).
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.b] The gains of the 1970s did not last. A weak economy in the 1980s sent many people abroad again.
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.0] Causes: recession, high unemployment and high taxes.
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.1] Net emigration of over 200,000 between 1981 and 1991, including many graduates. This loss of educated people is the so-called brain drain.
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.2] Destinations: Britain and the USA, where many emigrants were undocumented (living and working without legal permission).
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.3] US visa programmes in the late 1980s and early 1990s (the Donnelly and Morrison visas) regularised many Irish emigrants, meaning they gained legal status.
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.4] The State's population fell slightly between Census 1986 and Census 1991; both were about 3.5 million.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.b] From the mid-1990s a booming economy, nicknamed the Celtic Tiger, reversed the old pattern. Ireland now attracted workers from many countries.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.0] Net immigration from 1996, as returning Irish, EU migrants, non-EU workers on work permits and asylum seekers (people seeking protection from persecution) arrived.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.1] EU enlargement on 1 May 2004 added 10 new members, including Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Slovakia and the Czech Republic.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.2] Ireland, the UK and Sweden were the only existing members to open their labour markets immediately, so citizens of the new members could work here without delay.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.3] Census 2006: about 420,000 non-Irish nationals (about 10% of the population); Polish about 63,000, the largest group after UK nationals.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.4] Census 2006 State population was about 4.2 million.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.5] Ireland became a country of immigration, meaning it now received more people than it sent abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s3.10.b] The Celtic Tiger ended abruptly in 2008, and emigration returned. Many of those leaving were the young and skilled.
[geo-population-migration-s3.10.0] Causes: the property crash, the banking crisis, which began in 2008, unemployment, and the EU-IMF bailout (emergency loans from the EU and International Monetary Fund) agreed in November 2010.
[geo-population-migration-s3.10.1] Net emigration from about 2009 to the mid-2010s; many leavers were skilled young graduates, and many recent immigrants also left.
[geo-population-migration-s3.10.2] Emigration peaked at about 89,000 in the year to April 2013 (CSO estimate), the highest since the 1980s; about half were Irish nationals.
[geo-population-migration-s3.10.3] Main destinations: Australia, the UK, Canada and the USA. Australia's working holiday visa (a visa letting young people live and work there for a limited time) was widely used.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.b] As the economy recovered, the flow reversed once more. Net immigration returned in the mid-2010s and continued.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.0] Causes: economic recovery, job growth in multinationals and services, returning Irish emigrants, and non-EU skilled workers.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.1] Ukrainians came under EU temporary protection after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022; over 100,000 people from Ukraine came to Ireland by 2024.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.2] International protection applicants (people applying for asylum) also arrived.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.3] Housing pressure, because more people needing homes added to demand for housing.
[geo-population-migration-s3.12.b] Population change has two parts: natural increase (births minus deaths) and net migration. Comparing them shows which one drove growth.
[geo-population-migration-s3.12.0] Net migration of about 190,000 between 2016 and 2022 was larger than natural increase (about 170,000), so migration was the bigger source of population growth.
[geo-population-migration-s3.13.b] Decades of immigration have made Ireland's population much more varied. The 2022 census gives the size of the immigrant population and its main nationalities.
[geo-population-migration-s3.13.0] About 630,000 non-Irish nationals (about 12% of the population), plus about 170,000 dual Irish citizens (people who hold Irish and another citizenship).

[geo-population-migration-s3.14.b] Centuries of emigration have left Irish people spread around the world. This scattered population is called the diaspora.
[geo-population-migration-s3.14.0] Irish communities and people of Irish descent live in Britain, the USA and Australia.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.b] A donor region is a place that loses people through emigration. Losing so many people, mostly young, damaged Irish communities.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.0] Loss of young workers and skills (brain drain), and of the State's investment in their education, because the country paid to train people who then worked abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.1] Ageing rural population and an imbalanced age structure, because the young left and the old stayed.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.2] Rural decline: closed schools, fewer marriages and depopulated parishes.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.3] Lower birth rates, because fewer young adults remained to have children, and family separation.
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.b] Emigration was not all loss. It also brought some benefits to Ireland.
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.0] Emigrant remittances (money sent home by emigrants to their families).
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.1] Less pressure on jobs and welfare, because fewer people were looking for work or needing support.
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.2] Emigrants returned with skills, capital (money to invest) and experience in the 1970s and 1990s.
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.3] Diaspora links support trade, tourism and investment.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.b] A receiver region is a place that gains people through immigration. Immigration since the 1990s has brought Ireland a range of benefits.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.0] Fills labour shortages in construction, health care, hospitality and technology.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.1] Young workers pay taxes and offset ageing, because they balance a population with many older people.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.2] New skills and enterprise (business start-ups), because migrants bring training and ideas from abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.3] Cultural diversity, such as food, sport, languages and religions.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.4] Keeps rural schools and parishes open, because newcomers add children and residents.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.5] Boosts population growth, as net migration was the larger component of growth in 2016-2022.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.b] Rapid immigration also creates problems. Services and communities have to adjust to more people.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.0] Pressure on housing, schools and health services, because more people share the same supply.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.1] Exploitation of some migrant workers, such as being badly treated or underpaid.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.2] Language barriers in schools, where children may not speak English or Irish.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.3] Integration challenges (helping newcomers to take part fully in society) and racism, detailed in Ethnic, racial and religious issues arising from migration.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.4] Remittances leave Ireland, because migrants send money to families abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s3.19.b] Data questions ask why immigration to Ireland increased once the recession ended. These reasons explain the rise.
[geo-population-migration-s3.19.0] Economic recovery and job creation, which drew workers.
[geo-population-migration-s3.19.1] Multinational investment, as large foreign-owned companies created jobs.
[geo-population-migration-s3.19.2] EU free movement, which lets citizens of EU countries live and work in Ireland.
[geo-population-migration-s3.19.3] Return of Irish emigrants.
[geo-population-migration-s3.19.4] Ireland is English-speaking and in the EU.
[geo-population-migration-s3.20.b] Immigration dropped sharply in these years because the Celtic Tiger economy collapsed. Data questions ask for a reason.
[geo-population-migration-s3.20.0] The recession, the collapse of construction and rising unemployment, which removed the jobs that had attracted migrants.
[geo-population-migration-s3.20.1] Many EU accession migrants (those from the countries that joined in 2004) returned home.

CANDIDATES geo-population-migration-s4: 4. Migration policy in Ireland and the EU
[geo-population-migration-s4.0.b] Every government has to decide who may enter, work and stay. Governments balance the economic need for migrants against public opinion and their legal duties to refugees, and the key terms below show why different migrants are treated differently.
[geo-population-migration-s4.0.0] Aims of policy: to control the numbers admitted, fill labour and skills shortages, meet humanitarian and legal duties to refugees, protect security and support integration (helping newcomers take part in society).
[geo-population-migration-s4.0.1] EU citizens hold free movement rights within the EU, meaning they can live and work in any member state. Non-EU citizens are third-country nationals, so national policy controls their entry and stay.
[geo-population-migration-s4.0.2] An economic migrant moves mainly to work or to improve living standards, so the state can decide whether it needs that worker.
[geo-population-migration-s4.0.3] A refugee meets the 1951 Refugee Convention definition: a well-founded fear of persecution (serious harm or mistreatment) for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion.
[geo-population-migration-s4.0.4] An asylum seeker is a person who has applied for protection and is awaiting a decision, so not every asylum seeker is recognised as a refugee.
[geo-population-migration-s4.0.5] The Refugee Convention was signed in Geneva in 1951; its 1967 Protocol removed its geographic and time limits, so it applies to refugees anywhere and at any time.
[geo-population-migration-s4.0.6] UNHCR is the UN refugee agency.
[geo-population-migration-s4.1.b] People seek asylum (protection in another country) when staying at home is unsafe. Exams ask for one reason, so learn all three.
[geo-population-migration-s4.1.0] War and conflict force people to flee their country because their lives are in danger, for example the war in Syria.
[geo-population-migration-s4.1.1] Persecution for religion, ethnicity, political opinion or sexuality means people are targeted for who they are or what they believe.
[geo-population-migration-s4.1.2] Human rights abuses by the state against its own people leave citizens with no protection at home.
[geo-population-migration-s4.2.b] These are the pull factors (features of a destination that attract people) drawing migrants to Europe. They combine the quality of life in EU states with their position on the map.
[geo-population-migration-s4.2.0] Safety, the rule of law (everyone is subject to the same fair laws) and respect for human rights give people protection they lack at home.
[geo-population-migration-s4.2.1] High living standards, jobs and welfare provision (state support such as benefits) offer a better life and income.
[geo-population-migration-s4.2.2] Existing family and diaspora communities (people from the same homeland already settled abroad) already live in EU states, so newcomers have support and contacts.
[geo-population-migration-s4.2.3] Nearness to conflict zones in the Middle East and Africa makes Europe the closest destination for many.
[geo-population-migration-s4.3.b] Ireland's borders with the UK and the EU are governed by different arrangements, and Ireland is outside Schengen (the EU area without internal border checks).
[geo-population-migration-s4.3.0] The Common Travel Area lets Irish and British citizens move, live and work freely between Ireland and the UK. It dates from the 1920s and was kept after the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020.
[geo-population-migration-s4.3.1] Ireland is outside Schengen because of the Common Travel Area; Brexit (the UK leaving the EU) did not end it.
[geo-population-migration-s4.3.2] EU, EEA (the EU plus Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein) and Swiss citizens may live and work in Ireland without permits.
[geo-population-migration-s4.3.3] On 1 May 2004 Ireland, the UK and Sweden were the only existing members to give citizens of the new member states immediate access to their labour markets.
[geo-population-migration-s4.3.4] Ireland's labour market was later opened to Bulgaria and Romania, after a delay.
[geo-population-migration-s4.3.5] Polish nationals were the largest non-Irish group in Census 2022, at about 94,000, which shows the effect of the open labour market.
[geo-population-migration-s4.4.b] Non-EU workers need a permit before they can work in Ireland, and the system is skills-based: it favours the jobs the economy lacks workers for.
[geo-population-migration-s4.4.0] Employment permits are issued by the Department of Enterprise.
[geo-population-migration-s4.4.1] The Critical Skills Employment Permit covers occupations in short supply, such as ICT, engineering and medicine, and can lead to family reunification (the worker's family joining them in Ireland).
[geo-population-migration-s4.4.2] The General Employment Permit is the second main type, and a list of ineligible occupations restricts which jobs qualify.
[geo-population-migration-s4.4.3] A labour-market test checks that no suitable Irish or EU worker is available, so a non-EU worker is only hired when the job cannot be filled locally.
[geo-population-migration-s4.4.4] Purpose: to fill gaps in the labour market without displacing Irish or EU workers.
[geo-population-migration-s4.5.b] International protection is the Irish name for the asylum system, the legal process by which a person asks Ireland for refuge. Two laws set it up, and applications rose sharply in 2022 and 2023.
[geo-population-migration-s4.5.0] The Refugee Act 1996 established the original asylum system.
[geo-population-migration-s4.5.1] The International Protection Act 2015 introduced a single application procedure, in which all protection claims are considered together rather than one after another.
[geo-population-migration-s4.5.2] The International Protection Office decides first-instance claims (the first decision on each application, before any appeal).
[geo-population-migration-s4.5.3] Over 13,000 international protection applications were made in 2022, a record at the time; numbers in 2023 were comparable.
[geo-population-migration-s4.5.4] Recent countries of origin include Algeria, Georgia, Nigeria, Somalia and Afghanistan.
[geo-population-migration-s4.6.b] Direct provision is the accommodation system for international protection applicants while their claims are decided. It was heavily criticised, and reform has been slow.
[geo-population-migration-s4.6.0] Applicants receive accommodation and meals in centres, plus a small weekly allowance; the system was introduced in 2000.
[geo-population-migration-s4.6.1] Accommodation is now run by IPAS (International Protection Accommodation Services).
[geo-population-migration-s4.6.2] Criticisms: long stays, lack of privacy, unsuitability for children, isolation in rural centres and poor mental health.
[geo-population-migration-s4.6.3] The McMahon Report (2015) criticised direct provision, which added to pressure for reform.
[geo-population-migration-s4.6.4] A 2017 Supreme Court ruling found an absolute ban on work for applicants unconstitutional, so applicants gained a right to work after a waiting period from 2018.
[geo-population-migration-s4.6.5] The White Paper (a government policy plan) of February 2021 planned to end direct provision by the end of 2024; the plan was abandoned as numbers rose.
[geo-population-migration-s4.6.6] Shortages of accommodation, as numbers rose, left some applicants without accommodation.
[geo-population-migration-s4.7.b] Citizenship is full legal membership of the state. Ireland changed who gets it automatically in 2004, and others can still earn it.
[geo-population-migration-s4.7.0] The citizenship referendum (a public vote) of June 2004 (27th Amendment) ended the automatic right to citizenship for children born in Ireland to non-national parents.
[geo-population-migration-s4.7.1] About 79% of voters said yes to the 2004 amendment.
[geo-population-migration-s4.7.2] Non-nationals may apply for naturalisation (becoming a citizen by application) after a period of residence, and successful applicants take part in a citizenship ceremony.
[geo-population-migration-s4.8.b] Ireland also takes refugees through planned programmes and, since 2022, through a temporary route for people fleeing the war in Ukraine. The two routes work very differently.
[geo-population-migration-s4.8.0] The Irish Refugee Protection Programme, established in September 2015, committed to taking up to 4,000 people, including refugees from Syria, through EU relocation (moving asylum seekers between member states) and UNHCR resettlement.
[geo-population-migration-s4.8.1] The EU Temporary Protection Directive, from 4 March 2022, gave people fleeing Russia's invasion of Ukraine (which began on 24 February 2022) the immediate right to live, work and access services without making an asylum claim.
[geo-population-migration-s4.8.2] Temporary protection is not international protection, and Ukrainians are not counted in asylum applications.
[geo-population-migration-s4.8.3] Ireland received over 100,000 Ukrainians by 2024.
[geo-population-migration-s4.8.4] Housing pressure from Ukrainian arrivals contrasts with the accommodation-based system for international protection applicants.
[geo-population-migration-s4.9.b] Weighing Irish policy up means setting its strengths against its weaknesses. The result is an uneven system that favours some migrants and strains housing.
[geo-population-migration-s4.9.0] Policy is open to EU citizens and skilled workers, but restrictive for low-skilled non-EU workers.
[geo-population-migration-s4.9.1] The asylum system is slow and accommodation-based, relying on direct provision.
[geo-population-migration-s4.9.2] Labour needs pull in one direction (towards admitting more workers) and housing pressure in the other (towards limiting numbers).
[geo-population-migration-s4.9.3] Anti-immigration protests show public opposition. There is more on this in the notes on Ethnic, racial and religious issues arising from migration.
[geo-population-migration-s4.10.b] The EU removed barriers for its own citizens in two steps: the right to live and work anywhere in the Union, then the removal of border checks.
[geo-population-migration-s4.10.0] Free movement of workers, from the Treaty of Rome (1957), and EU citizenship, from the Maastricht Treaty (1992), let EU citizens live and work in any member state.
[geo-population-migration-s4.10.1] The Schengen Agreement was signed in 1985 at Schengen, Luxembourg, and came into effect in 1995; it removed internal border checks between members.
[geo-population-migration-s4.10.2] A common external border and common visa rules apply across the Schengen area, so a visa for one member lets a visitor cross the others.
[geo-population-migration-s4.10.3] The Schengen area had 29 countries from 2025, when Bulgaria and Romania became full members in January 2025; it includes non-EU Norway, Iceland, Switzerland and Liechtenstein.
[geo-population-migration-s4.10.4] Ireland and Cyprus are EU members outside Schengen; Ireland's reason is the Common Travel Area with the UK.
[geo-population-migration-s4.10.5] After 2015 some states temporarily reintroduced internal border checks, because of the large numbers of arrivals.
[geo-population-migration-s4.11.b] The Dublin system decides which EU state must handle an asylum claim. It is an EU rule, not an Irish law, and it was named after the city where the 1990 Convention was signed.
[geo-population-migration-s4.11.0] The member state where an asylum seeker first enters the EU is responsible for the claim.
[geo-population-migration-s4.11.1] The system is named after the city where the 1990 Dublin Convention was signed; it was replaced by the Dublin II Regulation (2003) and Dublin III (2013).
[geo-population-migration-s4.11.2] It is an EU asylum rule, not an Irish law.
[geo-population-migration-s4.11.3] It is meant to prevent asylum shopping, where applicants choose the state in which they claim.
[geo-population-migration-s4.11.4] It places most pressure on frontier states (those at the EU's edge, where migrants arrive first) such as Italy, Greece, Spain and Malta.
[geo-population-migration-s4.11.5] The rule broke down in 2015, when arrivals were too many for frontier states to handle.
[geo-population-migration-s4.12.b] The EU controls its outer border through Frontex and also opens legal routes for workers it needs.
[geo-population-migration-s4.12.0] Frontex, established in 2004 with its headquarters in Warsaw, is the EU border and coast guard agency.
[geo-population-migration-s4.12.1] Frontex runs patrols and returns (sending migrants back to their country of origin), and its search-and-rescue role is debated.
[geo-population-migration-s4.12.2] The EU Blue Card directive, adopted in 2009, admits highly skilled non-EU workers; seasonal worker rules also apply.
[geo-population-migration-s4.12.3] Labour shortages and ageing populations are reasons for admitting workers, because there are too few young workers to fill jobs. There is more on this in the notes on Changing population characteristics.
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.b] The main trends behind recent EU policy are the 2015 to 2016 crisis, Mediterranean routes and the Ukrainian movement. Each trend forced governments to change policy.
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.0] The war in Syria from 2011, along with conflict in Afghanistan, Iraq and Eritrea, drove large movements of people.
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.1] About 1.3 million asylum applications were made in the EU in 2015, and a similar number in 2016.
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.2] Over 1 million people crossed the Mediterranean to Europe in 2015, most reaching the Greek islands from Turkey, with Lesbos the main landing point.
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.3] The Eastern Mediterranean route runs from Turkey to Greece, and the Balkan route carries migrants onwards from Greece through the Balkans.
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.4] The Central Mediterranean route runs from Libya to Italy; Lampedusa, south of Sicily, is a main landing point.
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.5] The Lampedusa shipwreck of October 2013 killed over 360 people and led to Italy's Mare Nostrum search-and-rescue operation (2013 to 2014).
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.6] Deaths at sea and smuggling gangs accompanied these routes, and irregular arrivals (entry outside legal channels) continued.
[geo-population-migration-s4.13.7] The Ukrainian refugee movement from 2022 added to these pressures, because so many people arrived in a short time.
[geo-population-migration-s4.14.b] EU states reacted to the crisis in very different ways, from welcome to fences. The responses show how divided the EU was.
[geo-population-migration-s4.14.0] In August 2015 German Chancellor Angela Merkel said 'Wir schaffen das' ('We can do this'); Germany received the largest number of asylum seekers in the EU in 2015 to 2016.
[geo-population-migration-s4.14.1] In September 2015 the EU agreed to relocate 160,000 asylum seekers from Italy and Greece, to ease pressure on those frontier states.
[geo-population-migration-s4.14.2] Hungary, Poland and the Czech Republic refused to take part, and in 2020 the EU Court of Justice ruled that they had broken EU law.
[geo-population-migration-s4.14.3] Hungary, under Viktor Orbán, built a fence on its border with Serbia in 2015, so that migrants could not cross on foot.
[geo-population-migration-s4.14.4] The EU-Turkey statement of March 2016 required irregular migrants reaching the Greek islands to be returned to Turkey; the EU was to provide €6 billion for refugees in Turkey, and arrivals in Greece fell sharply.
[geo-population-migration-s4.14.5] Externalisation (moving border control outside EU territory): deals with Turkey and with Libya and other North African states aimed to control departures before migrants reached EU borders.
[geo-population-migration-s4.14.6] Frontex was strengthened and internal border checks were reintroduced, for example by states wanting to control arrivals.
[geo-population-migration-s4.15.b] After 2022 the EU used a protection tool created in 2001 for the first time, and in 2024 it adopted a new Pact on Migration and Asylum.
[geo-population-migration-s4.15.0] The Temporary Protection Directive (2001) was activated for the first time on 4 March 2022, because of the Ukraine war.
[geo-population-migration-s4.15.1] Over 4 million people held temporary protection in the EU in 2024.
[geo-population-migration-s4.15.2] The Pact on Migration and Asylum was adopted in May 2024 and applies from mid-2026.
[geo-population-migration-s4.15.3] The Pact's main ideas are faster border procedures and mandatory solidarity, under which each state either accepts relocated asylum seekers or pays into a fund.
[geo-population-migration-s4.15.4] Ireland chose to opt in to the Pact in 2024, so it takes part in the new system.
[geo-population-migration-s4.16.b] Large numbers of arrivals put pressure on public services and politics. Exams ask for two challenges, so know several.
[geo-population-migration-s4.16.0] Cost of reception and housing, because states must provide shelter and support for each arrival.
[geo-population-migration-s4.16.1] Slow processing and backlogs, because decisions take time and applications build up.
[geo-population-migration-s4.16.2] Integration, including language, jobs and schooling.
[geo-population-migration-s4.16.3] An unequal burden on frontier states, because under the Dublin rule they handle the claims of those who arrive first.
[geo-population-migration-s4.16.4] Political division and the rise of anti-immigration parties, because public opinion is split on admitting migrants.
[geo-population-migration-s4.16.5] Security screening of new arrivals, so that states can check who is entering.
[geo-population-migration-s4.16.6] Deaths at sea and smuggling gangs, because migrants take dangerous routes when there is no legal way to travel.
[geo-population-migration-s4.17.b] Evaluating EU policy means naming the tensions it cannot resolve. These tensions explain why agreement has been so hard.
[geo-population-migration-s4.17.0] Tension between humanitarian duty (protecting people in need) and border control.
[geo-population-migration-s4.17.1] Tension between solidarity between member states and national sovereignty (each state's right to make its own decisions), as the relocation dispute shows.
[geo-population-migration-s4.17.2] Europe needs workers but faces public opposition to immigration.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s3: 3. Cultural regions
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.b] A cultural region is an area where people share one or more cultural traits that set them apart from neighbouring areas. Language and religion are the traits the syllabus uses to define them.
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.0] Culture is the shared way of life of a group: its language, religion, customs, music, sport and sense of identity (feeling that you belong to that group).
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.1] Language and religion are the two cultural traits the syllabus uses. Both are easy to map because census data (the official count of the population, taken every few years) records them.
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.2] Cultural regions rarely match political borders (the lines between states). One state can contain several, as Belgium and Switzerland do, and one region can cross several states, as the Basque Country and the Islamic world do.
[geo-region-concept-s3.0.3] Their edges are usually transition zones, where the shared trait gradually thins out rather than stopping at a line, because people and customs mix across any boundary.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.b] The Gaeltacht is the officially recognised area where Irish is, or was, the community language (the language people use in everyday life). It is not one continuous region.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.0] It lies in seven counties: Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford and Meath.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.1] Main areas: Gaoth Dobhair (Gweedore), Co. Donegal; Conamara and the Aran Islands (Oileáin Árann), Co. Galway, the largest Gaeltacht area; Corca Dhuibhne (the Dingle Peninsula), Co. Kerry; Múscraí and Oileán Chléire (Cape Clear), Co. Cork; An Rinn (Ring), Co. Waterford; Ráth Chairn and Baile Ghib, Co. Meath.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.2] The pockets are scattered, mainly along the western seaboard (the Atlantic coast), rather than joined into one block.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.3] Remote, mountainous areas with poor farmland and poor communications (roads and transport links) had the least contact with English-speaking trade, towns and administration, which is why Irish survived there.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.4] The Gaeltacht therefore overlaps with the peripheral West of Ireland (the outlying, less developed area far from the main centres of population and wealth). There is more on this in the socio-economic regions subsection.
[geo-region-concept-s3.1.5] The State created a cultural region deliberately: the two Meath Gaeltachtaí (Gaeltacht areas) were formed in the 1930s by resettling Irish-speaking families from Connemara, Ráth Chairn among them.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.b] The State backs the Irish language with laws, institutions and media, because the Gaeltacht is small and under pressure. Several trends still threaten it.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.0] Irish is the first official language under Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution, which gives the language legal standing.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.1] Údarás na Gaeltachta, set up in 1980, promotes employment and the language in the Gaeltacht, so that people have jobs and can stay in their communities.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.2] Raidió na Gaeltachta (1972) and TG4 (first broadcast in 1996 as Teilifís na Gaeilge) broadcast in Irish, so people can hear and watch their language daily.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.3] State language policy is also set out in the Official Languages Act 2003.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.4] Irish-medium schools (where lessons are taught in Irish) and summer colleges bring learners into the Gaeltacht and sustain the language there.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.5] Threats: out-migration of young people (leaving to find work), in-migration of English speakers and holiday homes, and English-language media and tourism, all of which bring English into daily life.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.6] Census 2022 (whole State): about 1.9 million people said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system. This shows a big gap between ability and everyday use.
[geo-region-concept-s3.2.7] The Gaeltacht has shrunk and been redrawn since 1850, so its boundaries have changed. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.b] Belgium is split by a language border that runs east to west across the country, creating three language areas and a bilingual capital.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.0] Flanders, in the north: Dutch-speaking. Flemish is the Dutch spoken in Flanders.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.1] Wallonia, in the south: French-speaking, because it lies on the Latin-speaking side of the border.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.2] A small German-speaking community in the east, around Eupen, so Belgium has three language areas in all.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.3] The Brussels-Capital Region is officially bilingual (French and Dutch). It lies inside Flanders but is mostly French-speaking.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.4] The border marks the old boundary between Germanic-speaking and Latin-speaking peoples (the two language families of northern and southern Europe), and was fixed by law in 1962–63.
[geo-region-concept-s3.3.5] More than half of Belgians live in Flanders.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.b] Because the two main language groups live in separate areas, language shapes how Belgium is governed and how its people live. It also lines up with a difference in wealth.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.0] Belgium became a federal state in 1993, which means power is shared between a central government and regional and community governments.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.1] It has three regions (Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels-Capital) and three language communities (Flemish, French, German-speaking), each with its own parliament.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.2] Schools, media and political parties are divided by language, so the two main groups have separate public lives.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.3] In 1968 the university at Leuven split: its French-speaking section moved to a new town, Louvain-la-Neuve, in Wallonia.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.4] After the 2010 election Belgium went 541 days without a new government, because Flemish and French-speaking parties could not agree.
[geo-region-concept-s3.4.5] The language divide matches an economic divide: Flanders, with the port of Antwerp and newer industries, became the wealthier region, and many Flemish people resent money transferred to Wallonia, whose coal and steel industries in the Sambre–Meuse valley declined.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.b] Several other European regions are defined mainly by language. Exams use them in matching questions, so know what marks each one out.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.0] The Basque Country (Euskara): Basque is unrelated to any other European language, and the region crosses the border of Spain and France, so one cultural region lies in two states.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.1] Catalonia, north-east Spain: Catalan is the language and Barcelona is the capital. An unofficial independence referendum (a public vote on breaking away from Spain) was held there on 1 October 2017.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.2] Wales (Welsh) and Brittany (Breton) are Celtic-language regions, so each is defined by a Celtic language such as Welsh or Breton.
[geo-region-concept-s3.5.3] Switzerland has four national languages: German, French, Italian and Romansh, so it holds several language regions within one state.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.b] Religion divides Ireland in two ways. In the Republic it is changing as the Catholic share falls, and in Northern Ireland it is tied to politics and to where people live.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.0] The Republic is mainly Roman Catholic, but in Census 2022 about 69% were Roman Catholic, down from about 78% in Census 2016.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.1] About 14% said they had no religion in Census 2022, and that share is rising.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.2] Immigration has added other religions, such as Orthodox Christians and Muslims, because people bring their faith with them.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.3] The Plantation of Ulster (the settling of Ulster with colonists), begun in 1609, brought Protestant settlers from Britain and created a Protestant majority in much of the north-east of Ulster.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.4] Religion became tied to political identity: mostly Catholic nationalists (who want a united Ireland) and mostly Protestant unionists (who want to stay in the United Kingdom).
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.5] In Belfast, religion defines segregated residential areas, separated in places by peace walls (peace lines), barriers built between the neighbourhoods.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.6] Census 2021 showed, for the first time, more Catholics than Protestants in Northern Ireland.
[geo-region-concept-s3.6.7] The partition (the division of Ireland), the Troubles and the Good Friday Agreement shaped this situation. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.b] The Islamic world is a region defined by religion that stretches from Morocco to Indonesia. To explain it, you need to know where Islam began, how far it reaches and how it spread.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.0] Islam was founded by the Prophet Muhammad in Arabia in the 7th century. Mecca and Medina, in present-day Saudi Arabia, are its holiest cities.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.1] The Islamic calendar begins with the Hijra, Muhammad's move from Mecca to Medina, in 622.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.2] The Islamic world stretches from Morocco in North Africa, through the Middle East and Central Asia, to Pakistan, Bangladesh and Indonesia.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.3] Indonesia has the largest Muslim population of any country, so Islam is not confined to the Arab world.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.4] Muslims are almost a quarter of the world's population, so Islam is the second-largest religion after Christianity.
[geo-region-concept-s3.7.5] Arab conquest carried Islam across North Africa and into Spain, beginning in 711; trade routes carried it to South and South-East Asia; the Ottoman Turks later carried it into the Balkans (the region of south-east Europe).
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.b] A religion makes a region when shared beliefs and practices leave visible and lasting marks on the lives and landscape of the people. In the Islamic world these are the following.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.0] A shared faith built on the Five Pillars (the five duties of a Muslim): declaration of faith; prayer five times a day, facing Mecca; giving to charity; fasting in Ramadan; and the pilgrimage to Mecca (the Hajj).
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.1] Arabic is the language of the Qur'an (the holy book of Islam), read across the region, so it unites people who speak different everyday languages.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.2] Mosques (places of worship) and minarets (the towers beside them) mark the landscape.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.3] Islamic law (Sharia) shapes the laws of some states, so religion affects government as well as worship.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.4] Shared customs of dress, food and calendar, such as the Islamic calendar that begins with the Hijra, make everyday life similar across the region.
[geo-region-concept-s3.8.5] Sunni Muslims are the large majority overall. Shia Muslims are the majority in Iran and Iraq, and rivalry between the two branches of Islam adds to conflict in the Middle East.
[geo-region-concept-s3.9.b] The edges of the Islamic world are not fixed. History and migration have left Muslim communities beyond its core, and in some places Muslim rule has ended.
[geo-region-concept-s3.9.0] Ottoman rule (rule by the Ottoman Turks) left Muslim communities in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Albania and Kosovo, in the Balkans.
[geo-region-concept-s3.9.1] Spain was under Muslim (Moorish) rule until Granada fell to the Christian kingdoms in 1492.
[geo-region-concept-s3.9.2] Migration has created large Muslim communities in western Europe: North Africans in France, Turks in Germany, and Pakistanis and Bangladeshis in Britain.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.b] Europe divides into three broad Christian regions. Each has its roots in a split within the Church, and the divisions have sometimes led to conflict.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.0] Roman Catholic south and centre: Italy, Spain, Portugal, Poland, Ireland and southern Germany.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.1] Protestant north, so a region shaped by the Reformation: Scandinavia, northern Germany and Britain.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.2] Orthodox east and south-east: Greece, Russia, Romania and Serbia.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.3] The Catholic and Orthodox churches split, which is why Europe has two eastern and western Christian traditions, with Rome and Constantinople at the centre of the two traditions.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.4] The Protestant Reformation (the movement that broke away from the Catholic Church) began in Germany in 1517, with Martin Luther. Vatican City, in Rome, is the centre of the Catholic Church.
[geo-region-concept-s3.10.5] The break-up of Yugoslavia in the 1990s set Orthodox Serbs, Catholic Croats and Muslim Bosniaks against each other in war in Bosnia, showing how religious differences can fuel conflict.
