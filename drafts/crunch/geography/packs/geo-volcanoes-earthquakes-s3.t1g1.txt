[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.b] Magma (molten rock) that rises into the crust cools in one of two ways, and the way it cools decides what landform results. Both ways are endogenic (internal) processes, driven from inside the Earth.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.0] Extrusive (volcanic) activity: magma reaches the surface and cools quickly as lava, ash and other material, building landforms on the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.1] Intrusive (plutonic) activity: magma cools slowly underground, forming bodies of rock that become landforms only when the rock above them is eroded away.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.0.2] Plutonic features are not formed by exogenic processes (surface processes such as weathering and erosion); erosion only reveals them, because the magma had already cooled into rock underground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.b] Rock is rarely one solid block. Cracks called joints run through it, and they decide where weathering and erosion do their work.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.0] A joint is a crack in rock along which no movement has taken place, which is what separates it from a fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.1] In igneous rock (rock formed from cooled magma), joints form by contraction as the rock cools.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.2] In granite, joints also form by expansion when the overlying rock is removed and pressure on the granite is released.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.1.3] Weathering and erosion work along joints, so the joint pattern shapes the landform.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.b] Not all eruptions come from a single volcano. When lava escapes along a long crack, it can build a wide, high, flat landform instead of a cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.0] 1. Basic lava (runny lava that flows easily) pours out of long cracks in the crust, called fissures, rather than from a single vent (an opening in the crust).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.1] 2. Each lava flow spreads widely before it cools, because the lava is runny.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.2] 3. Repeated flows build up, one on top of another, so the plateau grows higher with each eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.3] 4. The result is a high, flat-topped plateau with steep edges.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.4] Example: the Antrim plateau, Co. Antrim.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.2.5] Diagram: cross-section of stacked lava flows forming a flat-topped plateau with steep edges.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.b] The Antrim plateau is an Irish extrusive example, and the granite Mournes are its intrusive neighbour from the same period. Keep the two apart.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.0] The Antrim plateau is made of basalt (a dark volcanic rock) in layers from fissure eruptions about 60 million years ago, linked to the opening of the North Atlantic.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.1] Along the Antrim coast, dark basalt lies on top of white chalk, which the basalt protected from erosion.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.2] The Mourne Mountains, Co. Down, are granite intrusions (granite is a plutonic rock that cooled underground) of the same period as the Antrim basalts, much younger than the Leinster granite.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.3.3] Slieve Donard is the highest peak of the Mournes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.b] Basalt columns form in a lava flow as it cools and contracts (shrinks). They are a joint pattern, not a volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.0] Columnar jointing forms when a thick lava flow cools slowly and evenly, so the whole flow shrinks in a regular pattern.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.1] As the flow contracts, cracks form at about 120° to each other, which is why the pattern is regular.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.2] The cracks produce columns at right angles to the cooling surface, mainly six-sided, because cracks meeting at about 120° enclose six-sided shapes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.3] Sea erosion exposes the columns on the north Antrim coast, where about 40,000 interlocking basalt columns, most of them six-sided, form the Giant's Causeway, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.4] Fingal's Cave, Isle of Staffa, Scotland, has columns from the same volcanic period.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.5] The Giant's Causeway is a cooled lava flow, not a volcano, and its columns did not form from growing crystals.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.4.6] Diagram: labelled sketch of hexagonal columns standing at right angles to the cooling surface of the flow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.b] A shield volcano is built by runny lava, which is why it is broad and low rather than tall and steep. Viscosity is how thick a liquid is: low viscosity means it flows easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.0] 1. Low-viscosity (runny) basic lava flows a long way before it cools.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.1] 2. Mostly effusive eruptions (lava pours out steadily rather than exploding) build a broad cone with gentle slopes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.2] 3. Successive flows spread out from the central vent, so the volcano is wide rather than steep.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.3] Example: Mauna Loa and Kilauea, Hawaii.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.5.4] Diagram: cross-section of a broad, low cone with gentle slopes and a summit crater (the bowl-shaped hollow at the top).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.b] Hawaii is the setting to use for shield volcanoes, with its own record-sized examples and two named lava types.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.0] Hawaii's shield volcanoes sit over a hot spot (a hot area under the crust where magma rises), which is why volcanoes form there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.1] Mauna Loa is the largest active volcano on Earth by volume, for example, which shows how far runny shield-volcano lava can spread.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.2] Mauna Kea is more than 10,000 m high when measured from its base on the sea floor, so most of it lies under the sea.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.6.3] Pahoehoe lava has a smooth, ropy surface; aa lava has a rough, blocky surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.b] A composite volcano is the opposite of a shield. Its magma is too thick to flow far, so it piles up into a steep cone built of alternating layers.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.0] 1. Viscous (thick, sticky) acidic or intermediate magma is too thick to flow far.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.1] 2. Explosive eruptions alternate with lava flows, so the cone is built of layers of ash and lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.2] 3. Over time these layers build a steep cone with a crater at the summit.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.3] 4. The cone also has a main vent and secondary vents.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.4] Examples: Mount Fuji, Japan; Vesuvius, Italy; Mount Etna, Sicily; Mount St Helens, USA.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.7.5] Diagram: cross-section of a steep cone showing alternating ash and lava layers, the crater, the main vent and secondary vents.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.b] The smallest volcanic cone is built from loose fragments blown out in short bursts rather than from flowing lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.0] A cinder cone is a small, steep cone built mainly of pyroclastic fragments (rock blown out by explosions) during short, explosive eruptions.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.1] Example: Parícutin, Mexico, which began in a farmer's cornfield in 1943.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.8.2] Diagram: cross-section of a small, steep cone of loose fragments around a central crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.b] Some eruptions are so large that they destroy the volcano's own summit. The result is a huge basin called a caldera.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.0] A caldera is a large basin formed when the summit of a volcano collapses into the magma chamber (the underground reservoir of magma) emptied by a huge eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.1] A caldera is much larger than a crater and forms by collapse, not by explosion alone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.2] A caldera may fill with water to form a crater lake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.9.3] Crater Lake, Oregon, USA: Mount Mazama collapsed about 7,700 years ago, and the caldera now holds the lake.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.b] Erosion can leave a volcano's hardened core behind after the rest of the cone has gone, because the core is harder than the cone around it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.0] 1. Magma solidifies in the vent of a volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.1] 2. The softer cone around the vent is eroded away.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.2] 3. The resistant plug (rock that resists erosion well) is left standing as a steep-sided hill.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.10.3] Examples: Slemish, Co. Antrim; Castle Rock, Edinburgh, Scotland, a volcanic plug whose crag-and-tail shape was later shaped by ice.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.b] Volcanic heat underground also produces smaller surface features, where hot water, steam and gas escape without any lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.0] A geyser erupts hot water and steam from the ground at intervals.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.1] A hot spring is hot water that rises to the surface and flows out without erupting, so it differs from a geyser.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3.11.2] A fumarole is a vent that emits gases and steam.

CANDIDATES geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1: 1. The geography of volcanoes
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.b] A volcano is an opening (vent) in the Earth's crust, the thin rocky outer layer, through which magma, gases and fragments escape, together with the landform built around it. Magma is molten rock below the surface; lava is magma that has reached the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.0] Magma chamber: the reservoir of magma beneath the volcano, where the molten rock collects before it rises.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.1] Main vent: the pipe (conduit) that carries magma up to the crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.2] Secondary (parasitic) vent: a smaller opening on the flanks (sides) of the cone, which lets magma escape away from the summit.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.3] Crater: the hollow at the top of the main vent.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.4] Cone: the hill of the volcano, built up over many eruptions from alternating layers of lava and ash.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.5] Lava flow: lava moving across the surface away from the vent, such as the flows drawn down the side of the cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.6] Diagram: draw a labelled cross-section showing the magma chamber, main vent, secondary vent, crater, cone of alternating lava and ash layers, lava flow, ash cloud and volcanic bombs, with the crust around the chamber.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.b] An eruption throws out liquid, gas and solid material. Each one is a hazard in its own right, so you need to know the names and what each does.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.0] Lava: molten rock that flows from the vent, so it can bury land and buildings around the volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.1] Gases: water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide, which are released as the magma rises and the pressure on it falls.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.2] Pyroclastic material (tephra): solid fragments of rock thrown into the air. It includes ash, lapilli (cinders), volcanic bombs and pumice.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.3] Ash: the finest pyroclastic particles, which are light enough to fall over wide areas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.4] Volcanic bombs: large lumps of lava thrown out of the vent while still partly molten.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.5] Pumice: a light, porous rock (full of tiny holes) formed from frothy lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.6] Pyroclastic flows: fast, very hot avalanches of gas, ash and rock moving down the slopes, so they destroy anything in their path.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.7] Lahars: volcanic mudflows of ash and water, for example when an eruption melts snow or ice.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.b] The silica content of magma (silica is the mineral material that makes rock) controls how runny the lava is, how much gas it traps, and so how the volcano erupts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.0] Basic (basaltic) lava is low in silica and hot, so it is runny (low viscosity, meaning it flows easily).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.1] Gases escape easily from basic lava, so eruptions are gentle (effusive, meaning lava pours out rather than blasting).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.2] Acidic lava is high in silica and cooler, so it is viscous (thick and sticky).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.3] Acidic lava traps gas, so pressure builds up and eruptions are explosive.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.4] Low-viscosity lava spreads widely and builds shield volcanoes (broad, gently sloping); viscous lava piles up into steep cones. The names and shapes of the landform types belong to the volcanic and plutonic landforms notes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.b] Volcanoes are grouped by how likely they are to erupt again. The groups depend on how long it has been since the last eruption and whether another is expected.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.0] Active: a volcano that has erupted recently and may erupt again soon. Examples: Mount Etna, Sicily, Italy; Kilauea, Hawaii; Stromboli, Italy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.1] Dormant (sleeping): a volcano that has not erupted for a long time but could erupt again. Example: Mount Fuji, Japan, last erupted in 1707.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.2] Extinct: a volcano not expected ever to erupt again. Example: Slemish, Co. Antrim, the remains of an extinct volcano, linked with St Patrick.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.b] Most volcanoes lie in narrow belts that follow plate boundaries (the lines where the huge slabs of crust, called plates, meet), not at random. Each type of boundary gives a different result.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.0] Constructive (divergent) boundaries: basic magma rises to fill the gap where plates separate, mostly under the sea along mid-ocean ridges (underwater mountain chains).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.1] Iceland is where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level; the North American and Eurasian plates are moving apart there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.2] Destructive (convergent) boundaries: oceanic crust is subducted (pushed down beneath another plate), the descending plate melts, and acidic magma rises to form explosive volcanoes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.3] Destructive boundaries form volcanoes in fold-mountain chains (mountains crumpled up where plates collide), such as the Andes where the Nazca plate is subducted beneath the South American plate.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.4] They also form volcanoes in island arcs (curved chains of volcanic islands) such as Japan and Indonesia.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.5] Continental-continental collision and transform (conservative) boundaries, where plates slide past each other, have few or no volcanoes, so magma rarely reaches the surface there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.b] A belt of volcanoes circles the Pacific Ocean, because most of the plate boundaries around that ocean are destructive.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.0] The Ring of Fire is a belt of volcanoes and earthquakes around the edges of the Pacific Ocean, mainly along destructive boundaries.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.1] It contains about three-quarters of the world's active volcanoes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.2] Examples: Mount St Helens (Cascade Range, Washington State, USA); Mount Fuji, Japan; Mount Pinatubo, the Philippines; Krakatoa, Indonesia; volcanoes of the Andes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.b] A hot spot is a plume of magma rising from deep in the mantle (the hot layer beneath the crust), away from plate boundaries. It explains volcanoes in places where no plates meet.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.0] As a plate moves over a stationary hot spot, a chain of volcanoes forms, with the volcanoes getting older with distance from the hot spot, because each one is carried away from the magma supply.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.1] Hawaii sits in the middle of the Pacific plate over a hot spot, so it is not on a plate boundary. The Pacific plate moves north-west, so the islands get older to the north-west.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.2] The active volcanoes Kilauea and Mauna Loa are on the Big Island in the south-east, over the hot spot.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.3] Other hot spots: Yellowstone, USA; Iceland, which is on a constructive boundary and also over a hot spot.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.b] Ireland has no active volcanoes now, but it had huge eruptions in the past. These were linked to the opening of the North Atlantic Ocean, which is the syllabus's national setting.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.0] Ireland lies in the middle of the Eurasian plate, far from a plate boundary, so it has no active volcanoes today.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.1] About 60 million years ago, North America and Greenland separated from Europe as the Atlantic opened. The crust stretched and cracked.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.2] Basic lava poured out of long fissures (cracks in the ground) in north-east Ireland, flow upon flow, in fissure eruptions. These lavas formed the Antrim basalts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.3] Basalt is an extrusive (volcanic) rock, meaning it formed from lava on the surface. The Antrim basalts came from fissures, not from a single cone volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.4] Landforms from these lavas include the Antrim plateau and the Giant's Causeway.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.b] Eruptions can kill people and damage land, buildings, travel and even the climate. Named examples show how large the damage can be.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.0] Loss of life, and destruction of buildings, farmland and infrastructure by lava, pyroclastic flows and lahars. In AD 79 Vesuvius, near Naples, Italy, buried the Roman towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum under ash and pyroclastic flows.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.7] Economic losses and evacuation of populations, because homes, farms and businesses are lost and people must leave the danger zone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.b] Volcanoes are dangerous, but they also give people land, energy, income and materials. This is why many people live close to them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.0] Fertile soils from weathered volcanic ash and lava, which break down into minerals plants can use. On the slopes of Mount Etna and Vesuvius they support vineyards and orchards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.1] Geothermal energy (heat from underground) and hot water: in Iceland geothermal energy heats about nine in ten homes and generates electricity.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.2] Tourism: volcanoes, geysers (jets of hot water and steam) and hot springs attract visitors, such as the Blue Lagoon and the geysers at Geysir, Iceland.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.3] Minerals and building materials, such as sulphur, pumice and basalt, which can be quarried or mined from volcanic rock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.4] New land: Surtsey, an island that emerged from the sea off southern Iceland in 1963.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.b] Mount St Helens is the full case study to learn, with date, place, plate setting, events, effects and response. The eruption was sideways, not vertical, which makes it unusual.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.0] Place: Cascade Range, Washington State, USA. The Juan de Fuca plate is subducted beneath the North American plate, a destructive boundary.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.1] For weeks before 18 May 1980, magma pushed the north flank (side) outwards into a bulge.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.2] A magnitude 5.1 earthquake triggered the collapse of the north flank as a huge landslide.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.3] The collapse released a sideways (lateral) blast that flattened forests.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.4] The summit lost about 400 m of height, leaving a horseshoe-shaped crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.b] The eruption did great damage, but the number of deaths was low because people had been kept away from the danger zone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.0] Ash fell across several US states.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.1] Lahars (mudflows of ash and water) flowed down river valleys, including the Toutle River, destroying bridges and homes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.2] 57 people died, not thousands.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.3] Most people had been kept out of a restricted zone, which kept the death toll low.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.b] Volcanoes usually give warning signs, because magma must move upwards before an eruption. Scientists measure these signs to judge when a volcano is becoming dangerous.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.0] Seismometers (instruments that record earthquakes) pick up swarms of small earthquakes as magma rises.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.1] Ground deformation: tiltmeters (which measure changes in slope), GPS and satellite radar measure swelling or bulging of the cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.2] Gas emissions: changes in the amount and type of gas, especially sulphur dioxide.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.3] Temperature: thermal imaging and satellites detect heating of the ground or of crater lakes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.4] Changes in groundwater and springs, for example because rising magma heats the water underground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.5] Studying the deposits and records of past eruptions shows how often a volcano erupts and which areas are at risk. Hazard maps show these areas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.6] Scientists can often forecast that an eruption is likely, but not its exact time, size or style.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.b] Eruptions cannot be stopped, but their effects can be limited. The main approaches are to watch the volcano, keep people out of danger, and sometimes to fight the lava itself.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.0] Monitoring by volcano observatories, so that warning signs are picked up early.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.1] Hazard zoning keeps settlement out of high-risk zones, so fewer people and buildings are in the path of an eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.2] Evacuation plans and warning systems, and public education.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.3] Diverting or slowing lava flows with barriers and channels, or cooling it with water.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.4] Heimaey, Iceland, 1973: the Eldfell eruption on Heimaey, Westman Islands, threatened the harbour. About 5,000 residents were evacuated overnight, mostly by the fishing fleet. Sea water was pumped onto the advancing lava to cool and halt it, saving the harbour.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.5] Pinatubo, the Philippines, 1991: scientists from the US Geological Survey and the Philippine volcano institute monitored earthquakes, gas emissions and swelling, and forecast the eruption. Tens of thousands of people were evacuated, including from the US Clark Air Base, saving thousands of lives.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s1: 1. The rock cycle and rock formation
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.b] Rock is never permanent. It is continually formed, modified, destroyed and reconstituted (built again from the broken pieces), so the three rock types are linked in one cycle. Two sets of forces drive it, one working from inside the earth and one at its surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.0] Endogenic (internal) forces, driven from within the earth, form and modify rock through magma (molten rock below the surface), heat and pressure.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.1] Exogenic (external) forces, working at the surface, destroy rock through weathering (breakdown of rock in place by weather) and erosion (wearing away and carrying off of rock fragments).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.0.2] Deposition (dropping of carried sediment, the loose fragments of rock) reconstitutes rock, because the sediment is later turned into new sedimentary rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.b] The cycle is easiest to learn as a circular flow chart in which each rock type is reached from another by a named process. Short-cuts across the circle show that rock does not have to follow the full loop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.0] 1. Magma cools and crystallises (forms crystals as it hardens) to make igneous rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.1] 2. Weathering and erosion break igneous rock down into sediment, because rock at the surface is attacked by weather.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.2] 3. Deposition, compaction and cementation turn the sediment into sedimentary rock, because the layers are squeezed and glued together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.3] 4. Heat and pressure change sedimentary rock into metamorphic rock, because buried rock is squeezed and heated without melting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.4] 5. Melting turns the metamorphic rock back into magma, and the cycle starts again.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.5] Short-cuts: any rock can be weathered into sediment, and any rock can be metamorphosed (changed by heat and pressure) or melted, so the arrows do not all follow one loop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.1.6] Diagram: draw a circular flow chart with magma and the three rock types as the nodes, and label each arrow with its process, for example crystallisation or compaction and cementation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.b] The rock cycle does not run at random. It is tied to the moving plates (the large slabs that make up the earth's crust), which decide where each stage happens.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.0] Rock is created at plate boundaries and at hotspots (fixed points of rising magma), because magma reaches the crust there.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.1] Rock is metamorphosed at collision zones, where plates push together and the heat and pressure are great.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.2.2] Rock is destroyed or recycled at subduction zones, where one plate sinks beneath another and the rock melts back into magma.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.b] Igneous rock forms when molten rock cools and crystallises. Magma is molten rock below the surface; lava is molten rock at the surface. Where the rock cools decides how big its crystals grow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.0] Crystal size depends on cooling rate: slow cooling gives large crystals because they have time to grow, and fast cooling gives small ones.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.1] Plutonic (intrusive) rock is igneous rock that cools slowly deep in the crust, so its crystals are large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.2] Volcanic (extrusive) rock is lava that cools quickly at the surface, so its crystals are small.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.3] Uses: granite is used as building stone, kerbs, worktops and headstones; basalt is crushed for road chippings and aggregate (crushed stone used in construction).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.b] Granite is a plutonic rock. When magma pushes into surrounding rock and cools there, it forms masses called intrusions, and their shape gives them different names.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.0] Granite is a plutonic rock built from quartz, feldspar and mica crystals, which are large enough to see because the magma cooled slowly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.1] Batholiths are huge dome-shaped masses of plutonic rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.2] Dykes are vertical intrusions that cut across the beds (layers) of the rock around them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.3] Sills are horizontal intrusions that lie along bedding planes (the horizontal surfaces between layers).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.4.4] Plutonic rock reaches the surface only after the rock above it has been eroded away, because it formed deep underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.b] Volcanic rock forms at the surface, so it cools fast. Which kind forms depends on the plate setting, the tectonic setting, in which the magma reaches the crust.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.0] Basalt is the commonest volcanic rock and forms from runny lava, which flows easily before it sets.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.1] Basalt forms at constructive (divergent) boundaries, where plates move apart, and at hotspots.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.2] Andesite and granite batholiths form above subduction zones at destructive boundaries, where one plate sinks beneath another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.b] Sedimentary rock is formed from sediment laid down in layers (strata), usually in seas, lakes, river basins and deserts. Loose sediment then has to be turned into solid rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.0] Sediment comes from weathered and eroded fragments of older rock, from the remains of plants and animals, or from minerals precipitated from water (that is, coming out of solution as solids).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.1] Sediment builds up in subsiding basins (areas of sinking land), on continental shelves (the shallow sea floor around a continent) and along trailing (passive) margins, where no plate boundary lies at the coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.2] Lithification is the conversion of loose sediment into solid rock by compaction and cementation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.3] Compaction: the weight of overlying layers squeezes out water and air, packing the grains together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.6.4] Cementation: minerals such as silica, calcite and iron oxide, carried by groundwater, bind the grains together like glue.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.b] Because sedimentary rock is laid down in layers from material at the earth's surface, it often contains fossils and shows bedding planes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.0] Many sedimentary rocks contain fossils, the remains or traces of past life, because living things were buried in the sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.1] Bedding planes are horizontal surfaces separating one layer from the next.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.2] Joints are vertical cracks that cut through the layers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.7.3] Many sedimentary rocks are porous, holding water in the spaces between grains. Porous is not the same as permeable (letting water pass through).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.b] Sedimentary rocks are sorted into three groups by the material they are made from.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.0] Clastic rocks are made of broken fragments: sandstone from sand, and shale from mud and clay.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.1] Organic rocks are made of the remains of living things: limestone from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures, and coal from plant remains that accumulated in swamps.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.8.2] Chemical rocks form from minerals that come out of solution: rock salt and other evaporites form as seawater evaporates and leaves its dissolved minerals behind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.b] Each Irish sedimentary rock records the conditions in which it formed, such as the climate and the setting at that time. For a longer answer, give the conditions as well as the rock and its place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.0] Carboniferous limestone (Carboniferous period, about 360 to 300 million years ago) formed when Ireland lay near the equator under a warm, shallow, clear sea, where shells and skeletons built up the deposit. It covers most of the central lowland and forms the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.1] Old Red Sandstone (Devonian period) formed in hot, semi-arid (very dry) conditions from sand and pebbles laid down by rivers; its red colour comes from iron oxide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.2] Old Red Sandstone forms the Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtees and the Comeraghs.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.3] Shale and sandstone of the Cliffs of Moher, Co. Clare, are layers of shale, siltstone and sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.4] Chalk lies beneath the basalt along the Antrim coast, so the chalk is the older rock and the lava flowed over it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.5] Coal at Arigna, Co. Roscommon, and Castlecomer, Co. Kilkenny, formed from plant remains in swamps; both are former coal-mining areas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.6] Rock salt is mined at the Kilroot salt mine, near Carrickfergus, Co. Antrim.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.b] Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by great heat and/or pressure without melting. The original rock is called the parent rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.0] The two factors that cause metamorphism are heat and pressure.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.1] Minerals recrystallise (re-form as new crystals) while the rock stays solid, so the rock usually becomes harder and denser.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.10.2] The rock may develop banding or cleavage (a tendency to split along flat surfaces).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.b] Metamorphism happens on two scales, depending on whether the heat and pressure come from a single intrusion or from the movement of plates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.0] Thermal (contact) metamorphism: rock beside an igneous intrusion is baked by its heat, forming a ring of altered rock called a metamorphic aureole; it is small in scale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.11.1] Regional metamorphism: heat and pressure act over huge areas during plate collision and mountain building; it is large in scale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.b] Each metamorphic rock comes from a particular parent rock, and the pairs can be asked either way round.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.0] Limestone becomes marble; Connemara marble is green and is quarried in Connemara, Co. Galway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.1] Sandstone becomes quartzite, so the parent of quartzite is a sedimentary rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.2] Shale (mudstone) becomes slate, and with more heat and pressure becomes schist, so schist is the more strongly altered rock. Slate is quarried on Valentia Island, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.3] Granite becomes gneiss, so gneiss is a metamorphic rock formed from an igneous parent.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.4] Quartzite forms Errigal, Co. Donegal; Croagh Patrick, Co. Mayo; the Great Sugar Loaf, Co. Wicklow; and the Twelve Bens, Connemara.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.5] Mica schist is found in Donegal and Connemara, and a metamorphic aureole of mica schist surrounds the Leinster granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.12.6] Gneiss at Inishtrahull, off Co. Donegal, is about 1.7 billion years old and is the oldest rock in Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.b] Ireland's rock types form a recognisable pattern across the island, and each reflects an event in its geological past.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.0] Limestone floors the central lowland, and Old Red Sandstone forms the ridges of Munster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.1] Granite outcrops (shows at the surface) in Wicklow, Donegal and the Mournes; basalt covers Co. Antrim; metamorphic rocks lie in Donegal, Connemara and Mayo.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.2] Plutonic example: the Leinster batholith, the granite core of the Wicklow Mountains, is about 400 million years old and is the largest granite mass in Ireland and Britain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.3] The Mourne granite, in the Mourne Mountains, Co. Down, is much younger, about 56 million years old.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.4] Volcanic example: the Antrim plateau was built by lava flows about 60 million years ago, as the Atlantic began to open. The Giant's Causeway, Co. Antrim, is basalt columns.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.5] The closing of the Iapetus Ocean during Caledonian mountain building produced the Leinster and Donegal granites and regional metamorphism.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.b] North America has two very different edges. The western edge is an active plate margin, where plates are moving and rock is made and changed. The eastern edge is a trailing (passive) margin, where no plate boundary lies at the coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.0] Active western margin: Pacific plates subduct (sink) beneath or slide past North America.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.1] Volcanic rock: the Cascade Range volcanoes, including Mount St Helens in Washington state (erupted 1980), lie above a subducting oceanic plate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.2] Plutonic rock: the Sierra Nevada granite batholith, California.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.3] Regional metamorphism affects the western mountain belts.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.4] The San Andreas Fault, California, is a transform boundary where plates slide past each other.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.5] Trailing eastern margin: there is no plate boundary at the coast, so there are no active volcanoes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.6] Because the margin is quiet, thick sediments accumulate on the Atlantic coastal plain, on the continental shelf and in the Mississippi delta.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.14.7] The Appalachians are old, worn-down fold mountains of sedimentary and metamorphic rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.b] Rocks can be told apart by what they look like, and photo questions rely on this. Each rock below has one or two clues that separate it from the others.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.0] Granite: speckled, with large interlocking crystals, because it cooled slowly underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.1] Basalt: dark and fine-grained because it cooled quickly; it may form columns, as at the Giant's Causeway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.2] Sandstone: gritty grains, often reddish or buff, and layered, because it is made of cemented sand laid down in beds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.3] Limestone: pale grey, and it may show fossils because it is made from shells and skeletons.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.4] Marble: white or coloured, with sparkling crystals and no fossils, which separates it from limestone, its parent rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.5] Quartzite: pale, very hard and glassy.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.6] Slate: dark grey, and it splits into thin sheets, which is the cleavage (splitting) that metamorphism can produce.

CANDIDATES geo-plate-tectonics-s3: 3. Plate boundaries as zones of crustal construction and destruction
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.b] Plates meet in only three ways: they move apart, move together or slide past each other. Each way has a name, and exams use all the synonyms, so learn them together.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.0] Constructive (also called divergent or separating) boundaries: plates move apart and crust is created, because magma fills the gap.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.1] Destructive (also called convergent or colliding) boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, because one plate sinks into the mantle, or modified where two continental plates collide.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.2] Passive (also called conservative or transform) boundaries: plates slide past each other and crust is conserved, meaning it is neither created nor destroyed, so no magma is involved.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.b] At constructive boundaries plates move apart, and new crust is formed in the gap. The process runs in three steps, driven by the mantle (the hot layer beneath the crust).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.0] 1. Convection currents (slow circulating flows in the hot mantle) carry the plates apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.1] 2. Pressure drops as the plates separate, so the mantle partly melts, producing magma (molten rock).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.2] 3. Magma rises into the gap and cools to form new basaltic oceanic crust, basalt being a dark, runny volcanic rock.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.b] Where the plates separate under the ocean, the new crust builds up a long underwater mountain chain called a mid-ocean ridge. This is the main oceanic example of a constructive boundary.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.0] A rift valley (a long valley formed where the crust splits and drops) runs along the crest of the ridge, with frequent shallow earthquakes (earthquakes starting close to the surface) and volcanic activity.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.1] Transform faults (cracks where plates slide past each other) cut across the ridge, so it is offset in sections.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.2] Where a mid-ocean ridge rises above sea level it forms volcanic islands, such as Iceland.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.3] The Mid-Atlantic Ridge also separates the South American and African plates in the South Atlantic.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.4] Iceland is a volcanic island on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the rift is visible on land at Þingvellir (Thingvellir).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.5] The Azores are also on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the East Pacific Rise is another mid-ocean ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.6] Surtsey, a new volcanic island, formed off the south coast of Iceland in an eruption that began in 1963.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.7] Diagram: cross-section of a ridge showing convection currents, rising magma, the rift valley and arrows showing the plates moving apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.b] Constructive boundaries can also start under a continent. Here the land is pulled apart instead of the sea floor, and if the process continues a new ocean can open.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.0] Where a continent starts to split, the crust stretches and a block drops between parallel faults (cracks in the crust) to form a rift valley, with volcanoes nearby.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.1] The East African Rift Valley is where the African plate is splitting apart; Mount Kilimanjaro, Tanzania, is a volcano associated with it.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.2] If spreading continues, the sea floods in and a new ocean begins; the Red Sea, between Africa and the Arabian plate, is at this stage.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.b] These are the landforms and activity to name when asked what is found at a constructive boundary. These features are found where plates pull apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.0] Features of constructive boundaries, where plates pull apart, are the mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, volcanic islands, fissure eruptions (eruptions from long cracks rather than one vent) of runny basalt lava, and shallow earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.b] Where two plates converge, one plate is forced down into the mantle. Which one sinks depends on the density of the crust.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.0] Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so where the two meet the oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.1] This process is subduction, and the zone where the plate sinks is the subduction zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.2] Crust is destroyed as the subducting plate melts in the hot mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.3] The place where the two plates meet at the surface is an ocean trench, a deep, narrow valley in the sea floor.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.b] When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks. This one collision produces a trench, fold mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes, which is why such coasts are so active.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.0] An ocean trench forms where the oceanic plate bends down, because the plate is forced into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.1] Sediments (loose material laid down on the sea floor) are scraped off the subducting plate and crumpled against the continent into fold mountains.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.2] Melting of the sinking plate produces magma that rises to form a chain of volcanoes on the continent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.3] Earthquakes occur along the sinking plate because it grinds past the rock around it, shallow near the trench and deeper inland; this inclined zone is the Benioff zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.4] The Nazca plate subducts beneath the South American plate: the Peru-Chile Trench lies offshore and the Andes, with many volcanoes, rise on land.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.5] The Juan de Fuca plate subducts beneath the North American plate, producing the Cascade Range volcanoes, including Mount St Helens, Washington, USA.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.6] Diagram: cross-section with ocean trench, subducting plate, melting zone, rising magma, volcano, fold mountains and earthquake foci (the points underground where earthquakes start).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.b] When two oceanic plates meet, neither is light enough to resist sinking, so one is forced under the other. The result is a trench and a curved line of volcanic islands rather than a mountain chain on a continent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.0] The older, denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the other, because denser crust sinks more easily.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.1] An ocean trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands, called an island arc, form, and earthquakes are frequent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.2] The Mariana Trench, about 11 km deep, is the deepest place on earth; it lies where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the Philippine plate, and the Mariana Islands form an island arc.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.3] The Aleutian Islands, Alaska, are an island arc where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the North American plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.4] Japan lies where the Pacific, Philippine, Eurasian and North American plates meet.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.b] Continental crust is too light to sink into the mantle, so when two continents meet there is no subduction. The crust has nowhere to go but up.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.0] Both plates are too light to subduct, so the crust between them, including sediments from the ocean that once separated them, is crushed, folded and pushed up into high fold mountains.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.1] Earthquakes are common, but there is little or no volcanic activity, because no plate sinks and melts to make magma.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.2] Crust is modified, thickened and folded, rather than destroyed, because it is too light to sink.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.3] The Indo-Australian plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate, forming the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau; the collision began about 50 million years ago and continues.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.4] Mount Everest, 8,849 m, is the highest mountain on earth; for example, marine fossils in limestone near its summit show the rocks were once on the floor of the Tethys Sea.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.5] The Alps formed where the African plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.b] Destructive boundaries shape the land and also cause hazards such as powerful earthquakes, tsunamis and explosive volcanic eruptions. For a discussion of their impacts, give landforms and hazards, each with a named example.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.0] Landforms: ocean trenches, fold mountains, volcanic mountain chains and island arcs, such as the Andes and the Mariana Trench.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.1] Earthquakes: Nepal, 25 April 2015, magnitude 7.8 (a measure of earthquake size), killed about 9,000 people on the Himalayan collision boundary.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.2] Undersea earthquakes and tsunamis (huge sea waves caused by earthquakes under the sea): the Tōhoku, Japan, earthquake of 11 March 2011 had magnitude 9.0; its tsunami killed about 18,000 people (dead and missing) and caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.3] The Indian Ocean earthquake of 26 December 2004 had magnitude 9.1 off Sumatra, Indonesia, where the Indo-Australian plate subducts; its tsunami killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.4] Volcanic eruptions: Mount St Helens erupted on 18 May 1980, killing 57 people.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.b] At passive (transform) boundaries two plates slide past each other horizontally along a transform fault. Because nothing is pulled apart or pushed under, no crust is made or lost.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.0] Friction locks the plates until stress builds up, and the stress is released suddenly as an earthquake.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.1] No magma is produced, so there is no volcanic activity, and no crust is created or destroyed.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.2] The plates may move in the same direction at different speeds, or in opposite directions, so both count as sliding past.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.3] The San Andreas Fault, California, USA, is a transform boundary where the Pacific plate moves north-west relative to the North American plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.4] The San Francisco earthquake of 18 April 1906 had magnitude about 7.8 and was followed by great fires; the Loma Prieta earthquake of 1989, magnitude 6.9, struck near San Francisco.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.5] The North and East Anatolian Faults in Turkey are transform faults; the earthquake of 6 February 2023 on the East Anatolian Fault, magnitude 7.8, killed over 50,000 people in Turkey and Syria.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.6] Diagram: plan view (looking down from above) of two plates with arrows in opposite directions along a fault line.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.b] Putting the boundary types together shows that the ocean floor is constantly being made and used up. This is the tectonic cycle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.0] Crust is created at ridges, carried across the ocean floor, and destroyed at subduction zones, so the ocean floor is continually recycled.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.1] Continental crust is too light to be subducted, which is why it survives for so long.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.2] Oceans open and close over time: the Iapetus closed and the Atlantic opened.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.b] This summary pairs each boundary type with its plate movement and what happens to the crust. Exams ask for an example of each, so the examples are added here.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.0] Constructive boundaries: plates move apart and crust is created. Examples: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the East African Rift Valley.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.1] Destructive boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, or modified where two continental plates collide. Examples: the Andes, the Mariana Trench and the Himalayas.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.2] Transform (passive) boundaries: plates slide past and crust is conserved. Example: the San Andreas Fault.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.b] Earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains are concentrated in narrow belts along plate boundaries, with very few in plate interiors. This is how plate tectonics explains where they occur.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.0] The Pacific Ring of Fire is the belt of destructive and some transform boundaries around the edge of the Pacific plate and its neighbours.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.1] The Ring of Fire, because it follows destructive boundaries, contains about 75% of the world's active volcanoes and about 90% of its earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.2] Ridges, subduction zones, collision zones and transform faults are the boundaries where these belts form, because plates interact there.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.3] Fold mountains are found only at convergent boundaries, as long chains along the plate edge; the Andes, Himalayas and Alps are examples.
