TARGET geo-option-culture-identity-s4: 4. Everyday expressions of culture and identity
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.b] Culture and identity are the broad concepts. Everyday expressions are the visible parts of culture that people practise, and they are what an essay on this topic is built from.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.0] Culture is a group's shared, learned way of life: its beliefs, customs, language, arts, food and dress. It is learned, not inherited, so each generation has to be taught it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.1] Identity is a person's or group's sense of who they are and where they belong, for example as Irish, Catalan or a member of a county club.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.2] Everyday expressions are the visible, practised parts of culture: people choose them, repeat them and pass them on.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.0.3] The main everyday expressions in this option are sport, traditions, costume, food, music, art and festivals.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.b] Customs are not just pastimes. They do real work for a group, and these five functions give you the points to explain in an essay.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.0] They mark belonging: taking part in a shared custom shows that a person belongs to the group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.1] They pass culture from one generation to the next, because children learn the games, songs and customs by taking part.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.2] They assert identity when a culture is under pressure, as Irish games and music did during the Gaelic Revival (a movement to bring Irish games and music back into everyday use).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.3] They attract tourists and bring income to the places where they are practised.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.1.4] They can also mark division: parades in Northern Ireland and football rivalries separate groups as well as unite them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.2.b] Globalisation (the growing connection between countries through trade, media and travel) spreads the same products, sports and customs to many countries, so cultures can become more alike.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.2.0] Fast food, Hollywood films, global sports and global brands are the main channels of Americanisation (the spread of American culture to other countries).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.2.1] McDonald's opened its first Irish restaurant on Grafton Street, Dublin, in 1977, an example of a global brand entering everyday Irish life.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.2.2] Halloween customs returned from the USA in a commercial form, although the festival itself has Irish origins in Samhain (the Celtic festival of 31 October to 1 November).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.3.b] Communities do not simply accept cultural change. Three responses try to keep traditional expressions alive.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.3.0] Revival movements, such as the Gaelic Revival, promote Irish games and music to keep them in everyday use.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.3.1] UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (living traditions and skills rather than buildings or objects) was created by its 2003 convention. Irish harping was added in 2019.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.3.2] State support for traditional music, sport and arts events helps keep these expressions in practice, because funding lets them continue and reach new audiences.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.4.b] Migration (people moving to live in another country) also changes everyday culture, because migrants bring their own customs with them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.4.0] Migrants bring new food, festivals, religious celebrations and sports, which become part of everyday life in their new country.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.4.1] New and existing expressions blend: multicultural festivals and fusion food (dishes that mix cooking traditions) combine traditions from different groups.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.b] The Gaelic Athletic Association (GAA) promotes native Irish games, chiefly hurling and Gaelic football. It is the strongest example of sport as an expression of Irish identity, so be ready to treat it in depth.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.0] The GAA was founded on 1 November 1884 in Hayes' Hotel, Thurles, Co. Tipperary, by Michael Cusack and others, to preserve and promote native Irish games.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.1] Maurice Davin was the GAA's first president.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.2] Players are amateurs, so the games stay rooted in the community, which means they play for their club and county without being paid.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.3] Clubs are based on parishes and teams represent counties, so the GAA reinforces local and county identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.4] The GAA is organised on an all-island basis and has clubs among the Irish diaspora (Irish people living abroad).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.5] Camogie, the women's form of hurling, is run by the Camogie Association, founded in 1904. Ladies' football is the women's form of Gaelic football.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.5.6] Hurling was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2018. It is a cultural symbol and is not Ireland's national sport by law.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.b] Croke Park is the stadium behind the GAA's best-known rule changes and events, so you can use it to show how sport and national history are linked.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.0] Croke Park, Dublin, holds about 82,300 spectators and is one of Europe's largest stadiums.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.1] Members were banned from playing "foreign games" (soccer, rugby, cricket and hockey), which kept the GAA focused on native Irish games. The ban was removed in 1971.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.2] A rule barring members of the British security forces from the GAA was removed in 2001.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.3] Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920: British forces opened fire at a Gaelic football match in Croke Park, killing 14 people.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.4] This is a different event from Bloody Sunday 1972 in Derry, and there is more on it in the notes on Religion as a cultural indicator.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.6.5] From 2007 Croke Park hosted rugby and soccer internationals while Lansdowne Road was rebuilt. England played Ireland at rugby there in February 2007.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.7.b] Other sports express identity too, and in Northern Ireland the team a person supports can signal which community they belong to.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.7.0] The Ireland rugby team represents the whole island, including Northern Ireland, so it is an all-island expression of identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.7.1] Sporting loyalties in Northern Ireland are divided between Irish and British teams, so the choice of team can express national identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.b] Traditional music is played informally, and sessions are the main setting in which it is shared.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.0] Traditional instruments include the fiddle, uilleann pipes (Irish bellows-blown bagpipes), tin whistle, flute, concertina, accordion, bodhrán (a hand-held frame drum) and harp.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.1] The harp is Ireland's national symbol and appears on coins and the presidential seal.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.2] Sessions are informal gatherings where musicians play together, which is how tunes and playing styles are passed on.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.8.3] Uilleann piping was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2017.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.b] Traditional music has an organisation behind it and a yearly festival, and it has a singing and dancing side too.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.0] Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann was founded in 1951 to promote traditional music.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.1] The first Fleadh Cheoil na hÉireann (a national music festival) was held in Mullingar in 1951. It is now held in a different town each August and draws hundreds of thousands of visitors.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.2] Sean-nós ("old style") is unaccompanied traditional singing in Irish, strongest in Conamara.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.9.3] Sean-nós dancing is the traditional informal dance form that sits alongside the singing tradition.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.10.b] Irish music and dance also reached a world audience, which shows an Irish expression spreading outwards through globalisation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.10.0] Riverdance began as the interval act (the performance between entries) of the Eurovision Song Contest, performed in Dublin on 30 April 1994.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.10.1] It became a worldwide touring show, carrying Irish dance to audiences abroad.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.10.2] Irish popular music with traditional roots combines the traditional instruments and melodies with modern styles.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.b] Festivals bring a community together on a fixed date. These points cover national and religious occasions, and St Patrick's Day shows Irish identity in the diaspora.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.0] St Patrick's Day, 17 March: parades are held across the world, for example in New York, and landmarks are lit green.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.1] St Patrick's Day is a global expression of Irish identity in the diaspora. Dublin's St Patrick's Festival began in 1996.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.2] St Brigid's Day (Lá Fhéile Bríde, around 1 February) became a public holiday in 2023, the first named after a woman.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.11.3] Samhain (31 October to 1 November) is the Celtic origin of Halloween.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.12.b] Local festivals and fairs tie a place to a custom, and each of these is held at the same time every year.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.12.0] Puck Fair, Killorglin, Co. Kerry, is held every August (10-12 August), when a wild goat is crowned king.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.12.1] Lisdoonvarna Matchmaking Festival, Co. Clare, is held every September.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.12.2] Ballinasloe Horse Fair, Co. Galway, is held every October and is one of Europe's oldest horse fairs. It is also important to Travellers, a group with a distinct everyday culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.13.b] Some festivals look outwards to the diaspora, and others celebrate writing and the arts.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.13.0] The Rose of Tralee, Co. Kerry, has been held since 1959 and is open to women of Irish birth or descent worldwide, so it links Ireland with its diaspora.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.13.1] Bloomsday, 16 June, celebrates James Joyce's Ulysses, which is set on 16 June 1904.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.13.2] The Galway International Arts Festival has been held since 1978. Wexford Festival Opera has been held since 1951.

[geo-option-culture-identity-s4.14.0] Wakes (gatherings held when someone has died), holy wells and pattern days (feast days held at holy wells) are older Irish traditions that are still practised in some places.

CANDIDATES geo-option-culture-identity-s3: 3. Religion as a cultural indicator
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.b] A cultural indicator is a feature that helps identify and map a cultural group. Religion is one of the strongest, because it affects how people think, live and look, and it leaves visible marks on the landscape.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.0] Religion shapes beliefs, values and moral codes (the rules about right and wrong), and so it often marks group identity more strongly than nationality does.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.1] It shows in the landscape, so a place's religion can be read from what is built there: churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, holy wells, wayside shrines and cemeteries.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.2] It shapes diet, such as halal (permitted under Islam), kosher (permitted under Judaism) and Hindu vegetarianism, and dress, such as the headscarf, the turban and the kippah (the Jewish skullcap).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.3] It shapes the calendar and festivals, such as Christmas, Easter, Eid, Diwali and Yom Kippur, and it also shapes names, education and law.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.0.4] Census questions on religion (a census is the official count of a population) make it easy to map, because the results can be compared place by place.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.1.b] Religion rarely stands alone. It is tied up with other indicators of culture, which is why it helps define a cultural group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.1.0] Religion is linked with language, because holy texts and worship use particular languages: Arabic and the Qur'an (the holy book of Islam), Latin and the Catholic Church in the past, and Hebrew and Judaism.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.1.1] Religion is linked with nationality, so that faith becomes part of national identity: Catholicism with Irish or Polish identity, and Orthodoxy with Serbian identity.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.1.2] Religion is also linked with politics, because religious groups often seek influence over laws and government.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.2.b] Secularisation is the decline of religion's role in public life and personal practice (a secular state or society is one not run by or tied to religion). The trend is uneven across the world.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.2.0] Religious practice and church influence are declining in much of Europe, including Ireland and France.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.2.1] Religion stays strong in many other parts of the world, such as Indonesia, so the trend is not global.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.2.2] Migration brings new religions into Europe, which means Europe is becoming more religiously varied even where older churches decline.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.3.b] These are Pew Research Center figures for 2020, published in 2025. They describe 2020 and should not be presented as current.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.3.0] Christians: about 2.3 billion, about 29% of the world, so this makes Christianity the largest religion.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.3.1] Muslims: about 2 billion, about 26% of the world.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.3.2] Religiously unaffiliated: about 1.9 billion, about 24%. The unaffiliated are people with no religion, not a religion in themselves.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.3.3] Hindus: about 1.2 billion, about 15%. Buddhists: about 320 million, about 4%. Jews: about 15 million.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.3.4] Islam was the fastest-growing major religion from 2010 to 2020.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.b] A hearth is the place where a religion began, before it spread outwards. Judaism, Christianity and Islam began in the Middle East, while Hinduism and Buddhism began in the Indian subcontinent (the region of India and its neighbours).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.0] Judaism is the oldest of the three Abrahamic faiths (Judaism, Christianity and Islam), so the other two grew after it, and Jerusalem is its holiest city.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.1] Christianity began with the life of Jesus in Roman Palestine, so its hearth is in the Middle East, for example Jerusalem.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.2] Islam was founded by the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, Arabia, in the early 7th century. The Hijra (the move of the early Muslims) to Medina in 622 begins the Islamic calendar.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.3] Hinduism has no single founder; it developed gradually in the Indian subcontinent.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.4] Buddhism was founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha) in north-east India around the 5th century BC. Bodh Gaya is where he is said to have gained enlightenment (spiritual awakening).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.4.5] Sikhism was founded by Guru Nanak in Punjab in the 15th century. The Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib) at Amritsar is its holiest site.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.b] Religions moved out of their hearths through five main routes: empire, missionaries, trade, colonialism and migration. Each route explains why a religion is found where it is today.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.0] Empire: Christianity spread through the Roman Empire, which made it the official religion in the 4th century. Islam spread with the Arab conquests from the 7th century, across the Middle East and North Africa and into Spain within about a century.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.1] Missionaries (people sent to spread their faith): Irish monks, such as St Columba on Iona and St Columbanus in continental Europe, spread Christianity in the early medieval period.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.2] Trade: Islam spread to Indonesia and West Africa, for example along trade routes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.3] Colonialism (rule over overseas territories by a foreign power): Christianity spread to the Americas, Africa and the Philippines.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.5.4] Migration: Islam, Hinduism and Sikhism spread into Europe as people moved there.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.b] Christianity split into three main branches, and each is strong in different regions. Two events, the Great Schism and the Reformation, created these divisions.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.0] Roman Catholic: southern Europe, Ireland, Poland, Latin America and the Philippines.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.1] Protestant, which began with the Reformation: northern Europe, the UK and the USA.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.2] Orthodox, which grew from the Great Schism: eastern Europe, Russia, Greece and Serbia.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.3] The Great Schism (the split) of 1054 divided the Catholic West (Rome) from the Orthodox East (Constantinople).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.4] The Reformation (the break from Catholicism that created Protestantism) began in 1517 with Martin Luther in Germany, and Calvinism spread from Geneva.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.6.5] Christianity is growing in sub-Saharan Africa (Africa south of the Sahara).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.7.b] Islam is spread across a broad belt of the world, and the largest Muslim population is not in the Arab world. It has two main branches, Sunni and Shia.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.7.0] Islam is strong in the Middle East, North Africa, Central Asia, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Indonesia, and parts of West and East Africa.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.7.1] Indonesia has the world's largest Muslim population, which shows Islam's centre of numbers is not in the Arab world.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.7.2] Sunnis are about 85–90% of Muslims. Shias are the majority in Iran and Iraq.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.7.3] Mecca and Medina, in Saudi Arabia, are Islam's holiest cities, because Islam was founded in Mecca and the Hijra went to Medina. The Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca is a duty for every Muslim able to make it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.7.4] Islam is growing in Europe through migration, so it is a new religion for many European countries.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.b] These four religions are more concentrated than Christianity or Islam. Each is tied to a homeland, with a diaspora (a population living scattered outside its homeland) elsewhere.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.0] Hinduism is found in India and Nepal and in the Indian diaspora. About four in five Indians are Hindu, and Varanasi on the Ganges is a major pilgrimage city.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.1] Buddhism, which began in India, is now found in Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Tibet, Japan and Vietnam.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.2] Judaism is found mainly in Israel and the USA, because most of the world's Jews live there.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.8.3] Sikhism is found in Punjab, with a diaspora in the UK and Canada.

[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.b] Countries differ in how far religion and government are joined. The four types run from religious rule to strict separation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.0] Theocracy: religious leaders rule the state, so religious authority is also political authority.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.1] An established or state church (an official church recognised by the state): the Church of England is the established church in England.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.2] Strict separation of church and state: France's laïcité (the French principle of secularism) and the USA's First Amendment.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.11.3] States in which one church had a dominant influence without being the official church: Ireland in the 20th century and Poland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.12.b] In a theocracy, or where religion and state are fused, religious law and religious leaders hold political power. Iran, Vatican City and Saudi Arabia show this in different ways.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.12.0] Iran has been an Islamic Republic since the 1979 revolution. A Supreme Leader, a senior cleric (religious leader), has the highest authority.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.12.1] Vatican City is an independent state ruled by the Pope, created by the Lateran Treaty with Italy in 1929.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.12.2] Saudi Arabia's law is based on Sharia (Islamic religious law), and the public practice of other religions is not permitted.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.13.b] The Church of England shows an established church, and the USA and France show separation. France's version is the strictest, and it has led to laws on religious dress.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.13.0] The Church of England is the established church. The monarch is its Supreme Governor, and 26 bishops sit in the House of Lords (the UK's second parliamentary chamber).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.13.1] The First Amendment (1791) to the US Constitution bars an established religion.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.13.2] France's 1905 law separated the churches and the state. Laïcité, or secularism, is a core principle of the Republic.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.13.3] A 2004 law banned conspicuous religious symbols, including the Islamic headscarf, in state schools.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.13.4] A 2010 law banned face coverings, including the burqa and niqab (veils that cover the face), in public places.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.14.b] A constitution can be secular while religion still presses on politics. India is the example of a secular state facing religious-nationalist pressure.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.14.0] India's constitution describes it as a secular republic, so the state is not tied to any one religion.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.14.1] Hindu nationalism, such as in the demand for a Hindu temple at Ayodhya (politics that treats India as a Hindu nation), has grown in Indian politics since the 1990s.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.15.b] Bunreacht na hÉireann (the Irish Constitution) reflected the Catholic majority's influence. Its religious content shows how close church and state were, yet it never made Catholicism a state religion.

[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.15.6] The Eighth Amendment (1983) gave the unborn a right to life equal to the mother's.

[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.16.0] Most primary schools, about nine in ten, are under Catholic patronage (the Church is the school's patron, which controls its running), and the Church controlled many hospitals.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.16.1] The Church influenced social policy on censorship and contraception.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s3.16.2] The Mother and Child Scheme (1950–51), a free health-care plan for mothers and children, was dropped after opposition from the Catholic hierarchy (the bishops) and doctors. The Minister for Health, Noel Browne, resigned.
