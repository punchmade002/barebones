TARGET geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6: 6. Climate and economic development
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.0.b] Climate is both a resource and a limit. Temperature, the amount of rainfall, its seasonal timing and its reliability, sunshine and wind decide what can be grown, how much water is available, where tourists go and what it costs to adapt.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.0.0] Rich economies can overcome climatic limits with technology: irrigation (supplying water to crops by channels or pipes), reservoirs, desalination (removing salt from seawater) and snow-making. They can afford to pay for these.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.0.1] Poor economies that depend on rain-fed farming (crops watered only by rainfall) are the most exposed, because they have no stored or piped water to fall back on when the rain fails.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.0.2] Climate change is shifting all of these. Its evidence and causes are taught in the notes on climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.1.b] Ireland's rainfall and mild temperatures give a long grass-growing season, so grass is the most important crop. Most farmland is grassland, used for dairy, beef and sheep (pastoral farming, meaning rearing animals). Rainfall and soils then split the country into farming regions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.1.0] Grass grows when soil temperature is above about 6 °C. In Ireland's mild climate that is for most of the year, and the season is longest in the south and south-west.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.1.1] Annual rainfall is about 750 mm in the driest east, about 1,000–1,250 mm across much of the west and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains. This is why farming differs so much between east and west.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.1.2] The south and east are drier and sunnier, and the south-east is the sunniest region. The 2020 Census of Agriculture counted about 135,000 farms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.1.3] Tillage (growing crops on ploughed land) is concentrated in the drier south and east, e.g. Co. Wexford, Co. Carlow and Co. Kildare. Barley, wheat, oats, potatoes and vegetables can be harvested there in dry conditions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.1.4] The Golden Vale (Co. Tipperary and Co. Limerick) has moderate rainfall and good soils on brown earths (well-drained fertile soils), which suit intensive dairying. EU milk quotas (limits on milk output) ended in 2015, after which dairying expanded.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.1.5] Gley soils (waterlogged, poorly drained soils) of the drumlin belt (the lowland of low hills in Co. Cavan, Co. Monaghan and Co. Leitrim) and Co. Clare carry mainly grass for cattle, with a short grazing season.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.1.6] The very wet west and north-west, with poorly drained gley and peat soils, suit mainly beef and sheep rearing on rough grazing, with small farms and low incomes. There is more on this in the notes on a peripheral Irish region.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.2.b] Too much rain is as much a problem for Irish farming as too little. Wet weather raises costs, delays work and spreads disease.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.2.0] Waterlogged soils are poached (trampled into mud) by cattle, and the grazing season is shortened. Animals must then be housed and fed silage (stored fermented grass) for longer, which raises costs.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.2.1] Wet spells make it hard to make silage and hay and to harvest cereals, because the crops cannot be cut and dried in dry conditions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.2.2] Heavy rain leaches nutrients from the soil (washes them down out of reach of roots), and slurry (liquid animal manure) runs off when it rains after spreading. There is more on this in the notes on environmental pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.2.3] Damp weather favours diseases: potato blight, and liver fluke (a parasite) in sheep and cattle.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.2.4] The summer of 2012 was exceptionally wet in Ireland. Poor silage and hay harvests, followed by a cold, late spring in 2013, caused a fodder (animal feed) crisis in spring 2013.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.2.5] Farmers ran out of feed in spring 2013. Fodder was imported, including from Britain and France, and the Government funded a fodder transport subsidy.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.2.6] The Great Famine (1845–49) followed potato blight, a disease favoured by mild, damp weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.3.b] Ireland is a wet country, but dry spells also hurt farmers because grass is the crop that feeds the animals.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.3.0] Grass stops growing in prolonged dry spells, so farmers have to feed winter fodder (stored feed) in summer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.3.1] Milk yields fall, and fodder runs short the following winter because the winter feed was used up in summer. Light soils (free-draining soils that dry out fast) of the south-east suffer most.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.3.2] Summer 2018: a long anticyclonic spell (settled high-pressure weather) from late May to mid-July brought heat and drought to Ireland. Met Éireann, the national weather service, recorded absolute drought (a spell with almost no rain) at many stations.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.3.3] Grass growth almost stopped that summer. Teagasc (the agriculture and food development authority) and the Government organised fodder imports and supports for the following winter.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.4.b] Climate change brings both gains and risks to Irish farming, and farming is itself a source of the gases that cause it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.4.0] Warmer temperatures lengthen the growing season, and maize and other crops become more feasible. Wetter winters and more frequent summer droughts add risk.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.4.1] Agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions (EPA, early 2020s). The EPA is the Environmental Protection Agency. There is more on this in the notes on environmental pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.5.b] In India, farming follows the monsoon, the seasonal wind that brings the summer rains. When the rains arrive, and how strong they are, set the farming year.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.5.0] The south-west monsoon reaches Kerala around 1 June, covers India by mid-July and brings about three-quarters of the annual rainfall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.5.1] Kharif (summer) crops such as rice and cotton are sown with the monsoon rains. Rabi (winter) crops such as wheat rely on stored water, irrigation and western disturbances (winter rain-bearing weather systems).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.5.2] A late or weak monsoon cuts harvests, farm incomes and GDP (the value of what the country produces), because so much of the year's water arrives in one season.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.5.3] The Green Revolution (high-yielding crop varieties) from the mid-1960s, in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, relied on irrigation. Canal irrigation in Punjab and Haryana brought salinisation (salt building up in the soil) and waterlogging.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.5.4] The Indira Gandhi Canal in Rajasthan carries irrigation water into the dry north-west.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.6.b] Egypt shows a country with almost no rain of its own, so farming depends entirely on one river. A dam turned an unpredictable flood into a controlled supply, but at a cost.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.6.0] Egypt is almost rainless, so farming depends on irrigation from the Nile. The green strip of the Nile valley and delta shows through the desert.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.6.1] The Nile's flood came from summer rain far upstream, and its uncertainty ended when the Aswan High Dam was completed in 1970.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.6.2] The dam created Lake Nasser, one of the world's largest reservoirs. This allows year-round (perennial) irrigation, more than one crop a year and hydroelectricity (electricity from flowing water).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.6.3] Costs: the fertile silt is trapped behind the dam, so farmers need artificial fertiliser. Salinisation and waterlogging have spread, and the delta is eroding.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.7.b] California's Central Valley is a farming region where the summers are dry, so its high-value crops depend on irrigation. It shows what happens when drought cuts that supply.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.7.0] California's Central Valley is one of the world's most productive farming regions. Its dry summers mean that high-value fruit, nuts and vegetables depend on irrigation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.7.1] Irrigation comes from large state and federal water projects, which carry snowmelt from the mountains, and from groundwater (water pumped from underground).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.7.2] In the drought of about 2012–2016, farmers left land fallow (unplanted) and over-pumped groundwater. Heavy groundwater pumping caused land subsidence (the ground sinking) in the valley.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.7.3] Governor Jerry Brown ordered a mandatory 25% cut in urban water use in April 2015, so cities as well as farms had to cut their water use.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.8.b] In the Sahel, the dry belt south of the Sahara, crops rely on rain alone. This shows that how reliable rainfall is matters as much as how much falls.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.8.0] The Sahel has one short wet season, in which millet and sorghum are grown alongside pastoral herding (keeping livestock).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.8.1] Any shortfall in rain means crop failure, with losses of food and livestock, because farmers have no irrigation to fall back on.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.8.2] Rainfall reliability matters as much as the total: the lower the average rainfall, the more variable it is from year to year. Dry regions therefore face both low and unpredictable harvests.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.9.b] Water for homes comes from rain that has collected in rivers, lakes, reservoirs and underground. So rainfall shapes how secure a household supply is, even in a wet country.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.9.0] Domestic water depends on rainfall refilling rivers, lakes, reservoirs and groundwater. Supply must be stored to cover dry periods.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.9.1] Demand peaks in hot, dry weather, exactly when supply is lowest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.9.2] Group water schemes (community-run supplies) and private wells serve many rural homes. Wells can run dry in drought and be contaminated after heavy rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.9.3] In arid and monsoon countries, water supply depends on seasonal rain, groundwater and long-distance transfers (carrying water from one region to another).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.9.4] Rich, dry countries such as the Gulf states and Israel rely heavily on desalination for domestic water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.9.5] Women and children in poor rural areas spend hours a day collecting water, which keeps girls out of school.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.10.b] Ireland has plenty of rain, yet water supply is still a problem. The cause is where the rain falls compared with where people live.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.10.0] Ireland's paradox: there is plenty of rain overall, but most of it falls in the west while most people live in the east, where rainfall is lowest (about 750 mm a year around Dublin).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.10.1] Census 2022 counted about 1.45 million people in Dublin county, out of about 5.1 million in Ireland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.10.2] Old, leaking pipes waste a large share of treated water, and treatment capacity (how much water can be cleaned for drinking) is tight.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.10.3] Dublin's water comes mainly from the River Liffey, through the Poulaphouca reservoir (also called the Blessington lakes, Co. Wicklow), and from the Vartry reservoir at Roundwood, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.10.4] The Water Supply Project would take water from the River Shannon at Parteen Basin, just south of Lough Derg, by a pipeline of about 170 km to the Greater Dublin Area. It is planned, not built.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.10.5] Uisce Éireann (Irish Water), which adopted its name in 2023, is responsible for public water supply.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.11.b] The 2018 drought showed that a wet country can still run short of water for homes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.11.0] Irish Water imposed a hosepipe ban (a Water Conservation Order, a legal limit on using water outdoors), first in the Greater Dublin Area in early July 2018 and later extended to the whole country.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.11.1] The ban shows a rainfall shortfall affecting domestic supply in a wet country.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.12.b] Cape Town came close to running out of water. It shows how a rainfall failure becomes a supply crisis, and how a city can respond.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.12.0] Cape Town, South Africa, has a Mediterranean climate (wet winters and dry summers). Three dry winters, about 2015–2017, left its reservoirs very low.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.12.1] In early 2018 the city warned of 'Day Zero', when household taps would be shut off.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.12.2] From 1 February 2018 residents were limited to 50 litres per person per day, with higher tariffs (prices), reduced water pressure and public campaigns.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.12.3] Day Zero was avoided after usage was roughly halved and the winter rains of 2018 returned.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.12.4] Lessons: diversify supply (groundwater, recycling and desalination), reduce leaks, and plan for longer droughts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.13.b] Drought is a long period with much less precipitation (rain, snow and other falling moisture) than normal for that place, so that water supply falls short of demand. It can be measured at three stages, from sky to soil to rivers.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.13.0] Meteorological drought is a shortfall of rain, so it is measured by rainfall totals.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.13.1] Agricultural drought is soil moisture too low for crops, so plants wilt even if some rain has fallen.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.13.2] Hydrological drought is low rivers, reservoirs and groundwater, which means the water supply itself runs short.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.13.3] Causes: blocking anticyclones (high-pressure systems that stay put and divert rain-bearing weather); failure or shift of the ITCZ (the belt of rising air and heavy rain near the equator) or monsoon; El Niño (a warming of the Pacific that disturbs rainfall worldwide); and climate change.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.14.b] Drought hits the economy through farming first, then spreads to prices, jobs, energy and public spending.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.14.0] Crop failure and loss of livestock, because grass and crops die without water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.14.1] Higher food prices, because less is produced, and loss of farm income and rural jobs.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.14.2] Famine in poor countries, and migration to cities and abroad, because people leave when farming can no longer feed them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.14.3] Less hydroelectricity and less cooling water for power stations, and river transport disrupted because water levels fall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.14.4] Wildfires, because dry vegetation burns easily, and government spending on relief.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.15.b] A rich economy can absorb drought, but not without losses. The Millennium Drought in Australia shows this.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.15.0] The Millennium Drought in south-eastern Australia lasted roughly from the late 1990s to 2009, hitting farming in the Murray–Darling Basin, Australia's main irrigated farming region.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.15.1] Losses were absorbed through insurance, government aid, water trading and technology, but farm output fell and towns faced water restrictions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.15.2] Australian cities, including Melbourne, built large desalination plants in response.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.15.3] Rich and poor responses contrast: a developed economy absorbs drought through insurance, aid, water trading, technology and desalination, while a poor economy with few of these faces famine. The Horn of Africa case below shows this.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.16.b] Where people have little saving, insurance or state support, drought turns into famine. The Horn of Africa in 2011 is the example.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.16.0] Drought across the Horn of Africa in 2011 followed two failed rainy seasons, linked to La Niña (the cool counterpart of El Niño in the Pacific).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.16.1] Crops failed, livestock died and food prices soared, because the failed rains cut food supply.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.16.2] The UN declared famine in parts of southern Somalia in July 2011, where conflict (al-Shabaab) blocked aid.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.16.3] A later UN-backed study estimated about 260,000 deaths in Somalia, about half of them children under five.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.16.4] Large numbers fled to the Dadaab camps in Kenya and to camps in Ethiopia.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.16.5] Ethiopia's famine of 1983–85 followed drought and was worsened by civil war. It led to Band Aid (1984) and the Live Aid concerts (July 1985) organised by Bob Geldof.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.17.b] Responses either warn of drought early, store or save water, or help people once it hits.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.17.0] Early warning systems using rainfall, satellite and market data, so that governments and aid agencies can act before famine sets in.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.17.1] Water storage in dams, tanks and rainwater harvesting (collecting rain from roofs and surfaces).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.17.2] Drought-tolerant crops and seed varieties, which can still yield with less water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.17.3] Irrigation efficiency, such as drip irrigation (water delivered slowly to plant roots, so less is wasted).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.17.4] Food aid and cash transfers, so that people can buy food; insurance, which pays out when crops fail.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.17.5] Restrictions such as hosepipe bans, which cut demand; desalination, which adds a supply that does not depend on rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.18.b] Desertification is often misunderstood as the Sahara advancing. It is really damage to land, and climate and people together cause it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.18.0] Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas caused by climatic variations and human activities (UNCCD definition, adopted 1994). The UNCCD is the UN Convention to Combat Desertification.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.18.1] It is the spread of desert-like conditions, not the advance of sand dunes from the Sahara.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.18.2] Drought is a climatic event; desertification is a process of land degradation that drought can trigger.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.18.3] 17 June is World Day to Combat Desertification.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.19.b] The Sahel is a key example of desertification. Here the focus is on rainfall, drought and the economy: harvests, incomes, food, migration and conflict.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.19.0] The Sahel runs from Senegal and Mauritania through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad to Sudan. Rainfall is roughly 200–600 mm, falling in one short summer wet season when the ITCZ (the rain belt) moves north.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.19.1] Droughts in the 1970s and 1980s caused failed harvests and livestock deaths.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.19.2] Pastoralists (herding peoples) lost their livelihoods, and the region became dependent on food imports.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.19.3] Migration to cities, including coastal cities, and abroad; conflict between herders and farmers over shrinking land and water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.19.4] Lake Chad has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s (about 90%, widely quoted), hitting fishing and farming around it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.19.5] There is more on the soil causes of Sahel degradation in the notes on human interference with soils.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.20.b] Responses to desertification range from international agreements to local farming methods that restore the land.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.20.0] The UNCCD, adopted in 1994, coordinates international action.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.20.1] The Great Green Wall, launched by the African Union in 2007, aims to run about 8,000 km from Senegal to Djibouti. Progress has been slow and well short of target.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.20.2] Zaï pits (planting holes that catch rain) and stone lines (lines of stones that slow runoff) in the Yatenga region of Burkina Faso, associated with the farmer Yacouba Sawadogo.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.20.3] In Niger, farmer-managed natural regeneration (farmers letting trees regrow on their land) has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.21.b] Desertification is not only a poor-country problem. The Dust Bowl in the United States shows drought and ploughing together wrecking farmland in a rich economy.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.21.0] Drought on ploughed grassland in the 1930s, on the Great Plains of Oklahoma and Kansas, produced dust storms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.21.1] Farm failures and migration followed, because the dust storms stripped the land of its topsoil.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.21.2] The US Soil Conservation Service, set up in 1935, promoted shelter belts (rows of trees that block wind), contour ploughing (ploughing along the slope's contours to slow runoff) and crop rotation (changing the crop each year).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.21.3] The Dust Bowl shows desertification-type damage in a rich economy. There is more on its soil side in the notes on human interaction and mass movement.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.22.b] Tourists choose places for their weather, so climate is a resource that tourism can sell.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.22.0] Sunshine, warmth, low rainfall and a warm sea attract 'sun, sea and sand' tourism.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.22.1] Reliable snow attracts winter sports, and a mild climate allows outdoor activities.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.22.2] Climate decides the tourist season, because visitors come when the weather suits the activity.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.23.b] The Mediterranean is the main case of climate creating a tourist economy. The same dry summers that attract visitors also create problems.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.23.0] Hot, dry, sunny summers have made the Mediterranean one of the world's most visited regions. Mass package tourism grew from the 1960s with cheap jet flights.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.23.1] Spain received about 85 million international tourists in 2023, a record, making it the world's second most visited country after France.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.23.2] Spain is the most popular foreign holiday destination for Irish people.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.23.3] Benefits: jobs, foreign earnings, infrastructure and regional development. There is more on this in the notes on a peripheral European region, the Mezzogiorno.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.23.4] Climate problem: tourists arrive in the driest season, so water demand from hotels, pools and golf courses peaks when supply is lowest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.23.5] Other problems: jobs are seasonal, and coasts are overdeveloped.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.23.6] Protests against mass tourism took place in 2024 in the Canary Islands and in Barcelona.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.24.b] Some destinations sell warmth in winter, when northern Europe is cold. The Canary Islands do this.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.24.0] The Canary Islands lie off north-west Africa at about 28° N.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.24.1] Mild, dry winters and the cooling Canary Current (a cool ocean current) give them a year-round tourist season that attracts northern Europeans.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.25.b] Ski resorts depend on snow, so they are very sensitive to a warming climate.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.25.0] Ski tourism depends on reliable snow cover, and it supports mountain economies in several European countries.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.25.1] Climate change is raising the snow line (the height above which snow lies) and shortening seasons, so lower-altitude resorts struggle or close.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.25.2] Resorts invest in artificial snow-making, which needs water, energy and cold enough nights.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.25.3] Resorts also diversify into summer walking and cycling, so they earn money when there is no snow.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.26.b] Tropical islands sell warm weather and coral reefs, but the same climate brings hurricanes, bleaching and rising seas.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.26.0] Tropical island destinations depend on warm weather and coral reefs. Their peak season is the dry season of the northern winter, and the Atlantic hurricane season (1 June to 30 November) is the low season.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.26.1] Hurricane Irma, a Category 5 hurricane (the top category) in September 2017, damaged or destroyed almost every building on Barbuda. Its whole population was evacuated to Antigua.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.26.2] Hurricane Maria, in September 2017, devastated Puerto Rico. An official study estimated about 3,000 deaths.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.26.3] Mass bleaching of the Great Barrier Reef (corals losing their colour as stressed by warm water) has occurred repeatedly since the late 1990s, damaging reef tourism.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.26.4] Sea-level rise threatens low-lying island nations such as the Maldives. There is more on this in the notes on environmental pollution.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.27.0] Ireland's cool, cloudy, wet and unreliable summer climate limits beach tourism and pushes Irish holidaymakers abroad.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.27.1] Irish tourism instead markets scenery (the green landscape produced by the rain), culture, heritage, golf, walking, surfing and other outdoor activities.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.27.2] The Wild Atlantic Way, a touring route of about 2,500 km along the west coast from Inishowen, Co. Donegal, to Kinsale, Co. Cork, was launched by Fáilte Ireland in 2014.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.27.3] Domestic holidays rise in warm, sunny summers because fewer people go abroad for the sun.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.28.b] A warming climate changes where and when people take holidays, helping some places and hurting others.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.28.0] Hotter Mediterranean summers bring heatwaves and wildfires that can deter or endanger tourists. In July 2023, wildfires on the Greek island of Rhodes forced thousands of tourists to be evacuated from resorts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.28.1] Visits may shift to spring and autumn, or to more northern destinations, possibly including Ireland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.28.2] Ski seasons shorten because there is less reliable snow.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.28.3] Reefs bleach because seas warm, and islands face flooding because sea level rises.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.29.b] Climate also affects energy, health and productivity, and a final judgement on how far it controls development.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.29.0] Energy: wind supplied about a third of Ireland's electricity in the early 2020s. Solar power is strong in sunny deserts, and hydroelectricity depends on high rainfall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.29.1] Climate also drives energy demand, for example heating in cold climates and air-conditioning in hot ones.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.29.2] Health and productivity: tropical climates favour diseases such as malaria; extreme heat lowers the capacity for outdoor work; heatwaves kill.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s6.29.3] Assessment: climate influences development but does not decide it alone. Rich countries with harsh climates, such as Israel, the Gulf states and Australia, overcome limits through capital and technology, while poor, rain-fed economies are the most vulnerable. Climate change widens this gap.

CANDIDATES geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3: 3. Exchanges of water between oceans and atmosphere
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.b] Water is constantly moving between the oceans, the air and the land. This continuous circulation is the hydrological (water) cycle, and it is the starting point for understanding how the oceans and atmosphere exchange water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.0] The cycle is a closed system, meaning nothing enters or leaves: the total amount of water on earth stays the same, and water only moves between stores (the places where it is held).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.1] Two forces drive the movement: solar energy, which powers evaporation (liquid water turning to vapour), and gravity, which pulls water down as precipitation (rain, snow and so on) and runoff (water flowing over the land).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.2] Diagram: draw a labelled cycle from ocean to atmosphere to land and back, with the stores drawn as boxes and the flows as arrows.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.1.b] A store is a place where water is held for a time. The oceans dwarf all the others, and only a tiny part of the earth's water is fresh liquid water at the surface.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.1.0] Oceans hold about 97% of the earth's water; ice sheets and glaciers hold about 2%, most of it in Antarctica; groundwater (water stored underground in soil and rock) holds under 1%.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.1.1] Lakes, rivers, soil moisture, the atmosphere and living things together hold only a tiny fraction. The atmosphere holds about 0.001% of the earth's water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.1.2] About 2.5-3% of all water is fresh (not salty), and most of that is frozen, so liquid fresh surface water is a very small part of the total.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.b] Flows are the processes that move water from one store to another. Each has its own name, and the ocean-atmosphere exchange depends on the first few.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.0] Evaporation: liquid water turns to vapour (water as an invisible gas), mainly from the ocean because the oceans are by far the biggest store.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.1] Transpiration: vapour is released from plant leaves; evapotranspiration is transpiration and evaporation combined over land.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.2] Sublimation: ice turns directly into vapour without first melting.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.3] Advection: wind carries water vapour horizontally from sea to land, so rain can fall far from where the water evaporated.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.4] Condensation: vapour turns to liquid droplets, forming cloud, fog or dew, for example when air cools.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.5] Precipitation, interception (rain caught by leaves), infiltration (soaking into the soil), surface runoff, throughflow (water moving sideways through soil) and groundwater flow return water to the sea. There is more on these drainage-basin flows in the chapter Rivers - Fluvial Processes and Landforms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.3.b] The oceans are both the main source and the main destination of the water that moves through the air. This shows how much of the cycle happens over the sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.3.0] Roughly 85-90% of global evaporation comes from the oceans, and roughly three-quarters of global precipitation falls on them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.3.1] Evaporation over the oceans exceeds the rain falling back on them, so the surplus is carried over the land by wind, and returns to the sea through rivers and groundwater.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.4.b] Residence time is how long a water molecule stays in a store before moving on. It differs enormously between stores.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.4.0] Water stays in the atmosphere for only about 9 days on average, but in the ocean for thousands of years and in the Antarctic ice sheet for far longer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.4.1] The atmosphere's water is therefore recycled quickly, while ocean water changes slowly.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.b] The exchange is not the same everywhere. Where evaporation and precipitation differ, distinctive climates result, and human activity can change the balance.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.0] Evaporation exceeds precipitation over the subtropical oceans, which helps explain their high salinity (saltiness), because water leaves but the salt stays. There is more on this in The atmosphere and oceans as linked systems.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.1] Precipitation exceeds evaporation in the equatorial belt and in the mid-latitude westerly belt (the zone of prevailing westerly winds).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.2] Deforestation reduces evapotranspiration and so the recycling of rainfall; a large share of the Amazon's rain is water recycled from the forest by evapotranspiration.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.3] Global warming intensifies the water cycle. There is more on this in Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.b] Humidity describes how much water vapour the air holds. It matters because it decides whether air can form cloud, fog or dew.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.0] Humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air. Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapour in a volume of air, in g/m³.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.1] Relative humidity is the amount of water vapour present as a percentage of the most the air could hold at that temperature. Saturated air (air holding all it can) has a relative humidity of 100%.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.2] Warm air can hold about 7% more water vapour for each 1 °C rise in temperature, so warm air holds much more than cold air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.3] Cooling air raises its relative humidity without adding any water, because the most it can hold falls; warming lowers it. This is why relative humidity is usually highest at dawn and lowest in mid-afternoon.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.4] Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated as it cools; further cooling causes condensation. There is more on measuring humidity in The atmosphere and oceans as linked systems.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.7.b] The amount of vapour in the air affects how evaporation works, and so how the climate feels and how temperatures behave.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.7.0] High humidity in the tropics makes heat oppressive, because sweat evaporates slowly into air that is already full of vapour.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.7.1] Low humidity in deserts allows rapid evaporation and large daily temperature ranges (the difference between day and night temperature).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.b] Evaporation is the step that puts water into the atmosphere. Its rate depends on energy, on the state of the air and on whether there is water to evaporate.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.0] Evaporation is water molecules gaining enough energy to escape as vapour. It takes latent heat (hidden heat energy) from the surface, cooling it, and that energy is released again on condensation. There is more on this in Solar energy and its redistribution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.1] Higher temperature increases evaporation because it gives more energy; strong sunshine and low air pressure also increase it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.2] Drier air, with low relative humidity, increases evaporation because it can take up more vapour.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.3] Wind increases evaporation by removing moist air and replacing it with drier air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.4] A large supply of water, such as oceans, lakes or wet ground, allows evaporation to continue.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.9.b] Because evaporation needs heat, wind and water together, it is strongest in some parts of the world and weak in others.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.9.0] Evaporation is greatest over the subtropical oceans under clear skies and steady trade winds (the regular winds blowing towards the equator), and along warm currents such as the Gulf Stream; it is least over cold polar seas.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.9.1] Potential evapotranspiration (the water that could evaporate if supply were unlimited) far exceeds rainfall in hot deserts, which is the cause of drought. There is more on this in Climate and economic development.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.10.b] Condensation is the reverse of evaporation and is how clouds begin. Vapour will not simply turn to droplets in clean air, so it needs something to form on.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.10.0] Condensation is water vapour turning into liquid droplets when air cools to its dew point (the temperature at which it is saturated).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.10.1] Condensation needs condensation nuclei: tiny particles of dust, sea salt, smoke or pollen around which droplets form.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.b] Air only condenses when it is cooled to dew point, so the question is what cools it. There are four ways.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.0] Adiabatic cooling (cooling caused by expansion, with no heat lost) of rising air is the main cause of cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.1] Contact cooling by radiation occurs when air touches ground that has lost heat at night.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.2] Advection cooling occurs when warm, moist air moves over a cold surface.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.3] Mixing of air masses of different temperatures can bring air to dew point, because the blend is cooler than the warm air alone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.b] Rising air cools even though no heat is taken from it. Understanding why explains cloud height and the rates at which air cools.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.0] Rising air expands because pressure falls with height, and it cools without losing heat to its surroundings; sinking air is compressed and warms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.1] Unsaturated air cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate (the rate of cooling with height), about 10 °C per 1,000 m.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.2] Once condensation begins, the latent heat released slows the cooling to the saturated adiabatic lapse rate, about 5-7 °C per 1,000 m, which varies with temperature.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.3] The height at which condensation begins is the condensation level, or cloud base.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.b] Whether rising air keeps rising depends on how its temperature compares with the air around it. This decides whether the sky gives towering storm cloud or flat layer cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.0] Unstable air: if rising air stays warmer than its surroundings, because the environment cools faster than the rising air does, it keeps rising and forms towering cumulus, showers and thunderstorms. The environmental lapse rate (the cooling of the surrounding air with height) averages about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.1] Stable air: if rising air becomes cooler than its surroundings, it sinks back, giving layer cloud or clear skies.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.2] A temperature inversion, with warmer air above cooler air, is very stable because the cooler air below cannot rise, and it traps fog and pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.3] Diagram: a temperature-height graph comparing the environmental and adiabatic lapse rates.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.b] Condensation also happens at ground level, where it gives dew, frost and fog. The type depends on how the air is cooled.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.0] Dew forms on surfaces cooled below dew point on clear, calm nights.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.1] Hoar frost forms when the dew point is below 0 °C, as vapour turns straight to ice crystals.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.2] Radiation fog forms on clear, calm nights, usually under anticyclones (areas of high pressure) in autumn and winter, in valleys and hollows; it burns off in the morning sun.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.3] Advection fog forms when warm, moist air moves over a cold sea or land. The Grand Banks off Newfoundland, Canada, are among the foggiest places on earth, where warm moist air from over the Gulf Stream crosses the cold Labrador Current.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.4] San Francisco's summer fogs are also advection fog: they form where moist Pacific air crosses the cold California Current.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.5] Hill fog is cloud on high ground, where air is forced up and cools; smog is fog mixed with smoke.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.b] Clouds form wherever air is lifted and cooled below its dew point. The ways air is forced upward give five causes of cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.0] Clouds are visible masses of tiny water droplets or ice crystals, formed when rising air cools below its dew point.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.1] Convection: uplift from surface heating, which warms the air so that it rises.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.2] Orographic (relief) uplift: air is forced up over high ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.3] Frontal uplift: warm air rises over cold air at a front (the boundary between two air masses).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.4] Convergence: air streams meet and, with nowhere else to go, are forced upward, as at the ITCZ (the Intertropical Convergence Zone, where the trade winds meet).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.5] Turbulence: mixing of air that lifts it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.b] Cloud names are built from a few Latin words that describe shape, height and rain. Once you know them, a name tells you what the cloud looks like and what weather it brings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.0] Luke Howard, an English pharmacist and amateur meteorologist, proposed the Latin cloud names cirrus, cumulus, stratus and nimbus in 1803.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.1] Cirrus means wispy and fibrous, a curl of hair; cumulus means heaped and puffy, a heap; stratus means a layered sheet.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.2] Nimbus or nimbo- means rain-bearing; alto- means middle level.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.3] The WMO's International Cloud Atlas sets out the ten main cloud types (the WMO is the World Meteorological Organization).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.17.b] High clouds sit above about 6,000 m and are made of ice crystals. All three types here are thin, wispy or veil-like clouds.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.17.0] Cirrus: thin wisps, often called mares' tails; fair weather, but often the first sign of an approaching warm front.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.17.1] Cirrostratus: a thin milky veil that produces a halo round the sun or moon; rain is likely within hours.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.17.2] Cirrocumulus: small ripples, known as a mackerel sky because the pattern looks like fish scales.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.18.b] Middle clouds sit between about 2,000 m and 6,000 m, which is why their names begin with alto-, meaning middle level.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.18.0] Altocumulus: grey or white patches in rolls.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.18.1] Altostratus: a grey sheet through which the sun shows as if through frosted glass; it thickens ahead of a warm front.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.19.b] Low clouds are the ones that most often sit over Ireland and bring its drizzle and steady rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.19.0] Stratus: a grey, featureless layer, so it has no towering shape, that produces drizzle and hill fog.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.19.1] Stratocumulus: a lumpy grey layer, the commonest cloud over Ireland, usually dry or giving light drizzle.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.19.2] Nimbostratus: a thick, dark rain cloud giving long periods of steady rain or snow at warm fronts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.b] Some clouds do not stay in one layer. Their base is low but their tops reach high, because strong rising air builds them upward.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.0] Cumulus: fair-weather heaps with flat bases on sunny days, which can grow into showers if the air keeps rising.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.1] Cumulonimbus: a towering cloud with an anvil-shaped top spreading at the tropopause (the top of the lowest layer of the atmosphere), bringing heavy showers, hail, thunder and lightning, and squalls (sudden strong gusts).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.2] Heights are approximate for mid-latitudes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.b] Clouds appear in a predictable order as a front approaches, so they can be used to forecast weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.0] Ahead of a warm front, cirrus is followed by cirrostratus, altostratus and nimbostratus in turn. There is more on this in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.1] Cumulonimbus marks a cold front.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.2] Diagram: a chart of the ten cloud types arranged by height.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.22.b] Clouds affect temperature on the ground as well as producing rain, and the effect depends on the type of cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.22.0] Low thick clouds cool the surface by reflecting sunlight; high thin clouds warm it by trapping long-wave radiation (heat given off by the earth); cloudy nights are milder.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.23.b] Cloud is not the same as rain. Droplets must first grow, by one of two processes, before they can fall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.23.0] Cloud droplets are far too small to fall; they must grow about a million times in volume to form a raindrop heavy enough to reach the ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.24.b] In cold clouds, ice crystals grow at the expense of water droplets. This is the main way rain forms in Ireland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.24.0] In clouds colder than 0 °C, supercooled droplets (liquid droplets still unfrozen below 0 °C) and ice crystals exist together. Vapour is deposited on the ice crystals, which grow at the droplets' expense, become heavy and fall as snow, melting into rain below the freezing level.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.24.1] This Bergeron-Findeisen process is the main one in mid-latitude clouds, including Ireland's.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.25.b] In clouds that are never cold enough for ice, droplets grow by colliding with each other instead.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.25.0] In warm clouds, which are above 0 °C throughout and are common in the tropics, larger droplets fall faster, collide with and absorb smaller ones (coalesce) until they are heavy enough to fall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.b] Precipitation can fall as liquid or ice. The form depends on the size of the drops and on the temperature of the air they fall through.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.0] Rain: liquid drops, such as the steady rain from nimbostratus.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.1] Drizzle: very small drops falling from stratus.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.2] Snow: ice crystals clumped as flakes, falling when the air is near or below freezing to the ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.3] Sleet: melting snow, or rain and snow mixed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.4] Hail: layered ice balls formed when strong updrafts in cumulonimbus carry frozen drops up and down repeatedly, adding layers until they are too heavy; hail is mostly a warm-season phenomenon.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.5] Freezing rain and glaze: rain that freezes on contact with cold surfaces.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.b] Convectional rain comes from air that is heated from below and rises. It is the daily rain of the equator.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.0] 1. The ground is heated strongly, warming the air above it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.1] 2. The warm air expands, rises and cools adiabatically.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.2] 3. When it reaches dew point, cumulus and then cumulonimbus clouds form.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.3] 4. Heavy showers, often with thunder, follow, usually in the afternoon, because that is when the ground is hottest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.4] This type is typical of the equatorial belt every day, and of Irish summer afternoons occasionally, especially inland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.5] Diagram: a column of rising air above heated ground, topped by cumulonimbus.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.b] Relief rain forms when moist air is forced up over mountains. It leaves a wet windward side and a dry leeward side.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.0] 1. Moist air from the sea is forced to rise over a mountain barrier.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.1] 2. It cools, condenses and gives heavy rain on the windward slope (the side facing the wind).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.2] 3. On the leeward side (the sheltered side) the air descends, is compressed and warms, so clouds evaporate and a rain shadow (a dry area) results.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.3] 4. A warm, dry föhn-type wind may blow down the lee side. There is more on this in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.4] Ireland's west coast mountains receive this rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.5] In India, the Western Ghats force the Arabian Sea branch of the monsoon to rise, giving heavy rain on the Konkan and Malabar coasts, with the Deccan in rain shadow; Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in the Khasi Hills, Meghalaya, are among the wettest places on earth.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.6] Diagram: a cross-section of a mountain barrier showing the windward wet slope and the leeward rain shadow.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.b] Frontal rain falls where warm air meets cold air in a depression (a low-pressure weather system). It is Ireland's main source of rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.0] 1. In a depression, warm, moist tropical air is forced to rise over cold polar air at the warm front, giving gentle ascent and long, steady rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.1] 2. At the cold front the warm air is undercut, causing sharp ascent and short, heavy showers.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.2] Frontal rain is Ireland's main source of rain. There is more on depressions in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.30.b] Rain also falls where winds meet, because air that cannot go sideways is forced upward.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.30.0] Where air streams meet they are forced to rise, as where the trade winds converge at the ITCZ, giving heavy rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.b] World rainfall follows the pressure belts, which are bands of rising or sinking air arranged by latitude. Rising air gives rain and sinking air gives dry conditions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.0] Equatorial belt (about 0-10°): heavy rain all year, over 2,000 mm a year, from low pressure, rising air, convergence at the ITCZ, convection and high evaporation from warm seas and forests. Manaus, central Amazon, Brazil, receives about 2,300 mm a year.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.1] Tropics with a wet and dry season (about 10-20°, savanna and monsoon lands): rain falls in summer when the ITCZ moves over them, and the winter is dry.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.2] Subtropical high-pressure belts (about 20-30°): very low rainfall and the world's great hot deserts, from sinking, warming, stable air and clear skies.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.3] The Atacama, Chile, is the driest non-polar desert on earth, and the Namib receives much of its moisture as fog from the cold Benguela Current.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.4] Mid-latitudes (about 40-60°): moderate to heavy rain from depressions in the westerlies, wettest on west coasts facing onshore winds (Ireland), decreasing into continental interiors.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.5] Polar regions: very low precipitation, since cold air holds little vapour and sinking air lies under the polar high pressure. Antarctica is the driest continent, a cold desert.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.6] Diagram: a pole-to-pole profile of rainfall under the three-cell model (the pattern of three circulation cells in each hemisphere).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.b] The latitude pattern is changed locally by five factors, each of which can make a place wetter or drier than its latitude suggests.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.0] Relief and rain shadow: the leeward side of a mountain barrier receives much less rain. There is more on this in the block on relief (orographic) rainfall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.1] Distance from the sea: continental interiors are dry because they lie far from the moisture of the oceans.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.2] Prevailing winds: onshore winds bring rain, while offshore winds bring dry air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.3] Ocean currents: warm currents add moisture and instability; cold currents chill the air and make it stable, so rain is rare though fog is common on the coasts named above.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.4] Seasonal migration: the pressure belts move north and south with the overhead sun, shifting the rain belts with them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.b] A rainfall regime is the pattern of rain through the year. Five regimes are worth knowing.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.0] Equatorial: rain all year because the air is warm and rising every day, often peaking after the equinoxes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.1] Monsoon: a very wet summer and a dry winter, as in India.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.2] Mediterranean: a wet winter and a dry summer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.3] Continental interiors: a summer maximum, because summer heating drives convection.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.4] Irish (oceanic): rain all year, with a maximum in autumn and winter.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.b] Ireland shows how wind, depressions and relief combine to make the west wet and the east drier.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.0] The west is wetter than the east, the mountains are wettest, and the west has more wet days, because moist Atlantic air arrives there first.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.1] Annual rainfall is about 750 mm in the driest parts of the east, around Dublin, about 1,000-1,250 mm across much of the west, and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.2] Wet days number about 150 a year on the east and south-east coasts, and up to about 225 a year in parts of the west.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.3] Causes: prevailing south-westerly winds, Atlantic depressions reaching the west first, relief uplift on the western mountains, and the rain shadow in the east.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.35.b] Variability is how much rainfall changes from year to year. It is a problem for farming and water supply.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.35.0] Rainfall is least reliable where totals are lowest. In the Sahel, annual rainfall is roughly 200-600 mm, falling in one short summer wet season when the ITCZ moves north.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.35.1] It varies greatly from year to year, so drought risk is greatest there. There is more on this in One major biome in detail.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.36.b] The exchanges of water between ocean and atmosphere give rise to distinctive weather and climate regimes in different parts of the world. Each is a one-line summary here.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.36.0] Equatorial regime: morning heating gives way to afternoon storms every day.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.36.1] Monsoon regime: moist sea air brings a wet summer and dry land air a dry winter over India, as the monsoon (a seasonal wind) reverses.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.36.2] Fog deserts: cold-current coasts, such as the Atacama and Namib, have fog rather than rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.36.3] Irish maritime regime: a cool, cloudy, wet climate fed by moisture from the Atlantic and warmed by the North Atlantic Drift.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.36.4] Snowy continental and lake-effect winters: cold, snowy winters with heavy snowfall downwind of large lakes and in continental interiors.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.36.5] Intensified water cycle: warming increases evaporation and brings heavier downpours. There is more on this in Climatic environments and climate change.
