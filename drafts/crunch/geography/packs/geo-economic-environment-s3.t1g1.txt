TARGET geo-economic-environment-s3: 3. Environmental pollution
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.b] Pollution is a problem caused by people, and it can be small or enormous in scale. Knowing how to define it, classify it and place it on a scale lets you tackle any pollution question.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.0] Pollution is the release by human activity of substances or energy (heat, noise, light) into the environment in amounts that harm living things, health or property. Harm is what makes a release pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.1] Types of pollution: air, water (fresh and marine), land and soil, noise and visual. For example, oil in the sea is water pollution and a motorway's roar is noise pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.2] Scales: local (one river or town), national (a country's water quality) and global (the whole atmosphere or ocean). The scale tells you how many people and places are affected.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.3] Transboundary pollution (pollution that crosses national borders) means the place it harms may be far from its source. Acid rain, rain made acidic by gases from burning fuels, is the European example, taught in the note Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.b] Pollution can be traced back to its source in two ways, depending on whether it leaves from one spot or from many. This matters because a single outlet is easier to find and control than thousands of small ones.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.0] Point sources come from one identifiable place, such as a factory pipe or a sewage outfall (the pipe where sewage enters a river or the sea).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.1] Diffuse sources come from many places at once, such as runoff (rainwater washing substances off the land) from many farms, or traffic.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.2] Economic activities that pollute include agriculture, manufacturing, energy generation, transport, mining, tourism, households and urban waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.b] Global warming is the main global-scale example of pollution. It starts with a natural process that human activity has strengthened.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.0] The natural greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases (gases in the atmosphere that trap heat) let incoming short-wave solar radiation through but absorb outgoing long-wave radiation (heat given off by the earth), which keeps the earth habitable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.1] The enhanced greenhouse effect: human activity adds greenhouse gases to the atmosphere, which trap more heat and so warm the planet further.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.b] Each greenhouse gas has its own human sources, and many of them are economic activities. Knowing which activity gives which gas lets you explain the causes of warming.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.0] CO2 (carbon dioxide) comes from burning fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas), deforestation (clearing forests, which removes trees that absorb CO2) and cement production.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.1] Methane comes from cattle and sheep, rice paddies, landfill (rubbish buried in the ground) and gas leaks.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.2] Nitrous oxide comes from nitrogen fertilisers and slurry (liquid animal manure).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.3] CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons, man-made gases) and other fluorinated gases are also greenhouse gases.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.b] Scientists measure warming in several independent ways, and they all point the same way. Each measurement below is a piece of evidence you can quote.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.0] Atmospheric CO2 was about 280 ppm (parts per million) before the Industrial Revolution. It passed 400 ppm in the mid-2010s and was over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s. These readings are measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, which has recorded them since 1958.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.1] Global average surface temperature has risen by about 1.1 to 1.2 °C since the late 1800s (IPCC, the UN's climate science panel).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.2] 2024 was the warmest year on record and the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels (Copernicus, the EU's Earth observation programme).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.3] Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.4] Global sea level rose by about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.5] Glaciers (large masses of ice on land) are retreating worldwide, which is a visible sign that temperatures are rising.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.b] A warmer atmosphere changes oceans, ice, weather and ecosystems, and these changes harm people and wildlife. The effects are worst for places that are low-lying or already vulnerable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.0] Sea-level rise, from thermal expansion of seawater (water expands as it warms) and melting land ice, threatens low-lying countries and deltas: Bangladesh (the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta), the Maldives, Tuvalu and Kiribati.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.1] More intense heatwaves, droughts, floods and storms. The Sahel (the dry belt south of the Sahara) has suffered droughts.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.2] Glacier retreat threatens water supplies, because many places depend on meltwater from glaciers.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.3] Coral bleaching (corals losing their colour and dying when seas get too warm): the Great Barrier Reef, Australia, has suffered repeated mass bleaching since the late 1990s.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.4] Species shifts and extinctions, crop failures in vulnerable regions, and climate refugees (people forced to leave their homes because of climate change).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.b] Ireland is not exempt from global warming. Its temperature has risen, and the effects are felt on land, at the coast and at sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.0] Ireland's average temperature has risen by about 0.9 °C since 1900 (EPA and Met Éireann, the national weather service).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.1] Warmer temperatures bring wetter winters and more flooding, a risk of summer drought, stronger storms and coastal erosion (the sea wearing away the coast).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.2] Effects also include changes to farming and to marine species, and a threat to low-lying coastal towns.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.3] Storm Éowyn, in January 2025, caused record damage and power cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.b] Ireland's emissions come mainly from a few sectors, and farming dominates. This is why so many Irish climate measures target agriculture.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.0] Agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, the largest share, mainly methane and nitrous oxide from cattle, slurry and fertiliser.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.1] Transport, mainly fuel burned by vehicles, is the next largest source, at about a fifth of emissions (EPA, early 2020s), followed by energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.2] Ireland's emissions per person are high for an EU country, which is largely because of its large farming sector.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.b] Warming is a global problem, so governments have tried to answer it through international agreements. Each one built on the one before.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.0] The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) was agreed at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in 1992.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.1] The Kyoto Protocol, agreed in 1997 and in force from 2005, set legally binding cuts for developed countries only. The USA never ratified it (it signed but did not formally approve it), so it was not a full party.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.2] The Paris Agreement, 2015, aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C. Each country sets its own national plan (nationally determined contribution).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.3] Countries meet every year at COP meetings (conferences of the countries that signed the UNFCCC). COP30 was held at Belém, Brazil, in November 2025.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.b] The EU and Ireland have turned the global agreements into laws, targets and charges. Together they put a price on emissions and set deadlines for cutting them.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.0] The EU Emissions Trading System began in 2005. Power stations and heavy industry must hold an allowance (a permit) for each tonne of CO2 they emit, which gives them a cost reason to cut emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.1] The European Green Deal (2019) aims for a climate-neutral EU by 2050 (no net emissions). Its Fit for 55 package aims to cut net emissions by at least 55% by 2030, compared with 1990.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.2] Ireland's Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 requires a 51% cut in emissions by 2030, compared with 2018, and net zero by 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.3] Sectoral ceilings (limits on each sector's emissions, set in 2022) require agriculture to cut its emissions by 25% by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.4] An Irish carbon tax (a charge on fuels based on their CO2) was introduced in 2010 and is rising in stages towards €100 a tonne by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.5] The Climate Action Plan aims to retrofit (upgrade with insulation and heating) 500,000 homes to a B2 energy rating by 2030, and includes renewables, electric vehicles and measures for agriculture.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.b] Governments cannot cut emissions alone, because homes, transport and food are all household choices. Everyday changes add up.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.0] Improve energy efficiency and insulate homes, so less fuel is burned for heating.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.1] Use public transport, cycling and walking in place of car journeys, so fewer fossil fuels are burned.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.2] Reduce food waste, because rotting food in landfill gives off methane.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.3] Switch to renewable heating, because it does not burn fossil fuels such as oil and gas, so it gives off less CO2.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.b] Agricultural water pollution is the Irish national case study of water pollution. Nutrients meant to grow crops end up in rivers and lakes, where they set off a chain of events called eutrophication.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.0] Cause: nitrogen and phosphorus from slurry, manure and chemical fertilisers wash off fields into rivers and lakes, especially after heavy rain or when spread on wet or frozen ground. Silage effluent (liquid from stored grass) and sewage from towns and septic tanks (private sewage tanks) add more.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.1] Eutrophication (water becoming overloaded with nutrients): the nutrients feed rapid algal growth, forming algal blooms (dense layers of algae).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.2] When the algae die, bacteria decomposing them use up the dissolved oxygen, so fish and other life suffocate in fish kills.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.3] The water turns green and smells. Some blue-green algae are toxic.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.b] Polluted water harms wildlife first, and then the people and businesses that rely on clean water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.0] Loss of fish and other wildlife, such as in a fish kill, and damage to angling and tourism, because few people visit or fish in green, smelly water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.1] Drinking water needs costly treatment, and nitrates (nitrogen compounds from fertiliser) contaminate groundwater (water held underground).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.2] Coastal waters, including estuaries (where rivers meet the sea), are affected by algal mats, because rivers carry the nutrients downstream.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.b] The EPA, which monitors water quality, shows that pollution is a national problem and not only a local one. Farming is the main pressure.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.0] An EPA assessment for 2016 to 2021 found that only about half of Ireland's rivers, lakes and estuaries were in good or high ecological status.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.1] Agriculture is the most common pressure on water quality, followed by urban wastewater (sewage from towns), because fertiliser and slurry wash into streams.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.2] Milk quotas (limits on how much milk farms could produce) were abolished on 1 April 2015. The dairy herd then grew strongly, especially in the south and east, and raised nitrogen levels there.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.b] Lough Neagh is a real example of eutrophication, and the 2023 bloom shows the processes above in action. Treat it as a single event, and do not claim it has been solved.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.0] Lough Neagh, Northern Ireland, is the largest lake on the island of Ireland. In summer 2023 a huge toxic blue-green algal bloom covered much of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.1] The bloom was blamed on nutrient pollution from farm runoff, sewage and septic tanks, together with warm weather and invasive zebra mussels that clear the water, letting more light reach the algae.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.2] Lough Neagh supplies a large share of Northern Ireland's drinking water, so a toxic bloom threatens a key water supply.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.b] Reducing the pollution means keeping nutrients on the land and out of water. Most of the rules limit when, where and how much farmers spread.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.0] The EU Nitrates Directive (1991) and Ireland's Nitrates Action Programme set closed periods each winter when slurry and fertiliser may not be spread, because rain would wash them straight into rivers.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.1] The Nitrates Action Programme also limits nitrogen per hectare, requires slurry storage, and requires buffer strips (strips of vegetation) beside watercourses, which trap nutrients before they reach the water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.2] The EU Water Framework Directive (2000) aims for good status for all waters, which gives countries a target to work towards.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.3] Other measures: upgrading urban wastewater treatment, EPA monitoring of water quality, and farm advisory programmes.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.16.b] Oil in the sea comes from accidents and from deliberate or careless releases. It is a global pollution problem because oil is moved and drilled for all over the world.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.16.0] Tanker accidents, drilling blow-outs (uncontrolled releases of oil from a well), illegal tank washing at sea, and pipeline leaks release oil into the sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.b] The Exxon Valdez is a tanker accident that caused huge damage to a wild coast and led to a change in law. Remember it is in Alaska, not the Gulf of Mexico.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.0] The tanker ran aground on Bligh Reef, Prince William Sound, Alaska, on 24 March 1989.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.1] About 11 million US gallons (about 37,000 tonnes) of crude oil were spilled, and about 2,000 km of coastline was oiled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.2] Hundreds of thousands of seabirds and thousands of sea otters were killed, and the herring fishery collapsed.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.3] The response was the US Oil Pollution Act 1990, which required double-hulled tankers (tankers with two layers of steel, so a hit is less likely to leak) in US waters.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.b] The Prestige shows that a tanker accident can damage the coasts of several countries and the fishing that depends on them.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.0] The single-hulled tanker Prestige broke in two and sank off Galicia, north-west Spain, in November 2002. It spilled tens of thousands of tonnes of heavy fuel oil.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.1] Oil reached the Costa da Morte and the coasts of Spain, Portugal and France. Galician shellfish and fishing industries were closed for months.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.2] The disaster sped up the EU's phase-out of single-hulled tankers, because a single hull is more likely to leak after damage.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.b] Deepwater Horizon is an example of oil pollution from drilling, and not from a tanker. It is in the Gulf of Mexico.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.0] The Deepwater Horizon rig (an offshore drilling platform), operated by BP, exploded on 20 April 2010 in the Gulf of Mexico. Eleven workers were killed.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.1] About 4.9 million barrels of oil were spilled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.b] Whatever the cause, oil spills have similar effects, and the same responses are used in each. Know the effects first, then the clean-up and the prevention.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.0] Effects: oiled seabirds and mammals, smothered shorelines, fishing bans and lost tourism, because beaches and catches are unusable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.1] Clean-up: booms (floating barriers) to contain the oil, skimmers (machines that scoop oil off the surface) to remove it, and dispersants (chemicals) to break it up.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.2] Prevention: double-hulled tankers, and routing ships away from coasts, so that any accident happens far from the shore.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.b] Plastic is cheap and lasts a very long time, which is why it is a problem once it is thrown away. It pollutes at local and global scale.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.0] Causes: single-use packaging that is thrown away after one use, litter, illegal dumping and landfill (burying rubbish in the ground).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.1] Plastics take hundreds of years to break down, and they fragment into microplastics (tiny pieces of plastic).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.2] Effects: litter in towns and countryside, and wildlife entangled in or eating plastic, which can injure or kill animals.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.3] Ocean garbage patches: the Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a large area of the North Pacific where floating plastic collects in an ocean gyre (a large circling ocean current).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.4] Microplastics enter food chains, as animals eat them. Landfill produces leachate (polluted liquid that drains from rubbish) and methane.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.b] Ireland has tried to cut plastic and waste by putting a price on it, by rewarding returns, and by ranking how waste should be handled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.0] The plastic bag levy (a charge on each bag) was introduced in March 2002 at 15 cent a bag, raised to 22 cent in 2007. Plastic bag use fell by over 90% almost at once, and the money funds environmental projects.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.1] The Deposit Return Scheme (Re-turn) began on 1 February 2024 for plastic drinks bottles and cans.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.2] The waste hierarchy ranks reduce, reuse, recycle, recover and dispose. The EPA licenses waste facilities.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.3] The Dublin Waste-to-Energy incinerator at Poolbeg (a plant that burns waste to make energy) has operated since 2017.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.b] Ozone depletion is a different problem from global warming, with a different cause and a different treaty. It is also a success story.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.0] CFCs, from aerosols, fridges and air conditioning, destroy stratospheric ozone (the ozone in the upper atmosphere), which absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.1] British Antarctic Survey scientists reported the Antarctic ozone hole (a thin area in the ozone layer over Antarctica) in 1985.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.2] More ultraviolet radiation causes skin cancer and eye cataracts, and damages plankton and crops.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.3] The Montreal Protocol, agreed in 1987, phased out CFCs. Every UN member state has ratified it, and it is often cited as the most successful global environmental agreement.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.4] The Antarctic ozone layer is expected to recover to 1980 levels around the 2060s (UN assessment, 2023).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.b] Pollution also occurs closer to home, from traffic, old mines and large structures. Each of these is a local example.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.0] Air pollution in towns: traffic produces nitrogen dioxide and fine particles. Home burning of solid fuel adds smoke, which is why smoke-free zones exist in some towns (see the note Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources for smoke-free zones).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.1] Mining pollution: acid mine drainage (acidic water flowing out of old mines) from old copper mines at Avoca, Co. Wicklow, discolours and pollutes the Avoca River.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.2] Noise and visual pollution (unwanted sound and ugly views) from airports, motorways, quarries, wind farms and pylons.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s2: 2. Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.b] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas formed from ancient living things) supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy (the energy taken straight from nature before conversion) in the 2020s. Their production is concentrated in a few places, so most countries must import them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.0] The Middle East (e.g. Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq) holds about half the world's proven oil reserves (oil known to be recoverable). Venezuela has the largest proven reserves of any single country.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.1] The USA has been the world's largest oil producer since the late 2010s, through shale oil and gas (trapped in rock and released by fracking, which fractures the rock with pressurised liquid).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.2] Russia is one of the largest gas exporters. Qatar is a leading exporter of liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so that ships can carry it).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.3] China produces and consumes about half the world's coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.4] Fossil fuels are traded over long distances by tanker, pipeline and LNG ship, so importers depend on exporters for their energy security (a steady, affordable supply of energy).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.b] Energy use is very uneven across the world. It follows wealth and industry, and the pattern is shifting towards Asia.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.0] Developed countries use far more energy per person than developing countries. Demand is now growing fastest in Asia, notably China and India, because their economies and populations are growing.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.1] The USA, Canada and Australia use many times more energy per person than India or most of sub-Saharan Africa.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.2] China has been the world's largest CO2 (carbon dioxide) emitter in total since the mid-2000s, but the USA emits far more CO2 per person.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.b] Burning a fossil fuel releases the carbon and impurities locked inside it. Each pollutant causes a different problem, so keep them apart.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.0] Carbon dioxide (CO2) is the main greenhouse gas released, and it drives global warming.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.1] Sulphur dioxide (SO2), mainly from coal and heavy oil (thick, impure oil), is a cause of acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.2] Nitrogen oxides (NOx), mainly from vehicles and power stations, are also a cause of acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.3] Smoke, fine particles (soot, tiny solid specks that can be breathed deep into the lungs) and carbon monoxide are released as well.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.b] The greenhouse effect is the natural trapping of heat by gases in the atmosphere. Burning fossil fuels adds extra gas to it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.0] CO2 traps heat in the atmosphere, so extra CO2 from burning fossil fuels enhances the greenhouse effect and causes global warming. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.b] Acid rain is a transboundary problem (one that crosses national borders): the countries that cause it are often not the ones that suffer.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.0] Normal rain is slightly acidic, at about pH 5.6, because CO2 dissolves in it naturally. Acid rain is rain with a pH below 5.6, not below pH 7.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.1] SO2 and NOx dissolve in water vapour to form sulphuric and nitric acids. These fall as wet deposition in rain, or as dry deposition of acidic particles and gases.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.2] Sources include coal-fired power stations, heavy industry and vehicles, because all of them burn fuels that contain sulphur or release nitrogen oxides.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.3] Emissions from Britain and Germany were carried by the prevailing south-westerly winds (the usual wind direction) to Norway and Sweden, which is why Scandinavia suffered from other countries' pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.4] The Black Triangle, the border region of former East Germany, Poland and Czechia, had lignite-burning power stations (lignite is a low-grade, smoky coal) that caused severe forest death in the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.b] Acid rain harms living things, buildings and people. The damage can appear far from the source, because the wind carries the pollution across borders.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.0] Forest damage: needle and leaf loss, and nutrients leached (washed out) from the soil. German forests, such as the Black Forest, were reported dying in the 1980s (Waldsterben, meaning forest death).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.1] Lake acidification: thousands of Scandinavian lakes were acidified and lost their fish. Aluminium released from the soil by the acid kills fish.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.2] Limestone and marble buildings and statues dissolve, through the same process as the chemical weathering of limestone (carbonation, where weak acid reacts with the rock).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.3] Corrosion of metal, for example, and respiratory (breathing) problems in people.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.b] Because acid rain crosses borders, responses had to be international as well as national. They work either by cutting emissions or by repairing the damage.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.0] The 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution, signed at UNECE (the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe) in Geneva, was an international agreement on air pollution that crosses borders.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.1] EU directives (laws binding on member states) limit emissions from large power stations.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.2] Flue-gas desulphurisation (scrubbers, which remove SO2 from the smoke before it leaves the chimney) is used at power stations. Moneypoint, Co. Clare, opened in 1985 and had scrubbers fitted in the late 2000s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.3] Low-sulphur fuels cut SO2 at source. Catalytic converters (exhaust devices that reduce NOx) became compulsory on new petrol cars in the EU in 1993.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.4] Sweden and Norway have limed acidified lakes (added lime to neutralise the acid) since the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.5] Countries switched from coal to gas and renewables, which release little or no SO2.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.6] Outcome: European SO2 emissions have fallen by well over three-quarters since 1990, and many lakes are recovering.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.b] Smog is smoke mixed with fog. It builds up when smoke from home fires cannot escape upwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.0] Burning coal and turf in homes releases smoke and fine particles, because the fuel burns incompletely in an open fire.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.1] In calm, cold winter weather an anticyclone (a high-pressure system with settled air) creates a temperature inversion, where a layer of warm air sits above cold air near the ground.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.2] The inversion traps the smoke near the ground, because the warm layer stops it rising and blowing away. This forms smog.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.3] Smog causes bronchitis, asthma, heart and lung disease, and extra deaths, because the smoke and fine particles are breathed in.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.b] Smog led governments to ban smoky fuels in city areas. London's disaster in 1952 was the earlier precedent, and Dublin's ban is the Irish case study.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.0] London's Great Smog, in December 1952, caused about 4,000 deaths at the time. Later estimates are far higher.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.1] The Great Smog led to the UK Clean Air Act 1956, which created smokeless zones (areas where smoky fuel may not be burned).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.2] Dublin suffered severe winter smogs in the early 1980s from household coal burning, which led to the ban.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.3] The sale of smoky (bituminous) coal was banned in Dublin from 1 September 1990. Black smoke levels fell by about 70%.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.4] The ban covered smoky coal only, so smokeless fuel and turf could still be burned in Dublin at first.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.5] A study published in The Lancet in 2002 estimated that the ban was linked to about 350 fewer deaths a year in Dublin from respiratory and heart disease.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.6] The ban was extended to Cork in 1995, and in stages to other cities and large towns as low-smoke zones. National rules from 2022 further restrict the sale of smoky solid fuels and wet wood.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.b] Beyond the air pollutants, getting and using fossil fuels harms land, water and rivers.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.0] Oil spills during transport, for example from tankers, pollute the sea and coasts. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.1] Landscape damage comes from coal mining and peat cutting, which scar and strip the land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.2] Thermal pollution of rivers is caused by the cooling water of power stations, which is returned to the river warmer than it was taken.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.b] Wind turns turbine blades that drive a generator. Turbines are built onshore on land and offshore at sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.0] Ireland: wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s, from onshore farms such as Galway Wind Park, Connemara (2017), and from Arklow Bank Wind Park, off Co. Wicklow (2004), Ireland's first offshore wind farm.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.1] Denmark: wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.2] Advantage: strong, frequent Atlantic winds give Ireland a good wind resource, and wind is the main renewable source in Irish electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.3] Disadvantage: output varies with the wind, and local residents object on grounds of noise, shadow flicker (flickering shadows cast by moving blades) and visual impact.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.4] Disadvantage: Derrybrien, Co. Galway, suffered a peat slide of about 450,000 m³ on 16 October 2003 during construction of a wind farm on bog. The EU Court of Justice fined Ireland in 2019 for the lack of a proper environmental impact assessment (a study of the likely harm before building).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.b] Hydroelectric power uses falling water to turn turbines, through three main methods.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.0] Dams and reservoirs release stored water through turbines at a controlled rate.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.1] Run-of-river schemes use the river's natural flow, so they need little or no stored water.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.2] Pumped storage pumps water uphill at times of low demand and releases it at peak times, so it acts like a store of energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.3] Ireland: Ardnacrusha on the River Shannon (Shannon Scheme, opened 1929, built by Siemens) and Turlough Hill, Co. Wicklow (1974), the country's pumped-storage station. Poulaphouca on the Liffey, Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid on the Lee, and Cathaleen's Fall at Ballyshannon on the Erne also generate power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.4] Advantage: output can be raised quickly at peak times (when demand is highest), as at Turlough Hill.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.5] Disadvantage: Ireland has little hydro potential left for large new schemes.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.6] Norway: hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of its electricity, for example.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.7] Three Gorges Dam, Yangtze River, China: the world's largest power station by installed capacity. It was completed in 2006, fully operational in 2012, and over one million people were relocated. It gives flood control downstream but traps sediment (silt that would otherwise travel downriver).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.b] Photovoltaic panels convert sunlight into electricity, and solar thermal panels use sunlight to heat water.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.0] Ireland: solar power has grown rapidly since the early 2020s, through large solar farms on farmland and panels on rooftops. SEAI (Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland) grants help homes to install panels.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.1] Disadvantage: Ireland's cloud cover and short winter days limit output, because panels need strong daylight.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.b] Waves and tides drive turbines or barrages (dams built across an estuary). The three technologies are different, so do not mix them up.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.0] Wave: the Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is a wave test site.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.1] Tidal barrage: La Rance, Brittany, France, a barrage across the Rance estuary, opened in 1966. It was one of the first and largest tidal power stations.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.2] Tidal stream: SeaGen in Strangford Lough, Co. Down, was installed in 2008 as the world's first commercial-scale tidal-stream turbine, using the fast tidal flow through the narrows. It has since been removed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.3] Advantage: tides are predictable, because they follow the moon's cycle. Disadvantages: high cost and marine impacts (harm to sea life and habitats).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.b] Geothermal energy uses heat from the Earth's interior, to heat water or to generate steam.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.0] Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (a plate boundary where the crust is thin) over a hotspot (a rising plume of hot rock). The Hellisheiði power station generates electricity from geothermal water, and about 9 in 10 Icelandic homes are heated by it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.1] Ireland uses low-temperature heat pumps only, which draw modest heat from the ground.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.2] Advantage: output does not depend on the weather. Disadvantage: high-temperature sources are found only where the crust is hot, as in Iceland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.b] Biomass is organic material (from plants or animal waste) burned or converted to energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.0] Sources: wood chip, energy crops such as willow and miscanthus, and biogas from slurry and food waste produced by anaerobic digestion (rotting without oxygen).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.1] Biofuels (fuels made from plants) are blended into petrol and diesel, by law in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.2] Edenderry, Co. Offaly, was converted from peat to biomass, an example of a power station switching fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.3] Advantage: carbon-neutral in principle, because the crops absorb as much CO2 as they release when burned. Disadvantage: energy crops compete with food production for land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.b] Nuclear power uses fission, the splitting of uranium atoms, to release heat that makes steam to drive turbines. Nuclear is not renewable.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.0] France: nuclear power supplies about two-thirds of its electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.1] Advantages: low carbon emissions, so little CO2, plus reliable and large output.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.2] Disadvantages, such as radioactive waste, accident risk, very high cost, and the cost of decommissioning (safely shutting down and dismantling) old reactors.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.3] Chernobyl, Ukraine (then USSR), 26 April 1986: a reactor exploded, and radioactive fallout spread across Europe, including Ireland. A 30 km exclusion zone remains.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.4] Fukushima Daiichi, Japan, 11 March 2011: an earthquake and tsunami knocked out cooling and caused meltdowns.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.b] Ireland has never had a nuclear power station. A combination of law, public opinion and practical limits explains why.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.0] The Electricity Regulation Act 1999 bans the use of nuclear fission to generate electricity in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.1] The ESB (Electricity Supply Board) planned a nuclear power station at Carnsore Point, Co. Wexford, in the 1970s. It was abandoned after large public protests and was never built.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.2] Other reasons: Ireland's small electricity grid, the high cost, the problem of waste disposal, and fears raised by Chernobyl.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.3] Sellafield, Cumbria, England, a nuclear reprocessing site on the Irish Sea coast, has discharges that Irish governments have long objected to, which adds to Irish unease about nuclear power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.b] Renewable energy comes from sources that are naturally replaced. These advantages apply to wind, hydro, solar, wave, tidal and geothermal power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.0] Renewables are inexhaustible, so supply is secure: the sun, wind, water and tides will not run out.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.1] They produce little or no CO2, SO2 or NOx, so they cause less global warming and acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.2] They reduce the fossil-fuel import bill and improve energy security.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.3] They create jobs, often in rural areas.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.4] Once a plant is built, the fuel is free.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.5] They help meet EU and national targets and avoid penalties for missing them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.b] Renewables also have costs and limits, and these explain why fossil fuels are still widely used.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.0] Renewables are intermittent (not constant), because wind and sun vary, so backup or storage is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.1] The initial cost of building a plant is high.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.2] Turbines and dams have visual impact, and cause noise and local objections.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.3] Remote sites need grid connections (cables linking them to the electricity network).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.4] Some habitats are damaged: birds are killed by turbines, and dams flood valleys. Peat slides can also happen at wind farms on bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.b] Ireland imports a large share of its energy, so renewable energy matters for both its environment and its economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.0] Ireland imported about three-quarters of its energy in the early 2020s, at a cost of billions of euro a year. Renewables reduce this import bill and the exposure to price shocks (sudden rises in world fuel prices).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.1] Ireland must meet its 2030 targets for renewable electricity and emissions. The Climate Action Plan aims for 80% renewable electricity by 2030, and missing EU targets exposes Ireland to the cost of buying compliance.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.2] Energy security improves through interconnectors (cables that link electricity grids): the East-West Interconnector to Wales (2012) and the Celtic Interconnector to France (due in the late 2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.3] Renewables provide jobs and income in rural areas, because wind farms, solar farms and dams are built there.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.4] Green power attracts multinational companies that want renewable electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.5] Cleaner air and water follow from burning less fossil fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.b] Map questions ask you to choose and justify a wind-farm site on an Ordnance Survey map, and to explain how conflict may arise. Match each feature of the site to a need of the wind farm.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.0] A wind farm needs a high, exposed upland or coastal site with strong winds, because output depends on wind speed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.1] It should be away from houses, to limit noise and shadow flicker.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.2] It needs an access road close by for construction, and a connection close to the electricity grid, by transmission line (the high-voltage cable that carries power).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.3] It should avoid protected areas and deep peat, because peat can slide when disturbed. It is best placed on land of low farming value.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.4] Conflict arises when a site lies near homes or in a protected or scenic area, so the site must balance wind strength against these constraints.

CANDIDATES geo-graph-skills-s1: Figure interpretation
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.b] Every figure question starts the same way: read the labels and units before reading any value. Most lost marks come from a wrong unit, year or column, because a correct reading of the wrong one still scores nothing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.0] Check the title, both axis labels (the words along the horizontal and vertical lines), the key or legend (the box explaining colours or symbols), the units (%, thousands, millions, €m, hectares, mm, °C, per 1,000), the years shown and the source line. Each one changes what a number means.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.1] Read a bar or line value by running a ruler from the top of the bar or point across to the vertical axis, estimating between gridlines, and quoting the value with its unit.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.2] In a table, find the row and the column and read the cell where they meet. Check the totals row before using a figure, so you know whether a number is a part or the whole.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.3] Compare two years by reading neighbouring columns. To spot the one that did not change, read across the whole row, because the unchanged value is the one that stays the same between years.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.4] For highest and lowest, scan every value, not just the first and last, because the extreme can sit in the middle. For a ranking, list the values from largest to smallest.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.5] To count how many items are above an average line, count the bars or points whose tops lie above it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.6] For 'twice as much', double the smaller value and look for the value that matches it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.7] An infographic (a single page combining headline numbers, icons, small charts and county maps) has several panels. Read each panel's own label and unit: headline figures are often totals, while panels may show percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.b] A description states what the figure shows. Using exact trend words and quoting numbers turns a vague answer into a complete one.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.0] Trend vocabulary: increase, rise or growth; decrease, fall or decline; steady, constant or no change; fluctuate (go up and down); peak (highest point); trough (lowest point); gradual or rapid (sharp); levelling off (the change slows and flattens).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.1] The overall trend is the long-term direction. A short-term change, such as a single year's dip, is described separately so it does not hide the main pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.2] To describe a trend, state the overall direction, the start and end values with their years and units, and the period of fastest change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.3] Name any peak, trough or reversal (a change of direction), and quantify the change as the difference, the percentage change, or both.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.4] Point out anomalies: values that break the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.b] Once a trend is described, the next marks come from comparing it with another, saying why it happened and judging whether the figure can be trusted.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.0] To compare two series (two lines or two sets of bars), say which is higher, when the lines cross, whether the gap widens or narrows, and the difference in a given year. For example, net migration = immigration minus emigration.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.1] To explain a trend, link the pattern to one cause from the relevant chapter, in one clause. Explaining means saying why the figure looks as it does, not just what it shows.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.2] To evaluate a figure, check whether an axis that does not start at zero or has uneven intervals exaggerates change, because a small rise then looks large.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.3] Also check whether unevenly spaced dates distort the trend, whether the sample (the group measured) is small, and whether the source is reliable and recent.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.4] Percentages can hide small totals: a large percentage rise may be a small change in numbers, because a small starting number is easy to double.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.b] These worked values come from the invented (illustrative) population of six towns, in thousands: Town A 18, Town B 32, Town C 25, Town D 41, Town E 12, Town F 29. Each statistic has a short method that you can repeat on any data set.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.0] Total (sum): add every value. 18 + 32 + 25 + 41 + 12 + 29 = 157 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.1] Difference: subtract the smaller value from the larger and say which is larger. Town B (32) exceeds Town C (25) by 7 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.2] Mean (average) = total of the values ÷ number of values: 157 ÷ 6 = 26.2 thousand, to one decimal place (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.3] Median (the middle value): put the values in order (12, 18, 25, 29, 32, 41); with an even number of values, average the middle two: (25 + 29) ÷ 2 = 27 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.4] Mode: the most frequent value. The six towns have no mode because no value repeats.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.5] Range = highest value minus lowest value: 41 − 12 = 29 thousand (illustrative), which shows how spread out the values are.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.b] Percentages let you compare parts of different-sized wholes, but the method matters: dividing by the wrong number, or mixing up a percentage with a percentage point, gives a wrong answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.0] Percentage of a total = part ÷ total × 100. Illustrative: Town D's share of the six towns is 41 ÷ 157 × 100 = 26.1%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.1] Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100. A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.2] Always divide by the old (earlier) value, because the change is measured against where you started. Illustrative Country P, broadband households: 20% in 2000 and 90% in 2025, so the percentage change is (90 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 350%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.3] Percentage points: when both values are already percentages, the change is a difference in percentage points. A rise from 20% to 30% is a 10 percentage-point increase, but a 50% increase in the percentage change, (30 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.4] When a question asks by what percentage a percentage changed, the subtraction is the usual reading: give the answer in percentage points and add the percentage change if unsure. Use the percentage-change formula when the values are numbers rather than percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.5] Missing value X: parts of a whole add to 100% (pie chart or percentage table) or to a stated total (stacked bar, a bar split into parts, or a total row), so X = total minus the sum of the known parts.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.6] Illustrative Country Q: if all segments except 'other' total 85%, then other = 100 − 85 = 15%, because the parts of a pie chart must reach 100%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.7] To find a number from a percentage: number = percentage ÷ 100 × total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.b] Raw numbers are hard to compare between places of different size, so geographers use rates, densities and ratios. How you present the answer matters too.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.0] Rate per 1,000 = number ÷ population × 1,000, which lets places of different size be compared. A birth rate is births ÷ population × 1,000.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.1] Density = number ÷ area, given as people per km².
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.2] Ratio: compare two values as a ratio, e.g. 2 : 1. Illustrative: Town D (41) is more than three times the size of Town E (12), a ratio of about 3.4 : 1.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.3] 'Twice as much' means two times the other value; 'half' means divided by two.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.4] Round to the precision the data uses, or to one decimal place where needed, and keep the unit and the multiplier (thousands, millions) in every answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.5] In a calculation question, show the working: a correct answer with working is safe, and the working can earn marks if the answer slips.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.b] When you are asked to draw a graph from a small table of data, marks are given for each of these rules, so each one is worth doing even when the shape of the graph is easy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.0] Use graph paper, a sharp pencil and a ruler; draw the graph large enough to fill most of the space.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.1] The title says what the data is, where and when, taken from the wording of the question.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.2] The horizontal (x) axis shows categories or time; the vertical (y) axis shows values. Label both axes with units.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.3] The scale starts at zero, is evenly divided, and its interval is chosen so the largest value fits (e.g. steps of 5 or 10 units).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.4] On a line graph, time intervals are spaced in proportion to the years. If the data is for 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2015, the last gap is half the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.5] Plot every value accurately. Bars have equal widths and equal gaps; points are joined with ruled straight lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.6] Use a key or legend (a box saying what each colour or shading means) whenever more than one colour or shading is used, and shade or colour each category distinctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.b] Illustrative data: population of six towns, in thousands, from the totals block above. Following the rules in order shows how each mark is earned.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.0] Graph choice: a bar chart, because the values are one measure for separate towns.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.1] Title: 'Population of six towns (thousands), illustrative'.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.2] x-axis: 'Town', with Towns A to F in order.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.3] y-axis: 'Population (thousands)', from 0 to 45 in steps of 5, so the largest value (41) fits.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.4] Six bars of equal width with equal gaps, each drawn to its value: A 18, B 32, C 25, D 41, E 12, F 29.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.5] A single data series (one set of values) needs no key; check each bar top against the scale before finishing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.b] The question 'draw a suitable graph' is answered by naming the graph type, then giving one sentence of justification. The type depends on what the data is. Each example below is illustrative.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.0] Values for separate categories, e.g. the populations of six towns: bar chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.1] Change over time, e.g. the percentage of an urban population from 1980 to 2020: line graph (a bar chart is usually also accepted).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.2] Parts of a whole, or percentage shares adding to 100%, e.g. car sales by fuel type in one year: pie chart or divided bar.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.3] Two related variables for the same places, e.g. income and birth rate for twenty countries: scatter graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.4] Three components that add to 100%, e.g. sand, silt and clay at a soil site: triangular graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.5] Age and sex structure of a population: population pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.6] Monthly temperature and rainfall for one place: climate graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.7] Values for areas on a map, e.g. population density by region: choropleth map (a map with areas shaded by value).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.b] Bar charts show amounts for separate categories. They come in several forms, depending on whether you are comparing categories, groups within a category, or parts of a total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.0] Simple bar chart: separate categories, one bar per category, equal widths and equal gaps; vertical or horizontal.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.1] Multiple (clustered or comparative) bar chart: two or more bars side by side per category, e.g. males and females, or two years; a key is needed to tell the bars apart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.2] Compound (stacked or divided) bar chart: each bar is split into components, and the top of the whole bar is the total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.3] To read a component in a compound bar, take the top of its segment minus the bottom of its segment.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.4] Percentage compound bar: every bar reaches 100%, so the segments show shares rather than totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.5] Illustrative Region R, tree planting: total 2,000 hectares, of which conifers 1,350 ha. Broadleaf = 2,000 − 1,350 = 650 ha, a share of 650 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 32.5%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.b] A histogram looks like a bar chart but is used for continuous data, while a line graph is the standard way to show one variable changing over time.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.0] Histogram: like a bar chart but for continuous data grouped in classes (e.g. age groups or rainfall amounts), drawn with no gaps between bars because one class runs straight into the next.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.1] Line graph (trend graph): one variable over time; plot the points at the correct years and join them with ruled lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.2] Multiple line graph: two or more series on the same axes, with a key so each line can be identified.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.3] The gradient (steepness) of a line shows the rate of change: a steeper section means faster change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.4] Illustrative Country P, households with broadband: 2000 20%, 2005 38%, 2010 61%, 2015 74%, 2020 86%, 2025 90%. The rise is continuous; the fastest change is 2005 to 2010 (+23 percentage points), and the rise slows to +4 points from 2020 to 2025 as the line levels off.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.5] Illustrative Country P: the change from 2000 to 2025 is +70 percentage points, which is not the same as the 350% percentage change above, because one is a subtraction and the other is measured against the old value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.b] A pie chart shows how a whole is split into parts. Because a full circle is 360° and the parts add to 100%, each 1% takes up 3.6° of the circle.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.0] Pie chart: parts of a whole. Degrees = percentage × 3.6, because 360° ÷ 100 = 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.1] For raw numbers rather than percentages: degrees = value ÷ total × 360. Do not multiply raw numbers by 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.2] Drawing steps: draw a circle with a compass, draw a radius (a line from the centre to the edge) to 12 o'clock, then measure each angle clockwise with a protractor, largest segment first.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.3] Shade, key or label each segment with its percentage, and check that the angles add to 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.4] Illustrative Country Q, electricity sources (gas 40%, wind 25%, solar 10%, coal 5%, hydro 5%, other 15%): angles 144°, 90°, 36°, 18°, 18° and 54°, totalling 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.5] Two pie charts compare the shares in two years, not the totals, unless the circles are drawn in proportion to the totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.6] Divided (percentage) bar: a single bar split into shares that add to 100%, an alternative to a pie chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.b] A pictogram uses icons to stand for quantities. A population pyramid is a special graph showing how many people of each age and sex a country has.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.0] Pictogram: each icon stands for a fixed quantity stated in the key, so check the key first; a part-icon is a fraction of that quantity.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.1] Population pyramid: a back-to-back horizontal bar chart, which is a histogram on its side.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.2] Age groups, usually in 5-year bands, sit on the central vertical axis: 0 to 4 at the bottom and the oldest group at the top.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.3] Males are drawn on the left and females on the right. The horizontal axis shows the percentage (or number) of the population, not age, scaled outward from zero on both sides.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.4] Reading a pyramid: a wide base means a high birth rate; a narrow top means low life expectancy; a narrowing base means a falling birth rate; bulges and indentations suggest baby booms, migration, war or famine.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.5] Describe the dependent groups (the young and the old, who rely on others) and the working-age group when reading a pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.6] Drawing one band (illustrative, Country Z): males aged 20 to 24 are 3.5% and females 3.4%, so at the 20 to 24 level draw a bar 3.5 units long left of the centre axis and one 3.4 units long to the right.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.7] The shapes of pyramids and the stages of demographic transition (how a country's birth and death rates change over time) are taught fully elsewhere. There is more on this in Changing population characteristics.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.b] A climate graph combines two kinds of data for one place on a single set of axes: rainfall as bars and temperature as a line, across 12 months.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.0] Months J to D run along the bottom axis. Rainfall is shown as bars against a left-hand axis in mm.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.1] Mean (average) monthly temperature is shown as a line, plotted at the middle of each month, against a right-hand axis in °C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.2] From the graph, read the hottest and coldest months, the wettest and driest months, and the seasonal pattern (rain all year, or a wet and dry season).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.3] Calculations from the graph: annual temperature range = maximum mean monthly temperature minus minimum; mean annual temperature = sum of the 12 monthly means ÷ 12; total annual rainfall = sum of the 12 bars.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.4] Illustrative Station M, mean monthly temperature (°C) J to D: 5, 5, 7, 9, 12, 14, 16, 16, 14, 11, 8, 6. Rainfall (mm) J to D: 110, 80, 85, 60, 65, 70, 70, 85, 90, 115, 110, 120.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.5] Illustrative Station M results: warmest in July and August (16°C); coldest in January and February (5°C); annual range 16 − 5 = 11°C; mean annual temperature 123 ÷ 12 = 10.3°C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.6] Illustrative Station M: total rainfall 1,060 mm; wettest month December (120 mm); driest month April (60 mm). Rain falls in every month with a winter maximum, and the mild winters and cool summers resemble a cool temperate oceanic pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.7] Identifying a climate type from a climate graph is taught elsewhere. There is more on this in Physical regions and Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.b] A scatter graph tests whether two measurements for the same set of places are related, for example whether land value changes with distance from a city centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.0] Scatter graph: two variables for the same set of places. The independent variable (the one that may cause the change) goes on the x-axis; each place is plotted as a point, and the points are not joined.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.1] Draw a line of best fit by eye through the middle of the points, so it shows the general direction.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.2] Positive correlation: both variables rise together. Negative correlation: one rises as the other falls. No correlation: no clear pattern. Strength is described as strong or weak.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.3] Outliers, or anomalies, are points that lie away from the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.4] Correlation does not prove cause: two things can move together without one causing the other.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.5] Illustrative distance from a city centre and land value at eight sites: 1 km €900 per m²; 2 km €700; 3 km €620; 4 km €450; 5 km €400; 6 km €250; 7 km €260; 8 km €150.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.6] Illustrative: the result is a strong negative correlation, with the 7 km site a slight anomaly because it is higher than the 6 km site. A one-clause explanation: accessibility falls with distance from the centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.b] A triangular graph is used when a total is split into exactly three parts. Each point is placed using all three values at once.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.0] Triangular graph: shows three components that always total 100%, e.g. the percentage employed in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.1] Each side is a 0 to 100% scale. Read each component along the gridlines parallel to the side where its 0 is, following the direction in which its numbers run.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.2] Check that the three readings add to 100, which confirms you have read the scales correctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.3] To plot a point: find the first value on its scale, follow its gridline in, and mark where it meets the second value's gridline; check the point with the third value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.4] A point clustered near a corner is dominated by the component that corner represents.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.5] Illustrative employment structure: Country X is primary 10%, secondary 25%, tertiary 65%; it sits near the tertiary corner, typical of a developed economy. Country Y is primary 60%, secondary 15%, tertiary 25%; it sits near the primary corner, typical of a less developed economy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.b] Statistical maps put values onto a map so you can see how they vary from place to place. The choropleth is the main one; four other types each show a different kind of data.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.0] Choropleth map: areas (counties, regions or countries) shaded by value class; darker shading means a higher value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.1] Classes must not overlap and must cover every value, e.g. 0 to 49, 50 to 99, 100 to 149, 150 and over. A key is essential.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.2] Choropleth maps suit densities and percentages. Their weakness is that they imply the value is even across each area and hide variation inside it, and the boundaries look like sudden changes.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.3] Illustrative regional population densities (people per km²): North 35, East 160, South 85, West 40, Midlands 60. North and West are the lightest shade and East the darkest; the empty 100 to 149 class still appears in the key.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.4] Dot map: each dot stands for a fixed number, showing the distribution of a value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.5] Isoline map: lines join places of equal value, e.g. isobars, isotherms, isohyets or contours.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.6] Flow-line map: arrow width is proportional to the amount moving, e.g. migration, traffic or trade.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.7] Proportional symbols: circles or bars sized in proportion to the value, placed on the map.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.b] Sometimes a table is a better way to present data than a graph, and tables, graphs and maps all feed into the Geographical Investigation.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.0] Use a table rather than a graph when many exact values must be given. A table cannot show a trend at a glance.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.1] Graphs, maps and tables are expected in the presentation of results in the Geographical Investigation report. There is more on this in The Geographical Investigation (20%).
