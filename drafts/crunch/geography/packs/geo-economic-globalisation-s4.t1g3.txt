TARGET geo-economic-globalisation-s4: 4. How the global economy is organised
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.b] A basic processing unit is a factory carrying out one stage of production: processing a raw material, making a component (a part that goes into a finished product) or assembling a product. Multinational companies (MNCs, firms operating in many countries) place each stage in whichever country suits it best, which is why production is so widely spread.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.0] The Intel and Apple chains, such as those in 'One multinational company (MNC)', are examples of stages spread across several countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.1] Cheap labour suits labour-intensive assembly (work that needs many workers rather than machines), so firms put assembly where wages are low.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.2] Units are placed close to raw materials or to markets, which cuts the distance inputs or finished goods must travel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.3] A factory inside a trade bloc (a group of countries with shared trade rules) sells to the bloc's members without the barriers that apply to imports from outside.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.4] Tax breaks (reduced tax bills) and grants (money from government) lower the cost of operating in a country, so they attract factories.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.5] Falling transport and communication costs make distant factories practical, because a plant far away can still be supplied and managed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.6] Spreading production across many countries means that one disaster or strike does not stop all production, so the firm spreads its risk.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.b] Nike shows how far a firm can spread production: it designs and sells shoes but owns almost none of the factories that make them. Do not say Nike owns its Asian factories.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.0] Founded as Blue Ribbon Sports in 1964 by Phil Knight and Bill Bowerman; renamed Nike in 1971.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.1] Headquarters, design and marketing are in Beaverton, Oregon, USA, so the high-skill, high-value work stays at home.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.2] Footwear is made by independent contract factories (outside firms paid to make the product), mainly in Vietnam (the largest), China and Indonesia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.3] Nike sells its products worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.4] Sweatshop criticism in the 1990s (attacks on factories with very low pay and poor conditions), including of factories in Indonesia, led to factory monitoring and published supplier lists (lists of the factories Nike uses).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.b] Governments attract factories by creating special areas with easy trade rules. In an export processing zone, firms import components and export finished goods free of customs duty (a tax on goods crossing a border), and they receive tax breaks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.0] Shannon Free Zone, Co. Clare, set up in 1959, was one of the world's first export processing zones and Ireland's pioneer. The duty-free shop at Shannon Airport was 1947, so do not swap the dates.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.1] Shenzhen, China, became a Special Economic Zone (a region with special business rules to attract foreign firms) in 1980.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.2] Maquiladoras are assembly plants along Mexico's border with the USA, such as those at Tijuana and Ciudad Juárez, set up from 1965.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.3] A maquiladora imports components duty-free and exports finished goods to the USA, so it is an example of an export processing zone at work.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.b] Spreading production across many countries brings risks as well as savings. The costs fall on supply chains, workers and the environment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.0] Long supply chains (the linked stages from raw material to finished product) are vulnerable to disruption, as the 2021 chip shortage, the Ever Given blockage and the Red Sea diversions showed. There is more on this in 'Patterns in world trade'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.1] Branch plants (factories owned by a firm whose headquarters is elsewhere) close when costs change, and their workers lose jobs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.2] Some plants pay low wages and have poor conditions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.3] Moving components and goods over long distances has an environmental cost.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.4] Responses include reshoring (moving production back to the home country) and friend-shoring (moving it to politically allied countries).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.b] At world scale, regions play different roles in the global economy. The core is the wealthy, powerful centre; the periphery is the poorer edge that supplies it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.0] Core regions have capital (money to invest), MNC headquarters, research and development, skilled labour, good infrastructure and political power.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.1] The core's examples are the USA, western Europe's Blue Banana (Europe's core belt of dense development) and Japan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.2] The periphery supplies raw materials, food and cheap labour, with weak infrastructure and out-migration of people (people leaving to find work elsewhere).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.3] Most of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America and parts of South Asia are periphery.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.b] Some countries sit between the core and the periphery. They are changing their role in the global economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.0] The semi-periphery is made up of newly industrialised countries (NICs, countries that have recently built large manufacturing industries) moving towards the core.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.1] South Korea did so earlier; China, India, Brazil and Mexico are doing so now.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.b] The core did not become rich by accident. An early start, overseas empires and a self-reinforcing cycle of growth built its lead.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.0] Britain industrialised first, from the late 18th century (the Industrial Revolution), followed by western Europe and the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.1] Colonial empires supplied the core with raw materials and markets, and profits were reinvested in the core, which strengthened it further.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.2] Cumulative causation, a model by Gunnar Myrdal (1957), says success attracts more investment, people and services, which increases success.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.3] Backwash effects drain capital and skilled people from the periphery to the core, so the core grows at the periphery's expense.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.4] The Blue Banana runs from south-east England and London through the Benelux countries and the Rhine valley to northern Italy. There is more on this in 'Socio-economic regions'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.b] The periphery's economy was shaped by outside rulers for their benefit, which left it badly placed after independence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.0] Colonial economies were built around exporting one or two raw materials, so they had little other industry.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.1] Railways ran from mines and plantations to ports, rather than linking regions to each other, because their purpose was export.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.2] Borders were drawn by the colonisers, not by the people who lived there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.3] After independence these economies depended on falling commodity prices (the prices of raw materials), were burdened by debt and suffered conflict.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.b] Core and periphery are not fixed. Some places have moved up the ladder, and the North-South model is a simple picture of the pattern.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.0] Spread effects (the benefits of growth passing outwards from the core) and investment can raise peripheral regions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.1] The Asian Tigers and China moved from periphery to semi-periphery or core in about two generations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.2] Ireland moved from the European periphery to a core-like economy through MNC investment. There is more on this in 'Irish Regions - Core and Peripheral'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.3] The Brandt Line, from the Brandt Report of 1980, is a simple North-South model (a line dividing richer northern from poorer southern countries) of the same pattern.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.b] Sub-Saharan Africa is the main region excluded from world manufacturing. It has a very small share of world manufacturing and exports mostly unprocessed raw materials.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.0] Nigeria: crude oil is most of its exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.1] Zambia: copper is most of its exports.

[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.0] Poor infrastructure: roads and ports are inadequate and electricity is unreliable, which makes running a factory costly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.1] Landlocked countries (with no coast): Africa has 16, such as Chad, Niger, Mali and South Sudan, and they face high transport costs to reach ports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.2] Political instability, conflict and corruption scare off investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.3] Low levels of education and skills, and poor health, limit the workforce.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.4] Small, poor home markets give firms little reason to produce locally.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.5] Heavy debt absorbs money that could fund development.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.6] The colonial legacy: economies were set up to export raw materials, and that pattern persists.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.7] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials, so processing at home does not pay; coffee beans enter rich markets with low tariffs, while roasted coffee faces higher ones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.b] Several measures try to help excluded regions join world trade and industry. They target debt, trade barriers and infrastructure.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.0] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative, begun in 1996, gives debt relief (cancelling or reducing debts) through the World Bank and IMF.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.1] The African Continental Free Trade Area, which removes trade barriers between African countries, began trading on 1 January 2021.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.2] Chinese and other foreign investment is building infrastructure.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.3] Fair trade (a system that pays producers a fairer price). There is more on this in 'Patterns in world trade'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.b] Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, MNCs, technology and the movement of people and ideas. It has changed what is traded, who trades and how goods move.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.0] About 80% of world trade is linked to the international production networks of MNCs (UNCTAD, 2013).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.1] Much of it is intra-firm trade, between parts of the same company or with its suppliers in global value chains (production split into stages across countries).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.2] Trade is increasingly in components rather than finished goods, because each stage of production crosses borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.3] Trade in services and digital trade has grown rapidly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.4] New trading powers, led by China, have entered world trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.5] The WTO (World Trade Organization) and trade blocs have lowered barriers, so goods cross borders more easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.6] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard steel boxes) and huge ports have cut the cost of shipping, so distant trade pays.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.7] E-commerce (online selling) brings small sellers into world markets, so they can reach buyers abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.b] Globalisation does not benefit everyone equally. Some groups and regions gain, others lose.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.0] Consumers gain cheaper goods, because production moves to low-cost countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.1] NICs and China gain jobs and growth, because factories locate there.

[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.3] Excluded regions fall further behind, because they get little of the new investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.4] Workers in low-wage assembly can face poor and unsafe conditions, as the Rana Plaza collapse showed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.5] Transporting goods over long distances has environmental costs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.b] Since 2018 some governments have pushed back against open trade, raising barriers between major economies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.0] The US-China trade war from 2018, the US tariffs of 2025 and Brexit have raised barriers between major economies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.1] Governments are reshoring strategic industries such as chips (the microchips in electronics), bringing production back home so they do not depend on distant suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.2] Supply-chain worries grew after COVID-19.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.b] India's economy was tightly controlled before 1991, and it opened to globalisation only after a crisis.

[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.1] Protection (tariffs and rules that shut out imports) shielded Indian industries from foreign competition.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.2] The Licence Raj: firms needed government licences to produce and expand, which limited competition and growth.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.3] A balance-of-payments crisis (when a country cannot pay for its imports and debts) in 1991 forced change.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.4] Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh removed most licences, cut tariffs and opened India to foreign investment.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s4: 4. Global issues and a justice perspective
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.b] Global issues are problems that cross borders and affect the whole world, such as poverty, inequality, trade rules, debt, climate change and migration. A justice perspective asks whether the distribution of wealth and power is fair, who decides, and who benefits. It treats development as a matter of rights, not charity.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.0] The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948, includes rights to food, health, education and decent work.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.1] Poverty denies these rights, so a justice perspective treats poverty as a rights failure rather than a matter of charity. In other words, people are owed these things, not given them as a favour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.b] Justice questions start from the scale of the problem. The figures below show how many people are extremely poor, where they live, and how unevenly wealth is shared.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.0] About 700 million people lived in extreme poverty in 2022, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa (the part of Africa south of the Sahara). The World Bank, the UN-linked development lender, measures extreme poverty as living on under 2.15 US dollars a day (2017 prices).
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.1] The share of the world's population in extreme poverty fell from over a third in 1990 to under a tenth by 2019, so the trend is strongly downward even though the numbers remain large.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.2] The richest 10% own about 76% of global wealth, while the poorest half own about 2% (World Inequality Report 2022). This shows how unequal the sharing of wealth is.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.b] The UN has set global targets to measure progress against poverty and injustice. The first set ran from 2000 to 2015 and the second runs to 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.0] The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000-2015) had eight goals. The target of halving the share of people in extreme poverty from 1990 was met by 2010, five years early, largely thanks to China.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.1] The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were adopted by the UN in September 2015. They have 17 goals, to be met by 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.2] The SDGs target poverty (SDG 1), hunger (SDG 2), quality education (SDG 4), gender equality (SDG 5), reduced inequalities (SDG 10) and climate action (SDG 13). Other targets cover health, clean water and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.3] The world is off track to meet most SDG targets by 2030, so shortfalls remain alongside the progress.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.b] Trade rules and prices can leave poor countries poorer. Many depend on selling raw materials, and the biggest profits are made further along the chain, in rich countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.0] Many developing countries depend on exporting a few primary commodities (raw materials such as coffee, cocoa, cotton and minerals), whose prices are low and unstable. This makes their income hard to plan around.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.1] The terms of trade (the amount of imports a country's exports can buy) tend to move against these countries. Their exports buy fewer imports over time, while they import manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.2] Most of the final price of coffee, chocolate or clothing goes to processors, brands and retailers in rich countries. The grower, at the start of the value chain (the steps from raw material to finished product), receives a small share.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.3] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials. This discourages poor countries from processing their own crops, so they stay stuck exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.b] Rich countries also help their own farmers with money, and this can push down the prices poor farmers receive. Cotton is the clearest example.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.0] Rich-country farm subsidies (government payments to farmers), such as US cotton subsidies and the EU's Common Agricultural Policy in the past, let surpluses be sold cheaply abroad. This is called dumping, and it undercuts poor farmers who cannot match the low price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.1] The Cotton-4 West African producers, Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad and Mali, were harmed by these subsidies.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.2] The WTO (World Trade Organization, the body that rules on trade disputes) ruled in Brazil's favour against US cotton subsidies in 2004-05.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.b] Governments and trade bodies have tried to make trade fairer for poor countries, with mixed success. Fair trade is a third response, because it works through consumers rather than governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.0] The WTO Doha Development Round (a set of global trade talks meant to help developing countries) was launched in 2001 but has stalled without agreement.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.1] The EU's 'Everything But Arms' scheme (2001) gives the least developed countries duty-free (no tariffs) and quota-free (no limit on amounts) access to the EU for all products except arms and ammunition.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.b] Fair trade is a trading model in which producers receive guaranteed terms and the Fairtrade Mark identifies certified products. It tries to give growers a bigger and steadier share than the normal market does.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.0] The Fairtrade Minimum Price covers the cost of sustainable production. It is a guaranteed floor, not a guaranteed higher price: when the market price is higher, producers receive the market price plus the premium.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.1] The Fairtrade Premium is paid to producer co-operatives (groups of farmers who work together and share the benefits) for community projects such as schools, clinics and clean water.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.2] Fair trade also requires long-term contracts, decent working conditions, no child labour, environmental standards and democratically run co-operatives.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.3] Fair trade covers coffee, tea, cocoa, bananas, sugar and cotton, for example, which are the kinds of crops whose growers get a small share of the price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.4] The first fair trade label, Max Havelaar, was launched in the Netherlands in 1988 for Mexican coffee. Fairtrade International now coordinates the Fairtrade Mark.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.b] Ireland has its own fair trade links, but fair trade has weaknesses that an evaluation should include.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.0] Fairtrade Ireland (Fairtrade Mark Ireland) licenses the Fairtrade Mark in Ireland, meaning it gives permission for products to carry it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.1] Irish towns and schools can apply for Fairtrade status. Fairtrade Fortnight takes place each year in late February and March.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.2] Limit: fair trade makes up only a tiny share of world trade, so it reaches few producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.3] Limit: certified products can cost consumers more, and certification is expensive for small producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.4] Limit: fair trade does not change the trade rules that cause unfair trade in the first place, so it treats the symptoms rather than the cause.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.b] In the 1970s and 1980s, cheap lending turned into a debt crisis for many developing countries. Each step below made repayment harder.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.0] 1. When oil prices rose sharply, oil-exporting countries deposited their surplus money in banks. Banks lent these deposits, called petrodollars, to developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.1] 2. Interest rates then rose sharply and commodity prices fell. Borrowers earned less from their exports while owing more, so many could not afford repayments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.2] 3. Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts, starting the Latin American debt crisis.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.3] 4. Many African and Latin American countries could not repay their debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.b] Heavy debt took money away from the people it was meant to help, and in some cases the people repaying had never benefited from the loan.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.0] Governments spent more on debt repayments than on health and education, so public services suffered.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.1] Countries took new loans to repay old ones, so the debt grew.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.2] The 1980s 'lost decade' hit Latin America and Africa hard, because debt blocked progress in those years.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.3] Some debts were 'odious': dictators borrowed the money and stole or wasted it, yet the people were made to repay it. This is the clearest justice argument against debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.b] Lenders did not simply hand over money. The IMF (International Monetary Fund) and the World Bank attached conditions to their loans, which is different from tied aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.0] IMF and World Bank loans came with conditions. These cut public spending, introduced school and health fees, privatised state firms (sold them to private owners), removed food subsidies, devalued the currency (lowered its value against others) and opened markets to imports.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.1] Structural adjustment programmes, as these packages of conditions were called, were criticised for hurting the poorest people most. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.b] Campaigns and lenders eventually cancelled some debt for the poorest countries, with conditions. Relief had real results, but debt is building up again.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.0] The Jubilee 2000 campaign pressed for debt cancellation by the millennium, so that poor countries could stop repaying old debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.1] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative was launched in 1996 by the IMF and World Bank.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.2] The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005) followed the G8 summit (a meeting of leaders of major rich countries) at Gleneagles, Scotland, in July 2005. It was backed by the Make Poverty History campaign and the Live 8 concerts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.3] More than 30 countries, most in Africa, have received debt relief under HIPC, on condition that the savings go to poverty reduction.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.4] Relief has funded results such as free primary education in some relieved countries, so more children are in school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.5] Debt is rising again in the 2020s, so relief has not ended the problem.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.b] Aid is help given to poorer countries. It comes in different forms, depending on its purpose, who gives it and how it is channelled.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.0] Emergency (humanitarian) aid responds to disasters and famines, so it is short-term and urgent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.1] Long-term development aid funds projects that build lasting capacity, such as a country's ability to meet its own needs.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.2] Bilateral aid passes from one government to another, so the donor government decides directly where it goes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.3] Multilateral aid is channelled through bodies such as the UN, the World Bank and the EU, so several donors pool their money.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.4] NGO (voluntary) aid is given by non-governmental organisations, which are charities independent of governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.5] Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, so it benefits the donor as well as the receiver.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.b] The UN has set a target for how much rich countries should give, and most do not meet it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.0] The UN target, set in 1970, is that rich countries give 0.7% of their gross national income (the total income earned by a country's people and firms) as official development aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.1] Norway, Sweden, Luxembourg and Denmark have reached or exceeded the target in recent years, so only a few countries reach it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.2] Ireland has not yet reached the target (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.b] Aid is neither all good nor all bad. A balanced answer gives both sides, with reasons, and ends with what better aid looks like.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.0] Helps: aid saves lives in emergencies and funds schools, clinics, wells and vaccination programmes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.1] Helps: aid builds roads and infrastructure, trains teachers, nurses and farmers, and supports women's groups and small businesses.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.2] Hinders: tied aid benefits donor firms and may supply unsuitable technology, because the receiver must buy what the donor sells.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.3] Hinders: corrupt elites can steal aid, and food aid can undercut local farmers, who cannot compete with free food.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.4] Hinders: aid can create dependency instead of self-reliance, and loans add to debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.5] Hinders: donors' political or strategic interests decide who gets aid, rather than need.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.6] Hinders: large prestige projects, such as dams, can displace people.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.7] Better aid is untied (not required to be spent in the donor country), targeted at the poorest, and designed with local participation and ownership. It supports self-help and co-operatives and is transparent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.b] Ireland gives aid through its government programme and through voluntary organisations, many of them founded in response to particular crises.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.0] Irish Aid is Ireland's official aid programme, part of the Department of Foreign Affairs. Its bilateral programme (government to government) began in 1974.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.1] Long-term partner countries include Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia in sub-Saharan Africa, and Vietnam.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.2] Ireland's aid is untied. 'A Better World' (2019), Ireland's international development policy, focuses on the furthest behind, gender equality, climate and governance.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.3] Concern Worldwide was founded in 1968 in response to the famine in Biafra during Nigeria's civil war.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.4] Trócaire was founded in 1973 by the Irish Catholic bishops. Its Lenten 'Trócaire box' raises funds, and it campaigns on human rights and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.5] GOAL was founded in 1977 by John O'Shea.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.6] Live Aid concerts in July 1985, organised by Bob Geldof, responded to the 1984-85 Ethiopian famine.
[geo-economic-development-s4.16.b] Ireland's past gives it a reason to care about hunger elsewhere.
[geo-economic-development-s4.16.0] The Great Famine (1845-49) killed about 1 million people and led about 1 million to emigrate. This history is one reason for Irish solidarity (support and fellow feeling) with developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.b] Multinationals (companies operating in many countries) move work to where labour is cheapest. This brings jobs, but it can also bring exploitation.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.0] Multinationals locate labour-intensive work (work needing many workers), such as clothing, electronics assembly and mining, in low-wage countries, because wage costs are lowest there.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.1] Benefits for workers include jobs, wages higher than farm work, and skills.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.2] Exploitation includes very low wages, long hours, unsafe factories, banned trade unions (organisations of workers) and child labour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.b] The collapse of Rana Plaza shows what can happen when global brands rely on cheap suppliers whose safety standards are not checked.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.0] Rana Plaza, an eight-storey building in Savar near Dhaka, housed garment factories. It collapsed on 24 April 2013, killing about 1,130 people and injuring about 2,500.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.1] Cracks had appeared the day before, but workers were ordered back in.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.2] The factories supplied Western brands, including Primark, which was founded in Dublin in 1969 as Penneys. Primark paid compensation to the victims.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.3] The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh was signed in May 2013 by global brands and trade unions, and eventually more than 200 brands joined. It provided independent factory inspections.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.4] Garments make up over four-fifths of Bangladesh's exports (2020s), so the country depends heavily on this industry.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.5] The case shows how brands profit from low-cost factories while the safety of workers depends on local suppliers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.b] Child labour is one of the worst forms of exploitation, and it appears in the supply chains of everyday products.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.0] About 160 million children were in child labour worldwide in 2020 (ILO and UNICEF), about 70% of them in agriculture.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.1] Child labour occurs in cocoa farming and in mining, for example, so everyday products such as chocolate and batteries can be linked to it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.2] The DR Congo produces about 70% of the world's cobalt, which is used in rechargeable batteries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.3] Amnesty International (2016) documented child labour in the artisanal (hand-dug) cobalt mines of the DR Congo.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.b] Exploitation also involves land. Poor countries and poor communities can lose the land they depend on.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.0] Foreign companies and governments buy or lease large areas of farmland in poor countries, a practice called land grabbing.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.1] Indigenous peoples (the original inhabitants of a region) lose land to mining, ranching and logging, as in the Amazon example in the Brazil section of Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.b] Pressure to improve conditions comes from consumers, from the law and from workers themselves.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.0] Ethical consumer campaigns and company codes of conduct (a company's own rules for how suppliers must treat workers) put pressure on brands.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.1] The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, adopted in 2024, requires very large companies to identify and address human-rights and environmental harm in their supply chains.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.2] Trade unions represent workers and campaign for safer conditions and better wages.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.b] Climate change is an injustice because the countries that caused it least suffer most from it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.0] The poorest countries have contributed least to greenhouse gas emissions (the gases that cause global warming) but suffer most from climate change.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.1] Bangladesh is a low-lying delta vulnerable to cyclones, floods and sea-level rise, because so much of its land is barely above the sea.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.2] Small island states such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives are threatened by sea-level rise, because their land is low.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.3] Sub-Saharan Africa has produced only a small share of historical emissions, yet its dry regions suffer drought.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.4] Responses include climate finance (money from rich countries to help poorer ones cope) and the Loss and Damage Fund, agreed at COP27 in Sharm el-Sheikh, Egypt (2022) and made operational at COP28 in Dubai (2023).
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.5] The Mary Robinson Foundation - Climate Justice was set up in 2010 by Mary Robinson, who was President of Ireland from 1990 to 1997 and UN High Commissioner for Human Rights from 1997 to 2002.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.b] A justice evaluation weighs real progress against the problems that remain.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.0] Extreme poverty has fallen greatly since 1990, especially in China. Child mortality has fallen, and more children attend school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.1] Sub-Saharan Africa lags behind other regions.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.2] Inequality within and between countries is high, so gains are not shared equally.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.3] Conflict, such as the war in Sudan, and climate change are reversing gains. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.4] The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 caused the first rise in global extreme poverty in about a generation (World Bank).
[geo-economic-development-s4.24.b] Justice is not only for governments. Individuals and Ireland can act.
[geo-economic-development-s4.24.0] Individuals can buy fair trade, campaign, and support NGOs. Ireland can press governments on aid, trade and debt policy.
