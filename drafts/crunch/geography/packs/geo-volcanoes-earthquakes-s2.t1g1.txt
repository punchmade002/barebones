TARGET geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2: 2. The geography of earthquakes
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.b] An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of stress (the squeezing, stretching or shearing force) built up in the crust, the Earth's rocky outer layer. It happens in four steps, which together make a repeating cycle.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.0] 1. Plates (the huge slabs of crust) moving past, towards or away from each other lock together by friction, because their rough edges grip and cannot slide.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.1] 2. Stress builds up in the rocks around the locked boundary, because the plates keep moving while the edges stay stuck.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.2] 3. When the stress exceeds the strength of the rock, the rock fractures or slips suddenly along a fault (a crack in the crust along which rock can move).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.3] 4. The stored energy is released as seismic waves, which are vibrations that travel through the Earth and shake the ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.4] This cycle of locking, stress build-up and sudden release is called elastic rebound: the rock stores energy like a bent stick, then snaps back.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.5] Foreshocks are smaller tremors before the main shock. Aftershocks are tremors after it, and can bring down buildings already weakened by the main shock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.b] Four key terms describe where an earthquake starts and how its energy spreads. Learn which is underground and which is on the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.0] Focus (hypocentre): the point inside the crust where the rupture begins, so it is always underground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.1] Epicentre: the point on the surface directly above the focus, where shaking is usually strongest.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.2] Fault (fault line): the fracture along which the rocks move.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.3] Seismic (shock) waves: waves of energy spreading out from the focus in all directions.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.4] Diagram: draw a cross-section showing the fault, the focus below ground, seismic waves spreading out, and the epicentre on the surface directly above the focus.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.5] Depth matters: a shallow-focus earthquake causes more surface damage than a deep-focus one of the same magnitude (the energy released), because the waves have less rock to travel through before reaching the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.b] Seismic waves travel at different speeds and in different ways, so they arrive at a recording station one after another. Each type has a different effect.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.0] P (primary) waves are the fastest seismic waves, so they arrive first.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.1] S (secondary) waves are slower than P waves and arrive second.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.2] Surface waves travel along the surface, arrive last and cause most of the damage, because they shake the ground and the buildings on it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.b] Scientists can record seismic waves with an instrument and use the records from several places to pinpoint where an earthquake struck.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.0] A seismograph (seismometer) is the instrument that detects and records seismic waves; the record it produces is a seismogram.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.1] A barometer measures air pressure and is not an earthquake instrument.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.2] Seismic stations (places where seismographs are installed) record the arrival times of P and S waves. The gap between the two arrivals shows the distance to the earthquake, because the waves travel at different speeds.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.3] Three or more stations locate the epicentre by triangulation: each station gives a distance, and the epicentre is where the three distances meet.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.b] Earthquakes are measured in two different ways: how much energy they release, and how much damage they do at a particular place. Two different scales do these jobs.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.0] The Richter scale measures magnitude, the energy released, from the size of the waves on a seismogram.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.1] The Richter scale is logarithmic (each step is a multiplication, not an addition): each whole number up means ten times the wave amplitude (the height of the wave) and about 30 times more energy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.2] The moment magnitude scale is the modern replacement for large earthquakes. Either scale is an accepted answer for a scale that measures magnitude.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.3] The modified Mercalli scale measures intensity, the observed effects and damage at a place, on a scale of I to XII.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.4] Intensity varies from place to place for one earthquake, because shaking is weaker far from the epicentre; magnitude does not vary, because it is the energy released at the source.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.5.b] Most earthquakes occur in narrow belts along plate boundaries, the lines where plates meet, because that is where plates lock and then slip. Each boundary type produces a different kind of earthquake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.5.0] The Pacific Ring of Fire, the horseshoe of plate boundaries around the Pacific Ocean, has about 90% of the world's earthquakes, including most of the most powerful ones.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.5.1] Ireland lies in the middle of the Eurasian plate, far from a boundary, so it has only rare, minor tremors.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.6.b] Where plates separate, the crust is pulled apart and rocks fracture.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.6.0] Earthquakes are frequent, shallow and generally weaker, because the rock is stretched and breaks before much stress can build.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.6.1] They occur along mid-ocean ridges (underwater mountain chains where plates move apart) and in Iceland, which lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.7.b] Where one plate is subducted (forced down) beneath another, the sinking plate generates earthquakes at a range of depths.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.7.0] These are the most powerful earthquakes; foci range from shallow to very deep along the sinking plate.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.7.1] Undersea earthquakes at subduction zones (where one plate sinks under another) can cause tsunamis, which are giant sea waves.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.7.2] Examples: Japan; Chile; Sumatra, Indonesia.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.8.b] Where continental plates collide, neither sinks easily, so rocks are crushed and folded into fold mountains (mountain ranges formed by crumpled rock).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.8.0] Collision boundaries produce powerful, shallow earthquakes in fold-mountain belts, such as the Himalayas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.8.1] Nepal, 25 April 2015: magnitude 7.8, from the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates. About 9,000 people died. Kathmandu was severely damaged, and the earthquake triggered avalanches on Mount Everest.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.b] At transform (conservative) boundaries plates slide past each other. The plates stick, then slip suddenly, which is the locking and release of elastic rebound.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.0] These earthquakes are shallow and often very damaging.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.1] San Andreas Fault, California: the Pacific plate and North American plate slide past each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.2] San Francisco, 18 April 1906: magnitude about 7.8, with about 3,000 deaths. Fires from broken gas mains burned for days and destroyed much of the city.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.3] Loma Prieta, 1989: magnitude 6.9, near San Francisco, on the San Andreas system.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.4] East Anatolian Fault, Turkey, a transform fault: on 6 February 2023 a magnitude 7.8 earthquake killed over 50,000 people in Turkey and Syria, and many apartment blocks collapsed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.10.b] Primary effects are caused directly by the shaking. They happen during the earthquake itself.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.10.0] Building and bridge collapse, deaths and injuries, and ground rupture (the ground splitting open along the fault).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.10.1] Broken gas, water and power lines, and damaged roads and railways.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.b] Secondary effects follow from the shaking, as knock-on hazards set off by the damage it causes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.0] Fires from broken gas mains and power lines, because leaking gas and sparks ignite easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.1] Tsunamis: the Tōhoku earthquake of 11 March 2011, magnitude 9.0 off north-east Japan, sent a tsunami that overtopped sea walls. About 18,000 people were killed or are missing.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.2] That tsunami also caused meltdowns at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.3] Landslides, as shaking brings down loose slopes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.4] Liquefaction: saturated soft sediment (soil full of water) behaves like a liquid, so buildings sink or topple.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.5] Christchurch, New Zealand, 22 February 2011: magnitude 6.3, 185 deaths, widespread liquefaction, and the cathedral spire collapsed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.6] Disease from contaminated water, homelessness and economic losses.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.b] Two earthquakes of the same magnitude can do very different damage. The outcome depends on the earthquake itself, on the ground, and on the people and buildings in its path.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.0] Magnitude: larger earthquakes release more energy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.1] Depth of focus: a shallow-focus earthquake causes more surface damage than a deep-focus one of the same magnitude.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.2] Distance from the epicentre.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.3] Rock and soil type: soft sediment shakes more and can liquefy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.4] Population density, and the time of day.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.5] Building quality and building codes (the rules a building must follow).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.6] Level of development and preparedness: wealth to prepare, respond and rebuild.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.7] Haiti, 12 January 2010: magnitude 7.0 near Port-au-Prince. Poorly built concrete buildings collapsed, and the country, a poor one, had little capacity to respond. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, all over 100,000.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.b] Kobe shows how a moderate-magnitude earthquake with a shallow focus, close to a big city, can be devastating. Begin a case-study answer with the date, place, plate setting and magnitude.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.0] Place: the Great Hanshin earthquake, Kobe, Japan, 17 January 1995. Its epicentre was near the northern tip of Awaji Island, and its focus was shallow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.1] Magnitude 6.9 on the moment magnitude scale.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.2] Kobe lies near where the Philippine Sea plate is subducted beneath the Eurasian plate. The rupture was on a fault close to the city.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.b] The shallow focus and the closeness of the fault to the city meant strong shaking in a built-up area, and weak points in the city's buildings and ground showed up quickly.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.0] Elevated sections of the Hanshin Expressway toppled over, cutting a major road route.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.1] Many older wooden houses with heavy tiled roofs collapsed, because the heavy roofs sat on weak frames.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.2] Fires from broken gas mains burned large areas, because the shaking broke the pipes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.3] Liquefaction badly damaged the port, which was built on reclaimed land (land made by filling in the sea, so the ground is loose and wet).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.4] About 6,400 people died, and about 300,000 were left homeless.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.5] Response: the government faced criticism for a slow response. Stricter building codes and retrofitting (strengthening existing buildings) followed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.15.b] This was an undersea earthquake at a subduction zone, and the seabed movement triggered a tsunami.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.15.0] Place: off the west coast of northern Sumatra, Indonesia. Magnitude 9.1.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.15.1] The Indo-Australian plate is subducted beneath the Burma plate. The seabed was thrust upwards, displacing the ocean and causing a tsunami.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.15.2] There was no tsunami warning system in the Indian Ocean at the time, so coastal people had no advance alert.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.16.b] The tsunami crossed the ocean and struck many countries, which is why the death toll was so high.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.16.0] About 230,000 people died in 14 countries.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.16.1] The worst-hit area was Banda Aceh, Sumatra. Sri Lanka, India and Thailand were also badly hit.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.16.2] Response: an Indian Ocean tsunami warning system was set up afterwards, operational from 2006.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.b] Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely: their exact time, place and magnitude are not known. Scientists identify where they are likely and estimate how often they occur.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.0] Mapping active faults and plate boundaries, to show where earthquakes are likely.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.1] Studying the history and frequency of past earthquakes, to estimate how often they occur.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.2] Seismic gap theory: a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time has the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.3] Monitoring foreshocks, ground deformation (GPS, tiltmeters and laser measurements across faults), radon gas release and changes in well-water levels. Tiltmeters measure small changes in the slope of the ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.4] Unusual animal behaviour is anecdotal (based on reports, not tested evidence) and unreliable as a warning.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.18.b] Three examples show how prediction and warning have worked, failed, and led to legal trouble.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.18.0] Haicheng, China, 4 February 1975: after foreshocks, the city was evacuated hours before a magnitude 7.3 earthquake, saving many lives.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.18.1] Tangshan, China, 28 July 1976: no warning was given, and the official death toll was about 240,000.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.18.2] L'Aquila, Italy, 6 April 2009: magnitude 6.3, about 300 deaths. In 2012 six scientists and an official were convicted of manslaughter for reassuring the public before the quake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.18.3] The scientists were acquitted on appeal in 2014.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.b] Prediction cannot give a time, but warning systems can give a short notice once an earthquake has started or a tsunami has been triggered.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.0] Early-warning systems detect the fast P waves, giving seconds of warning before the destructive waves arrive, because P waves outrun the surface waves.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.1] Those seconds are enough to stop trains, shut off gas supplies and take cover.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.2] Japan has a national earthquake early-warning system.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.3] Tsunami warning systems use seabed pressure sensors and buoys, so that a tsunami can be detected at sea, as in the Indian Ocean system set up after 2004.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.b] Buildings can be designed to survive shaking, which limits deaths and damage. Each measure either stops a building from being shaken or lets it move without breaking.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.0] Deep foundations anchored to bedrock (solid rock below the soil), so the building does not sit on soft ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.1] Base isolation: rubber and steel bearings or rollers under the building, so the ground moves beneath it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.2] Flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames that sway rather than crack, with cross-bracing and shear walls (extra supports that stiffen the structure).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.3] Counterweights (tuned mass dampers) in tall buildings, which swing against the shaking. Taipei 101, Taiwan, has a giant tuned mass damper, a steel ball hung near the top.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.4] Automatic shut-off valves on gas mains, and flexible joints on pipes, so pipes bend rather than break and gas does not leak.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.5] Retrofitting older buildings, which means strengthening them to modern standards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.b] Preparing people and places before an earthquake reduces harm even when the earthquake itself cannot be stopped or predicted.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.0] Strict building codes, so that new buildings can survive shaking, and land-use planning that avoids soft ground (which shakes more and can liquefy) and fault lines.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.1] Emergency drills, education and emergency supplies.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.2] Tsunami walls and evacuation routes, so that people near the coast can get to safety, for example in Japan, where the Tōhoku tsunami overtopped sea walls.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.3] Japan holds national Disaster Prevention Day drills each 1 September, the anniversary of the 1923 Great Kanto earthquake.

CANDIDATES geo-plate-tectonics-s3: 3. Plate boundaries as zones of crustal construction and destruction
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.b] Plates meet in only three ways: they move apart, move together or slide past each other. Each way has a name, and exams use all the synonyms, so learn them together.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.0] Constructive (also called divergent or separating) boundaries: plates move apart and crust is created, because magma fills the gap.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.1] Destructive (also called convergent or colliding) boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, because one plate sinks into the mantle, or modified where two continental plates collide.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.2] Passive (also called conservative or transform) boundaries: plates slide past each other and crust is conserved, meaning it is neither created nor destroyed, so no magma is involved.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.b] At constructive boundaries plates move apart, and new crust is formed in the gap. The process runs in three steps, driven by the mantle (the hot layer beneath the crust).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.0] 1. Convection currents (slow circulating flows in the hot mantle) carry the plates apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.1] 2. Pressure drops as the plates separate, so the mantle partly melts, producing magma (molten rock).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.2] 3. Magma rises into the gap and cools to form new basaltic oceanic crust, basalt being a dark, runny volcanic rock.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.b] Where the plates separate under the ocean, the new crust builds up a long underwater mountain chain called a mid-ocean ridge. This is the main oceanic example of a constructive boundary.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.0] A rift valley (a long valley formed where the crust splits and drops) runs along the crest of the ridge, with frequent shallow earthquakes (earthquakes starting close to the surface) and volcanic activity.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.1] Transform faults (cracks where plates slide past each other) cut across the ridge, so it is offset in sections.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.2] Where a mid-ocean ridge rises above sea level it forms volcanic islands, such as Iceland.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.3] The Mid-Atlantic Ridge also separates the South American and African plates in the South Atlantic.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.4] Iceland is a volcanic island on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the rift is visible on land at Þingvellir (Thingvellir).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.5] The Azores are also on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the East Pacific Rise is another mid-ocean ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.6] Surtsey, a new volcanic island, formed off the south coast of Iceland in an eruption that began in 1963.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.7] Diagram: cross-section of a ridge showing convection currents, rising magma, the rift valley and arrows showing the plates moving apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.b] Constructive boundaries can also start under a continent. Here the land is pulled apart instead of the sea floor, and if the process continues a new ocean can open.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.0] Where a continent starts to split, the crust stretches and a block drops between parallel faults (cracks in the crust) to form a rift valley, with volcanoes nearby.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.1] The East African Rift Valley is where the African plate is splitting apart; Mount Kilimanjaro, Tanzania, is a volcano associated with it.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.3.2] If spreading continues, the sea floods in and a new ocean begins; the Red Sea, between Africa and the Arabian plate, is at this stage.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.b] These are the landforms and activity to name when asked what is found at a constructive boundary. These features are found where plates pull apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.0] Features of constructive boundaries, where plates pull apart, are the mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, volcanic islands, fissure eruptions (eruptions from long cracks rather than one vent) of runny basalt lava, and shallow earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.b] Where two plates converge, one plate is forced down into the mantle. Which one sinks depends on the density of the crust.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.0] Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so where the two meet the oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.1] This process is subduction, and the zone where the plate sinks is the subduction zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.2] Crust is destroyed as the subducting plate melts in the hot mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.3] The place where the two plates meet at the surface is an ocean trench, a deep, narrow valley in the sea floor.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.b] When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks. This one collision produces a trench, fold mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes, which is why such coasts are so active.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.0] An ocean trench forms where the oceanic plate bends down, because the plate is forced into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.1] Sediments (loose material laid down on the sea floor) are scraped off the subducting plate and crumpled against the continent into fold mountains.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.2] Melting of the sinking plate produces magma that rises to form a chain of volcanoes on the continent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.3] Earthquakes occur along the sinking plate because it grinds past the rock around it, shallow near the trench and deeper inland; this inclined zone is the Benioff zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.4] The Nazca plate subducts beneath the South American plate: the Peru-Chile Trench lies offshore and the Andes, with many volcanoes, rise on land.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.5] The Juan de Fuca plate subducts beneath the North American plate, producing the Cascade Range volcanoes, including Mount St Helens, Washington, USA.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.6] Diagram: cross-section with ocean trench, subducting plate, melting zone, rising magma, volcano, fold mountains and earthquake foci (the points underground where earthquakes start).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.b] When two oceanic plates meet, neither is light enough to resist sinking, so one is forced under the other. The result is a trench and a curved line of volcanic islands rather than a mountain chain on a continent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.0] The older, denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the other, because denser crust sinks more easily.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.1] An ocean trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands, called an island arc, form, and earthquakes are frequent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.2] The Mariana Trench, about 11 km deep, is the deepest place on earth; it lies where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the Philippine plate, and the Mariana Islands form an island arc.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.3] The Aleutian Islands, Alaska, are an island arc where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the North American plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.4] Japan lies where the Pacific, Philippine, Eurasian and North American plates meet.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.b] Continental crust is too light to sink into the mantle, so when two continents meet there is no subduction. The crust has nowhere to go but up.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.0] Both plates are too light to subduct, so the crust between them, including sediments from the ocean that once separated them, is crushed, folded and pushed up into high fold mountains.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.1] Earthquakes are common, but there is little or no volcanic activity, because no plate sinks and melts to make magma.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.2] Crust is modified, thickened and folded, rather than destroyed, because it is too light to sink.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.3] The Indo-Australian plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate, forming the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau; the collision began about 50 million years ago and continues.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.4] Mount Everest, 8,849 m, is the highest mountain on earth; for example, marine fossils in limestone near its summit show the rocks were once on the floor of the Tethys Sea.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.5] The Alps formed where the African plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.b] Destructive boundaries shape the land and also cause hazards such as powerful earthquakes, tsunamis and explosive volcanic eruptions. For a discussion of their impacts, give landforms and hazards, each with a named example.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.0] Landforms: ocean trenches, fold mountains, volcanic mountain chains and island arcs, such as the Andes and the Mariana Trench.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.1] Earthquakes: Nepal, 25 April 2015, magnitude 7.8 (a measure of earthquake size), killed about 9,000 people on the Himalayan collision boundary.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.2] Undersea earthquakes and tsunamis (huge sea waves caused by earthquakes under the sea): the Tōhoku, Japan, earthquake of 11 March 2011 had magnitude 9.0; its tsunami killed about 18,000 people (dead and missing) and caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.3] The Indian Ocean earthquake of 26 December 2004 had magnitude 9.1 off Sumatra, Indonesia, where the Indo-Australian plate subducts; its tsunami killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.4] Volcanic eruptions: Mount St Helens erupted on 18 May 1980, killing 57 people.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.b] At passive (transform) boundaries two plates slide past each other horizontally along a transform fault. Because nothing is pulled apart or pushed under, no crust is made or lost.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.0] Friction locks the plates until stress builds up, and the stress is released suddenly as an earthquake.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.1] No magma is produced, so there is no volcanic activity, and no crust is created or destroyed.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.2] The plates may move in the same direction at different speeds, or in opposite directions, so both count as sliding past.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.3] The San Andreas Fault, California, USA, is a transform boundary where the Pacific plate moves north-west relative to the North American plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.4] The San Francisco earthquake of 18 April 1906 had magnitude about 7.8 and was followed by great fires; the Loma Prieta earthquake of 1989, magnitude 6.9, struck near San Francisco.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.5] The North and East Anatolian Faults in Turkey are transform faults; the earthquake of 6 February 2023 on the East Anatolian Fault, magnitude 7.8, killed over 50,000 people in Turkey and Syria.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.6] Diagram: plan view (looking down from above) of two plates with arrows in opposite directions along a fault line.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.b] Putting the boundary types together shows that the ocean floor is constantly being made and used up. This is the tectonic cycle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.0] Crust is created at ridges, carried across the ocean floor, and destroyed at subduction zones, so the ocean floor is continually recycled.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.1] Continental crust is too light to be subducted, which is why it survives for so long.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.2] Oceans open and close over time: the Iapetus closed and the Atlantic opened.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.b] This summary pairs each boundary type with its plate movement and what happens to the crust. Exams ask for an example of each, so the examples are added here.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.0] Constructive boundaries: plates move apart and crust is created. Examples: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the East African Rift Valley.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.1] Destructive boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, or modified where two continental plates collide. Examples: the Andes, the Mariana Trench and the Himalayas.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.2] Transform (passive) boundaries: plates slide past and crust is conserved. Example: the San Andreas Fault.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.b] Earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains are concentrated in narrow belts along plate boundaries, with very few in plate interiors. This is how plate tectonics explains where they occur.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.0] The Pacific Ring of Fire is the belt of destructive and some transform boundaries around the edge of the Pacific plate and its neighbours.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.1] The Ring of Fire, because it follows destructive boundaries, contains about 75% of the world's active volcanoes and about 90% of its earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.2] Ridges, subduction zones, collision zones and transform faults are the boundaries where these belts form, because plates interact there.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.3] Fold mountains are found only at convergent boundaries, as long chains along the plate edge; the Andes, Himalayas and Alps are examples.

CANDIDATES geo-plate-tectonics-s1: 1. The internal structure of the earth
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.0.b] The earth is not the same all the way through. It is built in layers, and each layer differs in what it is made of and whether it is solid or liquid.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.0.0] The earth is made of concentric layers (layers one inside another, like the rings of an onion). From the outside in they are the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.0.1] Density (how much mass is packed into a given volume) and temperature both increase towards the centre of the earth, so the innermost layers are the densest and hottest.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.0.2] This is why the core, at the centre, is the hottest and densest part, while the thin crust at the surface is the coolest and lightest.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.0.3] The earth's radius (the distance from the surface to the centre) is about 6,370 km.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.0.4] Diagram: draw a labelled cross-section of the earth showing every layer and both boundaries between them.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.b] The crust is the thin, solid outer layer of the earth, the part we live on. It exists in two types, which differ in thickness, density and rock type.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.0] Continental crust (the crust that makes up the land) is about 30–70 km thick and is thickest under high mountain ranges.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.1] Continental crust has an average density of about 2.7 g/cm³ (grams per cubic centimetre).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.2] Continental crust is mainly granite-type rock, rich in silica (a mineral made of silicon and oxygen) and aluminium; its old name is sial.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.3] Oceanic crust (the crust under the oceans) is about 5–10 km thick and has an average density of about 3.0 g/cm³.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.4] Oceanic crust is mainly basalt (a dark volcanic rock), rich in silica and magnesium; its old name is sima.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.5] Continental crust is thicker but less dense than oceanic crust, because oceanic crust is thinner but denser; compare 2.7 with 3.0 g/cm³.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.6] No oceanic crust is older than about 200 million years, while some continental rock is billions of years old, because oceanic crust is continually created and destroyed. There is more on this in the plate tectonics model and at plate boundaries.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.b] Where one layer meets the next, the rock changes sharply. These sharp boundaries have names, and exams ask you to tell them apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.0] A discontinuity is a sharp boundary between two layers of the earth, where the material changes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.1] The Moho (Mohorovičić discontinuity) is the boundary between the crust and the mantle, where earthquake waves speed up suddenly.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.2] The Moho is named after Andrija Mohorovičić, a Croatian seismologist (a scientist who studies earthquakes), who identified it in 1909 from earthquake records.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.3] The Gutenberg discontinuity is the boundary between the mantle and the core, at the base of the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.b] The mantle is the thickest layer. Its upper part contains the layers on which the crust rests and moves.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.0] The mantle extends to about 2,900 km depth and is made of dense rock rich in iron and magnesium.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.1] The mantle has an upper part and a lower part, and it is the upper part that holds the asthenosphere and the base of the lithosphere.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.2] Asthenosphere: the semi-molten (partly melted), plastic (able to flow slowly without breaking) layer in the upper mantle, beneath the lithosphere, on which the plates move. It is not liquid.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.3] Magma is molten rock in the mantle; it is called lava only once it erupts at the surface.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.4] Lithosphere: the rigid outer shell made of the crust plus the solid top of the upper mantle. It is about 100 km thick on average, thinner under the oceans and thicker under the continents.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.5] The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates (large slabs of rigid rock), which is why the rigid lithosphere can move as separate pieces over the plastic asthenosphere.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.4.b] The core is the centre of the earth. It is made of iron and nickel and is divided into a liquid outer part and a solid inner part.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.4.0] Exams ask for the two main metals of the core: iron and nickel.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.4.1] Outer core: liquid, from about 2,900 km to about 5,150 km depth. Movement of this liquid metal generates the earth's magnetic field (the field that makes a compass needle point north).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.4.2] Inner core: solid, from about 5,150 km to the centre of the earth. It is the hottest part of the earth, at about 5,500 °C, similar to the surface of the sun.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.4.3] The inner core is solid despite its heat because the pressure at that depth is enormous, which means the metal cannot melt.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.b] The inside of the earth is hot, and that heat keeps the mantle moving. This movement is what later drives changes in the crust.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.0] Internal heat comes from the decay of radioactive elements (unstable elements that give off heat as they break down) and from heat left over from the earth's formation.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.1] Convection currents are circular movements of semi-molten rock in the mantle, set going by heat from below, so for example hot rock rises while cooler rock sinks.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.2] 1. Heated rock near the core becomes less dense and rises.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.3] 2. Near the top of the mantle the rock spreads out sideways and cools.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.4] 3. Cooled rock becomes denser and sinks again, completing the circle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.6.b] The heat inside the earth powers forces that shape the crust. Geographers split the forces that shape the earth into those from inside and those from outside.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.6.0] Endogenic (internal) forces come from inside the earth and are powered by its internal heat.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.6.1] Endogenic forces include plate movement, folding (bending of rock layers), faulting (breaking and shifting of rock along cracks), volcanic activity and earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.6.2] Exogenic (external) forces act at the earth's surface, such as weathering (the breakdown of rock where it lies) and erosion (the wearing away and carrying off of rock and soil).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.b] Nobody has drilled through the earth, so its structure is worked out from earthquake (seismic) waves. Their speed and direction change as they pass through layers of different density.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.0] Seismic waves reveal the earth's structure because the changes in their speed and direction at each boundary are how the discontinuities were found.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.1] P-waves (primary waves) pass through both solids and liquids, so they can travel through the liquid outer core as well as through solid layers.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.2] S-waves (secondary waves) pass only through solids, so they are stopped by any liquid layer.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.3] S-waves do not pass through the outer core, which shows that the outer core is liquid.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.4] This leaves an S-wave shadow zone (an area where S-waves are not detected) on the far side of the earth from an earthquake.
