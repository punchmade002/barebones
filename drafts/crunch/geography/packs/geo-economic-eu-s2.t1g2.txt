TARGET geo-economic-eu-s2: 2. Irish trading patterns with the EU
[geo-economic-eu-s2.0.b] Irish trading patterns changed enormously after 1973, so you need to know the starting point. Before Ireland joined the EEC (the European Economic Community, the forerunner of the EU), its trade depended on one neighbour, the UK.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.0.0] Before 1973 Irish trade depended on the UK. In 1960 about three-quarters (74%) of Irish exports went to the UK, mainly live cattle and other farm produce, so Ireland sold mostly raw farm output to a single customer.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.0.1] The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement, signed in 1965, governed trade with the UK (free trade means few or no tariffs, the taxes charged on imports).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.0.2] Protectionism, meaning taxes and limits on imports to shield home industry, was dismantled from the late 1950s, which opened the Irish economy to outside trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.1.b] Membership of the EEC gave Ireland a way to trade beyond the UK. The decision was put to the people first.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.1.0] Ireland joined the EEC on 1 January 1973, together with the UK and Denmark.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.1.1] A referendum (a public vote on one question) on 10 May 1972 returned about 83% Yes, which shows strong public support for joining.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.2.b] Diversification means spreading sales across more customers instead of relying on one. After 1973 Ireland did this, and the UK's role shrank.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.2.0] The UK's share of Irish exports fell from about three-quarters in 1960 to a small fraction today. In February 2024 Great Britain took about 8% of Irish goods exports in that month alone, which is under a tenth.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.2.1] As the UK's share fell, the rest of the EU and the USA became far more important, so Ireland is no longer tied to a single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.3.b] It is not only where Ireland sells that changed but what it sells. Exports moved from raw farm produce to goods and services of much higher value.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.3.0] Exports moved from mainly agricultural goods and live animals to high-value manufactured goods (goods that sell for a lot relative to their size). Pharmaceuticals and chemicals, medical devices, and computers and electronics grew strongly in the 1990s and 2000s.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.3.1] Processed food and drink replaced live animals as the main farm exports, and services (such as software and finance, rather than physical goods) became a larger part of the economy's trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.3.2] Exports far exceed imports. Ireland has a very large goods trade surplus (exports worth more than imports), mainly because foreign multinationals (companies that operate in several countries) export most of what they make in Ireland.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.4.b] Use 2024 as the main current year: it is a normal year, unlike 2025, when exports surged. These three figures show the size of Ireland's trade and its surplus.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.4.0] Goods exports were about €224 billion in 2024, a record and up about 14% on 2023.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.4.1] Goods imports were about €135 billion in 2024, giving a goods surplus (exports minus imports) of about €89 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.5.b] A trading bloc is a group of countries that trade freely together. Ireland sells most to two destinations, the EU 27 (the 27 member states) as a bloc and the USA as a single country.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.5.0] The EU 27 took about €88 billion of Irish goods exports in 2024, about 40%, making it the largest bloc.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.5.1] The USA took about €73 billion, about one-third of exports, and is Ireland's largest single export market, ahead of any one EU country.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.5.2] The main EU markets in 2024 were the Netherlands (about €23 billion), Germany (about €20 billion), Belgium (about €17 billion) and then France.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.6.b] Imports are the goods Ireland buys from abroad. The EU is again the biggest source, but the pattern is more spread out than for exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.6.0] The EU 27 supplied about €50 billion of Irish imports in 2024, about 37%, making it the largest bloc.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.6.1] Asia supplied about €31 billion, the USA about €23 billion and the UK about €22 billion. The USA and the UK are the largest single countries.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.6.2] Asia, especially China, is growing as a source of imports.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.7.b] A few product groups make up most of what Ireland sells abroad. One of them dominates.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.7.0] Medical and pharmaceutical products are the largest export group, at about €100 billion in 2024 (about 45% of goods exports), roughly half the total.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.7.1] Organic chemicals, medical devices, and food and drink follow as the other main exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.8.b] What Ireland buys reflects what it lacks and what its industries and consumers need.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.8.0] Fuels, because Ireland imports most of its energy.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.8.1] Machinery and transport equipment, including aircraft for the leasing industry (firms that buy aircraft and rent them to airlines).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.8.2] Chemicals, consumer goods and food.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.b] 2025 was an unusual year. Preliminary CSO (Central Statistics Office) figures show a record, driven by firms moving exports forward to beat US tariffs (taxes on imported goods).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.0] Preliminary CSO figures show goods exports of about €260 billion in 2025, up about 16% and a record. Pharmaceutical exports to the USA were brought forward (front-loaded) before threatened US tariffs.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.1] Medical and pharmaceutical products were about €139 billion in 2025, about 53% of goods exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.2] The USA took about 43% of exports in 2025. Imports were about €144 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.3] Exports to the USA fell sharply at the end of 2025. The EU-USA deal of July 2025 capped US tariffs on most EU goods, including pharmaceuticals, at 15%.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.4] The surge shows how dependent the totals are on one product and one market, so 2024 is the better guide to a typical year.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.10.b] Ireland sells services as well as goods. Many are sold by multinationals based in Dublin, and much of this trade goes to EU customers.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.10.0] Ireland is a major exporter of services, especially computer and software services sold by multinationals such as Google, Meta and Microsoft, whose European headquarters are in Dublin.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.10.1] Financial services are based at the International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) in Dublin Docklands, which opened in 1987. Aircraft leasing is another major service.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.10.2] Much of this services trade is sold into EU markets from an Irish base.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.b] Farming is still central to Irish trade. Ireland produces far more food than it needs, so the surplus must be sold abroad.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.0] Ireland exports about 90% of its beef and dairy output, and produces far more food than its population eats.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.1] Food, drink and horticulture (growing fruit, vegetables and flowers) exports were about €17 billion in 2024, a record and up about 5% (Bord Bia, the Irish food board).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.2] Of these exports, about 35% (about €5.9 billion) went to the EU and about €5.2 billion to markets outside the EU and UK; the UK took the remainder, about a third.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.3] Dairy, at about €6.3 billion, was the largest category.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.b] Food exports show how Ireland sells to both the EU and the UK, and Irish brands carry the products into those markets.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.0] The UK remains the largest single market for Irish food, though not for Irish goods overall.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.1] Meat and livestock exports in 2024 went about 44% to the EU and about 42% to the UK, so they are split almost equally.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.2] Kerrygold butter, marketed by Ornua (formerly the Irish Dairy Board), sells in Germany, one of its biggest markets, and other EU countries.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.3] Irish beef is sold through EU retailers, and Irish whiskey and Baileys are sold internationally.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.b] Several features of EU membership make the EU the natural market for Irish firms. Each one lowers the cost or risk of selling there.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.0] Single market access: the single market lets goods move tariff-free and barrier-free among member states, giving Irish firms access to about 450 million consumers in 2024.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.1] The euro: Ireland adopted it in 1999 and notes and coins followed in 2002. Trade with eurozone countries (those using the euro) carries no exchange-rate risk, because there is no currency to change.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.2] Sterling (the British pound) fell sharply after the UK referendum of 23 June 2016, which hurt Irish food exporters to the UK because sterling earnings bought fewer euros. This makes UK trade riskier.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.3] CAP (the Common Agricultural Policy) access and common standards opened EU food markets to Irish farm produce. There is more on this in How the EU influences the Irish economy.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.14.b] Foreign direct investment (FDI) means a foreign company setting up operations in Ireland. It is another reason Irish trade with the EU is so large.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.14.0] US and other multinationals locate in Ireland to supply the EU market from inside it. Examples include Intel at Leixlip, Co. Kildare (since 1989), Apple in Cork (since 1980) and Pfizer.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.14.1] Ireland attracts them with an English-speaking, educated workforce, a corporation tax (tax on company profits) rate of 12.5%, and support from IDA Ireland, the state agency that attracts foreign investment.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.14.2] From 2024 firms with turnover over €750 million pay a minimum tax of 15%. There is more on the multinationals in The Global Economy notes.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.15.b] Ireland's position shapes how goods leave and arrive. Being an island makes sea links the main route to Europe.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.15.0] Ireland is an island on the periphery (the edge) of Europe, so most trade goes by sea.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.15.1] Dublin Port is Ireland's largest port. Rosslare Europort in Co. Wexford is the main port for direct ferries to continental Europe. Cork and Shannon Foynes also handle trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.15.2] High-value, light goods such as pharmaceuticals and electronics go by air.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.b] Brexit, the UK's departure from the EU, changed the rules for Ireland's trade with its nearest neighbour. It pushed Irish trade further towards the rest of the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.0] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020. The transition ended on 31 December 2020, and the Trade and Cooperation Agreement has applied since 1 January 2021.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.1] Trade with Great Britain now crosses an EU external border, with customs declarations (forms listing goods crossing the border) and checks on food and animals.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.2] The UK landbridge, trucks crossing Britain to reach the continent, became slower. From 2 January 2021 DFDS ran a direct Rosslare-Dunkirk freight ferry, six times a week. By April 2021 there were about 36 direct weekly sailings from Rosslare to continental Europe.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.3] Rosslare also has direct sailings to Cherbourg, Saint-Malo and Roscoff in France, and to Bilbao in Spain.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.4] Imports from Great Britain fell after 2021 as some importers switched to EU suppliers. Cross-border trade with Northern Ireland grew, because Northern Ireland stays in the EU single market for goods under the Windsor Framework of February 2023. There is more on this in The complexity of regions (i).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.5] Brexit raised the share of Irish trade with the rest of the EU and reduced Ireland's dependence on the UK further.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.17.b] The pattern has weaknesses as well as strengths. Concentrating trade in a few places leaves Ireland exposed.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.17.0] Over-reliance on a few multinationals, one sector (pharmaceuticals) and one market (the USA) leaves exports exposed to US tariffs and to corporate decisions, such as a firm moving production.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.17.1] Ireland's peripheral location adds transport costs, so sea links to the continent are vital.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-globalisation-s1: 1. One multinational company (MNC)
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.b] A multinational company (MNC) is a company based in one home country that runs production or service branches in several other countries. The syllabus asks you to study one MNC to understand how it is structured and organised, so the general pattern comes first.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.0] Definition: a company with its headquarters (the main control centre) in its home country and production or service branches in several host countries (the countries that receive the branches). It is also called a transnational corporation (TNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.1] Typical structure: headquarters, research and development (R&D, the work of inventing and designing new products) and top management stay in the home country as the core. Branch plants, regional headquarters, and sales and service offices are placed abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.2] Decisions are made at headquarters, and branch plants compete with each other inside the company for investment, so a plant that is less efficient or more costly risks losing out to another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.3] Production is split into stages: design and R&D; basic processing of raw materials and components; assembly and testing; distribution and sales. Each stage is placed where it is cheapest or most efficient, which spreads sourcing, basic processing and markets across the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.4] The largest MNCs have sales or market values (the total worth of the company's shares) greater than the gross domestic product (GDP, the value of all goods and services a country produces in a year) of many countries, and they control much of world trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.5] The largest companies by market value in the mid-2020s are American technology companies: Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Alphabet (Google's parent) and Amazon.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.b] Foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment by a company in another country, made by building or buying a plant there. Ireland has a State agency whose job is to win this investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.0] IDA Ireland (Industrial Development Authority), set up in 1949, is the State agency responsible for attracting and keeping FDI, so it is the body that supports foreign companies both before and after they arrive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.1] IDA client companies employ about 300,000 people in about 1,800 companies (2023-24), concentrated in the Dublin region, which has the largest labour pool, universities, Dublin Port and Airport, services and existing clusters of firms.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.2] Other Irish-based MNCs: Pfizer (Ringaskiddy, Co. Cork, and Grange Castle, Dublin), Google (European headquarters, Barrow Street, Dublin), Meta (international headquarters, Dublin), Microsoft (in Ireland since 1985) and Dell (Limerick).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.b] Ireland has no shortage of competition for foreign investment, so a company needs reasons to build here. These are the MNC-specific advantages of Ireland, and each one can be developed into an explained point.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.0] Low corporation tax (the tax a company pays on its profits) of 12.5%, rising to 15% for very large multinational groups (turnover over 750 million euro) from 2024 under the OECD global minimum tax agreement.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.1] A young, educated, English-speaking workforce, which suits high-skill and customer-facing work.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.2] EU membership, which Ireland joined in 1973 as the EEC (now the EU), gives access to the single market (one trading area with no tariffs between members).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.3] Since the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020, Ireland is the only euro-area country where English is the main language (Malta also has English as an official language).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.4] IDA grants and support for companies that invest here, which lower the cost of setting up.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.5] Good international links, including ports, airports and broadband, so goods, staff and data can move easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.6] Clusters of similar firms and suppliers already in the country, so a new plant can find skilled workers and local suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.7] Universities and research links with industry, which supply graduates and shared research.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.8] A stable democracy with a pro-business government, which gives a company confidence its investment is safe.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.b] Intel is the main case study. It is a clear example of an MNC whose headquarters and design work stay in the home country while manufacturing is spread across several continents. Intel was founded in 1968, which is earlier than Apple (1976).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.0] Intel Corporation was founded in 1968 in California by Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore. Its headquarters are in Santa Clara, California, in Silicon Valley (the area of California where many technology companies are based).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.1] Intel made the first commercial microprocessor (a computer's processing chip), the Intel 4004, in 1971.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.2] Intel is best known for the processors inside most personal computers: the x86 family, sold as Pentium and Core, and Xeon for servers (powerful computers that serve other computers).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.3] Wafer fabrication plants ('fabs', factories that make chips on silicon wafers) are at Hillsboro, Oregon (Intel's main research and development fabs); Chandler, Arizona; Rio Rancho, New Mexico; Leixlip, Ireland; and Kiryat Gat, Israel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.4] Assembly and test plants are at Penang and Kulim in Malaysia, Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam, and Chengdu in China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.5] Sales offices are located around the world, close to the customers they serve.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.b] Ireland's part of Intel is a manufacturing branch plant at Leixlip, Co. Kildare. It is the country's largest private-sector investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.0] Intel came to Leixlip, Co. Kildare, in 1989.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.1] Its campus is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, with over 30 billion euro invested by 2023. It has several fabs and employs several thousand people.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.2] Fab 34 opened in 2023 and was the first fab in Europe to make chips in high volume using extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography (printing the tiny circuit patterns onto the wafer with a very short wavelength of light).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.3] Fab 34 makes the Intel 4 and Intel 3 generations, such as Core Ultra processors.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.4] In 2024 Intel sold a 49% share of the Fab 34 joint venture (a business owned jointly by two companies) to Apollo Global Management, an American investment firm, for about 11 billion US dollars, to raise money for its other projects.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.b] A fab has very particular needs: easy transport, a big site, skilled people and huge supplies of water and electricity. Leixlip met them, which is the answer to the Ordinary question on the advantages of Ireland for Intel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.0] Leixlip is on the M4 motorway west of Dublin, giving road access to the capital, so staff and goods can travel easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.1] A large, flat greenfield site, which is undeveloped land, so there was room to build very large plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.2] Maynooth University is nearby in Co. Kildare, and Trinity College Dublin and University College Dublin are in the city, giving access to graduates and research links.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.3] A large labour pool in the Dublin area, so Intel can recruit many workers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.4] A water and electricity supply; fabs use huge amounts of both, so a plant cannot work without a dependable supply.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.b] For the syllabus you must study one product of the MNC. Intel's product is the microprocessor, and knowing how it is made explains why Intel needs the inputs and locations described next.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.0] A microprocessor, or chip, is the 'brain' of a computer, because it carries out the computer's instructions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.1] It is made by building billions of tiny transistors (tiny electronic switches) in layers on a thin disc of pure silicon called a wafer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.2] Wafers are processed in a dust-free clean room, because dust would ruin the tiny circuits.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.b] Sourcing means where a company gets the materials and parts it needs. A fab depends on a few specialist inputs, and some come from a single supplier.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.0] Silicon, refined from quartz sand, is bought as silicon wafers from specialist wafer makers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.1] Chemicals, specialty gases and metals, which are used up in processing the wafers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.2] Huge volumes of ultra-pure water and electricity, because the fab uses them continuously.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.3] Manufacturing machines, especially lithography machines (which print the circuit patterns onto wafers) from ASML, based at Veldhoven in the Netherlands. ASML is the only maker of EUV lithography machines, which cost hundreds of millions of euro each.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.b] Intel splits chip making into stages and puts each where it suits best. Following the chain from wafer to computer shows how the stages are spread across several continents.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.0] 1. Stage 1: wafers are processed in fabs at Leixlip, Hillsboro, Chandler and Kiryat Gat.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.1] 2. Stage 2: finished wafers are flown to assembly and test plants in Penang and Kulim (Malaysia), Ho Chi Minh City (Vietnam) and Chengdu (China), where they are cut into individual chips, packaged and tested.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.2] 3. Stage 3: the chips go to computer makers' assembly plants, mostly in Asia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.3] Chips are small, light and very valuable, so they are moved by air.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.4] The Leixlip plant makes processed wafers only. Cutting, packaging and testing of the chips are not done in Ireland.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.b] A market is where a product is sold, and a market can be a country, a region or a type of customer. Intel's main customers are computer makers, server makers, governments and businesses.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.0] Computer manufacturers are the main customers: Lenovo (China), HP and Dell (USA).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.1] Makers of servers for data centres and cloud computing (storing and running data and programs on remote computers).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.2] Governments and businesses worldwide, because they also buy computers and servers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.3] Markets are in North America, Europe and Asia, with China one of the largest.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.4] Two markets outside Ireland can be named as countries, for example the USA and China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.b] Intel faces pressures from rivals, costs and supply chains. The exam asks for one difficulty, so learn several to choose from and give each in one explained sentence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.0] Competition from AMD (USA); TSMC (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company), the world's largest contract chip maker; Samsung (South Korea); and Nvidia (USA), the leader in chips for artificial intelligence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.1] Loss of Apple as a customer: Apple began replacing Intel chips in its Mac computers with its own M1 chip in November 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.2] Very high cost of building new fabs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.3] Falling profits: about 15,000 global job cuts were announced in August 2024, with further cuts in 2025 under the new chief executive, Lip-Bu Tan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.4] In 2025 the US government took a stake of about 10% in Intel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.5] Risk that production moves abroad if Ireland's tax advantage narrows (the 15% minimum rate for very large groups) or if costs rise.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.6] High energy costs and pressure on Ireland's electricity grid, since fabs need large amounts of power.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.7] Dependence on Asian supply chains: the global chip shortage of 2021, caused by COVID-19 disruption, halted car and electronics production worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.b] Intel's presence helps Ireland in several ways. These benefits are described without numbers because no checked figures exist for them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.0] Direct jobs on the Leixlip campus, and indirect (spin-off) jobs with local suppliers and services, which grow because Intel buys goods and services locally.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.1] Corporation tax paid to the State, and exports from the plant.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.2] Skills development and research links with universities.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.b] Apple is the alternative case study to Intel. Its product is the iPhone, and it is a good example of a company that designs in one country and has others make its products. Apple was founded in 1976, later than Intel (1968).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.0] Apple was founded in 1976 by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak. Its headquarters are at the Apple Park campus in Cupertino, California, which opened in 2017.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.1] Products: the iPhone (2007), iPad (2010), Mac computers and Apple Watch (2015), plus services such as the App Store, iCloud and Apple Music.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.2] Design, software and management are based in California, which makes California the core of the company.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.b] Apple's Irish base is in Cork. It is mainly a European headquarters and operations centre, not a phone factory.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.0] Apple opened at Hollyhill, Cork, in 1980. It was Apple's first manufacturing plant in Europe.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.1] It employs about 6,000 people in Cork and is Apple's European headquarters.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.2] The Cork campus runs operations and supply chain, finance, and customer support (AppleCare) in many European languages. It also assembles some built-to-order iMac computers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.3] The Cork campus does not make iPhones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.14.b] Unlike a company that builds everything itself, Apple concentrates on design and management and pays other firms to make its products.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.14.0] Apple owns very little manufacturing itself and outsources production (pays other firms to make its products) to contract manufacturers, which lets it focus on design, software and management.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.14.1] The chief contract manufacturer is Foxconn (Hon Hai Precision Industry), a Taiwanese company.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.14.2] Apple runs its own retail stores and an online store.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.15.b] An iPhone is built from parts and materials gathered from several countries, so its sourcing is spread across Asia and beyond.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.15.0] Processor chips are designed by Apple and made by TSMC in Taiwan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.15.1] Screens are supplied by Samsung and LG (South Korea). Memory and other parts come from other Asian suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.15.2] Raw materials include aluminium, lithium and cobalt for batteries, rare earth metals and gold.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.15.3] Most of the world's cobalt is mined in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.16.b] Apple's design is done in California, but the phones are put together elsewhere. Final assembly is concentrated in a few huge plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.16.0] Final assembly is done by Foxconn at very large plants in China: Zhengzhou in Henan province, called 'iPhone City', and Shenzhen.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.16.1] A growing share is assembled in India, around Chennai and Bengaluru, by Foxconn and the Indian Tata group. This reduces dependence on China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.17.b] Apple sells its iPhones in almost every region, through its own outlets and through phone companies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.17.0] iPhones are sold worldwide. The Americas, Europe and China are the largest regions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.17.1] Sales are made through Apple Stores, Apple's online store and mobile phone networks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.18.b] Apple has faced legal, supply and reputation problems. As with Intel, one difficulty explained well is what the exam asks for.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.18.0] EU state aid tax case: in 2016 the European Commission ruled that Ireland had given Apple illegal tax benefits worth 13 billion euro, which must be recovered.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.18.1] The EU General Court overturned the ruling in 2020. In September 2024 the EU Court of Justice reinstated it, and Ireland received about 14 billion euro, including interest.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.18.2] This was recovery of illegal State aid (help from a government that gives a company an unfair advantage), which is unpaid tax, not a fine. Ireland had argued against collecting it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.18.3] Dependence on China for assembly, and trade tensions between the USA and China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.18.4] Competition from Samsung and from Chinese phone makers, which can take customers from Apple.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.18.5] Criticism of working conditions in supplier factories, which can harm Apple's reputation.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.18.6] Athenry, Co. Galway: Apple announced a data centre (about 850 million euro) in 2015 and abandoned it in 2018 after planning and appeal delays.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.19.b] Use map evidence to justify a location, and give one reason against a location. The map question asks for two reasons an MNC might locate in a mapped area, with map evidence, and one reason against.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.19.0] Look for transport: a motorway, national primary road, railway, port or airport, because an MNC needs to move goods and staff.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.19.1] Look for a large, flat site, shown by widely spaced contours (the lines joining points of equal height; wide spacing means gentle slopes).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.19.2] Look for a nearby town for labour and services, and for an industrial estate symbol, which shows land already set aside for industry.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.19.3] Look for a water supply, such as a river or lake, because industry needs water.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.19.4] Reasons against a site: steep or flood-prone land (closely spaced contours or marsh), poor roads, a remote location, or protected scenery.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.19.5] Invented illustration, not a real map: a flat site beside a motorway junction, a market town nearby and a river for water. Each point should give the feature, the location and the reason it suits an MNC.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.20.b] A data response may give a chart or table of the largest MNCs by value. You are then asked to draw a bar chart and calculate differences.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.20.0] Draw a bar chart with one bar per company, a labelled value axis starting at zero, and units in the title, so the bars can be compared fairly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.20.1] Calculate a difference by subtracting the smaller value from the larger, and state the answer with its units.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.20.2] The method is set out in the note Tables, Graphs and Data Skills.

CANDIDATES geo-graph-skills-s1: Figure interpretation
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.b] Every figure question starts the same way: read the labels and units before reading any value. Most lost marks come from a wrong unit, year or column, because a correct reading of the wrong one still scores nothing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.0] Check the title, both axis labels (the words along the horizontal and vertical lines), the key or legend (the box explaining colours or symbols), the units (%, thousands, millions, €m, hectares, mm, °C, per 1,000), the years shown and the source line. Each one changes what a number means.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.1] Read a bar or line value by running a ruler from the top of the bar or point across to the vertical axis, estimating between gridlines, and quoting the value with its unit.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.2] In a table, find the row and the column and read the cell where they meet. Check the totals row before using a figure, so you know whether a number is a part or the whole.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.3] Compare two years by reading neighbouring columns. To spot the one that did not change, read across the whole row, because the unchanged value is the one that stays the same between years.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.4] For highest and lowest, scan every value, not just the first and last, because the extreme can sit in the middle. For a ranking, list the values from largest to smallest.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.5] To count how many items are above an average line, count the bars or points whose tops lie above it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.6] For 'twice as much', double the smaller value and look for the value that matches it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.7] An infographic (a single page combining headline numbers, icons, small charts and county maps) has several panels. Read each panel's own label and unit: headline figures are often totals, while panels may show percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.b] A description states what the figure shows. Using exact trend words and quoting numbers turns a vague answer into a complete one.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.0] Trend vocabulary: increase, rise or growth; decrease, fall or decline; steady, constant or no change; fluctuate (go up and down); peak (highest point); trough (lowest point); gradual or rapid (sharp); levelling off (the change slows and flattens).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.1] The overall trend is the long-term direction. A short-term change, such as a single year's dip, is described separately so it does not hide the main pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.2] To describe a trend, state the overall direction, the start and end values with their years and units, and the period of fastest change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.3] Name any peak, trough or reversal (a change of direction), and quantify the change as the difference, the percentage change, or both.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.4] Point out anomalies: values that break the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.b] Once a trend is described, the next marks come from comparing it with another, saying why it happened and judging whether the figure can be trusted.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.0] To compare two series (two lines or two sets of bars), say which is higher, when the lines cross, whether the gap widens or narrows, and the difference in a given year. For example, net migration = immigration minus emigration.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.1] To explain a trend, link the pattern to one cause from the relevant chapter, in one clause. Explaining means saying why the figure looks as it does, not just what it shows.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.2] To evaluate a figure, check whether an axis that does not start at zero or has uneven intervals exaggerates change, because a small rise then looks large.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.3] Also check whether unevenly spaced dates distort the trend, whether the sample (the group measured) is small, and whether the source is reliable and recent.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.4] Percentages can hide small totals: a large percentage rise may be a small change in numbers, because a small starting number is easy to double.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.b] These worked values come from the invented (illustrative) population of six towns, in thousands: Town A 18, Town B 32, Town C 25, Town D 41, Town E 12, Town F 29. Each statistic has a short method that you can repeat on any data set.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.0] Total (sum): add every value. 18 + 32 + 25 + 41 + 12 + 29 = 157 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.1] Difference: subtract the smaller value from the larger and say which is larger. Town B (32) exceeds Town C (25) by 7 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.2] Mean (average) = total of the values ÷ number of values: 157 ÷ 6 = 26.2 thousand, to one decimal place (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.3] Median (the middle value): put the values in order (12, 18, 25, 29, 32, 41); with an even number of values, average the middle two: (25 + 29) ÷ 2 = 27 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.4] Mode: the most frequent value. The six towns have no mode because no value repeats.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.5] Range = highest value minus lowest value: 41 − 12 = 29 thousand (illustrative), which shows how spread out the values are.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.b] Percentages let you compare parts of different-sized wholes, but the method matters: dividing by the wrong number, or mixing up a percentage with a percentage point, gives a wrong answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.0] Percentage of a total = part ÷ total × 100. Illustrative: Town D's share of the six towns is 41 ÷ 157 × 100 = 26.1%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.1] Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100. A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.2] Always divide by the old (earlier) value, because the change is measured against where you started. Illustrative Country P, broadband households: 20% in 2000 and 90% in 2025, so the percentage change is (90 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 350%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.3] Percentage points: when both values are already percentages, the change is a difference in percentage points. A rise from 20% to 30% is a 10 percentage-point increase, but a 50% increase in the percentage change, (30 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.4] When a question asks by what percentage a percentage changed, the subtraction is the usual reading: give the answer in percentage points and add the percentage change if unsure. Use the percentage-change formula when the values are numbers rather than percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.5] Missing value X: parts of a whole add to 100% (pie chart or percentage table) or to a stated total (stacked bar, a bar split into parts, or a total row), so X = total minus the sum of the known parts.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.6] Illustrative Country Q: if all segments except 'other' total 85%, then other = 100 − 85 = 15%, because the parts of a pie chart must reach 100%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.7] To find a number from a percentage: number = percentage ÷ 100 × total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.b] Raw numbers are hard to compare between places of different size, so geographers use rates, densities and ratios. How you present the answer matters too.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.0] Rate per 1,000 = number ÷ population × 1,000, which lets places of different size be compared. A birth rate is births ÷ population × 1,000.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.1] Density = number ÷ area, given as people per km².
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.2] Ratio: compare two values as a ratio, e.g. 2 : 1. Illustrative: Town D (41) is more than three times the size of Town E (12), a ratio of about 3.4 : 1.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.3] 'Twice as much' means two times the other value; 'half' means divided by two.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.4] Round to the precision the data uses, or to one decimal place where needed, and keep the unit and the multiplier (thousands, millions) in every answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.5] In a calculation question, show the working: a correct answer with working is safe, and the working can earn marks if the answer slips.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.b] When you are asked to draw a graph from a small table of data, marks are given for each of these rules, so each one is worth doing even when the shape of the graph is easy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.0] Use graph paper, a sharp pencil and a ruler; draw the graph large enough to fill most of the space.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.1] The title says what the data is, where and when, taken from the wording of the question.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.2] The horizontal (x) axis shows categories or time; the vertical (y) axis shows values. Label both axes with units.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.3] The scale starts at zero, is evenly divided, and its interval is chosen so the largest value fits (e.g. steps of 5 or 10 units).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.4] On a line graph, time intervals are spaced in proportion to the years. If the data is for 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2015, the last gap is half the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.5] Plot every value accurately. Bars have equal widths and equal gaps; points are joined with ruled straight lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.6] Use a key or legend (a box saying what each colour or shading means) whenever more than one colour or shading is used, and shade or colour each category distinctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.b] Illustrative data: population of six towns, in thousands, from the totals block above. Following the rules in order shows how each mark is earned.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.0] Graph choice: a bar chart, because the values are one measure for separate towns.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.1] Title: 'Population of six towns (thousands), illustrative'.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.2] x-axis: 'Town', with Towns A to F in order.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.3] y-axis: 'Population (thousands)', from 0 to 45 in steps of 5, so the largest value (41) fits.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.4] Six bars of equal width with equal gaps, each drawn to its value: A 18, B 32, C 25, D 41, E 12, F 29.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.5] A single data series (one set of values) needs no key; check each bar top against the scale before finishing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.b] The question 'draw a suitable graph' is answered by naming the graph type, then giving one sentence of justification. The type depends on what the data is. Each example below is illustrative.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.0] Values for separate categories, e.g. the populations of six towns: bar chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.1] Change over time, e.g. the percentage of an urban population from 1980 to 2020: line graph (a bar chart is usually also accepted).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.2] Parts of a whole, or percentage shares adding to 100%, e.g. car sales by fuel type in one year: pie chart or divided bar.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.3] Two related variables for the same places, e.g. income and birth rate for twenty countries: scatter graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.4] Three components that add to 100%, e.g. sand, silt and clay at a soil site: triangular graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.5] Age and sex structure of a population: population pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.6] Monthly temperature and rainfall for one place: climate graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.7] Values for areas on a map, e.g. population density by region: choropleth map (a map with areas shaded by value).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.b] Bar charts show amounts for separate categories. They come in several forms, depending on whether you are comparing categories, groups within a category, or parts of a total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.0] Simple bar chart: separate categories, one bar per category, equal widths and equal gaps; vertical or horizontal.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.1] Multiple (clustered or comparative) bar chart: two or more bars side by side per category, e.g. males and females, or two years; a key is needed to tell the bars apart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.2] Compound (stacked or divided) bar chart: each bar is split into components, and the top of the whole bar is the total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.3] To read a component in a compound bar, take the top of its segment minus the bottom of its segment.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.4] Percentage compound bar: every bar reaches 100%, so the segments show shares rather than totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.5] Illustrative Region R, tree planting: total 2,000 hectares, of which conifers 1,350 ha. Broadleaf = 2,000 − 1,350 = 650 ha, a share of 650 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 32.5%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.b] A histogram looks like a bar chart but is used for continuous data, while a line graph is the standard way to show one variable changing over time.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.0] Histogram: like a bar chart but for continuous data grouped in classes (e.g. age groups or rainfall amounts), drawn with no gaps between bars because one class runs straight into the next.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.1] Line graph (trend graph): one variable over time; plot the points at the correct years and join them with ruled lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.2] Multiple line graph: two or more series on the same axes, with a key so each line can be identified.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.3] The gradient (steepness) of a line shows the rate of change: a steeper section means faster change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.4] Illustrative Country P, households with broadband: 2000 20%, 2005 38%, 2010 61%, 2015 74%, 2020 86%, 2025 90%. The rise is continuous; the fastest change is 2005 to 2010 (+23 percentage points), and the rise slows to +4 points from 2020 to 2025 as the line levels off.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.5] Illustrative Country P: the change from 2000 to 2025 is +70 percentage points, which is not the same as the 350% percentage change above, because one is a subtraction and the other is measured against the old value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.b] A pie chart shows how a whole is split into parts. Because a full circle is 360° and the parts add to 100%, each 1% takes up 3.6° of the circle.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.0] Pie chart: parts of a whole. Degrees = percentage × 3.6, because 360° ÷ 100 = 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.1] For raw numbers rather than percentages: degrees = value ÷ total × 360. Do not multiply raw numbers by 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.2] Drawing steps: draw a circle with a compass, draw a radius (a line from the centre to the edge) to 12 o'clock, then measure each angle clockwise with a protractor, largest segment first.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.3] Shade, key or label each segment with its percentage, and check that the angles add to 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.4] Illustrative Country Q, electricity sources (gas 40%, wind 25%, solar 10%, coal 5%, hydro 5%, other 15%): angles 144°, 90°, 36°, 18°, 18° and 54°, totalling 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.5] Two pie charts compare the shares in two years, not the totals, unless the circles are drawn in proportion to the totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.6] Divided (percentage) bar: a single bar split into shares that add to 100%, an alternative to a pie chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.b] A pictogram uses icons to stand for quantities. A population pyramid is a special graph showing how many people of each age and sex a country has.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.0] Pictogram: each icon stands for a fixed quantity stated in the key, so check the key first; a part-icon is a fraction of that quantity.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.1] Population pyramid: a back-to-back horizontal bar chart, which is a histogram on its side.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.2] Age groups, usually in 5-year bands, sit on the central vertical axis: 0 to 4 at the bottom and the oldest group at the top.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.3] Males are drawn on the left and females on the right. The horizontal axis shows the percentage (or number) of the population, not age, scaled outward from zero on both sides.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.4] Reading a pyramid: a wide base means a high birth rate; a narrow top means low life expectancy; a narrowing base means a falling birth rate; bulges and indentations suggest baby booms, migration, war or famine.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.5] Describe the dependent groups (the young and the old, who rely on others) and the working-age group when reading a pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.6] Drawing one band (illustrative, Country Z): males aged 20 to 24 are 3.5% and females 3.4%, so at the 20 to 24 level draw a bar 3.5 units long left of the centre axis and one 3.4 units long to the right.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.7] The shapes of pyramids and the stages of demographic transition (how a country's birth and death rates change over time) are taught fully elsewhere. There is more on this in Changing population characteristics.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.b] A climate graph combines two kinds of data for one place on a single set of axes: rainfall as bars and temperature as a line, across 12 months.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.0] Months J to D run along the bottom axis. Rainfall is shown as bars against a left-hand axis in mm.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.1] Mean (average) monthly temperature is shown as a line, plotted at the middle of each month, against a right-hand axis in °C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.2] From the graph, read the hottest and coldest months, the wettest and driest months, and the seasonal pattern (rain all year, or a wet and dry season).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.3] Calculations from the graph: annual temperature range = maximum mean monthly temperature minus minimum; mean annual temperature = sum of the 12 monthly means ÷ 12; total annual rainfall = sum of the 12 bars.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.4] Illustrative Station M, mean monthly temperature (°C) J to D: 5, 5, 7, 9, 12, 14, 16, 16, 14, 11, 8, 6. Rainfall (mm) J to D: 110, 80, 85, 60, 65, 70, 70, 85, 90, 115, 110, 120.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.5] Illustrative Station M results: warmest in July and August (16°C); coldest in January and February (5°C); annual range 16 − 5 = 11°C; mean annual temperature 123 ÷ 12 = 10.3°C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.6] Illustrative Station M: total rainfall 1,060 mm; wettest month December (120 mm); driest month April (60 mm). Rain falls in every month with a winter maximum, and the mild winters and cool summers resemble a cool temperate oceanic pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.7] Identifying a climate type from a climate graph is taught elsewhere. There is more on this in Physical regions and Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.b] A scatter graph tests whether two measurements for the same set of places are related, for example whether land value changes with distance from a city centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.0] Scatter graph: two variables for the same set of places. The independent variable (the one that may cause the change) goes on the x-axis; each place is plotted as a point, and the points are not joined.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.1] Draw a line of best fit by eye through the middle of the points, so it shows the general direction.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.2] Positive correlation: both variables rise together. Negative correlation: one rises as the other falls. No correlation: no clear pattern. Strength is described as strong or weak.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.3] Outliers, or anomalies, are points that lie away from the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.4] Correlation does not prove cause: two things can move together without one causing the other.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.5] Illustrative distance from a city centre and land value at eight sites: 1 km €900 per m²; 2 km €700; 3 km €620; 4 km €450; 5 km €400; 6 km €250; 7 km €260; 8 km €150.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.6] Illustrative: the result is a strong negative correlation, with the 7 km site a slight anomaly because it is higher than the 6 km site. A one-clause explanation: accessibility falls with distance from the centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.b] A triangular graph is used when a total is split into exactly three parts. Each point is placed using all three values at once.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.0] Triangular graph: shows three components that always total 100%, e.g. the percentage employed in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.1] Each side is a 0 to 100% scale. Read each component along the gridlines parallel to the side where its 0 is, following the direction in which its numbers run.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.2] Check that the three readings add to 100, which confirms you have read the scales correctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.3] To plot a point: find the first value on its scale, follow its gridline in, and mark where it meets the second value's gridline; check the point with the third value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.4] A point clustered near a corner is dominated by the component that corner represents.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.5] Illustrative employment structure: Country X is primary 10%, secondary 25%, tertiary 65%; it sits near the tertiary corner, typical of a developed economy. Country Y is primary 60%, secondary 15%, tertiary 25%; it sits near the primary corner, typical of a less developed economy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.b] Statistical maps put values onto a map so you can see how they vary from place to place. The choropleth is the main one; four other types each show a different kind of data.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.0] Choropleth map: areas (counties, regions or countries) shaded by value class; darker shading means a higher value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.1] Classes must not overlap and must cover every value, e.g. 0 to 49, 50 to 99, 100 to 149, 150 and over. A key is essential.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.2] Choropleth maps suit densities and percentages. Their weakness is that they imply the value is even across each area and hide variation inside it, and the boundaries look like sudden changes.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.3] Illustrative regional population densities (people per km²): North 35, East 160, South 85, West 40, Midlands 60. North and West are the lightest shade and East the darkest; the empty 100 to 149 class still appears in the key.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.4] Dot map: each dot stands for a fixed number, showing the distribution of a value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.5] Isoline map: lines join places of equal value, e.g. isobars, isotherms, isohyets or contours.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.6] Flow-line map: arrow width is proportional to the amount moving, e.g. migration, traffic or trade.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.7] Proportional symbols: circles or bars sized in proportion to the value, placed on the map.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.b] Sometimes a table is a better way to present data than a graph, and tables, graphs and maps all feed into the Geographical Investigation.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.0] Use a table rather than a graph when many exact values must be given. A table cannot show a trend at a glance.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.1] Graphs, maps and tables are expected in the presentation of results in the Geographical Investigation report. There is more on this in The Geographical Investigation (20%).
