TARGET geo-option-global-interdependence-s3 (part 1 of 2): 3. Empowering people: linking economic growth with human development
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.0.b] A country can get richer on paper without its people living better. Empowerment is what connects the two, and it is the idea every later block in this subsection comes back to.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.0.0] Economic growth, a rise in GDP (the total value of goods and services a country produces), does not automatically bring human development, meaning better health, education, freedom and choice.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.0.1] Growth can be captured by elites, so its benefits stay with a small group instead of reaching ordinary people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.0.2] Empowerment means giving people control over resources such as land, credit and income, and a voice in decisions, so that growth actually reaches them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.0.3] Empowerment is therefore the link that turns economic growth into human development.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.1.b] Many poor countries owe large sums to foreign lenders. The debt built up through a chain of events in the 1970s and early 1980s, and some of the money was badly used.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.1.0] Oil-price rises in the 1970s gave banks large deposits, which they lent to developing countries as “petrodollars” (oil-earned money recycled into loans).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.1.1] Interest rates soared about 1980 and commodity prices (the prices of raw materials such as coffee and copper) fell, so the loans became far harder to repay: repayments grew while export earnings shrank.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.1.2] Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts, starting the Latin American debt crisis.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.1.3] Some loans went to dictators or to prestige projects (grand schemes built for show) that earned no return, so there was no income to repay them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.2.b] Debt keeps poor countries poor because each stage of the cycle makes the next one worse. Draw it as a loop: debt, reduced spending, poor health and education, low productivity and more borrowing.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.2.0] 1. Debt repayments take up government revenue (the money a government collects), so less is left for everything else.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.2.1] 2. Less is spent on health, schools, water and infrastructure (roads, power and other basic systems).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.2.2] 3. Poor health and education keep productivity (output per worker) and incomes low.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.2.3] 4. Low tax revenue and low export earnings mean the government must borrow more.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.2.4] 5. Debt grows, and the cycle repeats.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.2.5] To earn the foreign currency needed for repayments, countries push exports of cash crops (crops grown for sale, not food) and raw materials, which can take land from food crops and damage the environment.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.3.b] When countries could not repay, lenders offered new loans, but only on strict conditions. These packages were called structural adjustment programmes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.3.0] Structural adjustment programmes were conditions attached to IMF and World Bank loans in the 1980s and 1990s.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.3.1] Conditions required governments to cut public spending, privatise state firms (sell them to private owners), open their markets to imports and devalue their currencies (lower their value against others).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.3.2] Governments were also pushed to introduce fees for schools and clinics and to remove food subsidies (payments that keep food prices low).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.3.3] The poorest people and women bore most of the cost of cuts to services and subsidies, because they depended most on cheap food and free public services.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.3.4] Zambia: copper is most of its exports, and its state copper mines (ZCCM) were privatised in the late 1990s as part of reform conditions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.4.b] Campaigns and international bodies eventually won the cancellation of some debt. The condition was that the money saved had to go on reducing poverty, so the results show debt relief empowering people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.4.0] The Jubilee 2000 campaign pressed for the cancellation of poor countries’ debts.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.4.1] The HIPC Initiative (Heavily Indebted Poor Countries) of 1996, run by the IMF and World Bank, offered relief to heavily indebted poor countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.4.2] The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative of 2005 was agreed after the G8 summit at Gleneagles (July 2005), backed by Make Poverty History and Live 8.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.4.3] More than 30 countries, most in Africa, received HIPC relief.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.4.4] Uganda introduced Universal Primary Education in 1997 and in 1998 became the first country to receive HIPC relief; savings went to schools, health and water.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.4.5] Tanzania abolished primary school fees in 2001, so more children could attend.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.4.6] Zambia reached HIPC completion point (the stage at which relief is finalised) in 2005 and in 2006 abolished user fees for health care in rural areas.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.5.b] Debt is rising again in the 2020s. Poor countries that cannot repay are forced into default (failing to pay), restructuring (agreeing new repayment terms) or crisis.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.5.0] Zambia defaulted on its foreign debt in November 2020, the first African default of the COVID-19 period, and later restructured it under the G20 Common Framework.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.5.1] Sri Lanka defaulted for the first time in 2022; shortages of fuel, food and medicine led to mass protests, and President Gotabaya Rajapaksa fled and resigned in July 2022.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.5.2] An IMF programme of about 2.9 billion US dollars followed Sri Lanka’s default in 2023.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.5.3] Ghana defaulted in December 2022, so the wave of defaults was not limited to one country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.5.4] UNCTAD (the UN trade and development body; “A World of Debt”, 2023): about 3.3 billion people live in countries that spend more on interest payments than on either health or education, so debt takes money from services that build human development.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.6.b] Not all new debt comes from Western lenders. China has become a major lender, and whether this helps or traps poor countries is disputed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.6.0] The Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, has channelled Chinese loans to developing countries, mainly for building infrastructure.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.6.1] Hambantota port, Sri Lanka, was built with Chinese loans and, because the loans were hard to repay, leased to a Chinese state company for 99 years in 2017.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.6.2] Critics call Hambantota a “debt trap” (lending designed to give the lender control of assets); others argue that Sri Lanka’s debts were mostly owed to Western lenders and bondholders.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.7.b] Ireland shows the weight of debt even in a rich country, and what it costs ordinary people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.7.0] The 2008 crash brought a debt crisis for the Irish state, because the cost of rescuing the banks fell on public finances.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.7.1] An EU–IMF bailout of €85 billion was agreed in November 2010.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.7.2] Austerity (tight government spending and higher taxes) brought cuts to services, tax rises and the emigration of young people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.7.3] Unemployment rose to about 15% in 2012.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.7.4] Emigration peaked at about 89,000 in the year to April 2013.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.7.5] Ireland exited the programme in December 2013.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.8.b] Some argue that debt is not only an economic problem but a question of fairness.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.8.0] Odious debt: loans taken out by a regime and used against its own people are argued to be unfair for the people who must repay them, because they never benefited from the money.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.9.b] Aid is help given to developing countries, but it comes in several forms that differ in purpose, route and conditions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.9.0] Emergency or humanitarian aid provides food, shelter and medical help after disasters and conflicts.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.9.1] Long-term development aid funds projects such as water, health, education and farming.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.9.2] Bilateral aid is given directly from one government to another; multilateral aid is pooled through international bodies such as the EU and UN agencies.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.9.3] NGO or voluntary aid is channelled through non-governmental organisations (independent, non-profit groups).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.9.4] Grants are gifts; loans must be repaid, so they add to debt.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.9.5] Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country; untied aid can be spent anywhere.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.10.b] The UN set a target for how much rich countries should give, but many fall short, and aid levels can swing with donor politics.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.10.0] The UN target of 1970 asks rich countries to give 0.7% of their gross national income (GNI, a country’s total income) as aid.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.10.1] Norway, Sweden, Luxembourg and Denmark have reached or exceeded the target, so it is achievable; Ireland has not.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.10.2] The UK cut its aid from 0.7% to 0.5% of GNI in 2021 and in 2025 announced a further cut to 0.3% by 2027.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.10.3] In 2025 the US government cancelled most USAID programmes (its aid agency) and folded the agency into the State Department; the USA had been the largest single donor by volume.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.10.4] Aid depends on donor politics: cuts by large donors leave recipients without the funds they had planned for.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.11.b] The case for aid starts with what it does for the people who receive it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.11.0] Emergency relief saves lives after famine, floods or conflict because it supplies food, shelter and medicine at once.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.11.1] Schools, clinics, wells and vaccination programmes improve health and education, which means people can work and earn more.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.11.2] Training and support for women’s groups and small businesses build skills, so incomes rise.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.11.3] Infrastructure such as roads and power supplies helps local economies to grow because goods and workers can move and businesses can operate.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.12.b] Aid often serves donor interests as well as recipients, which is the core of the “who benefits?” question.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.12.0] Tied aid returns money to donor firms, because the recipient must buy goods and services from the donor country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.12.1] UK aid for the Pergau Dam, Malaysia, was linked to a British arms deal; in 1994 the English High Court ruled that the aid was unlawful because it was not for sound development.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.12.2] Aid has been used to win political allies, trade deals and influence, as in Cold War aid and in competing offers from China and the EU.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.12.3] US food aid has long been required by law to be bought largely from US farmers and shipped largely on US ships, so it supports donor farmers and shipping.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.12.4] Consultants’ fees take a share of aid budgets, so less reaches the people the aid is meant for.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.13.b] Aid can also leave recipients worse off or less able to stand on their own.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.13.0] Corruption and elite capture: aid money can be diverted to powerful groups rather than the poor.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.13.1] Dependency: recipients can come to rely on aid rather than building their own economies, so they stay vulnerable if it stops.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.13.2] Cheap imports undercut local producers: in the mid-1990s Haiti cut its tariff (a tax on imports) on rice under pressure from lenders; cheap subsidised US rice flooded in and many Haitian rice farmers were ruined.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.13.3] In 2010 former US President Bill Clinton publicly apologised for the rice policy, saying it had been a mistake; Haiti is the poorest country in the Americas.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.13.4] Loans add to debt, and large projects can displace people from their homes and land.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.13.5] Unsuitable technology can be imposed on communities that cannot maintain it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.14.b] Writers disagree about whether aid works. Each argument is tied to a named author, which gives you ready examples for an essay.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.14.0] Jeffrey Sachs, The End of Poverty (2005): more aid can end extreme poverty.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.14.1] Dambisa Moyo, a Zambian economist, Dead Aid (2009): aid breeds corruption and dependency in Africa.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.14.2] William Easterly, The White Man’s Burden (2006): top-down planners (outsiders who design projects from above) fail, while bottom-up “searchers” (people who find what works locally) succeed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.14.3] Effective aid is untied, targeted at the poorest, locally owned, transparent and supports self-help, which answers the main criticisms above.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.15.b] Ireland’s own aid programme is the national example for the aid debate.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.15.0] Irish Aid is part of the Department of Foreign Affairs.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.15.1] Its bilateral aid programme began in 1974, with partner countries mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia, and Vietnam.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.15.2] Ireland’s aid is untied, so recipients are free to spend it anywhere.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.15.3] “A Better World” (2019) sets priorities: the furthest behind first, gender equality, climate and governance.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.15.4] Ireland has not reached the 0.7% target.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.16.b] The EU is the main international example of an aid donor, and it also shows how other policies can work against aid.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.16.0] The EU institutions and member states together, “Team Europe”, are the world’s largest aid donor, giving over two-fifths of all official development aid.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.16.1] The Lomé Convention (1975), the Cotonou Agreement (2000) and the Samoa Agreement (signed November 2023) link the EU with the Organisation of African, Caribbean and Pacific States.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.16.2] ECHO, the EU’s humanitarian aid office (it funds emergency help after disasters), was set up in 1992.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.16.3] Everything But Arms (2001) is a trade preference (cheaper access to EU markets) for the poorest countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.16.4] Critique: EU farm and fishing policies have at times undermined the same countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.16.5] Global Gateway, launched in 2021, offers up to €300 billion of investment in infrastructure abroad (2021–2027), seen as a response to China’s Belt and Road.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s3.17.b] NGOs are a major route for aid and a major force for empowerment, because they work directly with communities.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s4: 4. Global issues and a justice perspective
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.b] Global issues are problems that cross borders and affect the whole world, such as poverty, inequality, trade rules, debt, climate change and migration. A justice perspective asks whether the distribution of wealth and power is fair, who decides, and who benefits. It treats development as a matter of rights, not charity.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.0] The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948, includes rights to food, health, education and decent work.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.1] Poverty denies these rights, so a justice perspective treats poverty as a rights failure rather than a matter of charity. In other words, people are owed these things, not given them as a favour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.b] Justice questions start from the scale of the problem. The figures below show how many people are extremely poor, where they live, and how unevenly wealth is shared.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.0] About 700 million people lived in extreme poverty in 2022, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa (the part of Africa south of the Sahara). The World Bank, the UN-linked development lender, measures extreme poverty as living on under 2.15 US dollars a day (2017 prices).
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.1] The share of the world's population in extreme poverty fell from over a third in 1990 to under a tenth by 2019, so the trend is strongly downward even though the numbers remain large.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.2] The richest 10% own about 76% of global wealth, while the poorest half own about 2% (World Inequality Report 2022). This shows how unequal the sharing of wealth is.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.b] The UN has set global targets to measure progress against poverty and injustice. The first set ran from 2000 to 2015 and the second runs to 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.0] The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000-2015) had eight goals. The target of halving the share of people in extreme poverty from 1990 was met by 2010, five years early, largely thanks to China.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.1] The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were adopted by the UN in September 2015. They have 17 goals, to be met by 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.2] The SDGs target poverty (SDG 1), hunger (SDG 2), quality education (SDG 4), gender equality (SDG 5), reduced inequalities (SDG 10) and climate action (SDG 13). Other targets cover health, clean water and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.3] The world is off track to meet most SDG targets by 2030, so shortfalls remain alongside the progress.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.b] Trade rules and prices can leave poor countries poorer. Many depend on selling raw materials, and the biggest profits are made further along the chain, in rich countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.0] Many developing countries depend on exporting a few primary commodities (raw materials such as coffee, cocoa, cotton and minerals), whose prices are low and unstable. This makes their income hard to plan around.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.1] The terms of trade (the amount of imports a country's exports can buy) tend to move against these countries. Their exports buy fewer imports over time, while they import manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.2] Most of the final price of coffee, chocolate or clothing goes to processors, brands and retailers in rich countries. The grower, at the start of the value chain (the steps from raw material to finished product), receives a small share.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.3] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials. This discourages poor countries from processing their own crops, so they stay stuck exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.b] Rich countries also help their own farmers with money, and this can push down the prices poor farmers receive. Cotton is the clearest example.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.0] Rich-country farm subsidies (government payments to farmers), such as US cotton subsidies and the EU's Common Agricultural Policy in the past, let surpluses be sold cheaply abroad. This is called dumping, and it undercuts poor farmers who cannot match the low price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.1] The Cotton-4 West African producers, Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad and Mali, were harmed by these subsidies.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.2] The WTO (World Trade Organization, the body that rules on trade disputes) ruled in Brazil's favour against US cotton subsidies in 2004-05.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.b] Governments and trade bodies have tried to make trade fairer for poor countries, with mixed success. Fair trade is a third response, because it works through consumers rather than governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.0] The WTO Doha Development Round (a set of global trade talks meant to help developing countries) was launched in 2001 but has stalled without agreement.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.1] The EU's 'Everything But Arms' scheme (2001) gives the least developed countries duty-free (no tariffs) and quota-free (no limit on amounts) access to the EU for all products except arms and ammunition.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.b] Fair trade is a trading model in which producers receive guaranteed terms and the Fairtrade Mark identifies certified products. It tries to give growers a bigger and steadier share than the normal market does.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.0] The Fairtrade Minimum Price covers the cost of sustainable production. It is a guaranteed floor, not a guaranteed higher price: when the market price is higher, producers receive the market price plus the premium.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.1] The Fairtrade Premium is paid to producer co-operatives (groups of farmers who work together and share the benefits) for community projects such as schools, clinics and clean water.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.2] Fair trade also requires long-term contracts, decent working conditions, no child labour, environmental standards and democratically run co-operatives.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.3] Fair trade covers coffee, tea, cocoa, bananas, sugar and cotton, for example, which are the kinds of crops whose growers get a small share of the price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.4] The first fair trade label, Max Havelaar, was launched in the Netherlands in 1988 for Mexican coffee. Fairtrade International now coordinates the Fairtrade Mark.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.b] Ireland has its own fair trade links, but fair trade has weaknesses that an evaluation should include.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.0] Fairtrade Ireland (Fairtrade Mark Ireland) licenses the Fairtrade Mark in Ireland, meaning it gives permission for products to carry it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.1] Irish towns and schools can apply for Fairtrade status. Fairtrade Fortnight takes place each year in late February and March.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.2] Limit: fair trade makes up only a tiny share of world trade, so it reaches few producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.3] Limit: certified products can cost consumers more, and certification is expensive for small producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.4] Limit: fair trade does not change the trade rules that cause unfair trade in the first place, so it treats the symptoms rather than the cause.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.b] In the 1970s and 1980s, cheap lending turned into a debt crisis for many developing countries. Each step below made repayment harder.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.0] 1. When oil prices rose sharply, oil-exporting countries deposited their surplus money in banks. Banks lent these deposits, called petrodollars, to developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.1] 2. Interest rates then rose sharply and commodity prices fell. Borrowers earned less from their exports while owing more, so many could not afford repayments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.2] 3. Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts, starting the Latin American debt crisis.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.3] 4. Many African and Latin American countries could not repay their debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.b] Heavy debt took money away from the people it was meant to help, and in some cases the people repaying had never benefited from the loan.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.0] Governments spent more on debt repayments than on health and education, so public services suffered.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.1] Countries took new loans to repay old ones, so the debt grew.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.2] The 1980s 'lost decade' hit Latin America and Africa hard, because debt blocked progress in those years.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.3] Some debts were 'odious': dictators borrowed the money and stole or wasted it, yet the people were made to repay it. This is the clearest justice argument against debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.b] Lenders did not simply hand over money. The IMF (International Monetary Fund) and the World Bank attached conditions to their loans, which is different from tied aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.0] IMF and World Bank loans came with conditions. These cut public spending, introduced school and health fees, privatised state firms (sold them to private owners), removed food subsidies, devalued the currency (lowered its value against others) and opened markets to imports.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.1] Structural adjustment programmes, as these packages of conditions were called, were criticised for hurting the poorest people most. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.b] Campaigns and lenders eventually cancelled some debt for the poorest countries, with conditions. Relief had real results, but debt is building up again.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.0] The Jubilee 2000 campaign pressed for debt cancellation by the millennium, so that poor countries could stop repaying old debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.1] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative was launched in 1996 by the IMF and World Bank.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.2] The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005) followed the G8 summit (a meeting of leaders of major rich countries) at Gleneagles, Scotland, in July 2005. It was backed by the Make Poverty History campaign and the Live 8 concerts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.3] More than 30 countries, most in Africa, have received debt relief under HIPC, on condition that the savings go to poverty reduction.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.4] Relief has funded results such as free primary education in some relieved countries, so more children are in school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.5] Debt is rising again in the 2020s, so relief has not ended the problem.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.b] Aid is help given to poorer countries. It comes in different forms, depending on its purpose, who gives it and how it is channelled.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.0] Emergency (humanitarian) aid responds to disasters and famines, so it is short-term and urgent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.1] Long-term development aid funds projects that build lasting capacity, such as a country's ability to meet its own needs.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.2] Bilateral aid passes from one government to another, so the donor government decides directly where it goes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.3] Multilateral aid is channelled through bodies such as the UN, the World Bank and the EU, so several donors pool their money.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.4] NGO (voluntary) aid is given by non-governmental organisations, which are charities independent of governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.5] Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, so it benefits the donor as well as the receiver.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.b] The UN has set a target for how much rich countries should give, and most do not meet it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.0] The UN target, set in 1970, is that rich countries give 0.7% of their gross national income (the total income earned by a country's people and firms) as official development aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.1] Norway, Sweden, Luxembourg and Denmark have reached or exceeded the target in recent years, so only a few countries reach it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.2] Ireland has not yet reached the target (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.b] Aid is neither all good nor all bad. A balanced answer gives both sides, with reasons, and ends with what better aid looks like.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.0] Helps: aid saves lives in emergencies and funds schools, clinics, wells and vaccination programmes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.1] Helps: aid builds roads and infrastructure, trains teachers, nurses and farmers, and supports women's groups and small businesses.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.2] Hinders: tied aid benefits donor firms and may supply unsuitable technology, because the receiver must buy what the donor sells.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.3] Hinders: corrupt elites can steal aid, and food aid can undercut local farmers, who cannot compete with free food.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.4] Hinders: aid can create dependency instead of self-reliance, and loans add to debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.5] Hinders: donors' political or strategic interests decide who gets aid, rather than need.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.6] Hinders: large prestige projects, such as dams, can displace people.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.7] Better aid is untied (not required to be spent in the donor country), targeted at the poorest, and designed with local participation and ownership. It supports self-help and co-operatives and is transparent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.b] Ireland gives aid through its government programme and through voluntary organisations, many of them founded in response to particular crises.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.0] Irish Aid is Ireland's official aid programme, part of the Department of Foreign Affairs. Its bilateral programme (government to government) began in 1974.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.1] Long-term partner countries include Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia in sub-Saharan Africa, and Vietnam.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.2] Ireland's aid is untied. 'A Better World' (2019), Ireland's international development policy, focuses on the furthest behind, gender equality, climate and governance.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.3] Concern Worldwide was founded in 1968 in response to the famine in Biafra during Nigeria's civil war.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.4] Trócaire was founded in 1973 by the Irish Catholic bishops. Its Lenten 'Trócaire box' raises funds, and it campaigns on human rights and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.5] GOAL was founded in 1977 by John O'Shea.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.6] Live Aid concerts in July 1985, organised by Bob Geldof, responded to the 1984-85 Ethiopian famine.
[geo-economic-development-s4.16.b] Ireland's past gives it a reason to care about hunger elsewhere.
[geo-economic-development-s4.16.0] The Great Famine (1845-49) killed about 1 million people and led about 1 million to emigrate. This history is one reason for Irish solidarity (support and fellow feeling) with developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.b] Multinationals (companies operating in many countries) move work to where labour is cheapest. This brings jobs, but it can also bring exploitation.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.0] Multinationals locate labour-intensive work (work needing many workers), such as clothing, electronics assembly and mining, in low-wage countries, because wage costs are lowest there.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.1] Benefits for workers include jobs, wages higher than farm work, and skills.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.2] Exploitation includes very low wages, long hours, unsafe factories, banned trade unions (organisations of workers) and child labour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.b] The collapse of Rana Plaza shows what can happen when global brands rely on cheap suppliers whose safety standards are not checked.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.0] Rana Plaza, an eight-storey building in Savar near Dhaka, housed garment factories. It collapsed on 24 April 2013, killing about 1,130 people and injuring about 2,500.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.1] Cracks had appeared the day before, but workers were ordered back in.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.2] The factories supplied Western brands, including Primark, which was founded in Dublin in 1969 as Penneys. Primark paid compensation to the victims.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.3] The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh was signed in May 2013 by global brands and trade unions, and eventually more than 200 brands joined. It provided independent factory inspections.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.4] Garments make up over four-fifths of Bangladesh's exports (2020s), so the country depends heavily on this industry.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.5] The case shows how brands profit from low-cost factories while the safety of workers depends on local suppliers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.b] Child labour is one of the worst forms of exploitation, and it appears in the supply chains of everyday products.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.0] About 160 million children were in child labour worldwide in 2020 (ILO and UNICEF), about 70% of them in agriculture.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.1] Child labour occurs in cocoa farming and in mining, for example, so everyday products such as chocolate and batteries can be linked to it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.2] The DR Congo produces about 70% of the world's cobalt, which is used in rechargeable batteries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.3] Amnesty International (2016) documented child labour in the artisanal (hand-dug) cobalt mines of the DR Congo.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.b] Exploitation also involves land. Poor countries and poor communities can lose the land they depend on.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.0] Foreign companies and governments buy or lease large areas of farmland in poor countries, a practice called land grabbing.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.1] Indigenous peoples (the original inhabitants of a region) lose land to mining, ranching and logging, as in the Amazon example in the Brazil section of Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.b] Pressure to improve conditions comes from consumers, from the law and from workers themselves.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.0] Ethical consumer campaigns and company codes of conduct (a company's own rules for how suppliers must treat workers) put pressure on brands.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.1] The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, adopted in 2024, requires very large companies to identify and address human-rights and environmental harm in their supply chains.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.2] Trade unions represent workers and campaign for safer conditions and better wages.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.b] Climate change is an injustice because the countries that caused it least suffer most from it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.0] The poorest countries have contributed least to greenhouse gas emissions (the gases that cause global warming) but suffer most from climate change.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.1] Bangladesh is a low-lying delta vulnerable to cyclones, floods and sea-level rise, because so much of its land is barely above the sea.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.2] Small island states such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives are threatened by sea-level rise, because their land is low.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.3] Sub-Saharan Africa has produced only a small share of historical emissions, yet its dry regions suffer drought.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.4] Responses include climate finance (money from rich countries to help poorer ones cope) and the Loss and Damage Fund, agreed at COP27 in Sharm el-Sheikh, Egypt (2022) and made operational at COP28 in Dubai (2023).
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.5] The Mary Robinson Foundation - Climate Justice was set up in 2010 by Mary Robinson, who was President of Ireland from 1990 to 1997 and UN High Commissioner for Human Rights from 1997 to 2002.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.b] A justice evaluation weighs real progress against the problems that remain.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.0] Extreme poverty has fallen greatly since 1990, especially in China. Child mortality has fallen, and more children attend school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.1] Sub-Saharan Africa lags behind other regions.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.2] Inequality within and between countries is high, so gains are not shared equally.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.3] Conflict, such as the war in Sudan, and climate change are reversing gains. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.4] The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 caused the first rise in global extreme poverty in about a generation (World Bank).
[geo-economic-development-s4.24.b] Justice is not only for governments. Individuals and Ireland can act.
[geo-economic-development-s4.24.0] Individuals can buy fair trade, campaign, and support NGOs. Ireland can press governments on aid, trade and debt policy.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s3: 3. Case study of a developing economy
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.b] Colonialism is the control of one territory and its people by a foreign power. The colonising country rules the colony (the territory it controls), settles it and/or exploits its resources for its own benefit, and that purpose shaped the colony's economy.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.0] Imperialism is the wider policy of building an empire, that is, a group of territories ruled from one centre. Colonialism is how that policy works on the ground in each territory.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.1] The main European colonial powers were Britain, France, Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands, Belgium and Germany.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.2] The Americas were colonised first, then India and Asia, and then Africa, so the periods of colonial rule differ from region to region.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.3] Africa was divided among European powers in the Scramble for Africa after the Berlin Conference (1884–85), which set the rules for European claims. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia in Africa were still independent of European rule.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.4] Colonies supplied cheap raw materials (unprocessed goods) such as cotton, sugar, rubber and minerals to the colonial power, and bought its manufactured goods. This made the colony a supplier and a market rather than a manufacturer.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.5] Local industries that competed with these imports were destroyed, so the colony lost its own manufacturing.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.6] Plantations (large farms growing one crop for sale) and mines used forced or slave labour, which kept the cost of raw materials low.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.7] Food crops were replaced by cash crops (crops grown for sale and export, not for local food), a pattern called monoculture, meaning growing mainly one crop.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.b] Colonial powers built transport and institutions to serve their own needs, and independent states inherited the results. The legacy (what was left behind) was economic, political and social.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.0] Railways and roads ran from the interior to the ports so that raw materials could be exported. They were not built to link regions with each other, so movement between regions stayed poor.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.1] Ports and capitals grew into the main cities, so development was concentrated on the coast.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.2] Positive legacies are debated: railways, ports, some irrigation, legal and administrative systems, and a world language. Present both sides in an answer.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.3] Most of this infrastructure was built to serve the colonial power rather than the colony's own people, which is why its benefits are debated.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.4] Artificial borders drawn in Europe split some ethnic groups and grouped rival ones together, which created tension inside the new states.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.5] The colonial power's policy of divide and rule (keeping a population weak by setting its groups against one another) set groups against one another.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.6] European languages and religions spread, education reached only a small elite, racial hierarchies (rankings of people by race) were enforced, and land was owned by a few.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.7] Weak institutions (governments, courts and civil services) were handed over at independence, and conflict and coups (seizures of power by the army) followed in many new states.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.b] After independence, governments tried a sequence of strategies to make their economies work in a world market dominated by rich countries. Each country below follows some of these stages.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.0] Import substitution industrialisation: new home industries were protected from foreign competition by tariffs (taxes on imported goods), so local firms could make what had been imported.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.1] Neo-colonialism (new colonialism without direct rule): former colonies often stayed dependent on exporting a few raw materials to rich countries at low and unstable prices, and on foreign loans and companies.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.2] Debt crises in the 1980s and 1990s, when countries could not repay what they owed, led to IMF (International Monetary Fund) and World Bank structural adjustment programmes. These cut government spending, privatised state firms (sold them to private owners), opened markets to trade, and devalued the currency.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.3] Liberalisation: opening the economy to trade and foreign investment, and privatising state firms.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.4] Export diversification: moving beyond a few raw materials into manufactured and service exports, so the country is less exposed to one price.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.5] Joining the World Trade Organisation (WTO, the body that sets rules for trade between countries) and regional trading blocs (groups of countries that trade on easier terms).
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.6] Growth of services and of remittances (money sent home by emigrants).
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.b] British control of India grew in two steps: first a trading company, then the Crown. Learn the dates, because the answer should name them.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.0] The East India Company (a British trading company) was chartered on 31 December 1600 and first traded through trading posts.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.1] The Battle of Plassey in 1757, won by Robert Clive, began Company rule in Bengal, so a trading firm became a ruler.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.2] The Rebellion of 1857 led to the Government of India Act 1858, which transferred rule from the Company to the British Crown. This began direct British rule, known as the Raj.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.3] India gained independence, so British rule ended, and was partitioned (divided into separate states) on 15 August 1947.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.b] Under British rule India moved from being a major manufacturer to a supplier of raw materials. This is deindustrialisation, the loss of an economy's industry.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.0] India's large handloom textile industry, including Bengal's fine cottons and Dhaka (now in Bangladesh) muslin, was destroyed by cheap machine-made cloth from the cotton mills of Lancashire in north-west England.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.1] Raw cotton was exported from India to Britain to feed those mills, so India sent out the raw material and bought back the cloth.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.2] Economic historian Angus Maddison estimated India's share of world GDP (the value of all goods and services produced) at about a quarter around 1700 and about 4% by 1950. These are estimates, not exact figures.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.3] Cash crops replaced food crops: cotton, jute, indigo (blue dye), tea from plantations in Assam from the 1830s–40s, and opium grown in India and exported to China.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.4] Jute was processed in mills along the Hooghly near Calcutta, so the crop was processed near the port.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.b] British land taxes and the flow of money to Britain left Indian farmers poor and the country vulnerable to famine.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.0] The Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793, Lord Cornwallis) fixed land revenue (the tax paid on land) and collected it through zamindars, landlords who passed the tax burden on to peasants.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.1] Heavy land taxes left the peasantry in debt and poverty, because the tax burden was passed on to them.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.2] Famines (severe food shortages causing mass death) occurred under British rule: the Great Famine of 1876–78 and the Bengal famine of 1943.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.3] The Bengal famine killed about 3 million people (estimates range from about 2 to 3 million). Grain was still exported while people starved.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.4] The drain of wealth: revenue, profits and salaries were sent from India to Britain instead of being spent in India. Dadabhai Naoroji set out this argument in 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' (1901).
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.b] India's railways show how colonial infrastructure served the colonial power first.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.0] The first passenger train ran from Bombay to Thane on 16 April 1853.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.1] The railway network was built mainly to carry raw materials to the ports of Bombay and Calcutta and to move troops, so it served exports and control rather than Indian travellers.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.2] Indians came to use the railways heavily, so they brought some benefit, although they were not built for Indian passengers' needs.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.3] Ports and colonial cities grew, including Bombay (now Mumbai) and Calcutta (now Kolkata), because trade passed through them.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.4] Calcutta was the capital of British India, until 1911, when the capital moved to Delhi.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.b] British rule left India with lasting institutions, a language and a painful division, but also with a movement that ended it.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.0] English is the language of government and business. It has since become an asset for India's IT industry. There is more on this in India in A Continental / Subcontinental Region.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.1] India inherited a civil service, courts and a parliamentary system from British rule, so it did not start without institutions.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.2] At independence, literacy was very low: about 18% in the 1951 census. Life expectancy was around 32 years.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.3] Partition on 15 August 1947, the division of British India into separate states, displaced more than 10 million people, and hundreds of thousands were killed in the violence.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.4] The Kashmir region has been disputed with Pakistan since 1947, so Partition left a lasting political conflict.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.5] Mahatma Gandhi led a non-violent independence movement. Its methods included the boycott of British cloth, the promotion of swadeshi (buying Indian goods) and the spinning of khadi (hand-spun cloth), which struck at the cloth trade colonialism had built.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.6] The Salt March of 1930 was a major act of resistance against British control, which is why it is a key example of resistance.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.b] After independence India's first adjustment was to let the state plan and own heavy industry, behind trade barriers.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.0] The First Five-Year Plan (1951), under Jawaharlal Nehru, began a planned economy, in which the government sets targets for the economy in advance.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.1] Import substitution (making at home what had been imported) behind high tariffs protected home industry from foreign competition.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.2] The state built heavy industry, including steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.3] The 'Licence Raj' meant that firms needed government licences to set up or expand, which restricted competition and investment.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.4] Growth was slow, which is why it earned the nickname the 'Hindu rate of growth'.
[geo-economic-development-s3.9.b] Food was a second adjustment, because the colonial economy had left India short of food.
[geo-economic-development-s3.9.0] The Green Revolution used high-yield seed varieties with fertiliser and irrigation to raise crop output.
[geo-economic-development-s3.9.1] Food-grain output rose from about 51 million tonnes in 1950–51 to about 330 million tonnes in 2022–23.
[geo-economic-development-s3.9.2] As a result India became self-sufficient in food, meaning it could feed itself without relying on imports.
[geo-economic-development-s3.10.b] By 1991 state-led development had run into a financial crisis. India answered it by opening its economy, the third adjustment.
[geo-economic-development-s3.10.0] By 1991 foreign-exchange reserves (the foreign currency a country holds to pay for imports) had fallen to only enough for a few weeks of imports.
[geo-economic-development-s3.10.1] India pledged gold to raise loans and borrowed from the IMF (International Monetary Fund).
[geo-economic-development-s3.10.2] Reforms under Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh removed most licences, cut tariffs, welcomed foreign investment, privatised some state firms and devalued the rupee (lowered its value against other currencies).
[geo-economic-development-s3.10.3] India was a founding member of the WTO (World Trade Organisation) in 1995.
[geo-economic-development-s3.11.b] The 1991 reforms changed India's economy, so growth and the kind of work people did both shifted.
[geo-economic-development-s3.11.0] Growth became fast, and services (such as IT, as opposed to farming or manufacturing) now make up over half of GDP.
[geo-economic-development-s3.11.1] The IT and outsourcing industry (firms doing work for foreign companies) is based in Bengaluru, with firms such as TCS and Infosys.
[geo-economic-development-s3.11.2] Multinationals (firms operating in many countries) invest in India, encouraged by the Make in India programme (2014).
[geo-economic-development-s3.11.3] India is among the five largest economies in the world by GDP (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s3.11.4] India is the world's largest recipient of remittances (money sent home by emigrants) (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s3.12.b] Fast growth has not reached everyone. These problems are the other half of any answer on adjustment.
[geo-economic-development-s3.12.0] About nine-tenths of Indian workers are in the informal sector (2010s–2020s), without contracts, pensions or legal protection.
[geo-economic-development-s3.12.1] Agriculture still employs about 55% of workers (Census 2011), with low productivity, meaning each worker produces little.
[geo-economic-development-s3.12.2] Inequality exists between states: literacy was about 94% in Kerala and about 62% in Bihar (Census 2011).
[geo-economic-development-s3.12.3] Inequality also exists between rich and poor, so growth has not reached everyone.
[geo-economic-development-s3.12.4] India's HDI (Human Development Index, a measure combining income, health and education) is 0.644, 134th of 193 countries, in the medium category (Human Development Report 2023/24).
[geo-economic-development-s3.12.5] Infrastructure gaps and pollution remain serious problems, so growth is held back.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.b] Brazil was colonised by Portugal, not Spain, and its economy ran in cycles, each built on one export. Each cycle enriched Portugal and a small elite rather than the colony as a whole.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.0] The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) gave the east of South America to Portugal.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.1] Pedro Álvares Cabral reached Brazil on 22 April 1500, which is why the Portuguese claim began there.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.2] Brazil remained a Portuguese colony until independence on 7 September 1822, under Dom Pedro I.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.3] Brazilwood, a red dye wood, gave the country its name, so the colony took its name from an export.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.4] Sugar plantations in Pernambuco and Bahia in the North-East were the first major export.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.5] Gold was mined in Minas Gerais ('general mines'), another export cycle.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.6] Coffee was grown in São Paulo state in the 19th century, an export from the South-East.
[geo-economic-development-s3.13.7] The Amazon rubber boom centred on Manaus, from the late 19th century to about 1912, collapsed when plantations in British South-East Asia, grown from Brazilian seeds, undercut it.

[geo-economic-development-s3.14.0] About 4 to 5 million enslaved Africans were taken to Brazil, more than to any other country in the Americas. They worked on plantations and in mines. This is an estimate.
[geo-economic-development-s3.14.1] Slavery was abolished by the Lei Áurea (Golden Law) on 13 May 1888. Brazil was the last country in the Americas to abolish it.
[geo-economic-development-s3.14.2] The legacy of slavery includes racial inequality: Afro-Brazilians are over-represented among the poor and in favelas (poor urban neighbourhoods).
[geo-economic-development-s3.14.3] Indigenous peoples were killed by disease, war and enslavement, and pushed off their land, so they are now a small minority.
[geo-economic-development-s3.14.4] Census 2022 counted about 1.7 million indigenous people, under 1% of Brazil's population of about 203 million.
[geo-economic-development-s3.14.5] Huge estates (latifundia), originating in Portuguese land grants, are owned by a few families, while millions of rural workers are landless.
[geo-economic-development-s3.14.6] Brazil has one of the most unequal patterns of land ownership in the world, which means many rural workers have no land.
[geo-economic-development-s3.14.7] The MST (Landless Workers' Movement), founded in 1984, campaigns for land reform (a fairer sharing of land). Conflict over land in the Amazon continues.
[geo-economic-development-s3.15.b] Because each export cycle sat in one region, its decline left that region poor, and the country stayed tied to exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s3.15.0] When sugar declined, the North-East was left poor.
[geo-economic-development-s3.15.1] Wealth and industry shifted to the South-East: São Paulo (Brazil's largest city and industrial centre), Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais.
[geo-economic-development-s3.15.2] Migrants moved from the North-East to the South-East cities and their favelas, looking for work.
[geo-economic-development-s3.15.3] SUDENE, the agency for developing the North-East, was set up in 1959.
[geo-economic-development-s3.15.4] The colonial pattern of exporting raw materials continued after independence, with coffee dominating exports for much of that period. This left Brazil exposed to changes in world prices.
[geo-economic-development-s3.16.b] From the 1930s Brazil's first adjustment was state-led industrialisation behind trade barriers, replacing imports with goods made at home.
[geo-economic-development-s3.16.0] Getúlio Vargas was president from 1930 to 1945 and from 1951 to 1954. His government promoted state-led industrialisation, meaning the state drove it.
[geo-economic-development-s3.16.1] Petrobras, the state oil company, was founded in 1953, so the state controlled a key industry.
[geo-economic-development-s3.16.2] President Juscelino Kubitschek (1956–61) promised 'fifty years of progress in five' and built a car industry.
[geo-economic-development-s3.16.3] The new capital, Brasília, was inaugurated on 21 April 1960 to open up the interior, since development had been concentrated on the coast.
[geo-economic-development-s3.16.4] The Manaus Free Trade Zone (an area with tax benefits for firms) was created in 1967.
[geo-economic-development-s3.16.5] The 'Brazilian miracle', so called because of very fast growth, about 1968–73, took place under military rule (1964–85).
[geo-economic-development-s3.17.b] Growth was paid for with foreign loans, and the debt later caused a crisis that forced a change of course.
[geo-economic-development-s3.17.0] Brazil borrowed heavily from foreign lenders, which made it exposed when it could not repay.
[geo-economic-development-s3.17.1] The 1980s debt crisis, known as the 'lost decade', brought hyperinflation (prices rising extremely fast) and IMF programmes.
[geo-economic-development-s3.17.2] The Plano Real (1994) ended hyperinflation, which means prices became stable again, and introduced the real as the currency.
[geo-economic-development-s3.18.b] From the 1990s Brazil opened its economy to trade and investment, and a boom in commodities (raw materials traded in bulk) followed, with China as the main buyer.
[geo-economic-development-s3.18.0] The iron-ore miner Vale (formerly Companhia Vale do Rio Doce) was privatised (sold to private owners) in 1997. Its Carajás mines are in Pará.
[geo-economic-development-s3.18.1] Mercosur, the trade bloc of Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay and Brazil, was founded in 1991 by the Treaty of Asunción.
[geo-economic-development-s3.18.2] A commodity export boom sent soya, iron ore, oil, beef and coffee abroad, especially to China.
[geo-economic-development-s3.18.3] China has been Brazil's largest trading partner since 2009, because it buys so many of these commodities.
[geo-economic-development-s3.18.4] Brazil is the world's largest producer and exporter of soya beans and the largest producer of coffee (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s3.18.5] Brazil is a member of BRICS, a group of large emerging economies. The BRIC term was coined in 2001 by Jim O'Neill of Goldman Sachs, and the first BRIC summit was held in 2009.
[geo-economic-development-s3.18.6] Embraer, the aircraft maker in São José dos Campos (São Paulo state), is a high-tech export, which shows exports beyond raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.b] Brazil cut hunger and poverty, but adjustment had costs. Inequality, deforestation and dependence on commodity prices remain.
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.0] Bolsa Família, launched in 2003 under President Lula da Silva, gives cash to poor families on condition that children attend school and are vaccinated.
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.1] Brazil left the UN (FAO) World Hunger Map in 2014, which shows that Bolsa Família and growth cut hunger.
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.2] Brazil's HDI (a measure combining income, health and education) is about 0.76, in the high category (Human Development Report 2023/24).
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.3] Brazil is among the most unequal countries in the world by the Gini coefficient (a measure of how unequally income is shared).
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.4] Cattle ranching is the largest driver of Amazon deforestation, with soya expansion also contributing.
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.5] Commodity dependence brought a recession in 2015–16, when commodity prices fell.
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.6] The North-East is still the poorest region of Brazil, so the colonial regional gap has lasted.
[geo-economic-development-s3.19.7] The WTO ruled in Brazil's favour against US cotton subsidies (government payments to farmers) in 2004–05. There is more on this in Global issues and a justice perspective.
[geo-economic-development-s3.20.b] Sudan here means Sudan before 2011, including today's South Sudan. It was ruled by Egypt, then by a Mahdist state, and then in practice by Britain.
[geo-economic-development-s3.20.0] Egyptian conquest began in 1821, so Sudan was first ruled from Egypt.
[geo-economic-development-s3.20.1] The Mahdist state (1885–98) was led by Muhammad Ahmad, 'the Mahdi'. Its forces took Khartoum in 1885 and killed General Gordon.
[geo-economic-development-s3.20.2] British forces under Kitchener defeated the Mahdists at the Battle of Omdurman on 2 September 1898.
[geo-economic-development-s3.20.3] The Anglo-Egyptian Condominium (1899–1956), a joint-rule arrangement, was in practice British rule.
[geo-economic-development-s3.20.4] Sudan became independent on 1 January 1956. Its capital, Khartoum, lies at the confluence (meeting point) of the Blue and White Niles.
[geo-economic-development-s3.21.b] The Gezira Scheme shows a colonial economy built for the colonial power. It turned Sudan into a supplier of one crop.
[geo-economic-development-s3.21.0] The Gezira Scheme is an irrigation scheme between the Blue and White Niles, south of Khartoum.
[geo-economic-development-s3.21.1] The Sennar Dam on the Blue Nile was completed in 1925 to water the scheme, so cotton could grow on dry land. Do not confuse it with the Aswan Dam, which is in Egypt.
[geo-economic-development-s3.21.2] The scheme covers nearly 900,000 hectares and is one of the world's largest irrigation schemes.
[geo-economic-development-s3.21.3] It grew long-staple cotton for the Lancashire mills, not for Sudan's own industry.
[geo-economic-development-s3.21.4] Tenant farmers (farmers who rent their land) grew the cotton under strict control.
[geo-economic-development-s3.21.5] Cotton became Sudan's main export, so the economy depended on one crop and its world price.
[geo-economic-development-s3.21.6] Investment was concentrated in the Nile valley around Khartoum and Gezira. The south, Darfur and other peripheral regions (areas far from the centre) were neglected.
[geo-economic-development-s3.22.b] Britain ran Sudan as two regions and then joined them. This is the root of Sudan's civil wars.
[geo-economic-development-s3.22.0] From the 1920s Britain ran the Arab and Muslim north and the African, largely Christian and animist (following traditional spiritual beliefs) south as separate regions.
[geo-economic-development-s3.22.1] The Closed District Ordinance (1922) restricted northerners' entry to the south, so the two regions were kept apart.
[geo-economic-development-s3.22.2] English and Christian missions worked in the south, while Arabic was used in the north, so the two regions grew apart.
[geo-economic-development-s3.22.3] The regions were joined into one state at independence, and power went to a northern elite.
[geo-economic-development-s3.22.4] Colonial boundaries enclosed many ethnic, language and religious groups within one state. This laid the basis for civil war.
[geo-economic-development-s3.23.b] The colonial legacy of division led to decades of war and finally to the separation of the south.
[geo-economic-development-s3.23.0] The first civil war (1955–72) ended with the Addis Ababa Agreement (1972).
[geo-economic-development-s3.23.1] The second civil war (1983–2005) killed about 2 million people. It ended with the Comprehensive Peace Agreement in January 2005.
[geo-economic-development-s3.23.2] The Darfur conflict began in 2003. The UN estimated about 300,000 deaths, and the International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for President al-Bashir in 2009.
[geo-economic-development-s3.23.3] A referendum (a public vote on one question) in January 2011 gave about 99% support for independence. South Sudan became independent on 9 July 2011, with its capital at Juba.
[geo-economic-development-s3.23.4] Sudan here means Sudan before 2011, which included today's South Sudan.
[geo-economic-development-s3.24.b] Independence did not change the economy's basic shape. Sudan stayed tied to cotton and slid into debt.
[geo-economic-development-s3.24.0] Sudan remained dependent on Gezira cotton after independence, so it was still exposed to the world price.
[geo-economic-development-s3.24.1] Governments attempted state-led development, so the state tried to drive growth.
[geo-economic-development-s3.24.2] Heavy borrowing led to debt, so IMF programmes followed.
[geo-economic-development-s3.24.3] A famine caused by drought and war struck the country.
[geo-economic-development-s3.25.b] Oil brought fast growth from 1999, but most of it lay in the south, so Sudan lost much of it when the south separated.
[geo-economic-development-s3.25.0] Oil exports began in 1999 through a pipeline to Port Sudan on the Red Sea.
[geo-economic-development-s3.25.1] The main buyers and investors were Chinese state oil firms, with Malaysian and Indian firms also involved. China was the main buyer.
[geo-economic-development-s3.25.2] Oil became the main export, and GDP grew fast in the 2000s.
[geo-economic-development-s3.25.3] Most oil lay in the south. South Sudan's independence in 2011 took about three-quarters of Sudan's oil production.
[geo-economic-development-s3.25.4] The loss of oil caused an economic crisis, with inflation (rising prices) and protests.
[geo-economic-development-s3.25.5] South Sudan is landlocked, so it still exports its oil through Sudan's pipeline.
[geo-economic-development-s3.26.b] Sanctions and debt cut Sudan off from the global economy, while its other exports remained raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s3.26.0] The United States listed Sudan as a 'state sponsor of terrorism' from 1993 to 2020, when the listing was removed in December 2020. The sanctions (penalties restricting trade and finance) cut Sudan off from Western banks and investment.
[geo-economic-development-s3.26.1] Sudan has one of the world's largest debt burdens. It reached the HIPC decision point in 2021, a step in a debt-relief process for heavily indebted poor countries.
[geo-economic-development-s3.26.2] Sudan is the world's largest producer of gum arabic, from acacia trees, which is used in food and drinks.
[geo-economic-development-s3.26.3] Other exports include gold, livestock and sesame, so the economy still relies on primary products.
[geo-economic-development-s3.27.b] Political instability has now reversed Sudan's development.
[geo-economic-development-s3.27.0] President Omar al-Bashir was in power from 1989 to 2019 and was removed in April 2019 after mass protests.
[geo-economic-development-s3.27.1] A military coup took place in October 2021, so the 2019 change did not bring stable civilian rule.
[geo-economic-development-s3.27.2] Since 15 April 2023 the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have been at war.
[geo-economic-development-s3.27.3] More than 10 million people had been displaced (forced to leave their homes) by 2024, the largest displacement crisis in the world.
[geo-economic-development-s3.27.4] Famine was confirmed in Zamzam camp, North Darfur, in August 2024.
[geo-economic-development-s3.27.5] Development has gone into reverse because of war and displacement, and Sudan is in the low human development category.
