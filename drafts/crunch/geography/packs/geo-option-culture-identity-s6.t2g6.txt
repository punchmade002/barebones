[geo-region-european-s3.0.b] Ireland is a single island but two political regions: the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland. Their economic, political and cultural activities interact across a border. Both regions exist because of the 1921 partition (the division of the island into two political units), and their cultural groups are not evenly matched, so it helps to know the starting facts about each.
[geo-region-european-s3.0.0] Northern Ireland was established in 1921 with six counties: Antrim, Armagh, Down, Fermanagh, Londonderry (Derry) and Tyrone. It is part of the United Kingdom, so it is governed within the UK, not by the Irish state.
[geo-region-european-s3.0.1] The Irish Free State was established in 1922. Ireland left the Commonwealth (the association of former British territories) and was declared a republic in 1949.
[geo-region-european-s3.0.2] The Republic has about 5.15 million people (Census 2022, the official population count) and Northern Ireland about 1.9 million (Census 2021), so the two regions differ greatly in size.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.b] Northern Ireland's population is divided into two main cultural groups. Their identity, religion, schooling and language differ, and this is the background to the political interaction that follows.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.0] Nationalists, mostly Catholic, identify as Irish. Unionists (people who want Northern Ireland to stay in the UK), mostly Protestant, identify as British. Some people identify as Northern Irish instead.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.1] In Census 2021, by religion (or religion brought up in), Catholic was about 46% and Protestant and other Christian about 43%. This was the first census in which Catholics outnumbered Protestants.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.2] In the Republic, Census 2022 gave Catholic about 69% and no religion about 14%, which shows the Republic's population is far more uniformly Catholic.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.3] Most children in Northern Ireland attend schools of their own community, and housing is often segregated by community (the two groups live in separate areas), so everyday contact between the groups is limited.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.4] Irish and Ulster-Scots (a Scots-based language tradition of Ulster) are officially recognised under the Identity and Language (Northern Ireland) Act 2022.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.b] The two communities disagreed about whether Northern Ireland should be in the UK or united with the Republic, and the disagreement turned violent. The Good Friday Agreement of 1998 set up a new way for them to share power.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.0] The Troubles, from the late 1960s to 1998, killed over 3,500 people and left the two communities divided by violence and political deadlock (no side able to make decisions).
[geo-region-european-s3.2.1] The Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement was signed on 10 April 1998. A referendum (a public vote) approved it on 22 May 1998: about 71% Yes in Northern Ireland and about 94% Yes in the Republic.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.2] The Republic changed Articles 2 and 3 of its Constitution, which had claimed Northern Ireland as part of the national territory, so the Republic gave up that claim.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.3] Power-sharing: a Northern Ireland Assembly (its elected parliament) in which ministers from both communities share government, so neither side can govern alone.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.4] The North/South Ministerial Council and other cross-border bodies link the Irish government with the Northern Ireland executive (its government), so the two regions co-operate on shared issues.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.5] Consent principle: Northern Ireland remains in the UK unless a majority of its people vote otherwise. Under the Agreement, people may hold Irish or British citizenship or both.
[geo-region-european-s3.3.b] Power-sharing needs both communities to take part, so it is fragile. The Assembly shows how political interaction can break down and recover.
[geo-region-european-s3.3.0] The Assembly has been suspended several times, meaning it stopped working and its powers were paused, for example in the two-year collapse before 2024.
[geo-region-european-s3.3.1] After a two-year collapse it was restored in February 2024, when Michelle O'Neill became the first nationalist First Minister.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.b] After 1998, the border mattered less in economic life and the two regions worked together more. Conflict had held this back, so peace let activity grow across the whole island.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.0] Conflict deterred investment and tourism; after 1998, falling violence allowed both to recover on the island as a whole.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.1] Cross-border bodies include InterTradeIreland and Waterways Ireland. Tourism Ireland markets the whole island abroad, so visitors see one destination, not two.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.2] A Single Electricity Market for the whole island has operated since 2007, so electricity is bought and sold as one all-island market.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.3] The Dublin–Belfast Enterprise rail service and the M1/A1 road corridor link the two main cities, which makes cross-border travel and trade easier.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.4] The International Fund for Ireland was set up in 1986. The EU PEACE programme began in 1995 as PEACE I; the current PEACE PLUS programme runs from 2021 to 2027. Both fund peace-building and co-operation.
[geo-region-european-s3.5.b] Culture and sport do not follow the border either. Several organisations cover the whole island, although some cultural symbols still divide communities.
[geo-region-european-s3.5.0] The GAA (Gaelic Athletic Association), the Irish rugby team, the Catholic Church and the Church of Ireland are organised on an all-island basis, so they treat Ireland as one unit.
[geo-region-european-s3.5.1] The Catholic and Church of Ireland archbishops of Armagh are each Primate of All Ireland (the senior bishop of their church across the island).
[geo-region-european-s3.5.2] The Irish language and Irish sport cross the border, while parades and flags remain sources of tension between communities, because they express one community's identity.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.b] Brexit (the UK leaving the EU) shows the three processes interacting at the border. It affected trade, politics and identity on the island all at once.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.0] In the UK referendum on EU membership, 23 June 2016, about 52% voted Leave overall; about 56% voted Remain in Northern Ireland and about 62% Remain in Scotland.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.1] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition period (when old rules still applied) ended on 31 December 2020. The 2016 referendum did not itself end membership.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.2] The Irish–Northern Irish land border is about 499 km long and became the UK–EU land border, which raised the question of customs checks between the two regions.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.3] The Northern Ireland Protocol kept Northern Ireland aligned with many EU single-market rules for goods, avoiding checks at the land border (a hard border). The Windsor Framework (February 2023) revised it.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.4] The Common Travel Area between Ireland and the UK has existed since 1923. Irish passport holders in Northern Ireland keep EU free movement, the right to live and work in the EU.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.b] In Belgium the language communities and the political regions do not coincide, so politics is organised around language. Belgium is the main European example of cultural groups and political regions interacting.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.0] Belgium became independent in 1830. Its population is about 11.8 million (2024).
[geo-region-european-s3.7.1] Flanders (about 6.8 million) is Dutch-speaking in the north. Wallonia (about 3.7 million) is French-speaking in the south. Brussels-Capital (about 1.2 million) is officially bilingual, so both languages are used there.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.2] A German-speaking community in the east has fewer than 80,000 people.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.3] Dutch speakers are about 6 in 10 of the population and French speakers about 4 in 10.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.4] French was long the language of government, courts and universities, so the language of the minority (French, about 4 in 10) once held the main public institutions.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.b] Language differences in Belgium were sharpened by a shift in wealth between north and south. Politics then split along language lines.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.0] Economic reversal: Wallonia's coal and steel industries (Liège, Charleroi and the Sambre–Meuse valley) declined, while Flanders grew through the port of Antwerp, Europe's second-largest port, and modern industry.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.1] The Catholic University of Leuven split in 1968; its French-speaking section moved to a new town, Louvain-la-Neuve, in Wallonia, so even education separated by language.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.2] There are no nationwide parties: each party stands in one language community only, so no party represents voters in all of Belgium.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.3] Political consequences: Belgium went 541 days without a new government in 2010–11, which shows how hard it is to form a government when each party stands in one language community only.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.4] The Flemish nationalist N-VA has been the largest party since 2010, and its leader Bart De Wever became Prime Minister in February 2025.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.5] Belgium's federal solution (power divided between a national government and regional ones) gives its language communities and regions considerable self-government.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.6] Brussels hosts the main institutions of the EU and the headquarters of NATO (a military alliance).
[geo-region-european-s3.9.b] Other European countries also have cultural groups tied to regions. Some share power peacefully, while others have faced independence movements.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.0] Switzerland has four national languages: German (about 6 in 10), French (about 2 in 10), Italian (under 1 in 10) and Romansh (under 1%).
[geo-region-european-s3.9.1] Switzerland has 26 cantons (its regional units) with wide powers. Power is shared between language groups and cantons, which keeps the country stable.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.2] Basque (Euskara) is unrelated to any other European language. The Basque Country lies in both Spain and France.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.3] ETA, a Basque separatist group, declared a definitive end to violence in 2011 and dissolved in 2018.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.4] Catalonia has its own language, Catalan. It held an independence referendum on 1 October 2017 that Spain's courts declared illegal. Spain imposed direct rule (control from the central government) using Article 155 of its constitution.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.5] The Basque Country and Catalonia are autonomous communities of Spain (regions with self-government) and among its wealthiest regions.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.6] Scottish independence referendum, 18 September 2014: about 55% voted No.
[geo-region-european-s3.10.b] The examples above share one pattern, and a general statement of it can round off a 30-mark answer.
[geo-region-european-s3.10.0] Where cultural and political regions do not match, tension arises.
[geo-region-european-s3.10.1] Outcomes range from devolution or federalism (Belgium, Spain) and power-sharing (Northern Ireland) to violent conflict (the Troubles). Devolution means a central government passes some powers to a region.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.b] Political union is the EU's move beyond economic co-operation towards shared political institutions and decisions.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.0] The Maastricht Treaty created the EU and planned the single currency (its dates are in the s4 notes).
[geo-region-european-s3.11.1] The draft EU Constitution was rejected by French and Dutch voters in 2005, so it did not come into force.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.2] The Lisbon Treaty created a permanent President of the European Council and a High Representative for Foreign Affairs. It extended qualified majority voting and the powers of the European Parliament.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.3] Qualified majority voting: a decision passes when enough member states, representing enough of the EU population, support it. Unanimity requires every member state to agree, so one state can block a decision.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.4] European Parliament: 720 MEPs (Members of the European Parliament, elected by voters) after the 2024 elections; Ireland elects 14.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.5] Competing visions: a federal Europe with stronger central power, or a Europe of nation states co-operating on shared aims.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.6] Eurosceptic parties (parties sceptical of the EU) have grown across Europe, opposing further political integration.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.b] Economic union is the EU's shared market and shared currency. Not every member state takes part in both.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.0] The single market removes barriers to trade, services, capital and labour between member states, so goods, services, money and workers can move freely.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.1] 21 member states use the euro in 2026. Bulgaria joined on 1 January 2026, replacing the lev. Croatia joined in 2023.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.2] Euro countries: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia and Spain.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.3] Not in the euro: Denmark (legal opt-out, a formal exemption); Sweden (voted No in a 2003 referendum and stays out by not joining the exchange-rate mechanism); Poland, Czechia, Hungary and Romania.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.4] The European Central Bank in Frankfurt sets one interest rate for the euro area. Since November 2014 its Single Supervisory Mechanism has supervised the big banks.
[geo-region-european-s3.13.b] A state must meet set economic tests before joining the euro, which keeps weaker economies from harming the shared currency. Once in, people gain several benefits.
[geo-region-european-s3.13.0] Convergence (Maastricht) criteria: low inflation (slow price rises); government deficit (spending above income in a year) no more than 3% of GDP (the value of a country's output); government debt no more than 60% of GDP.
[geo-region-european-s3.13.1] Two more criteria: a stable exchange rate, held for two years in ERM II (a system that keeps a currency steady against the euro); and low long-term interest rates.
[geo-region-european-s3.13.2] Benefits for people in the eurozone: no currency exchange costs; easy price comparison between countries; no exchange-rate risk in trade and travel; generally low interest rates and inflation.
[geo-region-european-s3.14.b] The euro area met serious financial trouble after 2010. A bailout (emergency loans to a government) was needed in some countries.
[geo-region-european-s3.14.0] Ireland's EU/IMF bailout of about €85 billion was agreed in November 2010 and exited in December 2013.
[geo-region-european-s3.14.1] Greece received three bailouts (2010, 2012 and 2015), ending in 2018, which shows the crisis there lasted longer than in Ireland.
[geo-region-european-s3.14.2] NextGenerationEU, agreed in 2020, is a recovery fund of about €750 billion financed by joint EU borrowing, so member states borrow together.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.b] Sovereignty is the power to make decisions for a state. Membership pools some of it; some areas stay national.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.0] Pooling sovereignty means member states share decision-making in areas such as trade and the single market.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.1] Taxation, defence and foreign policy remain largely national, and decisions in these areas are mostly made by unanimity.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.2] Brexit is an example of national sovereignty being reasserted against pooled decision-making.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.3] Article 7 procedures, which could suspend a member's voting rights, were opened against Poland (2017) and Hungary (2018) over rule-of-law concerns (whether governments respect courts and legal limits).
[geo-region-european-s3.16.b] Ireland's own history in the EU gives specific examples of sovereignty being pooled, tested and defended.
[geo-region-european-s3.16.0] Crotty v An Taoiseach (1987): Ireland's Supreme Court ruled that significant EU treaty changes need a referendum.
[geo-region-european-s3.16.1] Irish voters rejected the Nice Treaty (2001) and approved it in 2002. They rejected the Lisbon Treaty in June 2008 and approved it in October 2009.
[geo-region-european-s3.16.2] Ireland's corporation tax (tax on company profits) rate is 12.5%. Under the OECD agreement, a 15% minimum rate applies to large firms (turnover over €750 million) from 2024.
[geo-region-european-s3.16.3] Apple ruling: on 10 September 2024 the EU Court of Justice ruled that Ireland must recover €13 billion, plus interest, in unpaid tax from Apple as illegal state aid (help from a state that unfairly favours one company).
[geo-region-european-s3.16.4] Ireland is militarily neutral and not in NATO. It is outside the Schengen area (the EU zone without passport checks), linked to its Common Travel Area with the UK.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.b] The EU faces a mix of social and economic challenges, and exams ask for both kinds.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.0] Social challenges: an ageing population; migration and asylum pressures; youth unemployment; rising populism (politics that claims to speak for ordinary people against elites) and euroscepticism; regional inequality.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.1] Economic challenges: public debt; competitiveness; energy security; the cost of enlargement; the economic effects of Brexit.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.2] Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine began on 24 February 2022. It caused an energy price shock and a large refugee movement into the EU.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.3] The challenges of political union and sovereignty include disagreement over how far to deepen integration, and the slowness of decisions that need unanimity.
[geo-region-european-s3.18.b] Enlargement means admitting new member states, and it is debated for and against. The benefits of further expansion are in the s4 notes.
[geo-region-european-s3.18.0] Candidate countries (officially recognised as being on the path to join, so they are the likely future members):Ukraine and Moldova (June 2022); Bosnia and Herzegovina (December 2022); Georgia (December 2023).
[geo-region-european-s3.18.1] Other candidates: Albania, Montenegro, North Macedonia and Serbia; Turkey (candidate since 1999, with talks frozen).
[geo-region-european-s3.18.2] Arguments against enlargement: the cost of accession (joining) and of EU funding, slower decision-making with more members, and doubts over whether candidates are ready.
