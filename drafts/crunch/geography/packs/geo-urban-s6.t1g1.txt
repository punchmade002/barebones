TARGET geo-urban-s6: 6. Urban planning and renewal
[geo-urban-s6.0.b] Cities try to solve their problems by controlling how land is used and by fixing areas that have decayed. Three terms describe the different approaches, and examiners expect you to keep them apart.
[geo-urban-s6.0.0] Urban planning is controlling the use and development of land through development plans (official documents setting out what may be built where), zoning (assigning each area a permitted use), planning permission and public investment.
[geo-urban-s6.0.1] Urban renewal is improving and rehabilitating existing run-down areas, keeping the community in place, so the buildings are upgraded but the people stay.
[geo-urban-s6.0.2] Urban redevelopment is clearing an area completely and rebuilding it, often with new uses and new people, so the old community is usually not the one that returns.
[geo-urban-s6.0.3] Renewal improves what is already there, while redevelopment clears and rebuilds. Ballymun and Limerick combined both, while the Docklands was mainly redevelopment because it was cleared and rebuilt.
[geo-urban-s6.0.4] Gentrification can be a side-effect of renewal or redevelopment: better-off people move in, prices rise and original residents are displaced, meaning they are forced out because they can no longer afford to live there.
[geo-urban-s6.1.b] Planners have a toolkit of legal controls, financial incentives and special bodies. Each one is used to steer where and how a city grows or is renewed.
[geo-urban-s6.1.0] Development plans and zoning decide the uses permitted in each area, such as housing, industry or open space, so that incompatible uses are kept apart.
[geo-urban-s6.1.1] Tax incentives in designated areas (areas officially chosen for support) reduce the cost of restoring or building there, which makes private developers willing to invest in run-down places.
[geo-urban-s6.1.2] Special development agencies with planning powers, such as the Dublin Docklands Development Authority (DDDA) and the London Docklands Development Corporation (LDDC), take charge of one area and drive its renewal.
[geo-urban-s6.1.3] Strategic Development Zones are fast-track planning for areas of national importance, so large projects avoid the usual delays.
[geo-urban-s6.1.4] Part V of the Planning and Development Act 2000 requires that part of new housing development be social (State-provided for low-income households) and affordable housing. Up to 20% of land zoned for housing could be required for this purpose.
[geo-urban-s6.1.5] The Part V share was reduced to 10% in 2015 and restored to 20% by the Affordable Housing Act 2021. Strategic Development Zones are created under the same Act.
[geo-urban-s6.2.b] Other tools aim at reusing unused land, connecting areas and involving the people who live there.
[geo-urban-s6.2.0] Derelict and vacant sites levies charge owners of unused property, to encourage its reuse instead of leaving it empty.
[geo-urban-s6.2.1] Public transport investment links new and renewed areas to the rest of the city, so residents can reach jobs and services.
[geo-urban-s6.2.2] New towns are planned as new settlements away from the existing city, taking growth from the older built-up area.
[geo-urban-s6.2.3] Regeneration companies with community participation bring residents into decisions about their area, so renewal is done with local people and not just to them.
[geo-urban-s6.3.b] The Advisory Regional Plan for Dublin, by Myles Wright in 1967, proposed the western new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown. It is the first national-scale plan to judge for effectiveness.
[geo-urban-s6.3.0] What it did: it housed rapid growth in new towns on the western edge of Dublin, rather than letting the existing city spread unplanned.
[geo-urban-s6.3.1] Effectiveness: the towns were built before services, jobs and transport, so their residents depended on distant jobs and facilities.
[geo-urban-s6.3.2] Tallaght's shopping centre, The Square, opened only in 1990, and the Luas Red Line (Dublin's light rail tram line) to Tallaght opened in 2004, decades after the first homes.
[geo-urban-s6.3.3] Criticism: the plan produced sprawl (unplanned spreading of the built-up area into the countryside) and the absence of community, because the towns lacked the facilities that would have made them places in their own right.
[geo-urban-s6.4.b] The National Spatial Strategy tried to balance growth across the country so that Dublin did not take everything. It did this by building up other centres.
[geo-urban-s6.4.0] What it did: it named nine gateways (large centres to drive regional growth: Dublin, Cork, Limerick/Shannon, Galway, Waterford, Dundalk, Sligo, Letterkenny with Derry, and Athlone-Tullamore-Mullingar) and nine hubs (smaller supporting centres), to balance growth away from Dublin.
[geo-urban-s6.4.1] The 2003 Budget announced a decentralisation programme (moving government jobs out of Dublin) that moved civil servants to dozens of towns.
[geo-urban-s6.4.2] Effectiveness: the plan was weakened by too many designated centres, which spread investment thinly, by decentralisation to towns not named in the plan, and by the property boom.
[geo-urban-s6.4.3] Dublin continued to sprawl, so the plan did not achieve its aim of balancing growth.
[geo-urban-s6.4.4] The strategy was replaced in 2018 by the National Planning Framework.
[geo-urban-s6.5.b] The National Planning Framework, known as Project Ireland 2040, was published in 2018 to replace the National Spatial Strategy. It plans where a growing population will live.
[geo-urban-s6.5.0] It plans for about 1 million more people in Ireland by 2040.
[geo-urban-s6.5.1] Half of future population growth is to be in Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford, the five cities.
[geo-urban-s6.5.2] Galway, Cork, Limerick and Waterford are each to grow by at least 50%, so that the regional cities act as a counterweight to Dublin.
[geo-urban-s6.5.3] Compact growth: 40% of new homes nationally are to be within the built-up footprint of existing settlements, on brownfield (previously used) and infill (gaps between existing buildings) land. This limits sprawl.
[geo-urban-s6.5.4] Regional Spatial and Economic Strategies are prepared by the three regional assemblies (regional bodies of local councillors), to apply the national plan at regional level.
[geo-urban-s6.5.5] The Urban Regeneration and Development Fund was set up in 2018 so as to fund renewal projects.
[geo-urban-s6.5.6] Effectiveness: too early to judge fully, but housing supply has lagged behind the targets.
[geo-urban-s6.6.b] Housing is one of three urban problems (with transport and pollution) on which planning effectiveness is judged. The strategies are the Part V requirement, Rent Pressure Zones and national housing targets.
[geo-urban-s6.6.0] Housing for All (2021) set a target of an average of about 33,000 new homes a year to 2030.
[geo-urban-s6.6.1] Rent Pressure Zones (areas where rent rises are capped) were introduced in December 2016, first in Dublin and Cork, to limit rent increases.
[geo-urban-s6.6.2] Part V social and affordable housing, described under Tools available to planners, adds homes within private developments, so new estates are not only for those who can pay market prices.
[geo-urban-s6.6.3] Effectiveness: completions (finished new homes) have risen, but they have stayed below the level of need, so shortages continue.
[geo-urban-s6.6.4] Over 15,000 people were in State-funded emergency accommodation in 2025, a record level of homelessness.
[geo-urban-s6.6.5] High rents remain a problem, which shows that the strategies have not yet matched demand.
[geo-urban-s6.7.b] The transport strategy covers public transport investment and cycling, with the aim of reducing congestion (traffic jams caused by too many vehicles on the roads).
[geo-urban-s6.7.0] DART (the electric coastal rail line) opened in 1984, and the Luas Cross City linked the Red and Green lines through the city centre in December 2017.
[geo-urban-s6.7.1] The Port Tunnel opened in 2006, and the 2007 restriction on heavy goods vehicles kept most five-axle lorries out of the city centre.
[geo-urban-s6.7.2] Dublin Bikes (a public bike-hire scheme) was launched in 2009 to encourage cycling, and BusConnects is the bus network redesign.
[geo-urban-s6.7.3] The NTA (National Transport Authority) Greater Dublin Area Transport Strategy 2022 to 2042 sets out the long-term plan.
[geo-urban-s6.7.4] Effectiveness: the Luas and the Port Tunnel succeeded in their aims, because they added rail capacity and took heavy lorries out of the centre.
[geo-urban-s6.7.5] Metro North, a planned rail line to Dublin Airport and Swords, was shelved by the Government in 2011. Its successor, MetroLink, is planned but has been repeatedly delayed, and no date or cost is yet set.
[geo-urban-s6.7.6] Congestion persists because land use remains dispersed (spread out), so people still need cars to reach jobs and shops.
[geo-urban-s6.8.b] Pollution policy mixes bans, charges and infrastructure upgrades. Some measures have clearly worked, while others have left problems behind.
[geo-urban-s6.8.0] Smoky coal ban: introduced in Dublin on 1 September 1990 and extended nationwide in October 2022.
[geo-urban-s6.8.1] Plastic bag levy, introduced in 2002 to make bags cost money, which cut plastic bag use sharply.
[geo-urban-s6.8.2] Ringsend wastewater treatment plant upgrade, so as to reduce discharges into Dublin Bay.
[geo-urban-s6.8.3] Clean Air Strategy, published in 2023 to tackle air pollution from traffic and solid fuel.
[geo-urban-s6.8.4] Effectiveness: smoke and smog were largely removed after the coal ban, because the main source of the smoke was banned.
[geo-urban-s6.8.5] Limits: traffic nitrogen dioxide (a gas from vehicle exhausts) and particulates (tiny airborne particles) from solid fuel remain problems.
[geo-urban-s6.9.b] The Docklands were derelict after the docks lost their trade to containerisation (goods moving in standard steel containers, which needed deep-water ports with more space). Renewal there was mainly redevelopment, clearing and rebuilding the area.
[geo-urban-s6.9.0] Actions: the Urban Renewal Act 1986 gave tax incentives in the designated area.
[geo-urban-s6.9.1] The Custom House Docks Development Authority was set up in 1986 to lead the renewal, and the International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) was set up in 1987. These are two different dates from the DDDA.
[geo-urban-s6.9.2] The Dublin Docklands Development Authority was set up in 1997, and was wound up in 2016 with its functions passing to Dublin City Council.
[geo-urban-s6.9.3] Flagship projects (high-profile showpiece developments): the Samuel Beckett Bridge (2009), the Convention Centre Dublin (2010), the Bord Gais Energy Theatre (2010) and the Luas Red Line extension to The Point (2009).
[geo-urban-s6.9.4] A Strategic Development Zone covering North Lotts and Grand Canal Dock was designated in 2014.
[geo-urban-s6.9.5] Results: new offices, homes and culture venues, and the Docklands became a centre for tech firms, including Google's European headquarters at Grand Canal Dock, known as 'Silicon Docks'.
[geo-urban-s6.9.6] Criticism: early schemes favoured offices and expensive apartments, while local residents got few of the jobs created. Social housing was slow to be built, and gentrification pushed up prices, so locals could not afford the area.
[geo-urban-s6.9.7] The DDDA was damaged by its role in the purchase of the Irish Glass Bottle site, Ringsend, in 2006 for about €412 million, at the peak of the property bubble. The land value collapsed after 2008.
[geo-urban-s6.10.b] Temple Bar is a renewal case: an existing run-down area was improved and turned into a cultural quarter rather than cleared.
[geo-urban-s6.10.0] Problem: the CIÉ (the State transport company) planned a central bus station in Temple Bar in the 1980s, and bought up property in the area.
[geo-urban-s6.10.1] Artists and small businesses moved in on short leases because rents were cheap in the run-down area, bringing new life to the area.
[geo-urban-s6.10.2] The bus station plan was dropped, and Temple Bar Properties was set up in 1991 under the Temple Bar Area Renewal and Development Act 1991.
[geo-urban-s6.10.3] Dublin was European City of Culture in 1991, which gave the area national attention.
[geo-urban-s6.10.4] Results: cultural venues, including the Irish Film Institute and the Project Arts Centre; restored buildings; and tourism.
[geo-urban-s6.10.5] Criticism: the area became dominated by pubs and night-time tourism, and few people live there permanently.
[geo-urban-s6.11.b] Ballymun combined renewal and redevelopment. Its 1960s high-rise flats were replaced by new low-rise housing and a new town centre.
[geo-urban-s6.11.0] Problems: seven 15-storey towers built in 1966 to 1969 suffered isolation, poor maintenance, unemployment and drugs.
[geo-urban-s6.11.1] Ballymun Regeneration Ltd was set up by Dublin Corporation in 1997.
[geo-urban-s6.11.2] The towers were demolished between 2004 and 2015, and new low-rise homes were built.
[geo-urban-s6.11.3] New facilities included Main Street, the Axis arts and community centre, and an IKEA store, which opened in 2009 as IKEA's first store in the Republic of Ireland.
[geo-urban-s6.11.4] Effectiveness: housing quality improved, and residents were involved in the regeneration, so people had a say.
[geo-urban-s6.11.5] Limits: the recession stalled the town centre, so it stayed unfinished; the promised Metro North rail link was never built (it was shelved in 2011), and unemployment stayed high.
[geo-urban-s6.12.b] Limerick Regeneration addresses estates of concentrated disadvantage (where poverty and social problems are clustered together), after the 2006 arson attack in Moyross and the 2007 report by John Fitzgerald.
[geo-urban-s6.12.0] Limerick Regeneration agencies were set up in 2007, and were later integrated into Limerick City and County Council, formed in 2014.
[geo-urban-s6.12.1] The programme covered Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston.
[geo-urban-s6.12.2] Actions: demolition of the worst estates, building of new homes, and social and economic programmes.
[geo-urban-s6.12.3] Criticism: the regeneration was slow, and demolition outpaced rebuilding, so families were left waiting for new homes.
[geo-urban-s6.12.4] Criticism: the populations of the estates fell during the process, because residents moved away.
[geo-urban-s6.13.b] Adamstown, near Lucan in South Dublin, is a planned new suburb built as a Strategic Development Zone. It tests whether planners can deliver services alongside homes, the failing of earlier new towns.
[geo-urban-s6.13.0] Designated a Strategic Development Zone in 2001, with fast-track planning.
[geo-urban-s6.13.1] Planned as a new suburb with its own railway station on the Kildare line, and with schools and services built in phases alongside the housing.
[geo-urban-s6.13.2] Effectiveness: a model of planned phasing, in which services arrive with the homes, so residents are not left without schools or transport.
[geo-urban-s6.13.3] Development stalled after the 2008 property crash and resumed in the late 2010s, showing how much the scheme depended on the market.
[geo-urban-s6.14.b] Renewal is not only a Dublin matter. Tax relief and strategic plans were also used in the smaller cities, with Cork the main example.
[geo-urban-s6.14.0] Living City Initiative, introduced in 2015: tax relief for refurbishing older buildings in special regeneration areas of Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway, Waterford and Kilkenny, later extended.
[geo-urban-s6.14.1] Cork Area Strategic Plan, 2001, a plan for the city and its surroundings, guided planning in the city.
[geo-urban-s6.14.2] The Cork city boundary was extended in 2019, so the city could plan for its growing suburbs.
[geo-urban-s6.14.3] Luas Cork: a light rail line proposed in the Cork Metropolitan Area Transport Strategy of 2020. It is proposed only, and no cost, route length or opening date has been set.
[geo-urban-s6.14.4] Cork Docklands is the next large regeneration project in the city.
[geo-urban-s6.15.b] The London Docklands were redeveloped after the docks closed in the 1960s to 1980s. The scheme was top-down (decided and run from above rather than by local people), led by an agency with its own planning powers.
[geo-urban-s6.15.0] London Docklands Development Corporation, 1981 to 1998, was an unelected body with planning powers.
[geo-urban-s6.15.1] An Enterprise Zone on the Isle of Dogs gave tax breaks and simplified planning, to attract investors.
[geo-urban-s6.15.2] Canary Wharf was developed, with One Canada Square completed in 1991.
[geo-urban-s6.15.3] The Docklands Light Railway opened in 1987, and London City Airport opened in 1987.
[geo-urban-s6.15.4] The Jubilee Line extension opened in 1999, which is why critics say transport came late.
[geo-urban-s6.15.5] Results: a second financial centre (after the City of London), so jobs and housing followed.
[geo-urban-s6.15.6] Criticism: the corporation was top-down and bypassed local councils, because it was unelected and held the planning powers.
[geo-urban-s6.15.7] Criticism: jobs went to commuters from outside the area, while local residents were priced out, and transport came late.
[geo-urban-s6.16.b] Paris gives a second developed-world example of planning by new towns and large estates.
[geo-urban-s6.16.0] The five new towns from the 1965 regional plan, so as to relieve pressure, took growth away from the city of Paris.
[geo-urban-s6.16.1] The grands ensembles, large housing estates in the banlieues (the suburbs around the city), became problem estates.
[geo-urban-s6.16.2] The Grand Paris Express, about 200 km of new automatic metro lines with 68 stations, aims to link the banlieues to the rest of the region.
[geo-urban-s6.17.b] Putting the strategies and case studies together gives three conclusions that you can use to finish a long answer.
[geo-urban-s6.17.0] Planning works best, for example in Adamstown, when housing, jobs, services and transport are delivered together, and when residents are involved in decisions.
[geo-urban-s6.17.1] Flagship property-led schemes create jobs and buildings, but do not always bring benefits to local people, as in the Docklands.
[geo-urban-s6.17.2] National plans are undermined when other policies pull in the opposite direction, such as tax incentives for development and decentralisation to towns outside the plan.

CANDIDATES geo-urban-s5: 5. Urban problems in developed-world cities
[geo-urban-s5.0.b] Congestion occurs when the volume of traffic on a road network exceeds the space and capacity available, so vehicles move slowly or stop. Most causes come from how cities grew and how people travel in them.
[geo-urban-s5.0.0] Rising car ownership puts more vehicles onto streets that were laid out for far fewer, so the same road space has to carry more traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.0.1] Low-density suburbs (spread-out housing with few homes per hectare) and commuter towns (towns whose residents travel to work elsewhere) are poorly served by public transport, so most journeys are made by car.
[geo-urban-s5.0.2] Radial roads (roads that run out from the centre like spokes) converge on a historic centre with narrow streets and few river crossings, funnelling traffic into one place.
[geo-urban-s5.0.3] Journeys to work and school peak at the same times of day, so the network is filled all at once.
[geo-urban-s5.0.4] Delivery vehicles and heavy goods vehicles (HGVs, large lorries) share the same streets as commuters and shoppers.
[geo-urban-s5.0.5] Jobs and shopping have dispersed (spread out) to suburbs that can be reached only by car, so even short trips add to road traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.0.6] Historically weak investment in rail has left cities without a fast alternative to the car.
[geo-urban-s5.1.b] Congestion costs a city time, money and health. Several of its effects feed back into more congestion.
[geo-urban-s5.1.0] Workers lose time and productivity, and businesses face higher costs because deliveries and staff are delayed.
[geo-urban-s5.1.1] Commuters suffer stress and a reduced quality of life.
[geo-urban-s5.1.2] Stop-start traffic causes air and noise pollution, with carbon emissions from idling engines (engines left running while the vehicle is stationary).
[geo-urban-s5.1.3] Crowded routes lead to road accidents.
[geo-urban-s5.1.4] Buses are slowed by the same traffic, which makes public transport less attractive and so adds to car use.
[geo-urban-s5.1.5] Businesses and tourism are harmed by poor access to the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.1.6] There is less space for pedestrians and cyclists, because road space is given over to traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.2.b] Dublin is the Irish city the exams use for traffic congestion. Its congestion comes from the way it spread out and the late arrival of alternatives to the car.
[geo-urban-s5.2.0] Sprawl (the spread of low-density development at the city edge) and car dependence bring large numbers of commuters into the city every day by car.
[geo-urban-s5.2.1] Radial routes converge on the centre, and there are few crossings of the River Liffey, so traffic is squeezed onto a handful of bridges.
[geo-urban-s5.2.2] The light rail network (electric trams) arrived late: the Luas opened in 2004, after the city's roads had already become congested.
[geo-urban-s5.2.3] Port traffic formerly ran through the city centre until the Port Tunnel opened in 2006.
[geo-urban-s5.3.b] Cities tackle congestion in two ways: by giving people a better alternative to the car and by managing where and how vehicles travel. These measures are mostly drawn from Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.3.0] Public transport: DART (the electric commuter rail along the coast, opened 1984), Luas (opened 2004), commuter rail and BusConnects (a redesign of the bus network), plus the planned MetroLink, which has no date or cost yet.
[geo-urban-s5.3.1] Luas Cross City linked the Red and Green lines through the city centre in December 2017, so passengers could travel between the two lines.
[geo-urban-s5.3.2] Bus lanes and quality bus corridors give buses priority over cars, so buses are not slowed by general traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.3.3] Park and ride: car parks at the edge of the city where drivers can switch to public transport, so fewer cars enter the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.3.4] Cycle lanes and Dublin Bikes, the public bicycle scheme launched in 2009.
[geo-urban-s5.3.5] Integrated ticketing: the Leap card, introduced in 2011, allows one card to be used across different transport services.
[geo-urban-s5.3.6] Orbital roads and bypasses: the M50 orbital motorway, built in stages from 1990, takes traffic around the city instead of through it.
[geo-urban-s5.3.7] The Port Tunnel, opened in 2006, took port traffic out of the city centre; a 2007 restriction banned most five-axle heavy goods vehicles from the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.4.b] The second group of measures aims to cut the number of car journeys, by making driving dearer or by making journeys unnecessary.
[geo-urban-s5.4.0] Pedestrianisation: the busiest shopping streets are closed to traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.4.1] Parking charges and limits discourage commuters from driving into the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.4.2] Congestion charging: the London Congestion Charge, introduced in February 2003, is a daily charge to drive into central London on weekdays, and traffic in the zone fell in its early years.
[geo-urban-s5.4.3] Car sharing, remote working (working from home) and staggered working hours (different start times) spread traffic over time.
[geo-urban-s5.4.4] Land-use planning that puts homes close to jobs and transport reduces the need to travel by car.
[geo-urban-s5.5.b] Urban decay is the physical, economic and social decline of part of a city, usually the inner city (the older area close to the centre). It is decline inside the built-up area, not growth at the edge.
[geo-urban-s5.5.0] Old industries and port jobs close, removing the economic base of the inner city.
[geo-urban-s5.5.1] Better-off residents move to the suburbs, leaving a smaller and poorer population behind.
[geo-urban-s5.5.2] Buildings are left derelict (empty and falling into disrepair) and are poorly maintained, particularly social housing (homes provided by the local authority).
[geo-urban-s5.5.3] Reduced rates income (the local tax paid by businesses and property) means the local authority has less money to maintain buildings and services.
[geo-urban-s5.5.4] Dereliction attracts crime and vandalism, which deter investment and residents.
[geo-urban-s5.5.5] Road schemes and planning blight: the threat of future road building leaves property uncertain, so owners stop investing in repairs.
[geo-urban-s5.6.b] Once decay begins, its effects reinforce each other, so a run-down area is hard to recover without outside help.
[geo-urban-s5.6.0] Unemployment and poverty among residents of the affected area, because local jobs have gone.
[geo-urban-s5.6.1] Derelict sites stand empty for long periods, because owners and investors see little reason to rebuild.
[geo-urban-s5.6.2] Population decline as families leave, so fewer people remain to support local shops and services.
[geo-urban-s5.6.3] Drug and crime problems, which grow where there are empty buildings and few jobs.
[geo-urban-s5.6.4] Poor services, because fewer people and less money remain to support them.
[geo-urban-s5.6.5] Stigma: the reputation of the area discourages investment and visitors.
[geo-urban-s5.6.6] A cycle of decline, in which each problem makes the others worse.
[geo-urban-s5.7.b] Dublin's inner city shows how the loss of port jobs and the clearing of old housing left an area empty and run down.
[geo-urban-s5.7.0] Dublin's docks lost most of their jobs with containerisation (the shift to shipping goods in standard metal containers handled by machinery, needing far fewer workers) from the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-urban-s5.7.1] Inner-city tenements (old, overcrowded rented housing) were cleared, and their residents moved to the suburbs.
[geo-urban-s5.7.2] Dereliction of inner-city streets and buildings followed through the 1970s and 1980s. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal, which covers the renewal of these areas.
[geo-urban-s5.8.b] Limerick is an Irish example of decay concentrated in social-housing estates rather than in the old centre.
[geo-urban-s5.8.0] Disadvantage (poverty and lack of opportunity) was concentrated in social-housing estates: Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston.
[geo-urban-s5.8.1] After an arson attack on two children in Moyross in 2006, the Government commissioned John Fitzgerald's report, published in 2007.
[geo-urban-s5.8.2] The report led to the Limerick Regeneration programme for Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston. There is more on its results in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s5.9.b] Detroit, Michigan, USA, is known as 'Motor City', the home of Ford, General Motors and Chrysler. Its decline shows decay on a city-wide scale.
[geo-urban-s5.9.0] Population peaked at about 1.85 million at the 1950 US census and fell to about 640,000 at the 2020 US census.
[geo-urban-s5.9.1] Middle-class, largely white families moved to the suburbs from the 1950s, helped by freeways (high-speed urban motorways), which left the city with fewer taxpayers.
[geo-urban-s5.9.2] Car-industry jobs moved out of the city or were automated (done by machines), so the city lost its main employer base.
[geo-urban-s5.9.3] Tens of thousands of houses were abandoned, because families had left and there was no demand for them.
[geo-urban-s5.9.4] Detroit filed for bankruptcy in July 2013, the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history, and exited it in December 2014.
[geo-urban-s5.9.5] Downtown and Midtown have since seen new investment, although many areas remain derelict.
[geo-urban-s5.10.b] Urban sprawl is the uncontrolled outward spread of low-density housing and other development over rural land at the edge of a city. It is growth at the edge, unlike urban decay, which is decline inside the city.
[geo-urban-s5.10.0] Population growth, high prices in the inner city, and demand for houses with gardens push development outwards.
[geo-urban-s5.10.1] Car ownership and motorways make distant sites accessible, so people can live far from where they work.
[geo-urban-s5.10.2] Planning that zoned greenfield land (land never built on before) for development, and developers' preference for cheaper greenfield sites, drive building at the edge.
[geo-urban-s5.10.3] Tax incentives during the Celtic Tiger boom (the period of rapid Irish economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s) encouraged building.
[geo-urban-s5.11.b] Sprawl spreads people thinly over a large area, which is costly for the people who live there and for the city they left behind.
[geo-urban-s5.11.0] Long commutes and congestion, and car dependence, because distances are too long to walk or cycle and public transport is poor.
[geo-urban-s5.11.1] Loss of farmland and habitats, because rural land is built on. There is more on this in Urban land use and planning.
[geo-urban-s5.11.2] Costly services, such as roads, water and schools, spread thinly over a large area, so each household costs more to serve.
[geo-urban-s5.11.3] Dormitory towns, whose residents sleep there and work elsewhere, leaving little local activity.
[geo-urban-s5.11.4] Decline of the city centre as people and spending move out, and higher carbon emissions from longer car journeys.
[geo-urban-s5.12.b] Dublin is the Irish example of sprawl, and its sprawl left a visible legacy when the property market collapsed.
[geo-urban-s5.12.0] Dublin's sprawl has extended into the commuter counties beyond the city boundary.
[geo-urban-s5.12.1] A Government survey in 2010 found almost 3,000 unfinished housing estates, known as ghost estates, after the 2008 property crash (the sudden collapse in house prices and building).
[geo-urban-s5.13.b] Solutions either stop the city spreading outwards or make better use of land already built on.
[geo-urban-s5.13.0] Green belts: a protected ring of open land around the city to stop its spread.
[geo-urban-s5.13.1] Compact growth and brownfield development (reusing land that was built on before), set out in the National Planning Framework, to build on land within the city.
[geo-urban-s5.13.2] Higher densities of housing near public transport, so that fewer people need cars.
[geo-urban-s5.13.3] New towns planned as self-contained settlements with their own services, so residents do not have to travel far.
[geo-urban-s5.13.4] Regeneration of inner-city sites that are vacant or derelict, so growth is directed inwards.
[geo-urban-s5.14.b] A community is a group of neighbours who know each other and share local life. In some urban areas the way housing was planned or built stops this from forming.
[geo-urban-s5.14.0] New estates are built quickly without schools, shops, community centres or public transport, so residents have nowhere to meet.
[geo-urban-s5.14.1] High-rise flats have no shared space where neighbours can meet.
[geo-urban-s5.14.2] Long commutes leave little time for local life.
[geo-urban-s5.14.3] Transient renting populations (tenants who move often) do not put down roots.
[geo-urban-s5.14.4] Old inner-city communities were demolished when tenements were cleared, breaking up established networks of family and neighbours.
[geo-urban-s5.14.5] Social segregation separates people by income and class into different areas.
[geo-urban-s5.15.b] When a place has no shared life, the people in it feel the lack, and the area itself suffers.
[geo-urban-s5.15.0] Isolation and loneliness among residents who do not know their neighbours.
[geo-urban-s5.15.1] Anti-social behaviour, which is harder to deal with where neighbours do not know each other.
[geo-urban-s5.15.2] Little sense of belonging or local organisation, so there are few residents' groups or clubs to take action on local problems.
[geo-urban-s5.16.b] Examples come from Dublin and from Paris. The responses aim to build the facilities and involvement that were missing.
[geo-urban-s5.16.0] Ballymun's tower blocks in Dublin: seven 15-storey towers built 1966 to 1969, demolished 2004 to 2015.
[geo-urban-s5.16.1] Commuter estates and the ghost estates of Dublin's commuter counties.
[geo-urban-s5.16.2] Paris banlieues (the suburbs on the edge of Paris): large grands ensembles (estates built in the 1950s to 1970s) such as Sarcelles, with high unemployment and the riots that began in Clichy-sous-Bois in October 2005.
[geo-urban-s5.16.3] Responses: community facilities built alongside housing, with phasing conditions (planning rules) so that schools and shops come before the homes are occupied.
[geo-urban-s5.16.4] Responses: regeneration with resident participation, mixed tenure (a mix of owned, rented and social homes in one area), and sports clubs and family resource centres.
[geo-urban-s5.17.b] Urban heritage includes historic buildings, streetscapes (the look of a street as a whole), archaeological sites and monuments, together with their setting. It is valuable in three ways.
[geo-urban-s5.17.0] Identity: heritage gives a city and its residents a sense of their history and place.
[geo-urban-s5.17.1] Tourism: historic buildings and monuments attract visitors and the income they bring.
[geo-urban-s5.17.2] Economic value: restored heritage buildings can support shops, hotels and cultural venues.
[geo-urban-s5.18.b] Heritage is easily lost because the pressures of a growing city work against old buildings.
[geo-urban-s5.18.0] Demolition for offices and roads, as when historic buildings are cleared for new development.
[geo-urban-s5.18.1] Neglect and dereliction of old buildings, so that without repair they decay and may have to be demolished.
[geo-urban-s5.18.2] Inappropriate new buildings, with heights that are out of scale with the historic streetscape.
[geo-urban-s5.18.3] Traffic vibration and pollution, which damage old buildings and monuments.
[geo-urban-s5.18.4] Tourist pressure, which can wear down historic sites and crowd out local residents.
[geo-urban-s5.19.b] Dublin has both losses and successes. The losses show the threats above, and the reuse examples show heritage put to work.
[geo-urban-s5.19.0] Lower Fitzwilliam Street: the ESB (Ireland's electricity company) demolished a row of 16 Georgian houses in 1965 to build its head office. That office block has itself since been demolished and replaced.
[geo-urban-s5.19.1] Wood Quay: one of the largest Viking-age sites in Europe. Despite protests and court cases in the late 1970s, Dublin Corporation (the city council) built its civic offices on the site.
[geo-urban-s5.19.2] 14 to 17 Moore Street, where the 1916 leaders spent their last hours before surrender, was declared a national monument in 2007.
[geo-urban-s5.19.3] Positive reuse: Temple Bar was regenerated in the 1990s, and Docklands warehouses have been reused for new purposes.
[geo-urban-s5.20.b] Heritage is protected by law, by voluntary groups and by international listing.
[geo-urban-s5.20.0] The Planning and Development Act 2000 created the Record of Protected Structures (a list of buildings the law protects) and Architectural Conservation Areas (whole areas protected for their character).
[geo-urban-s5.20.1] National monuments are protected by the State.
[geo-urban-s5.20.2] An Taisce (the National Trust for Ireland), founded in 1948, campaigns for the protection of heritage.
[geo-urban-s5.20.3] UNESCO World Heritage listing (international recognition of sites of outstanding value): Ireland's two World Heritage Sites are Brú na Bóinne, listed in 1993, and Skellig Michael, listed in 1996. Neither is urban.
[geo-urban-s5.20.4] International urban examples: the Old and New Towns of Edinburgh, listed by UNESCO in 1995, and the Historic Centre of Rome, listed in 1980.
[geo-urban-s5.21.b] Heritage attracts visitors in smaller cities as well as in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.21.0] Kilkenny's Medieval Mile attracts heritage tourism to a smaller Irish city. There is more on its medieval history in Pre-historic and historic settlement.
[geo-urban-s5.22.b] Environmental quality is how clean, quiet and pleasant an urban area is. Air quality is the first aspect, and Dublin's ban on smoky coal is the Irish example of a response to air pollution.
[geo-urban-s5.22.0] Sources of urban air pollution: smoke from coal fires, and traffic emissions of nitrogen dioxide and particulates (tiny solid particles in the air).
[geo-urban-s5.22.1] Dublin's severe winter smog (smoke and fog mixed) in the 1980s led to the ban on smoky coal in Dublin from 1 September 1990.
[geo-urban-s5.22.2] Research after the ban found that black smoke levels and winter death rates fell.
[geo-urban-s5.22.3] The ban on smoky coal was extended nationwide in October 2022.
[geo-urban-s5.23.b] Cities produce large amounts of sewage and polluted runoff, which must be treated before it reaches rivers and the sea.
[geo-urban-s5.23.0] The Ringsend wastewater treatment plant treats most of Dublin's sewage and discharges into Dublin Bay.
[geo-urban-s5.23.1] The Ringsend plant is being upgraded, so that it can keep treating the sewage of a growing city.
[geo-urban-s5.23.2] Storm overflows: heavy rain can send mixed sewage and surface water into urban rivers and the bay, because the drains cannot carry the extra volume.
[geo-urban-s5.23.3] Urban rivers carry pollution from paved surfaces, roads and drains.
[geo-urban-s5.24.b] Waste that is not recycled or treated becomes litter or goes to landfill, which harms the look and quality of an urban area. Ireland has used charges, recycling and energy recovery to cut it.
[geo-urban-s5.24.0] Litter and landfill: waste that is not recycled or treated goes to landfill (buried in the ground).
[geo-urban-s5.24.1] Plastic bag levy: introduced in March 2002 at 15 cent, after which plastic bag use fell sharply, showing a charge can change behaviour.
[geo-urban-s5.24.2] Recycling schemes reduce the amount of waste sent to landfill, because materials are reused instead of buried.
[geo-urban-s5.24.3] Poolbeg (Dublin Waste-to-Energy) incinerator, operating since 2017, burns residual waste (what is left after recycling) to generate energy.
[geo-urban-s5.25.b] Not all pollution is chemical. Noise and an ugly environment also lower the quality of life in a city.
[geo-urban-s5.25.0] Noise from traffic, construction and night-time activity affects residents, for example by disturbing sleep.
[geo-urban-s5.25.1] Visual pollution: graffiti, poor signage and unsightly derelict buildings reduce the quality of the streetscape.
[geo-urban-s5.25.2] Derelict sites: the Derelict Sites levy is a charge on owners of derelict property to encourage its reuse.
[geo-urban-s5.26.b] Green space gives wildlife a home and gives residents somewhere to relax. Dublin has several examples.
[geo-urban-s5.26.0] Parks provide habitats and recreation: Phoenix Park, about 700 hectares, established 1662.
[geo-urban-s5.26.1] Canals provide green corridors (strips of greenery that wildlife can move along) through the city: the Royal Canal and the Grand Canal.
[geo-urban-s5.26.2] North Bull Island, in Dublin Bay, is a green space beside the city.
[geo-urban-s5.26.3] Loss of green space to infill: small gaps between buildings are built on, reducing open space for wildlife and residents.
[geo-urban-s5.27.b] Building over land changes how water and heat behave in a city, which causes two problems.
[geo-urban-s5.27.0] Urban flooding: paved surfaces stop rain soaking into the ground, so runoff (water flowing over the surface) rises quickly into rivers and drains.
[geo-urban-s5.27.1] Hurricane Charley, in August 1986, caused severe flooding of the River Dodder in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.27.2] Urban heat island: built-up areas are warmer than the surrounding countryside because buildings and paving absorb and hold heat.

CANDIDATES geo-urban-s4: 4. Urban land use and planning
[geo-urban-s4.0.b] A large city is divided into zones of land use (areas where one kind of activity dominates). Each zone has its own pattern of buildings and activity, and its own land value. The zones below are typical of a large European city and can all be identified in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s4.0.0] CBD (central business district): the commercial heart of the city, with the highest land values, tall buildings and the most intensive use of land, meaning the most activity packed onto each site.
[geo-urban-s4.0.1] Zone in transition: the inner-city area of mixed old housing, warehouses and small industry, where decay and renewal exist side by side. It is called a zone of transition because it is changing from one use to another.
[geo-urban-s4.0.2] Inner residential zone: older terraced housing (houses joined in rows) close to the centre, so it lies near the CBD and jobs.
[geo-urban-s4.0.3] Suburbs: lower-density housing, mainly semi-detached houses (pairs of houses sharing one wall), built later and further from the centre, so there is more space for each house.
[geo-urban-s4.0.4] Industrial zones: the port, inner-city industry, suburban industrial estates and business parks (landscaped sites of modern offices and light industry).
[geo-urban-s4.0.5] Open space and recreation: parks, playing fields and other green areas within the built-up city, such as Phoenix Park in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s4.0.6] Transport land: roads, railway lines, the port and the airport, such as Dublin Port and Dublin Airport.
[geo-urban-s4.0.7] Rural-urban fringe: the transitional land at the edge of the built-up area, where urban and rural land uses meet and compete for the same sites.
[geo-urban-s4.1.b] The CBD is the most intensively used part of any city. Almost everything about it follows from one fact: it is the most accessible place, so everyone wants a site there.
[geo-urban-s4.1.0] Land values are highest here because accessibility is greatest, so the most valuable sites are used for the most profitable activities.
[geo-urban-s4.1.1] Tall buildings maximise the use of expensive land, with several floors of offices or shops on a small site.
[geo-urban-s4.1.2] Vertical zoning: shops occupy the ground floor, with offices or other uses above, so each floor of a costly site earns money.
[geo-urban-s4.1.3] Department stores, chain stores and specialist shops occupy the main shopping streets, because these sites bring the most customers.
[geo-urban-s4.1.4] Offices, banks and the head offices of companies cluster together in the centre, because they can pay the high land values and need to be close to each other and to clients.
[geo-urban-s4.1.5] Government and cultural buildings are located in the centre, where they are most accessible to the whole population.
[geo-urban-s4.1.6] Few people live in the CBD, so it is crowded during the working day and quiet at night, when workers and shoppers go home.
[geo-urban-s4.1.7] Pedestrian streets are used in the busiest shopping areas, where traffic and crowds would otherwise congest the streets.
[geo-urban-s4.2.b] Dublin's CBD shows these features on a real map, and it is the Irish example to use for the CBD.
[geo-urban-s4.2.0] Dublin's CBD lies on both sides of the River Liffey.
[geo-urban-s4.2.1] Its main shopping streets are Grafton Street, south of the Liffey, and Henry Street, north of it. Both are pedestrianised, meaning closed to traffic.
[geo-urban-s4.2.2] O'Connell Street is the city's main street.
[geo-urban-s4.3.b] Three models describe how land uses arrange themselves around a city centre. Each is a simplified generalisation, so it is judged by how well it fits the real city. The first is the oldest, and it draws the city as a set of rings.
[geo-urban-s4.3.0] Ernest Burgess proposed the concentric zone model in 1925, based on Chicago. Land uses form rings around the CBD, with land values and densities (the number of people per area) falling with distance from the centre.
[geo-urban-s4.3.1] Strength: it shows the regular fall in land value and density outwards from the CBD. Limitation: it ignores transport routes, so it does not fit cities whose growth follows roads and rail lines.
[geo-urban-s4.3.2] Diagram: concentric rings drawn around a central point labelled CBD, with the zones of transition, residential and commuter land shown in turn.
[geo-urban-s4.4.b] The second model answers the weakness of the rings: cities do not grow evenly in all directions. Roads, railways and rivers pull growth outwards in lines.
[geo-urban-s4.4.0] Homer Hoyt proposed the sector model in 1939. Land uses extend outwards in wedge-shaped sectors along transport routes, and a high-class residential sector lies away from industry.
[geo-urban-s4.4.1] Strength: it explains why sectors of the city are elongated along roads, rail lines and rivers, because transport routes make the land beside them more accessible. Limitation: it does not show the separate suburban centres that grow in large cities.
[geo-urban-s4.4.2] Diagram: a circle divided into wedge-shaped sectors, with radial transport routes (routes running out from the centre) marked and the high-class residential sector shown away from the industrial sector.
[geo-urban-s4.5.b] The third model drops the idea of a single centre. In a large modern city, activities gather around several centres (nuclei) at once.
[geo-urban-s4.5.0] Chauncy Harris and Edward Ullman proposed the multiple nuclei model in 1945. A city grows around several separate centres, such as the CBD, suburban shopping centres, an airport and business parks.
[geo-urban-s4.5.1] Strength: it explains the growth of suburban centres. Limitation: it gives no simple pattern of rings or sectors, so it is less clear as a general description.
[geo-urban-s4.5.2] Diagram: a city outline with several separate centres marked, each surrounded by its own cluster of land use.
[geo-urban-s4.5.3] Dublin fits the multiple nuclei and sector models better than the concentric model: the CBD is one nucleus, while suburban shopping centres and business parks are other nuclei, and growth follows the main roads out of the city.
[geo-urban-s4.6.b] Dublin shows the zones of a modern European city, with the inner city in transition and the outer city growing along the M50 (the orbital motorway circling the city) and the western new towns.
[geo-urban-s4.6.0] Georgian core: the Georgian squares (planned 18th-century squares), such as Merrion Square and Fitzwilliam Square, are now largely offices.
[geo-urban-s4.6.1] The Wide Streets Commissioners, set up in 1757, widened and improved the city's streets, shaping the centre's wide thoroughfares.
[geo-urban-s4.6.2] Inner-city docklands and the north inner city are in transition, with older industrial and port land being renewed.
[geo-urban-s4.6.3] Older inner suburbs lie between the centre and the outer suburbs, so their older housing forms an inner residential zone.
[geo-urban-s4.6.4] Outer suburbs and the western new towns: Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown, planned in the Wright Report of 1967 (a planning report for the growth of the city).
[geo-urban-s4.6.5] Business and industrial parks lie near the M50 and the N7 (the national road running south-west from Dublin): Grange Castle, Citywest, Park West and Sandyford. Roads give them access for lorries and workers.
[geo-urban-s4.6.6] The port and airport are major transport and industrial land uses: Dublin Port and Dublin Airport.
[geo-urban-s4.6.7] Open space includes Phoenix Park, about 700 hectares and one of Europe's largest enclosed city parks, established in 1662; the Dublin Mountains and Dublin Bay are further open spaces at the edge of the city.
[geo-urban-s4.7.b] Paris is the world city studied for comparison with Dublin. Its land uses are concentrated in the inner city and organised around a ring road and a set of outer business and housing areas.
[geo-urban-s4.7.0] The City of Paris has about 2.1 million people on about 105 km2, divided into 20 arrondissements (administrative districts). The wider Ile-de-France region has about 12 million people.
[geo-urban-s4.7.1] The CBD and government quarter lie in the inner arrondissements, the most central districts.
[geo-urban-s4.7.2] Haussmann's boulevards: Haussmann rebuilt central Paris between 1853 and 1870, creating wide boulevards that give the inner city its formal street pattern.
[geo-urban-s4.7.3] La Défense, west of the city, is one of Europe's largest purpose-built business districts, an office district outside the historic inner city.
[geo-urban-s4.7.4] The Boulevard Périphérique, completed in 1973, is the ring road that forms the boundary of the inner city.
[geo-urban-s4.7.5] The banlieues are the suburbs beyond the Périphérique, where grands ensembles (large public housing estates built in the 1950s to 1970s) are found, for example Sarcelles.
[geo-urban-s4.7.6] The 1965 regional plan created five new towns (planned towns built to take growth away from the city): Cergy-Pontoise, Évry, Marne-la-Vallée, Melun-Sénart and Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines.
[geo-urban-s4.8.b] Industry has moved out of the inner city to the edge, where land is cheaper and road access is better. This is the first of three movements (industrial, residential, commercial) that change a city's land-use pattern.
[geo-urban-s4.8.0] Old inner-city industry beside the port, canals and railways (the transport of the time) included brewing, biscuit-making and distilling.
[geo-urban-s4.8.1] Many of these sites closed or moved to suburban industrial estates and business parks near motorways, so that lorries can reach them easily.
[geo-urban-s4.8.2] Reasons for the move: lack of space for expansion in the inner city, cheaper land on the edge, road access for lorries, less congestion, planning policy, and new industries needing clean, modern sites.
[geo-urban-s4.8.3] The vacated inner-city sites are brownfield land (land previously developed, now available for reuse), which can then be redeveloped for other uses.
[geo-urban-s4.8.4] The Guinness brewery is an exception, having stayed at St James's Gate since Arthur Guinness's lease of 1759.
[geo-urban-s4.8.5] Jacob's biscuit factory moved from Bishop Street in the inner city to Tallaght in the 1970s. The old factory now houses the National Archives, an example of a brownfield site reused.
[geo-urban-s4.8.6] Paris: Renault's Boulogne-Billancourt plant closed in 1992, an example of industry leaving the inner city.
[geo-urban-s4.9.b] Housing has also moved outwards, but in recent years some people have moved back in. Each change raises planning issues.
[geo-urban-s4.9.0] Inner-city tenements (old multi-occupied houses) were cleared in the 20th century, and their residents were moved to new suburbs and new towns.
[geo-urban-s4.9.1] Growth of suburban housing spread the city outwards, with sprawl (uncontrolled spread of building) into commuter counties, the counties around the city whose residents travel in to work.
[geo-urban-s4.9.2] The trend has been reversed in places, with a return to inner-city apartment living in areas such as the Docklands, through regeneration (investment to renew a run-down area).
[geo-urban-s4.9.3] Issues raised by residential change: the density of housing, the height of buildings and the social mix of the area.
[geo-urban-s4.10.b] Shops and offices have followed the roads and the car-owning population to the edge of the city. This takes trade and activity away from the centre.
[geo-urban-s4.10.0] Suburban shopping centres and retail parks have been built beside motorway junctions, where there is car access, free parking and cheaper land.
[geo-urban-s4.10.1] The Square, Tallaght, opened in 1990; Blanchardstown Centre in 1996; Liffey Valley in 1998; and Dundrum Town Centre in 2005. All are beside major roads or the M50.
[geo-urban-s4.10.2] Out-of-town office parks have been built in the same way, on sites with good road access, so that workers can drive to them.
[geo-urban-s4.10.3] Effects on the city centre: vacant units and lost trade, because shoppers move to the edge.
[geo-urban-s4.10.4] Responses: pedestrianisation of the centre, regeneration of central streets and limits on the size of retail parks, so that the centre can keep its shoppers.
[geo-urban-s4.10.5] Paris: La Défense takes offices out of the inner city, while the suburban business districts of the region take other commercial uses.
[geo-urban-s4.11.b] Industry, housing and shops all moving at once creates problems that planners have to manage. These are the main issues.
[geo-urban-s4.11.0] Zoning: planning authorities divide land into use zones through development plans (official plans for an area) to control where each use may be located.
[geo-urban-s4.11.1] Brownfield and greenfield development: reuse of old urban land must be balanced against building on undeveloped edge land.
[geo-urban-s4.11.2] Height and density: taller and denser buildings in the centre raise questions of design, light and services.
[geo-urban-s4.11.3] Transport: new zones must be linked to public transport, or they generate car journeys and congestion.
[geo-urban-s4.11.4] Mixed land use: combining housing, offices and shops in one area reduces travel but can cause conflict between uses.
[geo-urban-s4.11.5] Conflict between business, residents and heritage: new offices or roads can threaten housing or historic buildings.
[geo-urban-s4.12.b] Bid-rent theory, put forward by William Alonso in 1964, explains why land values and land uses change with distance from the centre. Bid-rent is the most a user can afford to pay for a plot of land.
[geo-urban-s4.12.0] Accessibility (how easily a site can be reached by people) is greatest at the centre, so competition for sites is fiercest there and land is dearest at the peak land value intersection (PLVI), the point of highest value.
[geo-urban-s4.12.1] Land values fall with distance from the centre, because each step outwards means less accessibility.
[geo-urban-s4.12.2] Commerce outbids industry for central sites, and industry outbids housing, because each use can pay a different amount for the same land.
[geo-urban-s4.12.3] Secondary peaks of land value form at suburban centres, ring-road junctions and along main roads, where accessibility is also high.
[geo-urban-s4.12.4] Effect on building: tall buildings and intensive use occur at the centre, while large, low-density uses such as warehouses and car parks occur at the edge.
[geo-urban-s4.13.b] Social stratification is the division of a city's population into groups by income, class, ethnicity, age and family stage, and segregation is the separation of these groups into different areas.
[geo-urban-s4.13.0] Causes: land and house prices, the allocation of social housing (housing provided by the State for those who cannot afford to buy or rent privately), planning decisions, transport access, household choice and discrimination.
[geo-urban-s4.13.1] Results: affluent areas are set against areas of concentrated disadvantage, and gated developments (housing estates closed off by gates) create closed residential areas.
[geo-urban-s4.13.2] Gentrification: better-off people move into and renovate a poorer inner-city area, raising prices and displacing lower-income residents. It is a different process from urban renewal and redevelopment. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s4.14.b] Both of the case-study cities show segregation on the map. Use these named examples, and keep descriptions of places factual.
[geo-urban-s4.14.0] Dublin: affluent south-east suburbs, including Ballsbridge, Donnybrook, Dalkey and Foxrock, contrast with lower-income areas in parts of the north inner city and in some outer social-housing estates.
[geo-urban-s4.14.1] Ballymun is the case of concentrated social housing in Dublin, with seven 15-storey tower blocks built between 1966 and 1969.
[geo-urban-s4.14.2] Gentrification around the Docklands: the inner-city area has attracted new residents and businesses, including Google's European headquarters at Grand Canal Dock, known as 'Silicon Docks'.
[geo-urban-s4.14.3] Paris: the wealthy western districts contrast with the poorer north-eastern banlieues of Seine-Saint-Denis, the poorest département (administrative area) in mainland France.
[geo-urban-s4.14.4] Immigrant residents are concentrated in grands ensembles, and the riots that began in Clichy-sous-Bois in October 2005 showed the tensions in these areas.
[geo-urban-s4.15.b] As cities grow, they take in rural land at the rural-urban fringe. The pressures on rural land are caused by population growth and by the choices made by households and firms.
[geo-urban-s4.15.0] Causes: population growth and in-migration (people moving in from elsewhere); demand for cheaper, larger houses; car ownership and motorways; high house prices in the city; and jobs moving to the suburbs.
[geo-urban-s4.15.1] Loss of farmland: housing, roads and business parks replace fields, and rising land prices encourage farmers to sell, because the land is worth more built on than farmed.
[geo-urban-s4.15.2] Hobby farms and horse paddocks spread across the fringe, which takes land out of commercial farming.
[geo-urban-s4.15.3] Commuter villages gain new estates, and their character and community can be lost as their population changes.
[geo-urban-s4.15.4] Other uses on the fringe: retail parks, business parks, data centres (buildings full of computer servers), landfill sites, quarries and golf courses.
[geo-urban-s4.15.5] Environmental effects: loss of habitats and hedgerows, and flooding as paved surfaces replace soil that would absorb rainwater.
[geo-urban-s4.15.6] Conflicts between farmers, residents and developers over the future of the fringe.
[geo-urban-s4.16.b] Dublin's growth shows these pressures at work in the counties around it.
[geo-urban-s4.16.0] Sprawl into Meath, Kildare and Wicklow. The Greater Dublin Area had about 2.1 million people at the Census of 2022, about 40% of the State.
[geo-urban-s4.16.1] Commuter towns: Navan, Ashbourne, Naas, Newbridge, Maynooth, Bray and Greystones, with one-off housing (single houses built in the countryside) across the commuter belt.
[geo-urban-s4.16.2] Data centres on the city edge used about a fifth of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO), most of them in the Dublin region.
[geo-urban-s4.17.b] Planners have tried to control expansion by protecting land, by building new settlements and by deciding in advance where growth may go.
[geo-urban-s4.17.0] Green belts (protected rings of open land around a city): London's Metropolitan Green Belt was protected from the 1940s and 1950s to stop the outward sprawl of London.
[geo-urban-s4.17.1] New towns (planned towns built to take growth away from a city): Britain's New Towns Act 1946 led to Stevenage, the first new town designated, in 1946, and Milton Keynes, designated in 1967.
[geo-urban-s4.17.2] Paris's five new towns, from the 1965 plan, took population growth away from the city, so that it did not all go into the existing built-up area.
[geo-urban-s4.17.3] Dublin's western new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown were planned after the Wright Report of 1967, so that growth went into planned towns.
[geo-urban-s4.17.4] Compact growth and brownfield targets, set out in the National Planning Framework, aim to build on land within existing settlements. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s4.17.5] Development plans zone land for particular uses, so that the authorities decide where expansion may take place.

CANDIDATES geo-urban-s2: 2. Rural settlement
[geo-urban-s2.0.b] Rural settlement is the farmhouses, hamlets (very small groups of houses) and villages in the countryside. Its pattern is the way the buildings are spread over the land, and geographers sort patterns into three types.
[geo-urban-s2.0.0] Dispersed: individual farmhouses scattered across the land, each on its own farm.
[geo-urban-s2.0.1] Clustered (also called nucleated): buildings grouped together in one place, usually for a shared reason such as a crossroads or a water supply.
[geo-urban-s2.0.2] Linear (also called ribbon): houses strung along a line such as a road, a coast road, a valley or a ridge.
[geo-urban-s2.0.3] Clustered and nucleated mean the same pattern, and linear is the same as ribbon when it extends out of a town along approach roads, so there are three patterns, not four.
[geo-urban-s2.0.4] In Census 2016 (the national count of the population), about 63% of Ireland's population lived in urban areas and about 37% in rural areas.
[geo-urban-s2.1.b] Dispersed settlement is the dominant rural pattern in Ireland. It exists for farming, historical and physical reasons.
[geo-urban-s2.1.0] Irish farms are family-owned, and each family lives on its own holding (its own farm), so the houses end up spread out across the countryside.
[geo-urban-s2.1.1] The Land Commission was set up in 1881 under the Land Law (Ireland) Act 1881. After the Land Acts, consolidation of holdings (joining scattered plots into one compact farm) moved houses out of clusters onto individual farms.
[geo-urban-s2.1.2] The Wyndham Land Act of 1903 helped tenants to buy their holdings, which strengthened the pattern of families owning and living on their own land.
[geo-urban-s2.1.3] Fertile, well-drained lowland with plentiful water lets houses be built almost anywhere, because no site is much better than another.
[geo-urban-s2.1.4] Pastoral farming (rearing animals, especially cattle and sheep) keeps the farmer close to the animals, so the house sits on the farm.
[geo-urban-s2.2.b] Buildings are grouped together where a shared reason made the site worth using. Each reason below explains why people chose to build close together instead of apart.
[geo-urban-s2.2.0] Route focus: a crossroads where roads meet brings travellers and trade, so buildings gather there.
[geo-urban-s2.2.1] A bridging point or ford (a shallow place where a river can be crossed) draws settlement because routes must pass through it.
[geo-urban-s2.2.2] A church, a castle or estate gates act as a focus, and people settle beside them for work, worship or protection.
[geo-urban-s2.2.3] Defence: a hill or other defensible site was chosen so the settlement could be defended.
[geo-urban-s2.2.4] Water supply at a spring or well gives a wet-point site, where people settle because they need water.
[geo-urban-s2.2.5] A dry-point site is a patch of dry ground above flood land, chosen so houses stay dry.
[geo-urban-s2.2.6] A harbour is the reason for a fishing village, because the boats and the catch are landed there.
[geo-urban-s2.2.7] Services such as a shop, pub, school and Garda station (police station) gather in the cluster, which then attracts further settlement.
[geo-urban-s2.2.8] Estate village: Enniskerry, Co. Wicklow, lies at the gates of the Powerscourt estate (a large landowner's property), and Adare, Co. Limerick, grew by the Dunraven estate, with thatched cottages.
[geo-urban-s2.3.b] Before the Famine many farmers in the west lived in clachans instead of on separate farms. The way land was held in common explains why.
[geo-urban-s2.3.0] A clachan is a cluster of farmhouses of related families, with no church, shop or other services. It is not a village, because a village has services.
[geo-urban-s2.3.1] Rundale was a landholding system in which families worked land in common, in scattered strips of infield (the better land near the houses) and outfield (poorer land further out). It explains the clachan; it is not a settlement pattern.
[geo-urban-s2.3.2] Clachans and rundale were common in the west before the Famine.
[geo-urban-s2.3.3] The Great Famine (1845-49), when the potato crop failed and many people died or emigrated, hit the clachan districts of the west hardest.
[geo-urban-s2.3.4] Clachans broke up as the population fell and holdings were consolidated. The Congested Districts Board, set up in 1891 for the poor western districts, consolidated holdings, built roads and piers, and rehoused families on separate farms.
[geo-urban-s2.3.5] The Land Commission also moved houses onto individual farms, which is how dispersed settlement replaced the clachans.
[geo-urban-s2.3.6] A few clachans survive in the west.
[geo-urban-s2.4.b] Linear settlement is the third pattern. Houses line up along a feature that offers a route or dry, firm ground.
[geo-urban-s2.4.0] Houses are strung along a line: a road, a coast road, a valley floor, a river or a raised ridge such as an esker (a long ridge of gravel left by a glacier) or the edge of a drumlin (an oval hill of glacial deposit).
[geo-urban-s2.4.1] Linear settlement avoids marshy or steep land, because it is hard and costly to build on.
[geo-urban-s2.5.b] Some land on a map has no settlement at all, and exams ask you to explain why. There is more on reading this from OS maps in Map interpretation.
[geo-urban-s2.5.0] Uplands are steep, exposed and have poor soils, so they are hard to build on and farm.
[geo-urban-s2.5.1] Bogs are wet, poorly drained land, so houses cannot be built safely on them.
[geo-urban-s2.5.2] Flood plains (flat land beside a river that floods) are unsuitable for houses because of the risk of flooding.
[geo-urban-s2.5.3] Lakes take up land that could hold houses.
[geo-urban-s2.5.4] Forestry plantations are large areas of planted trees with no houses.
[geo-urban-s2.6.b] Two European examples show how planning and history shape rural settlement very differently from the Irish pattern.
[geo-urban-s2.6.0] Noordoostpolder, Netherlands, a polder (land drained from water) drained in 1942, is a planned rural landscape with a central town, Emmeloord, ringed by ten planned villages. There is more on this in Urban settlement: site, hierarchy, hinterland and central place theory.
[geo-urban-s2.6.1] In southern Italy, the Mezzogiorno's rural population lived in hilltop villages. The 1950 land reform scattered new farmhouses on reclaimed lowlands, so settlement moved from clustered to dispersed. There is more on this in A Peripheral European Region: the Mezzogiorno.
[geo-urban-s2.7.b] Ribbon development is houses built one plot deep along the roads leading out of towns and villages, and along rural roads generally. Several pressures made roadsides the favoured building sites.
[geo-urban-s2.7.0] Sites are cheaper than in towns, so buyers get more for their money.
[geo-urban-s2.7.1] Road frontage gives access to services such as electricity, water mains and phone or broadband lines, which already run along the road.
[geo-urban-s2.7.2] Farmers sold roadside sites, because these were the easiest plots to sell.
[geo-urban-s2.7.3] People wanted rural living near a town, with the countryside around them and work or schools close by.
[geo-urban-s2.7.4] Car ownership made commuting (travelling daily to work) and rural living practical.
[geo-urban-s2.7.5] Planning control was historically weak, so few limits stopped houses going up along roads.
[geo-urban-s2.7.6] Tax incentives and the property boom of the Celtic Tiger (the period of rapid economic growth in Ireland) encouraged building.
[geo-urban-s2.8.b] Ribbon housing suits the people who live in it, but it causes problems for roads, services, the environment and towns.
[geo-urban-s2.8.0] Traffic hazards: many entrances onto fast roads, because every house needs its own access and drivers have to slow or turn.
[geo-urban-s2.8.1] It is costly to service. School buses, post, waste collection, water and sewers must all reach scattered houses, which are far apart.
[geo-urban-s2.8.2] Septic tanks (private tanks that treat a house's own sewage) can pollute groundwater and private wells.
[geo-urban-s2.8.3] Loss of scenic landscape, because the roadsides fill with houses.
[geo-urban-s2.8.4] Car dependence: residents need a car for almost every journey, as shops and services are far away.
[geo-urban-s2.8.5] Town centres decline as people live outside them and spend less in them.
[geo-urban-s2.8.6] The boundary between town and country becomes blurred, because houses line the roads where the town should end.
[geo-urban-s2.9.b] Rural people often see ribbon housing differently, because it helps keep their communities going.
[geo-urban-s2.9.0] Ribbon development keeps rural populations, schools, GAA clubs and shops alive, because they need people living nearby to survive.
[geo-urban-s2.9.1] It lets families live near relatives and farms.
[geo-urban-s2.9.2] Housing is cheaper than in towns.
[geo-urban-s2.10.b] Planning is how the State controls where building takes place. It works through the local authority (the county or city council), with plans made at county and local level.
[geo-urban-s2.10.0] Planning permission is granted by the local authority.
[geo-urban-s2.10.1] Decisions can be appealed to An Bord Pleanála, the national planning appeals board, set up in 1977. It was renamed An Coimisiún Pleanála in 2025.
[geo-urban-s2.10.2] The Planning and Development Act 2000 requires each planning authority to make a County Development Plan every six years.
[geo-urban-s2.10.3] The County Development Plan sets zoning (deciding which land may be used for housing, industry or farming), rural housing policy, design rules and protected areas.
[geo-urban-s2.10.4] Local Area Plans cover towns and villages.
[geo-urban-s2.11.b] National policy sets the aims that county plans must follow. Rural housing is treated differently depending on how much pressure an area is under.
[geo-urban-s2.11.0] The National Spatial Strategy (2002) and the National Planning Framework (2018, also called Project Ireland 2040) set rural housing aims. There is more on both in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s2.11.1] Sustainable Rural Housing Guidelines for Planning Authorities, 2005, tell councils how to treat different types of rural area.
[geo-urban-s2.11.2] In areas under strong urban influence (close to a city or large town), rural houses are limited to people with a local economic or social need, to prevent commuter housing spreading.
[geo-urban-s2.11.3] In structurally weak areas (areas losing people and jobs), rural housing is encouraged to keep the population.
[geo-urban-s2.12.b] County Development Plans turn national aims into rules for each county. These are the typical rural policies.
[geo-urban-s2.12.0] Local-needs criteria restrict who may build a house in some rural areas, for example to those with a local economic or social need.
[geo-urban-s2.12.1] New entrances onto national and regional roads are restricted, because many entrances onto fast roads are a traffic hazard.
[geo-urban-s2.12.2] New housing is directed into villages and serviced sites (sites with water and sewers), which avoids scattered one-off houses.
[geo-urban-s2.12.3] Design guidelines cover siting, materials and hedgerows.
[geo-urban-s2.12.4] Minimum site sizes apply to septic tanks, so that there is enough land to treat the waste safely.
[geo-urban-s2.12.5] Scenic areas and views are protected from development.
[geo-urban-s2.12.6] Land is zoned around towns, so that building is guided to where it is planned.
[geo-urban-s2.13.b] Septic tanks serve houses with no connection to a public sewer, so poorly maintained ones can pollute the water that rural households drink.
[geo-urban-s2.13.0] Water Services (Amendment) Act 2012: domestic septic tanks must be registered, and they are inspected under an EPA (Environmental Protection Agency) plan.
[geo-urban-s2.13.1] Ireland has about half a million domestic wastewater treatment systems, mostly septic tanks (EPA estimate).
[geo-urban-s2.14.b] These programmes respond to rural depopulation (people leaving rural areas) and the decline of services such as post offices, Garda stations and pubs. They aim to sustain rural communities.
[geo-urban-s2.14.0] LEADER, an EU rural development programme, from 1991.
[geo-urban-s2.14.1] CLÁR, a State programme for rural areas suffering depopulation, from 2001.
[geo-urban-s2.14.2] The Town and Village Renewal Scheme, from 2016.
[geo-urban-s2.14.3] Our Rural Future, the Government's rural development policy for 2021-2025, published in 2021, supports remote working hubs (shared workplaces where people can work remotely from home areas).
[geo-urban-s2.15.b] To judge how well rural planning works, weigh the aims against each other and against what has happened.
[geo-urban-s2.15.0] The central tension is keeping rural communities alive while preventing sprawl (unplanned spread of housing).
[geo-urban-s2.15.1] Local-needs conditions have been criticised as unfair. In 2013 the EU Court of Justice ruled against a Flemish (Belgian) rule restricting who could buy land in some communes, which raised doubts about Irish local-needs conditions.
[geo-urban-s2.15.2] Planning has reduced ribbon development but has not stopped it.
[geo-urban-s2.15.3] Villages need sewage treatment before they can take more housing, which limits the policy of directing housing into villages.
