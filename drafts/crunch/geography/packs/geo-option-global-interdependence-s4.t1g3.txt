TARGET geo-option-global-interdependence-s4 (part 1 of 2): 4. Sustainable development as a model for the future
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.0.b] Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland, 1987). It is the model this whole section asks you to judge.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.0.0] It has three pillars, environmental, economic and social, which must be balanced against each other, because gains in one at the expense of the others do not count as sustainable.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.0.1] It contrasts with growth-only models, which measure development by rising output (the total goods and services produced), such as Rostow’s stages ending in mass consumption. There is more on these models in Views of development and underdevelopment.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.b] The idea grew over several decades, from a warning about limits in 1972 to global targets in 2015. Knowing the sequence lets you give a dated origin for the model.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.0] “The Limits to Growth” (1972), a report for the Club of Rome, used computer models to warn that unchecked population and economic growth would exhaust resources within about a century.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.1] The Brundtland Report, “Our Common Future”, was produced by the UN World Commission on Environment and Development in 1987. It gave the definition above.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.2] The Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro (1992) produced Agenda 21 (a global action plan) and the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (a treaty on tackling climate change).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.3] The Millennium Development Goals (eight goals, 2000–2015) were followed by the Sustainable Development Goals: 17 goals adopted in September 2015, with a target year of 2030.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.4] The Paris Agreement (2015) committed countries to tackle climate change.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.5] The world is off track to meet most of the Sustainable Development Goals, so the targets are not being reached on time.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.b] The case for making sustainability a priority rests on the fact that today’s growth can undermine tomorrow’s. Use these reasons when an essay asks whether it should be a priority.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.0] Resources are finite (there is a fixed amount) and are being overused, so they cannot support unlimited use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.1] Climate change, biodiversity loss (the decline of plant and animal species) and inequality between and within generations threaten future development.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.2] The poorest people suffer first when resources are degraded, because they depend most directly on land, water and fuel and have least to fall back on.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.3] Growth that destroys the resources it depends on cannot last, because the base of that growth is used up.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.b] The model is widely accepted but hard to apply. A good evaluation says where it falls short as well as where it works.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.0] It is vague and open to greenwashing, where firms and governments claim sustainability without real change, because nobody can say exactly what counts as sustainable.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.1] The pillars conflict: jobs can be at odds with protecting the environment, so balancing all three is difficult.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.2] Poorer countries argue that rich countries grew without limits and now ask them to slow down, which they see as unfair.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.3] The costs of change fall unequally, and progress on the Sustainable Development Goals is behind schedule.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.4.b] If development is not just rising output, it needs other yardsticks. Two alternatives measure wellbeing and environmental pressure instead.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.4.0] Bhutan’s Gross National Happiness, put forward in the 1970s by King Jigme Singye Wangchuck, measures wellbeing beyond income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.4.1] The ecological footprint measures the land and resources a population needs to support its consumption.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.b] Sustainable resource use means using resources so that they last. The principles below differ for resources that regrow and those that do not.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.0] Renewable resources (those that regenerate, such as fish and forests) should be used no faster than they regenerate. This rate is the sustainable yield.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.1] Non-renewable resources (those that cannot be replaced, such as fossil fuels) should be used efficiently and replaced by alternatives where possible.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.2] Reduce, reuse and recycle waste, so that fewer resources are taken and less waste is dumped.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.3] Protect biodiversity (the variety of living things).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.4] Involve local people in decisions about resources, since they live with the results.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.b] Fish are a renewable resource, but only if catches stay below what the stock can replace. Irish fishing policy has been shaped by what happened when this was ignored.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.0] Irish fishing has been shaped by past overfishing in EU waters, which is why rules now limit catches.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.1] The Common Fisheries Policy of 1983 was reformed in 2013, introducing maximum sustainable yield (the largest catch that can be taken without shrinking the stock) and a landing obligation (a discard ban), phased in during 2015–2019.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.2] The Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority was set up in 2007.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.3] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland’s largest fishing port.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.4] The North Sea herring fishery was closed from 1977 to the early 1980s and recovered under quotas (limits on catch), showing that management can work.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.5] The Grand Banks cod moratorium (a ban on fishing) of 2 July 1992 cost about 30,000 jobs off Newfoundland and is the warning for overfishing.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.b] Bord na Móna has moved from burning peat to renewable energy under its “Brown to Green” strategy. Peat is partly decayed plant matter dug from bogs, once burned for electricity.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.0] Bord na Móna was founded in 1946.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.1] The Lanesborough and Shannonbridge peat stations closed in December 2020, and peat harvesting ended in January 2021.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.2] Cutaway bogs (bogs where the peat has been removed) are being rehabilitated and rewetted, and peatland is valued as a store of carbon.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.3] Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly, is one of the rehabilitated sites.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.4] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo, has wind farms on cutaway bog.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.b] Moving electricity generation from fossil fuels to renewables is a central part of sustainable resource use. Ireland has set targets in law.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.0] Wind provides about a third of Irish electricity, and renewables about two-fifths (early 2020s).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.1] The Climate Action Plan targets 80% renewable electricity by 2030 and at least 5 GW (gigawatts, a measure of power capacity) of offshore wind by 2030.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.2] The Climate Act 2021 requires a 51% cut in emissions by 2030 (from 2018) and net zero (no more greenhouse gas added than removed) by 2050.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.3] The East–West Interconnector (2012), a cable that lets electricity flow between countries, links Ireland and Britain, and the Celtic Interconnector to France is due in the late 2020s.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.b] Farming shapes most Irish land, so how it is run matters for sustainability. Schemes reward good practice, but dairy expansion pulls the other way.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.0] BurrenLIFE (2005–2010) and then the Burren Programme pay farmers according to habitat scores for each field, so they are paid for results.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.1] Agri-environment schemes (payments to farmers for protecting the environment) have included REPS (1994), GLAS (2015) and ACRES (2023).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.2] Origin Green, Bord Bia’s sustainability programme, began in 2012.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.3] Milk quotas (limits on how much milk each farm could produce) were abolished on 1 April 2015, and the dairy herd grew strongly afterwards.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.4] Agriculture produces over a third of Irish greenhouse gas emissions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.5] Only about half of Irish waters are in good or high ecological status (EPA, 2016–2021).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.6] Dairy expansion is therefore in tension with water quality and emissions targets.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.10.b] Sustainability also depends on changing everyday behaviour. Two Irish measures show how charges and deposits can cut waste.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.10.0] The plastic bag levy (a charge on each bag) began in March 2002 at 15 cent, and plastic bag use fell by over 90%.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.10.1] The Deposit Return Scheme (Re-turn) began on 1 February 2024.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.b] Only about 11% of Ireland is forest, and much of that is not native. Forestry is a final Irish example of resource use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.0] Forest cover in Ireland is about 11%, mostly conifer plantations of Sitka spruce.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.1] Coillte was set up in 1988 to manage state forests.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.2] Remnants of native woodland, such as the Killarney oakwoods, show what Ireland has lost.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.3] Ireland needs more native broadleaf woodland, because plantations of one conifer support less variety of life than native woods.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.4] Managed Scandinavian forests are another example of forest use. There is more on this in An interdependent global economy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.b] Costa Rica, in Central America between Nicaragua and Panama, shows how a country can reverse resource loss.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.0] In the 1970s and 1980s forests were cleared for cattle pasture, and by the late 1980s forest covered only about a fifth of the country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.1] The Payment for Environmental Services programme, started in 1997 and run by the national forestry fund FONAFIFO, pays landowners to protect and replant forest, so keeping trees earns income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.2] The programme is paid for largely by a tax on fuel.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.3] Forest cover has since risen to over half of the country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.4] About a quarter of the land is in national parks and other protected areas.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.5] Ecotourism (tourism based on nature) is one of the largest sources of foreign income, giving forests an economic value.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.13.b] Costa Rica’s sustainability also shows in how it generates power and spends public money.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.13.0] Since 2015 almost all of Costa Rica’s electricity has come from renewable sources (over 98% in most years, but about 95% in 2023, when drought cut hydropower), mainly hydroelectric (from flowing water), with geothermal (from underground heat) and wind.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.13.1] Costa Rica abolished its army in 1948 and spends instead on education and health.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.13.2] In 2021 Costa Rica won the first Earthshot Prize (Protect and Restore Nature) for its forest recovery.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.14.b] Costa Rica is not a perfect model. Two weaknesses are worth including in an evaluation.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.14.0] Transport still runs mainly on imported oil, so it has not left fossil fuels behind.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.14.1] Tourism puts pressure on protected areas.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.15.b] Where resources are used unsustainably, the damage can be severe and lasting. Two cases serve as warnings.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.15.0] The Aral Sea: rivers were diverted from the 1960s to irrigate Soviet cotton, and the sea shrank to a fraction of its size; the Kok-Aral dam was built in 2005.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.15.1] Amazon deforestation is another warning of unsustainable use. There is more on this in An interdependent global economy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.b] Fair trade exists because farmers in poorer countries often get a poor deal from world markets. The six points below are the problems it tries to solve.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.0] Commodity prices (prices of raw products such as coffee and cocoa) are low and unstable, and small farmers receive a tiny share of the final price.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.1] Middlemen (traders between the farmer and the buyer) take a large share of the value of farmers’ crops.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.2] Poor working conditions and child labour are common in producing countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.3] Terms of trade decline as the prices of raw materials fall relative to manufactured goods, so the same crop buys less each year.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.4] Tariff escalation (tariffs, or import taxes, that rise as a product is processed): coffee beans enter rich markets with low tariffs, but roasted, processed coffee faces higher ones.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.5] Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana grow over half of the world’s cocoa, mostly exported as beans.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.b] Fair trade is a response to those problems, with a short but dated history.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.0] Fair trade is a trading partnership based on dialogue, transparency and respect that seeks greater equity (fairness) in international trade.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.1] The first fair trade label, Max Havelaar, was launched in the Netherlands in 1988 for Mexican coffee.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.2] Fairtrade International coordinates the Fairtrade Mark, the certification label on products.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.3] Garstang, Lancashire, England, declared itself the world’s first Fairtrade town in 2000.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.b] Fairtrade protects producers through prices, contracts, co-operatives and standards. Each feature solves part of the problem set out above.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.0] The Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.1] The minimum is a floor, not a guaranteed higher price: when the market price is above the minimum, producers receive the market price plus the premium.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.2] The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative, spent on projects the members choose democratically.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.3] Long-term contracts and pre-finance give producers credit before the harvest, so they are not forced into debt to middlemen.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.4] Producers are organised in democratic co-operatives (groups owned and run by their members).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.5] Labour standards ban forced and child labour, protect the freedom to join unions and require safe conditions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.6] Environmental standards restrict pesticides, protect water and forests and ban GM (genetically modified) crops.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.7] The Fairtrade Mark is a guarantee to consumers that these standards have been met.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.19.b] Fairtrade covers many crops and reaches a large number of people, but it remains small in world terms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.19.0] Fairtrade products include crops such as coffee, cocoa, tea, bananas, sugar, cotton and flowers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.19.1] Close to 2 million farmers and workers belong to Fairtrade-certified producer organisations in about 70 countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.b] Kuapa Kokoo is a Fairtrade cocoa farmers’ co-operative in Ghana, the world’s second-largest cocoa producer. It stands out because the farmers also part-own a chocolate brand.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.0] Kuapa Kokoo, meaning “good cocoa farmer” in Twi, was founded in 1993.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.1] Divine Chocolate was set up in Britain in 1998, part-owned by Kuapa Kokoo. Since 2020 the German chocolate maker Ludwig Weinrich has held the majority; Kuapa Kokoo kept about 20% of the shares and seats on the board.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.2] Farmers receive a share of Divine’s profits as well as the Fairtrade price and premium.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.3] Premium spending has included wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.4] Kuapa Kokoo encourages women to take leadership roles in its village societies.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.5] Farmers share in the profits of the brand, not just the beans they sell, so some of the value added in making chocolate stays with them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.21.b] The Oromia union in Ethiopia shows how a co-operative can sell directly to buyers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.21.0] The union was founded in 1999 and led by Tadesse Meskela.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.21.1] It sells members’ coffee directly to buyers, including Fairtrade coffee, cutting out middlemen who would otherwise take a share of the price.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.21.2] Its premium has paid for schools, clinics and water.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.21.3] Its story is told in the documentary “Black Gold” (2006).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.21.4] Ethiopia is the original home of arabica coffee.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.b] Fair trade can contribute to development by changing the position of producers in trade. This is the core of an essay on what fair trade can contribute.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.0] Higher and more stable income gives farmers money to invest and plan.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.1] The premium funds community projects such as education, health and clean water.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.2] Democratic co-operatives empower producers (give them control) to take decisions together.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.3] Women’s participation in co-operatives gives women a voice and income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.4] Organic farming and reduced clearing of land help environmental sustainability.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.5] For example, child labour can be reduced when families earn a fairer income, because children are less needed to earn money.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.6] Producers learn processing and marketing skills.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.22.7] Fair trade raises consumer awareness in rich countries and pressures large firms to change.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.23.b] Fair trade is also visible in Irish towns, schools and shops.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.23.0] Fairtrade Ireland licenses the Fairtrade Mark in Ireland.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.23.1] Clonakilty, Co. Cork, became Ireland’s first Fairtrade town in 2003.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.23.2] Towns, schools and universities can apply for Fairtrade status.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.23.3] Fairtrade Fortnight takes place each year in late February and March.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.23.4] Supermarkets stock Fairtrade ranges, and Irish NGOs (non-governmental organisations, voluntary groups) promote fair trade.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.b] Fair trade helps those it reaches, but it has real limits. A balanced essay includes them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.0] Fair trade is only a tiny share of world trade.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.1] Higher prices put off some consumers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.2] Certification costs exclude the poorest farmers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.3] A 2014 study by the School of Oriental and African Studies (SOAS), University of London, of Ethiopia and Uganda found that hired labourers on some Fairtrade-certified farms were paid less than those on comparable non-certified farms; Fairtrade disputed parts of the findings.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.4] Benefits go mainly to co-operative members rather than hired labourers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.5] Market prices sometimes exceed the minimum price, so the floor then gives no extra protection.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.6] Fair trade does not change the trade rules, such as subsidies and tariff escalation.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.24.7] Cadbury Dairy Milk carried the Fairtrade Mark from 2009 and switched in 2017 to Mondelēz’s own Cocoa Life scheme in Britain and Ireland. Most value is still added in rich countries unless producers process their own crops, which Divine Chocolate does.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.25.b] Fair trade is a model of sustainable, just trade that improves the lives of those involved and shows what is possible.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.25.0] Its impact on development depends on its scale and on wider changes in trade rules.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.26.b] Sustainable development is about fairness as well as resources. A project cannot be called sustainable if it harms the people in its way.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.26.0] Development is not sustainable if it excludes or harms groups of people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.26.1] Minority groups often lack political power, so their land and rights are taken for development projects such as dams, mines, plantations and wind farms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.27.b] International law sets out the rights of minorities and indigenous peoples (the original inhabitants of a region). Three documents form the framework.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.27.0] The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted on 10 December 1948.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.27.1] ILO Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention (No. 169) was adopted in 1989.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.27.2] The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples was adopted by the UN General Assembly on 13 September 2007; it includes free, prior and informed consent, meaning communities must agree in advance and with full information.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.28.b] Indigenous peoples of the Amazon in Brazil show how development can take land and harm health. Their forests are also central to sustainable resource use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.28.0] Indigenous peoples have lost land to ranching, soya, logging, mining and dams.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.28.1] Brazil’s Census 2022 counted about 1.7 million indigenous people, under 1% of the population.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.28.2] In 2023 the Brazilian government declared a public health emergency in Yanomami territory after illegal gold mining caused malnutrition and disease.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.28.3] The Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River, completed in 2019, was opposed by the Kayapó and displaced thousands of people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.28.4] Indigenous territories are among the best-protected forests, linking justice to sustainable resource use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.29.b] Chico Mendes became a symbol of the struggle to protect the Amazon forest and the people who live from it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.29.0] Chico Mendes, a rubber tapper (a worker who collects rubber from trees), was murdered on 22 December 1988 at Xapuri, Acre.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.29.1] Extractive reserves, where forest is harvested sustainably, were set up after his death.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.b] Irish Travellers are an ethnic group, not a race. Their case shows how inequality and discrimination block sustainable development for a minority.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.0] Irish Travellers were formally recognised as an ethnic minority on 1 March 2017.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.1] The 2022 Census counted about 33,000 Travellers, under 1% of the population.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.2] The All Ireland Traveller Health Study (2010) found that Traveller men lived about 15 years less than men in the general population, and Traveller women about 11.5 years less.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.3] The same study found that the suicide rate among Traveller men was about six times the general rate.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.4] The 2016 Census found that about 80% of Travellers in the labour force were unemployed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.5] Accommodation shortages persist, and local authority Traveller accommodation budgets have gone unspent.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.6] Discrimination in services is outlawed by the Equal Status Acts (2000).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.30.7] Responses include equality law, ethnic status and the National Traveller and Roma Inclusion Strategy 2017–2021.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.31.b] The Roma are a European minority whose case reached the European Court of Human Rights. They show justice issues beyond Ireland.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.31.0] The Roma are Europe’s largest ethnic minority, about 10–12 million people (Council of Europe estimate), many in Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, Slovakia and Spain.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.31.1] The Roma face poverty, segregated housing and schools, and discrimination; they were persecuted in the Holocaust.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.31.2] In D.H. and Others v Czech Republic (European Court of Human Rights, 2007) the court ruled that placing Roma children in “special schools” for children with learning disabilities was unlawful discrimination.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.31.3] EU frameworks include the Framework for National Roma Integration Strategies (2011) and the Roma strategic framework for equality, inclusion and participation (2020–2030).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.32.b] The Fosen case shows that green development must also be just. A renewable energy project can still harm a minority.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.32.0] The Sámi are the indigenous people of northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia’s Kola Peninsula; reindeer herding is central to their culture.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.32.1] Fosen, in central Norway, is one of Europe’s largest onshore wind farms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.32.2] On 11 October 2021 Norway’s Supreme Court ruled that the turbines, built on reindeer grazing land, violated the herders’ right to enjoy their culture.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.32.3] The turbines were not removed and kept running after the ruling, which was the cause of the protests.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.32.4] In 2023 Sámi activists, joined by Greta Thunberg, blocked government buildings in Oslo in protest.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.32.5] In 2023–24 the herders reached agreements with the state and the wind-farm owners.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.33.b] Two more minority groups belong to other chapters of the course, where they are taught in full.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.33.0] The Rohingya and Uyghurs are discussed in An interdependent global economy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.33.1] Aboriginal Australians are discussed in Racial groupings.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.34.b] Self-reliance is the last aspect of the model: development as self-help, in which people rely on themselves rather than outsiders.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.34.0] Self-reliance means communities and countries meet their own needs with their own resources, skills and decisions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.34.1] It reduces dependence on aid, loans, foreign companies and imports, and links to bottom-up development (development that starts with local people) and appropriate technology (technology suited to local needs and skills).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.b] Tanzania under Julius Nyerere, its first president, is a mixed example: strong social gains, but economic failure.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.0] Tanganyika gained independence in 1961 and united with Zanzibar as Tanzania in 1964.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.1] The Arusha Declaration of 1967 set out socialism and self-reliance, known as Ujamaa.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.2] Ujamaa (“familyhood”) villages and collective farming were created, with education for self-reliance.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.3] Kiswahili was promoted as a uniting national language, and national unity and peace were achieved.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.4] Literacy and school enrolment rose.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.5] Villagisation from 1973 to 1976 moved millions of people, often by force, into planned villages.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.6] Food and export output fell, and the economy went into crisis.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.7] Nyerere stepped down as president in 1985, and in 1986 Tanzania agreed an IMF (International Monetary Fund) programme and began market reforms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.35.8] Evaluation: Ujamaa gave social gains but was not an economic success, and forced villagisation damaged the model.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.36.b] Self-reliance was also a tool against colonial rule.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.36.0] Mahatma Gandhi promoted swadeshi (buy Indian) and khadi (hand-spun cloth) as self-reliance against colonial dependence.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.37.b] Small-scale technology can help communities help themselves.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s1: 1. Renewable and non-renewable resources in the economy
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.b] A resource is anything in the environment that people use to meet their needs. Resources are classed by whether nature can replace them, and this decides whether a country can keep using them for ever.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.0] Resources include energy, minerals, water, soil, forests, fish, land and scenery, so the word covers far more than fuels.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.1] Non-renewable (stock, finite) resources formed over millions of years, so they are used up far faster than they form and will eventually run out. Examples are coal, oil, natural gas, peat, metal ores and uranium.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.2] Renewable (flow) resources are replaced naturally, so they will not run out however much is used. Examples are wind, solar, hydro (running water), wave, tidal and geothermal heat (heat from inside the Earth).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.3] Some resources are renewable only if managed sustainably (used no faster than they are replaced): forests, fish stocks, soils, groundwater and biomass (plant material used as fuel). Overuse turns them into exhausted resources, as happens with fish stocks. There is more on this in Sustainable economic development.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.b] Three kinds of fuel are non-renewable. Fossil fuels and peat formed from once-living material, and uranium is a finite mineral.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas and peat) formed from the remains of plants or marine organisms buried and compressed over millions of years. Burning them releases the carbon they stored.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.1] Peat is non-renewable because a bog (wet ground where dead plants build up as peat) grows only about 1 mm a year, far slower than it is cut.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.2] Nuclear energy from uranium is an alternative to fossil fuels with low carbon emissions, but it is not renewable because uranium is finite.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.b] Energy is the resource that the rest of the economy runs on. This is why a country's energy supply matters so much to its wealth and security.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.0] Primary energy is energy in its raw form, such as oil, gas or wind. Electricity is a secondary form made from primary sources, for example by burning gas or by turning turbines with wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.1] Renewables give a far larger share of electricity than of total energy, because much of total energy is oil used for transport and heating, which renewables supply little of.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.2] Energy powers industry, transport, farming and homes, so resources are central to the economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.3] Energy security means a secure and affordable supply that is not disrupted by price shocks (sudden large rises in price) or supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.4] A country that imports its energy pays a large import bill (the money spent buying energy from abroad) and is exposed to price shocks and supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.b] Ireland has few fossil fuels of its own, so it depends heavily on imported energy. The mix of fuels it uses shows where that dependence lies.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.0] Import dependence (the share of energy bought from abroad): Ireland imported close to 90% of its energy in the years before Corrib gas came ashore in 2015.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.1] The share fell after Corrib began and has risen again as Corrib's output declines. In the early 2020s Ireland still imported about three-quarters of its energy (SEAI, the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.2] Early 2020s primary energy mix (SEAI): oil just under half and the largest single source; natural gas about a third; renewables a little over a tenth; coal and peat small and falling.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.3] Adding oil, gas, coal and peat shows that fossil fuels still supply most of Ireland's primary energy, while renewables are growing.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.b] Oil is Ireland's largest single source of primary energy, and Ireland produces none of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.0] All of Ireland's oil is imported, which adds to the import bill and leaves the country exposed to price shocks.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.1] Oil is used mainly for transport and home heating.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.2] Whitegate, Co. Cork, is Ireland's only oil refinery (the plant that turns crude oil into fuels).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.b] Natural gas is Ireland's second-largest primary energy source and its largest source of electricity. Home gas fields supplied it for decades but are now running down.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.0] Kinsale Head gas field, off Co. Cork, was discovered in 1971 and began production in 1978 as Ireland's first commercial gas field. Production ended in 2020 when the field was depleted (used up), so it is no longer a working field.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.1] Corrib gas field, off north-west Co. Mayo, was discovered in 1996 and first flowed in December 2015. Its gas is processed at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.2] For a time after 2015 Corrib supplied over half of Ireland's gas. Its output is now declining, which is why import dependence has risen again.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.3] Most of Ireland's gas now comes from Britain through interconnector pipelines (cross-border links) from Moffat, Scotland.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.4] Gas is the largest single source of Irish electricity, because gas-fired power stations generate more of it than any other fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.b] Ireland's own coal fields are small and long closed. Moneypoint, the big coal-burning station, ran on imported coal and has now stopped burning coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.0] Ireland's native coalfields are small and long closed. They are Arigna, Co. Roscommon, where mining ended in 1990 and which is now the Arigna Mining Experience, and Castlecomer, Co. Kilkenny.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.1] Moneypoint, Co. Clare, on the Shannon Estuary, is an ESB (Electricity Supply Board) coal-fired station opened in 1985. At about 900 MW (megawatts, a measure of power output) it is Ireland's largest power station.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.2] Moneypoint burned imported coal, brought in by large ships to its deep-water jetty (a landing pier where water is deep enough for big ships).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.3] Moneypoint stopped burning coal in 2025.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.b] Peat, cut from the bogs of the Midlands, was Ireland's own fuel and powered stations for decades. It has now ended, and the cutaway bogs (bogs from which the peat has been removed) are being given new uses.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.0] Bord na Móna, the State peat company, was founded in 1946.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.1] Peat-fired stations at Lanesborough (Lough Ree) and Shannonbridge (West Offaly) closed in December 2020. Bord na Móna ended all peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.2] Edenderry, Co. Offaly, switched from peat to biomass, a renewable fuel of wood and energy crops.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.3] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo: the peat station closed in 2003 and was replaced by wind farms on the cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.4] Cutaway bogs are reused. Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly, has turned a cutaway bog into parkland, wetlands and walking and cycling trails.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.b] Hydroelectric power uses falling water to turn turbines (machines that spin to drive generators). The Shannon Scheme at Ardnacrusha was an early large scheme, and little potential is left for more.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.0] Ardnacrusha, on the River Shannon near Limerick, was built by the German firm Siemens and opened in 1929 as the Shannon Scheme. The ESB was set up in 1927 to run it, and for some years it supplied most of the country's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.1] Other stations: Poulaphouca on the River Liffey, Co. Wicklow; Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid on the River Lee, Co. Cork; and Cathaleen's Fall at Ballyshannon on the River Erne, Co. Donegal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.2] Turlough Hill, Co. Wicklow, opened in 1974 as an ESB pumped-storage station. Water is pumped uphill when demand is low and released through turbines at peak times, so it stores energy for when it is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.3] Little potential is left for large new hydro schemes, because the best sites were already used.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.b] Wind is Ireland's main renewable source. The country's position on the Atlantic edge of Europe gives it an advantage.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.0] Wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s. Renewables, mostly wind, supplied about two-fifths.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.1] Strong, frequent Atlantic winds make the western coasts and uplands the best sites for wind farms.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.2] Bellacorick wind farm, Co. Mayo (1992), was Ireland's first commercial wind farm. Oweninny wind farm was built on the same cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.3] Galway Wind Park, Connemara, completed in 2017, is one of Ireland's largest onshore (on land) wind farms.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.4] Arklow Bank Wind Park, off Co. Wicklow, opened in 2004 as Ireland's first offshore (at sea) wind farm, with seven turbines. Offshore wind is planned to grow strongly, with at least 5 GW targeted by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.b] Ireland's other renewable sources are smaller than wind. Some are growing quickly and some are still at an early stage.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.0] Solar power has grown rapidly since the early 2020s, through large solar farms on farmland and panels on rooftops. SEAI grants support home installations.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.1] The Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is Ireland's wave-energy test site, where wave machines are trialled.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.2] Geothermal energy is limited to low-temperature ground-source heat pumps (devices that take heat from the ground to warm buildings).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.3] Tidal energy harnesses the rise and fall of the sea, and it is at an early stage in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.4] Biomass (wood and energy crops) and biofuels (fuels made from plant material) are the remaining renewable sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.b] Governments set targets to push renewables up and cut emissions. Ireland missed its 2020 target and has set tougher ones for 2030 and 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.0] Ireland's 2020 EU target was 16% of gross final energy consumption (all the energy used by final users such as homes, industry and transport) from renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.1] Ireland reached only about 13.5% and missed the target. It was one of the furthest from its target of any EU member state.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.2] Climate Action Plan: 80% of electricity from renewables by 2030, and at least 5 GW (gigawatts) of offshore wind by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.3] Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021: cut greenhouse gas emissions by 51% by 2030, compared with 2018, and reach climate neutrality (net zero, meaning emissions no higher than what is removed) by 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.b] Ireland's renewable share fell short of its 2020 target for six linked reasons. Several come from how Irish people live and travel, and some from delays in building.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.0] Heavy dependence on cars and road freight for transport, which runs on oil, so renewables supply little of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.1] Oil used for home heating in a dispersed rural settlement pattern (homes spread out, so gas networks and shared heating are hard to provide).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.2] Slow planning and grid connection for wind farms, which delayed new capacity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.3] No large hydro potential left for new schemes, because the best sites were already used.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.4] A late start on offshore wind, which left Ireland with less renewable capacity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.5] Strong economic and population growth raising energy demand, which made the renewable share harder to raise.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.b] Interconnectors are cables or pipelines that link Ireland's energy network to other countries. They help security of supply because power and gas can flow in when Ireland needs them.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.0] The East-West Interconnector, opened in 2012, links Co. Dublin to north Wales by electricity cable.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.1] The Celtic Interconnector, from Co. Cork to Brittany, France, is under construction and due to link Ireland to the EU mainland grid in the late 2020s.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.2] Gas also arrives by interconnector pipelines from Scotland, which is how most of Ireland's gas now reaches it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.b] Knowing which sectors use most energy shows where demand is highest and where savings or renewables matter most.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.0] Transport is the largest energy-using sector in Ireland (SEAI).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.1] Heat for homes and businesses comes next, then electricity generation for industry and services.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.2] Data centres (buildings full of computer servers) used about a fifth of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office). Most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.b] Europe depends heavily on imported energy, and its main oil and gas comes from the North Sea and from suppliers outside the EU.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.0] The EU imports close to three-fifths of its energy (early 2020s, Eurostat, the EU's statistics office).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.1] The North Sea, shared by the UK and Norway, is Europe's main oil and gas province (an area rich in oil and gas).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.2] Norway is not an EU member but is one of the EU's main gas suppliers, by pipeline.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.b] Relying on one supplier for a vital fuel is risky. Europe learned this when Russia, its biggest gas supplier, invaded Ukraine.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.0] Before 2022 Russia supplied about two-fifths of the EU's imported gas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.1] Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022. Gas and electricity prices soared across Europe.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.2] REPowerEU plan, May 2022: cut dependence on Russian fossil fuels by saving energy, importing more liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so ships can carry it, much of it from the USA) and speeding up renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.3] Russia's share of EU gas imports has since fallen sharply.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.17.b] National energy mixes differ widely across Europe. France relies on nuclear power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.17.0] Nuclear power (electricity made from splitting uranium atoms) supplies about two-thirds of France's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.b] Germany is changing its energy mix by moving to renewables and ending nuclear power, though it still burns coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.0] The Energiewende (energy transition) is Germany's long-term switch to renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.1] After the Fukushima disaster in Japan in March 2011 (a nuclear accident), Germany decided to phase out nuclear power. Its last three reactors closed on 15 April 2023.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.2] Germany still burns lignite (brown coal) from huge open-cast mines (mines dug from the surface) in the Rhineland. A 2020 law sets 2038 at the latest as the end of coal-fired power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.19.b] Poland shows a country whose power still rests on coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.19.0] Coal generates well over half of Poland's electricity (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.b] Denmark shows a country that has built its electricity supply around wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.0] Wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity (2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.1] Denmark was an early leader in wind power and is home to the turbine maker Vestas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.b] Two European countries that are not EU members get nearly all their electricity from renewable sources, thanks to their landscapes.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.0] Hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of Norway's electricity, from its mountains, high rainfall and glacial lakes, which give plenty of falling water.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.1] Geothermal and hydro supply almost all of Iceland's electricity, and about 9 in 10 Icelandic homes are heated geothermally.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.b] The EU sets renewable targets for all its members. It met its 2020 target and has raised the goal for 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.0] The EU target of 20% renewable energy by 2020 was achieved, at about 22%.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.1] The revised Renewable Energy Directive (2023, an EU law binding on member states) requires at least 42.5% renewable energy by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.2] Renewables supplied about a quarter of EU gross final energy consumption in 2023, so the EU still has far to go to reach 42.5%.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.b] Despite the growth of renewables, fossil fuels still supply most of the EU's energy. Final energy (the energy used by consumers) is spread across several sectors.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.0] Fossil fuels (oil, gas and coal) still supply most of the EU's energy. Oil is the largest single source, mainly for transport.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.1] Transport, households and industry each take roughly a quarter to a third of final energy. Services take a smaller share.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.b] Energy production and consumption are not spread evenly across Europe. Which role a country plays depends on its industry and its natural resources.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.0] Core industrial countries, such as Germany and France, consume most of the energy, because their large industry and populations need so much.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.1] Resource-rich countries, such as Norway, export energy, because they produce more than they need.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.2] Peripheral countries, such as Ireland, depend on imports, because they have few fuels of their own.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s4: 4. Global issues and a justice perspective
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.b] Global issues are problems that cross borders and affect the whole world, such as poverty, inequality, trade rules, debt, climate change and migration. A justice perspective asks whether the distribution of wealth and power is fair, who decides, and who benefits. It treats development as a matter of rights, not charity.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.0] The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948, includes rights to food, health, education and decent work.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.1] Poverty denies these rights, so a justice perspective treats poverty as a rights failure rather than a matter of charity. In other words, people are owed these things, not given them as a favour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.b] Justice questions start from the scale of the problem. The figures below show how many people are extremely poor, where they live, and how unevenly wealth is shared.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.0] About 700 million people lived in extreme poverty in 2022, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa (the part of Africa south of the Sahara). The World Bank, the UN-linked development lender, measures extreme poverty as living on under 2.15 US dollars a day (2017 prices).
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.1] The share of the world's population in extreme poverty fell from over a third in 1990 to under a tenth by 2019, so the trend is strongly downward even though the numbers remain large.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.2] The richest 10% own about 76% of global wealth, while the poorest half own about 2% (World Inequality Report 2022). This shows how unequal the sharing of wealth is.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.b] The UN has set global targets to measure progress against poverty and injustice. The first set ran from 2000 to 2015 and the second runs to 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.0] The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000-2015) had eight goals. The target of halving the share of people in extreme poverty from 1990 was met by 2010, five years early, largely thanks to China.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.1] The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were adopted by the UN in September 2015. They have 17 goals, to be met by 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.2] The SDGs target poverty (SDG 1), hunger (SDG 2), quality education (SDG 4), gender equality (SDG 5), reduced inequalities (SDG 10) and climate action (SDG 13). Other targets cover health, clean water and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.3] The world is off track to meet most SDG targets by 2030, so shortfalls remain alongside the progress.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.b] Trade rules and prices can leave poor countries poorer. Many depend on selling raw materials, and the biggest profits are made further along the chain, in rich countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.0] Many developing countries depend on exporting a few primary commodities (raw materials such as coffee, cocoa, cotton and minerals), whose prices are low and unstable. This makes their income hard to plan around.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.1] The terms of trade (the amount of imports a country's exports can buy) tend to move against these countries. Their exports buy fewer imports over time, while they import manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.2] Most of the final price of coffee, chocolate or clothing goes to processors, brands and retailers in rich countries. The grower, at the start of the value chain (the steps from raw material to finished product), receives a small share.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.3] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials. This discourages poor countries from processing their own crops, so they stay stuck exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.b] Rich countries also help their own farmers with money, and this can push down the prices poor farmers receive. Cotton is the clearest example.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.0] Rich-country farm subsidies (government payments to farmers), such as US cotton subsidies and the EU's Common Agricultural Policy in the past, let surpluses be sold cheaply abroad. This is called dumping, and it undercuts poor farmers who cannot match the low price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.1] The Cotton-4 West African producers, Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad and Mali, were harmed by these subsidies.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.2] The WTO (World Trade Organization, the body that rules on trade disputes) ruled in Brazil's favour against US cotton subsidies in 2004-05.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.b] Governments and trade bodies have tried to make trade fairer for poor countries, with mixed success. Fair trade is a third response, because it works through consumers rather than governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.0] The WTO Doha Development Round (a set of global trade talks meant to help developing countries) was launched in 2001 but has stalled without agreement.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.1] The EU's 'Everything But Arms' scheme (2001) gives the least developed countries duty-free (no tariffs) and quota-free (no limit on amounts) access to the EU for all products except arms and ammunition.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.b] Fair trade is a trading model in which producers receive guaranteed terms and the Fairtrade Mark identifies certified products. It tries to give growers a bigger and steadier share than the normal market does.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.0] The Fairtrade Minimum Price covers the cost of sustainable production. It is a guaranteed floor, not a guaranteed higher price: when the market price is higher, producers receive the market price plus the premium.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.1] The Fairtrade Premium is paid to producer co-operatives (groups of farmers who work together and share the benefits) for community projects such as schools, clinics and clean water.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.2] Fair trade also requires long-term contracts, decent working conditions, no child labour, environmental standards and democratically run co-operatives.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.3] Fair trade covers coffee, tea, cocoa, bananas, sugar and cotton, for example, which are the kinds of crops whose growers get a small share of the price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.4] The first fair trade label, Max Havelaar, was launched in the Netherlands in 1988 for Mexican coffee. Fairtrade International now coordinates the Fairtrade Mark.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.b] Ireland has its own fair trade links, but fair trade has weaknesses that an evaluation should include.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.0] Fairtrade Ireland (Fairtrade Mark Ireland) licenses the Fairtrade Mark in Ireland, meaning it gives permission for products to carry it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.1] Irish towns and schools can apply for Fairtrade status. Fairtrade Fortnight takes place each year in late February and March.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.2] Limit: fair trade makes up only a tiny share of world trade, so it reaches few producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.3] Limit: certified products can cost consumers more, and certification is expensive for small producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.4] Limit: fair trade does not change the trade rules that cause unfair trade in the first place, so it treats the symptoms rather than the cause.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.b] In the 1970s and 1980s, cheap lending turned into a debt crisis for many developing countries. Each step below made repayment harder.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.0] 1. When oil prices rose sharply, oil-exporting countries deposited their surplus money in banks. Banks lent these deposits, called petrodollars, to developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.1] 2. Interest rates then rose sharply and commodity prices fell. Borrowers earned less from their exports while owing more, so many could not afford repayments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.2] 3. Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts, starting the Latin American debt crisis.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.3] 4. Many African and Latin American countries could not repay their debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.b] Heavy debt took money away from the people it was meant to help, and in some cases the people repaying had never benefited from the loan.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.0] Governments spent more on debt repayments than on health and education, so public services suffered.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.1] Countries took new loans to repay old ones, so the debt grew.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.2] The 1980s 'lost decade' hit Latin America and Africa hard, because debt blocked progress in those years.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.3] Some debts were 'odious': dictators borrowed the money and stole or wasted it, yet the people were made to repay it. This is the clearest justice argument against debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.b] Lenders did not simply hand over money. The IMF (International Monetary Fund) and the World Bank attached conditions to their loans, which is different from tied aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.0] IMF and World Bank loans came with conditions. These cut public spending, introduced school and health fees, privatised state firms (sold them to private owners), removed food subsidies, devalued the currency (lowered its value against others) and opened markets to imports.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.1] Structural adjustment programmes, as these packages of conditions were called, were criticised for hurting the poorest people most. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.b] Campaigns and lenders eventually cancelled some debt for the poorest countries, with conditions. Relief had real results, but debt is building up again.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.0] The Jubilee 2000 campaign pressed for debt cancellation by the millennium, so that poor countries could stop repaying old debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.1] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative was launched in 1996 by the IMF and World Bank.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.2] The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005) followed the G8 summit (a meeting of leaders of major rich countries) at Gleneagles, Scotland, in July 2005. It was backed by the Make Poverty History campaign and the Live 8 concerts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.3] More than 30 countries, most in Africa, have received debt relief under HIPC, on condition that the savings go to poverty reduction.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.4] Relief has funded results such as free primary education in some relieved countries, so more children are in school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.5] Debt is rising again in the 2020s, so relief has not ended the problem.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.b] Aid is help given to poorer countries. It comes in different forms, depending on its purpose, who gives it and how it is channelled.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.0] Emergency (humanitarian) aid responds to disasters and famines, so it is short-term and urgent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.1] Long-term development aid funds projects that build lasting capacity, such as a country's ability to meet its own needs.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.2] Bilateral aid passes from one government to another, so the donor government decides directly where it goes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.3] Multilateral aid is channelled through bodies such as the UN, the World Bank and the EU, so several donors pool their money.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.4] NGO (voluntary) aid is given by non-governmental organisations, which are charities independent of governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.5] Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, so it benefits the donor as well as the receiver.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.b] The UN has set a target for how much rich countries should give, and most do not meet it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.0] The UN target, set in 1970, is that rich countries give 0.7% of their gross national income (the total income earned by a country's people and firms) as official development aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.1] Norway, Sweden, Luxembourg and Denmark have reached or exceeded the target in recent years, so only a few countries reach it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.2] Ireland has not yet reached the target (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.b] Aid is neither all good nor all bad. A balanced answer gives both sides, with reasons, and ends with what better aid looks like.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.0] Helps: aid saves lives in emergencies and funds schools, clinics, wells and vaccination programmes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.1] Helps: aid builds roads and infrastructure, trains teachers, nurses and farmers, and supports women's groups and small businesses.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.2] Hinders: tied aid benefits donor firms and may supply unsuitable technology, because the receiver must buy what the donor sells.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.3] Hinders: corrupt elites can steal aid, and food aid can undercut local farmers, who cannot compete with free food.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.4] Hinders: aid can create dependency instead of self-reliance, and loans add to debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.5] Hinders: donors' political or strategic interests decide who gets aid, rather than need.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.6] Hinders: large prestige projects, such as dams, can displace people.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.7] Better aid is untied (not required to be spent in the donor country), targeted at the poorest, and designed with local participation and ownership. It supports self-help and co-operatives and is transparent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.b] Ireland gives aid through its government programme and through voluntary organisations, many of them founded in response to particular crises.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.0] Irish Aid is Ireland's official aid programme, part of the Department of Foreign Affairs. Its bilateral programme (government to government) began in 1974.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.1] Long-term partner countries include Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia in sub-Saharan Africa, and Vietnam.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.2] Ireland's aid is untied. 'A Better World' (2019), Ireland's international development policy, focuses on the furthest behind, gender equality, climate and governance.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.3] Concern Worldwide was founded in 1968 in response to the famine in Biafra during Nigeria's civil war.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.4] Trócaire was founded in 1973 by the Irish Catholic bishops. Its Lenten 'Trócaire box' raises funds, and it campaigns on human rights and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.5] GOAL was founded in 1977 by John O'Shea.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.6] Live Aid concerts in July 1985, organised by Bob Geldof, responded to the 1984-85 Ethiopian famine.
[geo-economic-development-s4.16.b] Ireland's past gives it a reason to care about hunger elsewhere.
[geo-economic-development-s4.16.0] The Great Famine (1845-49) killed about 1 million people and led about 1 million to emigrate. This history is one reason for Irish solidarity (support and fellow feeling) with developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.b] Multinationals (companies operating in many countries) move work to where labour is cheapest. This brings jobs, but it can also bring exploitation.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.0] Multinationals locate labour-intensive work (work needing many workers), such as clothing, electronics assembly and mining, in low-wage countries, because wage costs are lowest there.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.1] Benefits for workers include jobs, wages higher than farm work, and skills.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.2] Exploitation includes very low wages, long hours, unsafe factories, banned trade unions (organisations of workers) and child labour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.b] The collapse of Rana Plaza shows what can happen when global brands rely on cheap suppliers whose safety standards are not checked.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.0] Rana Plaza, an eight-storey building in Savar near Dhaka, housed garment factories. It collapsed on 24 April 2013, killing about 1,130 people and injuring about 2,500.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.1] Cracks had appeared the day before, but workers were ordered back in.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.2] The factories supplied Western brands, including Primark, which was founded in Dublin in 1969 as Penneys. Primark paid compensation to the victims.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.3] The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh was signed in May 2013 by global brands and trade unions, and eventually more than 200 brands joined. It provided independent factory inspections.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.4] Garments make up over four-fifths of Bangladesh's exports (2020s), so the country depends heavily on this industry.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.5] The case shows how brands profit from low-cost factories while the safety of workers depends on local suppliers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.b] Child labour is one of the worst forms of exploitation, and it appears in the supply chains of everyday products.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.0] About 160 million children were in child labour worldwide in 2020 (ILO and UNICEF), about 70% of them in agriculture.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.1] Child labour occurs in cocoa farming and in mining, for example, so everyday products such as chocolate and batteries can be linked to it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.2] The DR Congo produces about 70% of the world's cobalt, which is used in rechargeable batteries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.3] Amnesty International (2016) documented child labour in the artisanal (hand-dug) cobalt mines of the DR Congo.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.b] Exploitation also involves land. Poor countries and poor communities can lose the land they depend on.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.0] Foreign companies and governments buy or lease large areas of farmland in poor countries, a practice called land grabbing.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.1] Indigenous peoples (the original inhabitants of a region) lose land to mining, ranching and logging, as in the Amazon example in the Brazil section of Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.b] Pressure to improve conditions comes from consumers, from the law and from workers themselves.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.0] Ethical consumer campaigns and company codes of conduct (a company's own rules for how suppliers must treat workers) put pressure on brands.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.1] The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, adopted in 2024, requires very large companies to identify and address human-rights and environmental harm in their supply chains.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.2] Trade unions represent workers and campaign for safer conditions and better wages.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.b] Climate change is an injustice because the countries that caused it least suffer most from it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.0] The poorest countries have contributed least to greenhouse gas emissions (the gases that cause global warming) but suffer most from climate change.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.1] Bangladesh is a low-lying delta vulnerable to cyclones, floods and sea-level rise, because so much of its land is barely above the sea.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.2] Small island states such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives are threatened by sea-level rise, because their land is low.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.3] Sub-Saharan Africa has produced only a small share of historical emissions, yet its dry regions suffer drought.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.4] Responses include climate finance (money from rich countries to help poorer ones cope) and the Loss and Damage Fund, agreed at COP27 in Sharm el-Sheikh, Egypt (2022) and made operational at COP28 in Dubai (2023).
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.5] The Mary Robinson Foundation - Climate Justice was set up in 2010 by Mary Robinson, who was President of Ireland from 1990 to 1997 and UN High Commissioner for Human Rights from 1997 to 2002.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.b] A justice evaluation weighs real progress against the problems that remain.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.0] Extreme poverty has fallen greatly since 1990, especially in China. Child mortality has fallen, and more children attend school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.1] Sub-Saharan Africa lags behind other regions.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.2] Inequality within and between countries is high, so gains are not shared equally.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.3] Conflict, such as the war in Sudan, and climate change are reversing gains. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.4] The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 caused the first rise in global extreme poverty in about a generation (World Bank).
[geo-economic-development-s4.24.b] Justice is not only for governments. Individuals and Ireland can act.
[geo-economic-development-s4.24.0] Individuals can buy fair trade, campaign, and support NGOs. Ireland can press governments on aid, trade and debt policy.
