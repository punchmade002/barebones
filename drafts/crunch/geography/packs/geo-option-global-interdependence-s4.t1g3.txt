TARGET geo-option-global-interdependence-s4 (part 1 of 2): 4. Sustainable development as a model for the future
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.0.b] Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland, 1987). It is the model this whole section asks you to judge.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.0.0] It has three pillars, environmental, economic and social, which must be balanced against each other, because gains in one at the expense of the others do not count as sustainable.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.0.1] It contrasts with growth-only models, which measure development by rising output (the total goods and services produced), such as Rostow’s stages ending in mass consumption. There is more on these models in Views of development and underdevelopment.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.b] The idea grew over several decades, from a warning about limits in 1972 to global targets in 2015. Knowing the sequence lets you give a dated origin for the model.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.0] “The Limits to Growth” (1972), a report for the Club of Rome, used computer models to warn that unchecked population and economic growth would exhaust resources within about a century.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.1] The Brundtland Report, “Our Common Future”, was produced by the UN World Commission on Environment and Development in 1987. It gave the definition above.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.2] The Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro (1992) produced Agenda 21 (a global action plan) and the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (a treaty on tackling climate change).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.3] The Millennium Development Goals (eight goals, 2000–2015) were followed by the Sustainable Development Goals: 17 goals adopted in September 2015, with a target year of 2030.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.4] The Paris Agreement (2015) committed countries to tackle climate change.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.1.5] The world is off track to meet most of the Sustainable Development Goals, so the targets are not being reached on time.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.b] The case for making sustainability a priority rests on the fact that today’s growth can undermine tomorrow’s. Use these reasons when an essay asks whether it should be a priority.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.0] Resources are finite (there is a fixed amount) and are being overused, so they cannot support unlimited use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.1] Climate change, biodiversity loss (the decline of plant and animal species) and inequality between and within generations threaten future development.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.2] The poorest people suffer first when resources are degraded, because they depend most directly on land, water and fuel and have least to fall back on.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.2.3] Growth that destroys the resources it depends on cannot last, because the base of that growth is used up.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.b] The model is widely accepted but hard to apply. A good evaluation says where it falls short as well as where it works.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.0] It is vague and open to greenwashing, where firms and governments claim sustainability without real change, because nobody can say exactly what counts as sustainable.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.1] The pillars conflict: jobs can be at odds with protecting the environment, so balancing all three is difficult.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.2] Poorer countries argue that rich countries grew without limits and now ask them to slow down, which they see as unfair.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.3.3] The costs of change fall unequally, and progress on the Sustainable Development Goals is behind schedule.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.4.b] If development is not just rising output, it needs other yardsticks. Two alternatives measure wellbeing and environmental pressure instead.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.4.0] Bhutan’s Gross National Happiness, put forward in the 1970s by King Jigme Singye Wangchuck, measures wellbeing beyond income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.4.1] The ecological footprint measures the land and resources a population needs to support its consumption.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.b] Sustainable resource use means using resources so that they last. The principles below differ for resources that regrow and those that do not.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.0] Renewable resources (those that regenerate, such as fish and forests) should be used no faster than they regenerate. This rate is the sustainable yield.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.1] Non-renewable resources (those that cannot be replaced, such as fossil fuels) should be used efficiently and replaced by alternatives where possible.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.2] Reduce, reuse and recycle waste, so that fewer resources are taken and less waste is dumped.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.3] Protect biodiversity (the variety of living things).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.5.4] Involve local people in decisions about resources, since they live with the results.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.b] Fish are a renewable resource, but only if catches stay below what the stock can replace. Irish fishing policy has been shaped by what happened when this was ignored.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.0] Irish fishing has been shaped by past overfishing in EU waters, which is why rules now limit catches.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.1] The Common Fisheries Policy of 1983 was reformed in 2013, introducing maximum sustainable yield (the largest catch that can be taken without shrinking the stock) and a landing obligation (a discard ban), phased in during 2015–2019.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.2] The Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority was set up in 2007.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.3] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland’s largest fishing port.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.4] The North Sea herring fishery was closed from 1977 to the early 1980s and recovered under quotas (limits on catch), showing that management can work.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.6.5] The Grand Banks cod moratorium (a ban on fishing) of 2 July 1992 cost about 30,000 jobs off Newfoundland and is the warning for overfishing.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.b] Bord na Móna has moved from burning peat to renewable energy under its “Brown to Green” strategy. Peat is partly decayed plant matter dug from bogs, once burned for electricity.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.0] Bord na Móna was founded in 1946.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.1] The Lanesborough and Shannonbridge peat stations closed in December 2020, and peat harvesting ended in January 2021.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.2] Cutaway bogs (bogs where the peat has been removed) are being rehabilitated and rewetted, and peatland is valued as a store of carbon.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.3] Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly, is one of the rehabilitated sites.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.7.4] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo, has wind farms on cutaway bog.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.b] Moving electricity generation from fossil fuels to renewables is a central part of sustainable resource use. Ireland has set targets in law.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.0] Wind provides about a third of Irish electricity, and renewables about two-fifths (early 2020s).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.1] The Climate Action Plan targets 80% renewable electricity by 2030 and at least 5 GW (gigawatts, a measure of power capacity) of offshore wind by 2030.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.2] The Climate Act 2021 requires a 51% cut in emissions by 2030 (from 2018) and net zero (no more greenhouse gas added than removed) by 2050.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.8.3] The East–West Interconnector (2012), a cable that lets electricity flow between countries, links Ireland and Britain, and the Celtic Interconnector to France is due in the late 2020s.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.b] Farming shapes most Irish land, so how it is run matters for sustainability. Schemes reward good practice, but dairy expansion pulls the other way.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.0] BurrenLIFE (2005–2010) and then the Burren Programme pay farmers according to habitat scores for each field, so they are paid for results.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.1] Agri-environment schemes (payments to farmers for protecting the environment) have included REPS (1994), GLAS (2015) and ACRES (2023).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.2] Origin Green, Bord Bia’s sustainability programme, began in 2012.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.3] Milk quotas (limits on how much milk each farm could produce) were abolished on 1 April 2015, and the dairy herd grew strongly afterwards.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.4] Agriculture produces over a third of Irish greenhouse gas emissions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.5] Only about half of Irish waters are in good or high ecological status (EPA, 2016–2021).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.9.6] Dairy expansion is therefore in tension with water quality and emissions targets.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.10.b] Sustainability also depends on changing everyday behaviour. Two Irish measures show how charges and deposits can cut waste.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.10.0] The plastic bag levy (a charge on each bag) began in March 2002 at 15 cent, and plastic bag use fell by over 90%.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.10.1] The Deposit Return Scheme (Re-turn) began on 1 February 2024.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.b] Only about 11% of Ireland is forest, and much of that is not native. Forestry is a final Irish example of resource use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.0] Forest cover in Ireland is about 11%, mostly conifer plantations of Sitka spruce.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.1] Coillte was set up in 1988 to manage state forests.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.2] Remnants of native woodland, such as the Killarney oakwoods, show what Ireland has lost.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.3] Ireland needs more native broadleaf woodland, because plantations of one conifer support less variety of life than native woods.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.11.4] Managed Scandinavian forests are another example of forest use. There is more on this in An interdependent global economy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.b] Costa Rica, in Central America between Nicaragua and Panama, shows how a country can reverse resource loss.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.0] In the 1970s and 1980s forests were cleared for cattle pasture, and by the late 1980s forest covered only about a fifth of the country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.1] The Payment for Environmental Services programme, started in 1997 and run by the national forestry fund FONAFIFO, pays landowners to protect and replant forest, so keeping trees earns income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.2] The programme is paid for largely by a tax on fuel.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.3] Forest cover has since risen to over half of the country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.4] About a quarter of the land is in national parks and other protected areas.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.12.5] Ecotourism (tourism based on nature) is one of the largest sources of foreign income, giving forests an economic value.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.13.b] Costa Rica’s sustainability also shows in how it generates power and spends public money.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.13.0] Since 2015 almost all of Costa Rica’s electricity has come from renewable sources (over 98% in most years, but about 95% in 2023, when drought cut hydropower), mainly hydroelectric (from flowing water), with geothermal (from underground heat) and wind.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.13.1] Costa Rica abolished its army in 1948 and spends instead on education and health.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.13.2] In 2021 Costa Rica won the first Earthshot Prize (Protect and Restore Nature) for its forest recovery.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.14.b] Costa Rica is not a perfect model. Two weaknesses are worth including in an evaluation.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.14.0] Transport still runs mainly on imported oil, so it has not left fossil fuels behind.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.14.1] Tourism puts pressure on protected areas.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.15.b] Where resources are used unsustainably, the damage can be severe and lasting. Two cases serve as warnings.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.15.0] The Aral Sea: rivers were diverted from the 1960s to irrigate Soviet cotton, and the sea shrank to a fraction of its size; the Kok-Aral dam was built in 2005.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.15.1] Amazon deforestation is another warning of unsustainable use. There is more on this in An interdependent global economy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.b] Fair trade exists because farmers in poorer countries often get a poor deal from world markets. The six points below are the problems it tries to solve.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.0] Commodity prices (prices of raw products such as coffee and cocoa) are low and unstable, and small farmers receive a tiny share of the final price.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.1] Middlemen (traders between the farmer and the buyer) take a large share of the value of farmers’ crops.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.2] Poor working conditions and child labour are common in producing countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.3] Terms of trade decline as the prices of raw materials fall relative to manufactured goods, so the same crop buys less each year.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.4] Tariff escalation (tariffs, or import taxes, that rise as a product is processed): coffee beans enter rich markets with low tariffs, but roasted, processed coffee faces higher ones.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.16.5] Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana grow over half of the world’s cocoa, mostly exported as beans.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.b] Fair trade is a response to those problems, with a short but dated history.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.0] Fair trade is a trading partnership based on dialogue, transparency and respect that seeks greater equity (fairness) in international trade.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.1] The first fair trade label, Max Havelaar, was launched in the Netherlands in 1988 for Mexican coffee.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.2] Fairtrade International coordinates the Fairtrade Mark, the certification label on products.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.17.3] Garstang, Lancashire, England, declared itself the world’s first Fairtrade town in 2000.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.b] Fairtrade protects producers through prices, contracts, co-operatives and standards. Each feature solves part of the problem set out above.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.0] The Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.1] The minimum is a floor, not a guaranteed higher price: when the market price is above the minimum, producers receive the market price plus the premium.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.2] The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative, spent on projects the members choose democratically.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.3] Long-term contracts and pre-finance give producers credit before the harvest, so they are not forced into debt to middlemen.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.4] Producers are organised in democratic co-operatives (groups owned and run by their members).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.5] Labour standards ban forced and child labour, protect the freedom to join unions and require safe conditions.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.6] Environmental standards restrict pesticides, protect water and forests and ban GM (genetically modified) crops.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.18.7] The Fairtrade Mark is a guarantee to consumers that these standards have been met.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.19.b] Fairtrade covers many crops and reaches a large number of people, but it remains small in world terms.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.19.0] Fairtrade products include crops such as coffee, cocoa, tea, bananas, sugar, cotton and flowers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.19.1] Close to 2 million farmers and workers belong to Fairtrade-certified producer organisations in about 70 countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.b] Kuapa Kokoo is a Fairtrade cocoa farmers’ co-operative in Ghana, the world’s second-largest cocoa producer. It stands out because the farmers also part-own a chocolate brand.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.0] Kuapa Kokoo, meaning “good cocoa farmer” in Twi, was founded in 1993.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.1] Divine Chocolate was set up in Britain in 1998, part-owned by Kuapa Kokoo. Since 2020 the German chocolate maker Ludwig Weinrich has held the majority; Kuapa Kokoo kept about 20% of the shares and seats on the board.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.2] Farmers receive a share of Divine’s profits as well as the Fairtrade price and premium.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s4.20.3] Premium spending has included wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s4: 4. Global issues and a justice perspective
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.b] Global issues are problems that cross borders and affect the whole world, such as poverty, inequality, trade rules, debt, climate change and migration. A justice perspective asks whether the distribution of wealth and power is fair, who decides, and who benefits. It treats development as a matter of rights, not charity.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.0] The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948, includes rights to food, health, education and decent work.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.1] Poverty denies these rights, so a justice perspective treats poverty as a rights failure rather than a matter of charity. In other words, people are owed these things, not given them as a favour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.b] Justice questions start from the scale of the problem. The figures below show how many people are extremely poor, where they live, and how unevenly wealth is shared.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.0] About 700 million people lived in extreme poverty in 2022, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa (the part of Africa south of the Sahara). The World Bank, the UN-linked development lender, measures extreme poverty as living on under 2.15 US dollars a day (2017 prices).
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.1] The share of the world's population in extreme poverty fell from over a third in 1990 to under a tenth by 2019, so the trend is strongly downward even though the numbers remain large.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.2] The richest 10% own about 76% of global wealth, while the poorest half own about 2% (World Inequality Report 2022). This shows how unequal the sharing of wealth is.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.b] The UN has set global targets to measure progress against poverty and injustice. The first set ran from 2000 to 2015 and the second runs to 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.0] The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000-2015) had eight goals. The target of halving the share of people in extreme poverty from 1990 was met by 2010, five years early, largely thanks to China.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.1] The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were adopted by the UN in September 2015. They have 17 goals, to be met by 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.2] The SDGs target poverty (SDG 1), hunger (SDG 2), quality education (SDG 4), gender equality (SDG 5), reduced inequalities (SDG 10) and climate action (SDG 13). Other targets cover health, clean water and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.3] The world is off track to meet most SDG targets by 2030, so shortfalls remain alongside the progress.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.b] Trade rules and prices can leave poor countries poorer. Many depend on selling raw materials, and the biggest profits are made further along the chain, in rich countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.0] Many developing countries depend on exporting a few primary commodities (raw materials such as coffee, cocoa, cotton and minerals), whose prices are low and unstable. This makes their income hard to plan around.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.1] The terms of trade (the amount of imports a country's exports can buy) tend to move against these countries. Their exports buy fewer imports over time, while they import manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.2] Most of the final price of coffee, chocolate or clothing goes to processors, brands and retailers in rich countries. The grower, at the start of the value chain (the steps from raw material to finished product), receives a small share.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.3] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials. This discourages poor countries from processing their own crops, so they stay stuck exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.b] Rich countries also help their own farmers with money, and this can push down the prices poor farmers receive. Cotton is the clearest example.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.0] Rich-country farm subsidies (government payments to farmers), such as US cotton subsidies and the EU's Common Agricultural Policy in the past, let surpluses be sold cheaply abroad. This is called dumping, and it undercuts poor farmers who cannot match the low price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.1] The Cotton-4 West African producers, Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad and Mali, were harmed by these subsidies.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.2] The WTO (World Trade Organization, the body that rules on trade disputes) ruled in Brazil's favour against US cotton subsidies in 2004-05.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.b] Governments and trade bodies have tried to make trade fairer for poor countries, with mixed success. Fair trade is a third response, because it works through consumers rather than governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.0] The WTO Doha Development Round (a set of global trade talks meant to help developing countries) was launched in 2001 but has stalled without agreement.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.1] The EU's 'Everything But Arms' scheme (2001) gives the least developed countries duty-free (no tariffs) and quota-free (no limit on amounts) access to the EU for all products except arms and ammunition.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.b] Fair trade is a trading model in which producers receive guaranteed terms and the Fairtrade Mark identifies certified products. It tries to give growers a bigger and steadier share than the normal market does.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.0] The Fairtrade Minimum Price covers the cost of sustainable production. It is a guaranteed floor, not a guaranteed higher price: when the market price is higher, producers receive the market price plus the premium.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.1] The Fairtrade Premium is paid to producer co-operatives (groups of farmers who work together and share the benefits) for community projects such as schools, clinics and clean water.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.2] Fair trade also requires long-term contracts, decent working conditions, no child labour, environmental standards and democratically run co-operatives.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.3] Fair trade covers coffee, tea, cocoa, bananas, sugar and cotton, for example, which are the kinds of crops whose growers get a small share of the price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.4] The first fair trade label, Max Havelaar, was launched in the Netherlands in 1988 for Mexican coffee. Fairtrade International now coordinates the Fairtrade Mark.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.b] Ireland has its own fair trade links, but fair trade has weaknesses that an evaluation should include.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.0] Fairtrade Ireland (Fairtrade Mark Ireland) licenses the Fairtrade Mark in Ireland, meaning it gives permission for products to carry it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.1] Irish towns and schools can apply for Fairtrade status. Fairtrade Fortnight takes place each year in late February and March.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.2] Limit: fair trade makes up only a tiny share of world trade, so it reaches few producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.3] Limit: certified products can cost consumers more, and certification is expensive for small producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.4] Limit: fair trade does not change the trade rules that cause unfair trade in the first place, so it treats the symptoms rather than the cause.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.b] In the 1970s and 1980s, cheap lending turned into a debt crisis for many developing countries. Each step below made repayment harder.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.0] 1. When oil prices rose sharply, oil-exporting countries deposited their surplus money in banks. Banks lent these deposits, called petrodollars, to developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.1] 2. Interest rates then rose sharply and commodity prices fell. Borrowers earned less from their exports while owing more, so many could not afford repayments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.2] 3. Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts, starting the Latin American debt crisis.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.3] 4. Many African and Latin American countries could not repay their debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.b] Heavy debt took money away from the people it was meant to help, and in some cases the people repaying had never benefited from the loan.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.0] Governments spent more on debt repayments than on health and education, so public services suffered.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.1] Countries took new loans to repay old ones, so the debt grew.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.2] The 1980s 'lost decade' hit Latin America and Africa hard, because debt blocked progress in those years.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.3] Some debts were 'odious': dictators borrowed the money and stole or wasted it, yet the people were made to repay it. This is the clearest justice argument against debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.b] Lenders did not simply hand over money. The IMF (International Monetary Fund) and the World Bank attached conditions to their loans, which is different from tied aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.0] IMF and World Bank loans came with conditions. These cut public spending, introduced school and health fees, privatised state firms (sold them to private owners), removed food subsidies, devalued the currency (lowered its value against others) and opened markets to imports.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.1] Structural adjustment programmes, as these packages of conditions were called, were criticised for hurting the poorest people most. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.b] Campaigns and lenders eventually cancelled some debt for the poorest countries, with conditions. Relief had real results, but debt is building up again.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.0] The Jubilee 2000 campaign pressed for debt cancellation by the millennium, so that poor countries could stop repaying old debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.1] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative was launched in 1996 by the IMF and World Bank.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.2] The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005) followed the G8 summit (a meeting of leaders of major rich countries) at Gleneagles, Scotland, in July 2005. It was backed by the Make Poverty History campaign and the Live 8 concerts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.3] More than 30 countries, most in Africa, have received debt relief under HIPC, on condition that the savings go to poverty reduction.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.4] Relief has funded results such as free primary education in some relieved countries, so more children are in school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.5] Debt is rising again in the 2020s, so relief has not ended the problem.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.b] Aid is help given to poorer countries. It comes in different forms, depending on its purpose, who gives it and how it is channelled.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.0] Emergency (humanitarian) aid responds to disasters and famines, so it is short-term and urgent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.1] Long-term development aid funds projects that build lasting capacity, such as a country's ability to meet its own needs.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.2] Bilateral aid passes from one government to another, so the donor government decides directly where it goes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.3] Multilateral aid is channelled through bodies such as the UN, the World Bank and the EU, so several donors pool their money.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.4] NGO (voluntary) aid is given by non-governmental organisations, which are charities independent of governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.5] Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, so it benefits the donor as well as the receiver.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.b] The UN has set a target for how much rich countries should give, and most do not meet it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.0] The UN target, set in 1970, is that rich countries give 0.7% of their gross national income (the total income earned by a country's people and firms) as official development aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.1] Norway, Sweden, Luxembourg and Denmark have reached or exceeded the target in recent years, so only a few countries reach it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.2] Ireland has not yet reached the target (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.b] Aid is neither all good nor all bad. A balanced answer gives both sides, with reasons, and ends with what better aid looks like.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.0] Helps: aid saves lives in emergencies and funds schools, clinics, wells and vaccination programmes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.1] Helps: aid builds roads and infrastructure, trains teachers, nurses and farmers, and supports women's groups and small businesses.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.2] Hinders: tied aid benefits donor firms and may supply unsuitable technology, because the receiver must buy what the donor sells.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.3] Hinders: corrupt elites can steal aid, and food aid can undercut local farmers, who cannot compete with free food.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.4] Hinders: aid can create dependency instead of self-reliance, and loans add to debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.5] Hinders: donors' political or strategic interests decide who gets aid, rather than need.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.6] Hinders: large prestige projects, such as dams, can displace people.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.7] Better aid is untied (not required to be spent in the donor country), targeted at the poorest, and designed with local participation and ownership. It supports self-help and co-operatives and is transparent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.b] Ireland gives aid through its government programme and through voluntary organisations, many of them founded in response to particular crises.

[geo-economic-development-s4.16.b] Ireland's past gives it a reason to care about hunger elsewhere.

[geo-economic-development-s4.17.b] Multinationals (companies operating in many countries) move work to where labour is cheapest. This brings jobs, but it can also bring exploitation.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.0] Multinationals locate labour-intensive work (work needing many workers), such as clothing, electronics assembly and mining, in low-wage countries, because wage costs are lowest there.

[geo-economic-development-s4.20.b] Exploitation also involves land. Poor countries and poor communities can lose the land they depend on.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.0] Foreign companies and governments buy or lease large areas of farmland in poor countries, a practice called land grabbing.

[geo-economic-development-s4.21.b] Pressure to improve conditions comes from consumers, from the law and from workers themselves.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.0] Ethical consumer campaigns and company codes of conduct (a company's own rules for how suppliers must treat workers) put pressure on brands.

[geo-economic-development-s4.21.2] Trade unions represent workers and campaign for safer conditions and better wages.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.b] Climate change is an injustice because the countries that caused it least suffer most from it.

CANDIDATES geo-region-continental-s2: 2. Brazil
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.b] The region studied is the Federative Republic of Brazil, a country so large that it fills about half of a continent. Its position across the tropics shapes almost everything else about it.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.0] Brazil is in eastern South America and covers about 8.5 million km², the world's fifth-largest country by area and about half of South America.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.1] The equator crosses the far north, near the mouth of the Amazon, and the Tropic of Capricorn (the line of latitude south of the equator marking the edge of the tropics) passes close to São Paulo, so most of Brazil is tropical.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.2] It has an Atlantic coastline of about 7,400 km, which is why early settlement and today's biggest cities cluster near the sea.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.3] Brazil borders every South American country except Chile and Ecuador, but those neighbours are only neighbours, not part of the region studied.
[geo-region-continental-s2.0.4] The country has 26 states and the Federal District, and its capital is Brasília.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.b] A region is an area with something that unites it. Brazil is big enough to be treated like a continent in its own right, and it holds together as one unit.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.0] Its continental size: it is larger than most continents' individual countries and contains very different climates, landscapes and economies.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.1] It is a single federal state (a country made of states that share a central government) with one language, Portuguese, surrounded by Spanish-speaking countries, which sets it apart culturally.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.2] It has its own culture and economy, shaped by Portuguese colonisation, African and Indigenous peoples and later immigrants.
[geo-region-continental-s2.1.3] It has clear internal regions, the five macro-regions, which let you study how physical, economic and human processes interact inside one country.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.b] Brazil is divided officially into five macro-regions (large groupings of states). You will use these names in almost every answer, so learn what each is known for.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.0] North: the Amazon, the largest region by area and the least populated.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.1] Northeast: the sugar coast and the semi-arid interior called the sertão; it is the poorest region.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.2] Centre-West: the Cerrado (tropical savanna), the soya frontier and the capital, Brasília.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.3] Southeast: São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais; this is the economic core of the country.
[geo-region-continental-s2.2.4] South: a cooler region of European immigrant settlement with prosperous farming and industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.b] A sketch map earns marks for an accurate outline and for showing and naming each feature asked for. Learn one example of each feature and exactly where it goes. Names may go on the map or in a key.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.0] Outline: a broad northern bulge across the Amazon, an eastward shoulder at the Northeast, then the coast running south-west and the country tapering towards the south.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.1] Relief: outline the Brazilian Highlands (the east-centre, inland from the coast, from the Northeast down to the south) or the Amazon Basin lowland (the north-west and north).
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.2] River: draw the Amazon entering in the far west, flowing east past Manaus, where the Rio Negro joins from the north-west, and ending at a wide estuary close to the equator. Mark Marajó Island at the mouth.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.3] Urban centres as dots: São Paulo just inland behind the south-east coast near the Tropic of Capricorn, and Rio de Janeiro on the coast east-north-east of São Paulo.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.4] Agricultural region as an outlined area: the soya region of Mato Grosso in the centre-west, south of the Amazon lowland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.3.5] Industrial region as an outlined area: the triangle joining São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and Belo Horizonte. Other placing cues: Manaus in the centre-north, Brasília in the centre and Salvador on the Northeast coast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.b] Six controls explain why Brazil has so many climates inside one country. Each one pushes temperature or rainfall up or down in a particular area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.0] Latitude: most of Brazil lies between the equator and the Tropic of Capricorn, so it gets strong sun and is mostly hot.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.1] The ITCZ (Intertropical Convergence Zone, the belt where winds from north and south meet and air rises to give heavy rain) moves north and south with the overhead sun, so it brings rain to different areas in different seasons.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.2] The south-east trade winds blow in off the warm Atlantic, carrying moist air onto the east coast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.3] Altitude: the plateau is cooler than the coast because air cools with height.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.4] Relief: the coastal escarpment (a steep slope) forces moist air to rise, cool and drop rain on the seaward side.
[geo-region-continental-s2.4.5] Cold fronts from the south reach the South and parts of the Southeast, bringing sudden falls in temperature and sometimes frost.
[geo-region-continental-s2.5.b] The North has an equatorial climate (found on or near the equator): hot, wet and with almost no seasons. This is the climate that supports the rainforest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.5.0] It is hot all year, with Manaus monthly means of about 26-28 degrees C, so the annual temperature range is small.
[geo-region-continental-s2.5.1] Manaus gets about 2,300 mm of rain a year, with heavy rain in every month, because strong heating makes warm moist air rise and form storm clouds (convectional rain).
[geo-region-continental-s2.5.2] The climate sustains the Amazon rainforest biome, which is dealt with in the Geoecology option.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.b] The Cerrado has a tropical wet-and-dry (savanna) climate, with a clear rainy summer and a long dry winter. The change comes from the movement of the ITCZ and the overhead sun.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.0] The rainy season runs from about October to April, when the ITCZ and the overhead sun move south, so warm moist air rises over the Centre-West.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.1] The dry season runs from about May to September, when the ITCZ has moved north, so the winter is long and dry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.2] Brasília is the example: it follows this wet and dry pattern, and its altitude of about 1,100 m keeps it milder than the lowlands.
[geo-region-continental-s2.6.3] This clear wet season matters for farming, because crops such as soya are planted with the first rains.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.b] The interior of the Northeast is the driest part of Brazil even though it lies in the tropics. The sertão (the semi-arid Northeast interior) has low and unreliable rain, which is behind many of the region's problems.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.0] The sertão gets roughly 250-800 mm a year, falling in a short rainy season and varying greatly from year to year.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.1] It lies in the rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of high ground) of the coastal plateau, so moist air from the Atlantic loses its rain before it arrives.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.2] Sinking air limits convection, and the ITCZ reaches the area only briefly, so there are few chances for heavy rain.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.3] El Niño years (when the Pacific Ocean is unusually warm) bring drought, so the rains can fail altogether.
[geo-region-continental-s2.7.4] The officially defined drought-prone area is the drought polygon (Polígono das Secas). A severe drought hit the Northeast from 2012 to 2017.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.b] The east coast is wet while the interior behind it is dry. Winds off the ocean and the shape of the land explain the difference.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.0] The south-east trade winds bring moisture off the warm Atlantic, so the air arriving at the coast is already full of water vapour.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.1] Where the air meets the coastal escarpment, such as the Serra do Mar behind the coast of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro states, it is forced to rise and cool, giving relief rain.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.2] This creates a wet coastal strip that once carried the Atlantic Forest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.8.3] The wet coast contrasts with the semi-arid sertão further inland, which lies beyond the rain-giving slopes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.9.b] In the Southeast, height above sea level matters as much as latitude. The plateau is noticeably cooler than the coast at the same latitude.
[geo-region-continental-s2.9.0] Temperatures fall with altitude, so the plateau moderates the heat of the tropics.
[geo-region-continental-s2.9.1] São Paulo, on the plateau, is cooler than Rio de Janeiro on the coast, which is why the highlands are comfortable for people and suit crops such as coffee.
[geo-region-continental-s2.9.2] This cooler climate is one reason the plateau became the centre of settlement, farming and industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.b] The far south lies furthest from the equator, and it is the only part of Brazil with a real winter. This affects what can be grown.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.0] It has four seasons and rain all year, because cold fronts from the south bring rain in every season.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.1] Winter frosts occur, and there is occasional snow in the highlands of Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.2] Porto Alegre is the main city of the far south.
[geo-region-continental-s2.10.3] The frost risk shaped where coffee is grown, because frost kills the trees. This is why coffee moved north after the frost of 1975, which the coffee notes deal with in full.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.b] Brazil's climate is not safe or steady. Droughts and floods both cause deaths and damage, and some hazards are linked to El Niño and to forest loss.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.0] Droughts in the Northeast are repeated and severe, as in 2012 to 2017.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.1] In 2023 a severe Amazon drought brought the Rio Negro at Manaus to its lowest level on record, in October 2023, stranding river communities.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.2] In May 2024 floods in Rio Grande do Sul killed more than 170 people and displaced hundreds of thousands, and Porto Alegre was flooded.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.3] In January 2011 floods and landslides in the mountain region of Rio de Janeiro state killed more than 900 people, Brazil's worst natural disaster. Landslides also hit Petrópolis in February 2022.
[geo-region-continental-s2.11.4] El Niño links droughts in the north and east to Pacific warming, and deforestation reduces rainfall recycling (the forest returning water to the air), which may make drought worse.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.b] Climate decides what can be grown, where people live and how energy is produced. Four effects are worth learning.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.0] Warm temperatures in the tropics allow two or three crops a year, which raises farm output.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.1] Drought drives migration out of the sertão, because people cannot farm when the rains fail.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.2] Heavy rainfall over much of the country feeds the rivers that power hydroelectricity.
[geo-region-continental-s2.12.3] Beaches, sun and warm weather attract tourists, so climate supports the tourist industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.b] Many people expect tropical soils to be rich because of the lush forest. In fact most Amazon and Cerrado soils are poor, and the reason is climate.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.0] Latosols (also called ferralsols or oxisols) are deep, red-yellow soils that cover much of the Amazon and Cerrado.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.1] Heat and heavy rain leach them (wash nutrients out of them), so they are acidic and low in nutrients.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.2] The fertility is held in the living vegetation and its litter, not in the soil, so cleared land loses its fertility fast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.13.3] The process that produces these soils, laterisation, is explained in the Geoecology option.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.b] The Cerrado soils were once thought useless for crops. Science changed that, and now they are a major farming area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.0] Cerrado soils are acidic and high in aluminium, which damages plant roots.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.1] Liming (adding crushed limestone) cuts the acidity, and fertiliser adds nutrients.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.2] EMBRAPA (Brazilian Agricultural Research Corporation, founded 1973) developed these methods.
[geo-region-continental-s2.14.3] The result was that infertile savanna became productive farmland, a change that underlies the soya belt covered in the agriculture notes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.b] Two soils explain much of Brazil's farming history. Both are deep and fertile, and each was the base of a famous crop.
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.0] Terra roxa means purple earth in Portuguese, from its dark red colour. It is deep and fertile.
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.1] It weathers from the basalt of the Paraná Plateau in Paraná and São Paulo states, and it was the soil of the coffee boom.
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.2] Massapê is a dark, clay-rich, fertile soil found on the humid Northeast coast, in the Zona da Mata (the wet coastal strip around Recife and Salvador).
[geo-region-continental-s2.15.3] Massapê was the base of the colonial sugar plantations, which is why sugar grew there first.
[geo-region-continental-s2.16.b] Two soils break the rule that Amazon soils are poor. One is natural and one is made by people.
[geo-region-continental-s2.16.0] Várzea (floodplain) soils are renewed every year by silt left by floods, so they are among the few fertile Amazon soils.
[geo-region-continental-s2.16.1] Terra preta (black earth) is dark, fertile soil made by pre-Columbian Amazon peoples (those living there before European contact) by adding charcoal, food waste and pottery.
[geo-region-continental-s2.16.2] Terra preta shows that people can improve tropical soils, and not only damage them.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.b] Soils can be lost or damaged as well as improved. Three main problems affect Brazilian farmland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.0] Sertão soils are thin and stony, and easily eroded because there is little vegetation to hold them.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.1] Erosion follows forest clearance, because rain then washes away the exposed topsoil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.2] Old pasture suffers nutrient loss and compaction (being pressed hard by cattle) so grass grows poorly.
[geo-region-continental-s2.17.3] Salinisation (a build-up of salt) occurs in irrigated areas where the water evaporates and leaves the salt behind.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.b] Brazil has six biomes (large natural vegetation regions). They show that Brazil is not all rainforest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.0] Amazon rainforest covers about 49% of Brazil's area, the largest biome.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.1] Cerrado (tropical savanna) covers about 24%, and is often called the world's most biodiverse savanna.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.2] Atlantic Forest covers about 13%, but only about an eighth of the original forest remains because it grew where most Brazilians live.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.3] Caatinga (dry thorn scrub of the sertão) covers about 10% and is found only in Brazil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.18.4] Pampa (grassland) covers about 2% and so does the Pantanal (wetland).
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.b] Each biome matches a climate and soil. Link the vegetation to what causes it.
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.0] The Cerrado has a wet and dry climate and acidic soils, so it supports tropical grassland with scattered trees.
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.1] The Caatinga's dry thorn scrub matches the semi-arid climate of the sertão, because only drought-tolerant plants survive there.
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.2] The Atlantic Forest grew on the wet east coast fed by the trade winds, and its heavy loss came from settlement and farming along the coast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.19.3] The Pantanal is a seasonally flooded wetland, and the Pampa is a grassland in the south where gaúchos herd cattle in Rio Grande do Sul.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.b] Relief is the shape of the land. Brazil is mostly old, low or gently rising ground, with five main divisions to learn.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.0] The Brazilian Highlands (Planalto) cover the centre, east and south.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.1] The Amazon lowland (basin) lies across the north.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.2] The Guiana Highlands lie on the northern border.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.3] The Pantanal lowland is in the Centre-West, and a narrow coastal plain runs along the Atlantic.
[geo-region-continental-s2.20.4] These divisions are the starting point for the sketch map relief feature.
[geo-region-continental-s2.21.b] Brazil sits in the middle of a tectonic plate, which explains why its landscapes are old and quiet.
[geo-region-continental-s2.21.0] Brazil lies in the centre of the South American plate, far from plate boundaries, so it has no active volcanoes and few strong earthquakes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.21.1] The highlands are part of the ancient Brazilian Shield (an old crystalline block of hard rock), once part of Gondwanaland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.21.2] Mesosaurus fossils are found in Brazil and southern Africa, evidence that the two were joined.
[geo-region-continental-s2.22.b] The Highlands are the main block of high ground. They are old, worn-down and tilted, and this shapes where rivers go.
[geo-region-continental-s2.22.0] They are old, worn-down plateaus and tablelands, mostly 300-900 m high, shaped over a very long time by weathering and erosion.
[geo-region-continental-s2.22.1] They tilt from a high eastern edge towards the interior, so many rivers rise near the coast but flow inland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.22.2] This tilt is one reason the Paraná and São Francisco flow away from the nearest sea.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.b] Behind the coast of the Southeast the land rises steeply. This steep edge has had a big effect on settlement and transport.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.0] The Serra do Mar and Serra da Mantiqueira rise steeply behind the coast, so the coastal plain is very narrow.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.1] São Paulo sits on the plateau about 800 m above sea level and about 70 km from the port of Santos.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.2] The slope is a barrier to transport between ports and the plateau, so roads and railways were costly to build.
[geo-region-continental-s2.23.3] It also forces moist air to rise, giving the relief rain described under the east coast climate.
[geo-region-continental-s2.24.b] In the south the Highlands are covered by lava. This basalt created the best coffee soil and one of the world's great waterfalls.
[geo-region-continental-s2.24.0] Thick layers of basalt (a dark volcanic rock) formed from ancient fissure eruptions, flood basalts about 130 million years old.
[geo-region-continental-s2.24.1] The basalt weathers into terra roxa, the soil of the coffee boom.
[geo-region-continental-s2.24.2] The Iguaçu Falls drop over its basalt edge on the Iguaçu River on the Brazil-Argentina border, about 275 separate falls and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.b] The north has a very different relief from the Highlands. One block is the lowest, flattest part of Brazil, and the other holds its highest peak.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.0] The Amazon lowland is a vast, low, flat basin of sedimentary rocks and alluvium (river-deposited sediment), between the Guiana and Brazilian Highlands.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.1] It widens to the west.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.2] The Guiana Highlands lie on the northern border with Venezuela.
[geo-region-continental-s2.25.3] Pico da Neblina, about 2,995 m, in Amazonas state, is Brazil's highest peak.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.b] The Pantanal is a lowland that floods and drains with the seasons. It is a special type of landscape in Brazil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.0] It is a huge, flat, seasonally flooded depression in the Centre-West, mainly in Mato Grosso and Mato Grosso do Sul.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.1] It is flooded for several months each wet season, because rivers spill over the flat land.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.2] It is the world's largest tropical wetland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.26.3] It extends into Bolivia and Paraguay, so it is a shared feature.
[geo-region-continental-s2.27.b] The coast has two very different kinds of scenery, hard rock in the Southeast and sand in the Northeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.27.0] At Rio de Janeiro, granite domes and steep hills rise by the sea. Sugarloaf Mountain (Pão de Açúcar) is a granite-gneiss dome about 396 m high.
[geo-region-continental-s2.27.1] In the Northeast, Lençóis Maranhenses in Maranhão has white sand dunes with rain-filled lagoons.
[geo-region-continental-s2.27.2] Both are tourist attractions, which links relief to the tertiary sector.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.b] Relief decides where people farm, build and travel. Four patterns stand out.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.0] The plateau carries most farming, industry and cities, because the land is gently sloping and the climate is cooler.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.1] The escarpment limited early inland settlement and needs costly roads and railways.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.2] The lowland Amazon is sparsely settled, because of dense forest, flooding and difficult access.
[geo-region-continental-s2.28.3] Steep coastal hills host favelas (informal housing areas) that are prone to landslides.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.b] A river basin is the area drained by a river and its tributaries. Brazil has several enormous ones, and the pattern is shaped by the tilt of the Highlands.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.0] The Amazon basin drains the north.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.1] The Paraná-Paraguay basin drains the south and centre.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.2] The São Francisco basin drains the east.
[geo-region-continental-s2.29.3] The Tocantins-Araguaia basin is a fourth, flowing north through the Centre-West.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.b] The Amazon is the world's largest river by discharge and basin area. Do not call it the longest, because that is disputed with the Nile.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.0] It rises in the Andes of Peru, is about 6,400 km long and has a basin of about 7 million km², the world's largest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.1] It carries about a fifth of all the fresh water that rivers deliver to the oceans, and it has over a thousand tributaries.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.2] In Brazil it is called the Solimões until it meets the Rio Negro at Manaus, the Meeting of the Waters, where dark and pale water flow side by side for several kilometres before mixing.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.3] It flows east to the Atlantic near the equator, ending in a wide estuary around Marajó Island rather than a large delta.
[geo-region-continental-s2.30.4] Ocean-going ships can reach Manaus, about 1,500 km inland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.b] The Amazon is not a simple channel. It floods, it has two kinds of water and it is shaped by the tide at its mouth.
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.0] The annual flood rises many metres and spreads into the várzea (forest and land flooded by silt-laden white-water rivers).
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.1] It also spreads into the igapó (flooded black-water forest).
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.2] Black-water rivers such as the Rio Negro are acidic and low in silt, while white-water rivers such as the Solimões are silt-laden from the Andes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.31.3] The pororoca (a tidal bore, a wave that travels upstream) occurs at the mouth.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.b] The Paraná is the main river of the south and centre. Its flow is controlled by dams that supply the Southeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.0] It rises on the Brazilian Highlands and flows south-west into Argentina and the Río de la Plata.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.1] It is heavily dammed for hydroelectricity.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.2] Itaipu Dam, on the Brazil-Paraguay border and shared by the two countries, began generating in 1984 and is one of the world's largest hydroelectric plants by output.
[geo-region-continental-s2.32.3] The Iguaçu Falls are on a tributary.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.b] The São Francisco brings water to the dry Northeast, which is why it is called the river of national integration.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.0] It rises in the Serra da Canastra, Minas Gerais, is about 2,800 km long, flows north through the semi-arid Northeast and turns east to reach the Atlantic between Alagoas and Sergipe.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.1] It is a lifeline for the sertão, because it is one of few permanent water sources in a dry area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.2] Dams give power, and the Sobradinho Dam forms one of the world's largest artificial lakes by area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.3] Petrolina (Pernambuco) and Juazeiro (Bahia) face each other across the river and export irrigated grapes and mangoes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.33.4] The transposition project, begun in 2007, carries water by canals to the drier states of Ceará, Paraíba, Pernambuco and Rio Grande do Norte.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.b] Dams supply cheap power, but they also remove land and people from their homes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.0] Itaipu on the Paraná supplies power to the industrial Southeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.1] Tucuruí Dam on the Tocantins, Pará (1984), powers aluminium smelting.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.2] Belo Monte Dam on the Xingu, Pará, was completed in 2019.
[geo-region-continental-s2.34.3] Belo Monte was opposed by the Kayapó, displaced thousands of people and flooded forest, so the controversy is about the cost to people and nature.
[geo-region-continental-s2.35.b] Rivers are under pressure from mining and cities. Three examples show the problem.
[geo-region-continental-s2.35.0] Mercury from illegal gold mining pollutes rivers in Yanomami territory in Roraima, which harms the health of people who eat the fish.
[geo-region-continental-s2.35.1] The Rio Doce was polluted by mine waste after the Mariana disaster, which the mining notes explain.
[geo-region-continental-s2.35.2] The Tietê at São Paulo is heavily polluted by sewage from the city.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.b] Rivers provide transport, food and water, but both floods and droughts can disrupt them.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.0] The Amazon is the main highway of the North, because there are few roads in the forest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.1] Rivers supply fish, and water for irrigation and cities.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.2] Hydroelectricity comes from rivers with a steep fall and a large flow.
[geo-region-continental-s2.36.3] Floods and droughts both disrupt river transport, as in the 2023 drought when the Rio Negro fell to a record low.
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.b] Brazil is an agricultural superpower. Its farms feed the world market far more than they employ its own people, which shows how mechanised and commercial the leading sector is.
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.0] Brazil is the world's largest producer and exporter of soya beans, and the largest producer and exporter of coffee, which is about a third of world output (2020s).
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.1] It is also the largest producer of sugar cane and orange juice, and the largest exporter of beef and chicken, so it leads the world in several different foods.
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.2] Agriculture itself is under a tenth of GDP and of jobs (early 2020s). This is small because a few huge, machine-run farms produce the crops with few workers.
[geo-region-continental-s2.37.3] Agribusiness as a whole (farming plus processing and trade) is much larger than farming alone, and it makes up a large share of Brazil's exports.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.b] Brazilian farming ranges from giant mechanised farms to tiny family plots. Exams ask for two types, so learn each with a crop and a place.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.0] Large commercial mechanised farms grow soya, maize and cotton in the Cerrado (the tropical savanna of the Centre-West). Machines suit the flat land, so a few workers farm huge areas.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.1] Plantations grow one crop on a large estate for sale abroad, such as sugar cane, coffee and oranges.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.2] Extensive cattle ranching uses large areas of land with few animals per hectare and little labour, on the Cerrado, the Pantanal and cleared Amazon land.
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.3] Subsistence and family farming grows food for the household and local sale: cassava (manioc), beans and maize. It includes shifting cultivation in the Amazon and small farms in the sertão (the dry Northeast interior).
[geo-region-continental-s2.38.4] Irrigated fruit farming uses river water to grow export fruit in the dry Northeast, for example grapes and mangoes.
