TARGET geo-region-ireland-s2 (part 1 of 2): 2. A Peripheral Irish Region: the West of Ireland
[geo-region-ireland-s2.0.b] This case study uses the West Region, made up of Counties Galway (including Galway city), Mayo and Roscommon. It is a peripheral region, meaning it lies far from the country's economic core around Dublin and from European markets.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.0.0] Galway and Mayo lie on the Atlantic coast, so they face the ocean that shapes the region's weather, while Roscommon is inland.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.0.1] Galway city is the region's only city, so it dominates the region's services and jobs, and Castlebar is the largest town in Mayo.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.0.2] The three counties together form the State's West regional unit for statistics, so this is the standard definition of the region to use throughout.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.b] A region is peripheral when several disadvantages add up and keep it behind the core. In the West they reinforce each other: distance and a harsh physical environment lead to a weak economy, and that drives people away.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.0] Remote location: long distances to Dublin and to European markets raise transport costs for goods.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.1] Difficult physical conditions: high rainfall, poor soils and rugged relief (the shape and height of the land) limit farming and building.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.2] The population is small, scattered and ageing, after a long history of out-migration (people leaving the region) since the Great Famine of the late 1840s.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.3] The economy depends on primary activities (farming, forestry, fishing) and the public sector, with lower incomes than the core.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.4] Infrastructure (roads, rail, broadband) is weaker than in the core, with Galway city as the main exception, because it is a growth centre (a place planned and chosen to attract investment and population).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.b] A sketch map of the region is set as a question. Build it in layers: the outline first, then water, then towns, then relief (upland and lowland).

[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.b] The West faces the Atlantic Ocean, which controls its weather. Moist, mild air arrives from the south-west and meets the western mountains, so the region is wet, windy, mild and cloudy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.0] The climate is cool temperate oceanic (mild and wet all year, with no extremes), strongly influenced by the Atlantic Ocean.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.1] Prevailing south-westerly winds (the winds that blow most often) bring frequent depressions (low-pressure weather systems that bring rain and wind) and gales from the Atlantic.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.2] Relief rainfall: moist Atlantic air is forced up over the western mountains, where it cools and drops its rain.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.3] Annual rainfall is about 1,000 to 1,250 mm on lowlands and over 2,000 mm on some mountains, against about 750 to 1,000 mm in the east.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.4] Winters are mild: mean January temperatures are about 4 to 7 °C, warmed by the North Atlantic Drift (a warm ocean current), so frost and snow are rare at the coast.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.5] Summers are cool: mean July temperatures are about 14 to 16 °C, so the annual temperature range (the gap between winter and summer) is small.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.6] The west is cloudier and less sunny than the east, because the moist Atlantic air arrives there first.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.b] The wet, windy climate shapes what the land can be used for. It suits grass but works against almost everything else.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.0] Grass grows well, but waterlogged land and wet weather make tillage (growing crops) difficult and hay hard to save, because it needs dry spells to dry.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.1] Exposure to Atlantic gales limits the land that can be farmed and the crops that can be grown.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.2] Gales and storms damage forests and power lines, because both stand exposed to the wind.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.3] For example, Storm Éowyn, January 2025, caused widespread damage and power cuts across the West.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.b] Relief (the shape and height of the land) divides the West in two: rugged, hard-rock uplands in the west and low limestone land in the east. Between them lies an ice-scoured lowland, and the coast is cut up by the sea.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.0] Rugged uplands in the west: the Twelve Bens and Maumturks in Connemara, and Mweelrea, Nephin and Croagh Patrick in Co. Mayo.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.1] Mweelrea, 814 m, is the highest peak in Connacht (the province containing the West), so it is the tallest of these; Nephin is 806 m and Croagh Patrick is 764 m.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.2] These are hard rocks, which resist erosion and so form the high ground: quartzite forms the Twelve Bens and Croagh Patrick; granite and mica schist form the uplands of Connemara.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.3] The ice-scoured Connemara lowland has bare rock and many small lakes, because glaciers scraped away the soil and left hollows.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.4] A low, flat to rolling limestone lowland lies in east Galway and Roscommon; Lough Corrib roughly divides it from the Connemara uplands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.5] Clew Bay's drowned drumlins are glacial hills (low hills of glacial deposit) that the sea now covers in part.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.6] The Atlantic coast is heavily indented (cut by many inlets): Killary Harbour, a fjord (a long narrow inlet carved by a glacier) on the Galway–Mayo border, Clew Bay, and Achill Island.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.7] Achill Island is the largest island off the Irish coast, so it is the biggest of Ireland's offshore islands; the Aran Islands are Carboniferous limestone, a continuation of the Burren limestone.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.b] Soils follow relief and rainfall. Wet, steep, acidic ground gives poor soils in the west, and only the limestone lowland in the east has good ones. This is why most of the region suits only grazing.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.0] Blanket bog (peat, a thick wet layer of partly rotted plants) covers much of west Mayo, around Bellacorick, and Connemara, where high rainfall keeps the ground waterlogged.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.1] Leached, acidic podzols (soils with nutrients washed out of the top layer) with an iron pan (a hard, iron-rich layer) lie on the uplands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.2] Heavy, poorly drained gley soils (sticky, waterlogged clay soils) lie on the drumlins and clay lowlands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.3] Better-drained, more fertile brown earths lie on the limestone of east Galway and Roscommon.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.4] Thin, rocky soils cover the limestone pavement (bare, flat limestone rock) of the Aran Islands, where islanders made soil from layers of sand and seaweed on bare rock.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.b] Drainage means how water leaves the land through rivers, lakes and underground. In the West, limestone sends some water underground, while poor natural drainage elsewhere leaves land liable to flood.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.0] The River Corrib flows only a few kilometres from Lough Corrib through Galway city to Galway Bay.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.1] Lough Corrib is the largest lake in the Republic of Ireland, so it is a major feature of the drainage.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.2] Lough Mask's water flows underground to Lough Corrib near Cong, through the Cong 'dry canal', because limestone lets water drain through it.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.3] Turloughs, seasonal lakes on limestone, such as Rahasane turlough near Craughwell, Co. Galway, flood in winter and drain away underground.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.4] The River Moy reaches Killala Bay at Ballina, so Ballina lies on the river near its mouth.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.5] The River Shannon and Lough Ree form much of Roscommon's eastern boundary; the River Suck flows through Ballinasloe to the Shannon.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.6] Poor natural drainage leaves low land beside the Shannon liable to flooding.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.7] The Moy arterial drainage scheme (deepening and straightening a river), run by the Office of Public Works under the Arterial Drainage Act 1945, was built to reduce flooding on the Moy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.b] Physical factors give the West a short effective grazing season (the part of the year when grass can actually be grazed) and restrict farming to grazing on most of the land.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.0] High rainfall, waterlogged and leached soils, steep uplands and exposure give a short effective grazing season.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.1] Tillage is restricted to the better-drained limestone soils of east Galway and Roscommon, because crops need drained soil.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.2] Brown earths on the limestone of east Galway and Roscommon are the region's most fertile farmland, so this is where the best farming is.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.3] Blanket bog and gley soils restrict most of the rest of the region to grazing, because they are too wet and poorly drained for crops.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.b] Human and economic factors add to the physical limits. Farms are small, incomes are low and the region is far from the buyers of its produce.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.0] Farms are small and often fragmented (split into several separate plots), a legacy of the pre-Famine rundale system (shared land held in scattered strips) and of population pressure on the land.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.1] The Congested Districts Board, set up in 1891, improved conditions in the poor western districts through land consolidation (joining scattered plots), piers and cottage industries.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.2] The Land Commission later consolidated holdings, which reduced fragmentation further.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.3] Part-time farming, with off-farm jobs supplementing low farm incomes, because farm income alone is not enough.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.4] Income depends heavily on subsidies and EU payments, which come from the EU's Common Agricultural Policy (CAP).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.5] Distance from markets and ports adds to the cost of moving livestock and produce.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.b] Because most of the land suits only grass, the West's farming is based on animals, mainly cattle and sheep.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.0] Cattle rearing is the main enterprise: suckler herds (cows that rear their own calves) rear calves, and young store cattle (young animals not yet ready for slaughter) are sold through local marts (livestock markets) to farmers in the east to be fattened.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.1] Sheep graze the hill commonage (shared hill land) of Connemara and the Mayo mountains.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.2] There is little tillage or dairying compared with the south and east, because the wet, poorly drained land is poorly suited to both.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.3] The October Fair at Ballinasloe, Co. Galway, is one of Europe's oldest horse fairs, an example of the West's animal-based farming.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.4] The Connemara pony is Ireland's native pony breed, another example of the region's livestock.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.b] Low farm incomes and the pressure of subsidy-driven sheep farming brought two kinds of problem: people leaving the land and damage to the hills. Schemes then tried to put these right.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.0] Low incomes, ageing farmers and few young successors lead to land abandonment, because no one is left to work the farm.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.1] CAP headage payments (payments per animal kept) from the 1980s raised sheep numbers on the hill commonage of Connemara and the Mayo mountains.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.2] Overgrazing stripped vegetation from the hills and eroded the peat.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.3] The Rural Environment Protection Scheme (REPS), from 1994, was a response to the damage.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.4] Commonage Framework Plans, from the early 2000s, set out how shared hill land is to be managed.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.5] Area-based payments paid farmers on the area of land they farm, not per animal, so there was no reward for keeping more sheep.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.6] Diversification (adding new income sources) into agri-tourism and forestry.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.b] Forestry is a primary activity (using natural resources) that suits land too poor for farming. Trees can be grown on wet, acidic ground where grazing pays badly.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.0] Conifer plantations, mainly Sitka spruce (native to western North America), cover marginal land (poor land where farming barely pays) and blanket bog where farming pays poorly.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.1] Coillte, founded in 1988, is the State forestry company, so it is the main planter and manager of State forests.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.2] Planting grants and premiums (payments to landowners) encourage forestry on farmland and bog.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.3] Benefits: income from timber, jobs, and carbon storage in the trees.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.4] Forest cover in Ireland is about 11% (2020s), which is low, so there is room for more planting.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.b] Forestry on this scale has costs for water, landscape and the stability of the ground.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.0] Acidification of streams from conifer plantations, which harms the water for fish.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.1] Monoculture (one species grown over a large area) and landscape impact of large blocks of single-species forest.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.2] Peat slide (a mass of wet peat slipping downhill) at Derrybrien, Slieve Aughty Mountains, Co. Galway, on 16 October 2003: about 450,000 m³ of peat slid during wind-farm construction on forested bog.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.3] The EU Court of Justice fined Ireland in 2019 over Derrybrien, so the slide had legal consequences.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.14.b] The Atlantic coast and the inland lakes and rivers give the West fishing, fish farming (aquaculture), seaweed harvesting and angling (fishing with rod and line). The sheltered bays are used for farming the sea.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.14.0] Rossaveel, Co. Galway, is one of six State Fishery Harbour Centres (State-supported fishing ports) and the ferry port for the Aran Islands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.14.1] Killary Harbour: rope-grown mussels are farmed on mussel rafts.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.14.2] Salmon farms in Connemara bays raise concerns over sea lice (parasites on the fish) and pollution.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.14.3] Seaweed, a resource of the shore, is harvested and processed by Arramara Teoranta at Kilkieran, Connemara.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.14.4] Angling: salmon on the River Moy at Ballina, including the Ridge Pool, and brown trout on Lough Corrib and Lough Mask.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.14.5] Problems: EU fishing quotas (limits on the catch), loss of quota after Brexit (the UK's departure from the EU), small boats and distance from markets.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.15.b] Bellacorick shows the West's energy changing from peat to wind. The region's exposure to the Atlantic, a problem for farming, is an advantage for renewable energy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.15.0] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo, had a peat-fired power station, which closed in 2003.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.15.1] Bellacorick wind farm (1992) was Ireland's first commercial wind farm, so it began wind energy in the same place as the peat station.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.15.2] The Oweninny wind farm is on cutaway bog (bog where the peat has been removed) nearby, so worked-out land is reused for energy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.15.3] Strong Atlantic winds and waves give the region high renewable energy potential.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.15.4] The Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, tests wave energy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.16.b] The West has had gas, coal, marble and gold. Some projects went ahead, one caused conflict and one was refused.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.16.0] The Corrib gas field, off north-west Mayo, was discovered in 1996.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.16.1] Gas first flowed in December 2015, many years after discovery; gas is processed at the Bellanaboy terminal.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.16.2] The Shell to Sea campaign opposed the project, for example through protest, and the 'Rossport Five' were jailed in 2005.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.16.3] Arigna, Co. Roscommon: coal mining ended in 1990; the site is now the Arigna Mining Experience, a visitor attraction.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.16.4] Connemara marble is quarried, using the region's hard metamorphic rock.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.16.5] Croagh Patrick: gold was found in the 1980s, but mining was not allowed after public opposition.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.17.b] Manufacturing (making goods in factories) is a secondary activity. In the West it is thin and clustered in a few places, which reflects the region's peripheral position.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.17.0] Manufacturing is limited and concentrated in Galway city and a few towns: Castlebar, Westport and Ballina.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.17.1] There are few heavy industries, so most rural areas have little manufacturing.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.17.2] Small food-processing and craft firms are the main rural manufacturers, for example firms using local produce.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.18.b] A cluster is a group of related firms in one place that support each other. Galway's medical-technology cluster grew from a factory closure that left skilled workers looking for jobs.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.18.0] Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) closed its Galway manufacturing plant in 1993, leaving a skilled workforce.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.18.1] Boston Scientific opened in Galway in 1994, and Medtronic followed at Parkmore.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.18.2] Suppliers and start-ups clustered around the multinationals (large firms that operate in many countries), with links to the University of Galway.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.18.3] Galway is now one of Europe's leading medical-technology clusters.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.19.b] Mayo has no large city, but several multinationals have set up in its towns. Each is a named example of foreign investment in the West.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.19.0] For example, Baxter Healthcare, Castlebar.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.19.1] Allergan, now part of AbbVie, Westport, which makes Botox, a medical product.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.19.2] Hollister, Ballina, which spreads manufacturing beyond Galway.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.19.3] Coca-Cola concentrate plant, Ballina, an example of a multinational that does not make medical products.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.20.b] State and EU policy tries to steer industry towards the peripheral West with grants, designated centres and agencies. Exams ask for any two factors, and this is one of the five.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.20.0] IDA Ireland (the State agency that attracts foreign firms) and regional incentives attract foreign manufacturing firms to the region.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.20.1] Údarás na Gaeltachta, set up in 1980 with its head office at Na Forbacha, Co. Galway, gives grants to firms in Gaeltacht areas (the Irish-speaking districts).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.20.2] The National Spatial Strategy (2002) named Galway a gateway (a major centre intended to drive regional growth) and Castlebar–Ballina a linked hub (a smaller centre working together as one unit).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.20.3] The National Planning Framework (Project Ireland 2040), 2018, requires Galway, Cork, Limerick and Waterford each to grow by at least 50% by 2040.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.20.4] The Western Development Commission, a statutory body (set up by law) under the Western Development Commission Act 1998, promotes development in the west.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.21.b] Firms locate where they can find skilled workers. Galway has a supply, but the rest of the region loses its skilled young people.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.21.0] The University of Galway, founded in 1845 as Queen's College Galway and given its present name in 2022, supplies graduates, so firms can recruit trained staff locally.

[geo-region-ireland-s2.29.4] The Western Rail Corridor's Ennis–Athenry section reopened in 2010; the line north of Athenry to Claremorris has not reopened.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.29.5] Ferries link the islands to the mainland, because the islands have no road link.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.30.b] Even with improvements, transport in the West has weaknesses that come from its thin population. Good transport still matters for the region's economy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.30.0] Low population density makes public transport uneconomic outside the towns, because there are too few passengers.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.30.1] Poor minor roads and heavy dependence on the car, because buses and trains are too few.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.30.2] Congestion in Galway city: the Galway City Ring Road has been proposed for many years but not built.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.30.3] Long journey times to Dublin, which adds to peripherality.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.30.4] Transport supports tourism and industry and reduces peripherality.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.31.b] Services besides tourism and transport also provide jobs, and most are based in Galway city.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.31.0] A major hospital in Galway city serves as a regional centre for the west, so patients from the three counties travel there.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.31.1] Public-sector jobs, including decentralised government offices (offices moved out of Dublin).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.32.b] Galway is the West's only city and the urban area to use for a question on one urban area. Its location where a river meets the sea explains why it began.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.32.0] Galway city lies where the River Corrib reaches Galway Bay.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.32.1] It began as an Anglo-Norman walled town, from the 13th century, so the walls protected the settlement.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.32.2] The Spanish Arch recalls medieval trade with Spain, so it shows Galway's past as a trading port.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.32.3] 'City of the Tribes' is the city's name for the 14 merchant families who ran its trade.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.32.4] Eyre Square is the city's central open space, so it is a landmark of the city centre.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.33.b] Galway has grown into the region's centre for education, industry, health and planning. Growth has also brought urban problems.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.33.0] Galway city had about 85,000 people in the Galway City Council area in Census 2022.

CANDIDATES geo-region-ireland-s2: 2. A Peripheral Irish Region: the West of Ireland
[geo-region-ireland-s2.33.1] The University of Galway has been in the city since 1845, which gives the city graduates and research.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.33.2] Galway is a designated growth centre in national planning policy, named a gateway in 2002 and set to grow by at least 50% by 2040 under the National Planning Framework.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.33.3] Galway is a medical-technology cluster, which shows its industrial growth since DEC and the medtech firms.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.33.4] For example, Oranmore is a growing satellite town (a smaller settlement near a city that takes some of its growth).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.33.5] Suburban sprawl (the spread of housing over the edge of the city) and housing costs in the growing city.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.33.6] Traffic congestion, because the Galway City Ring Road is still not built.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.34.b] Beyond Galway, the West's towns are small. They serve local areas rather than forming a strong network of larger towns.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.34.0] Castlebar is the county town of Mayo, so it is the administrative centre of the county.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.34.1] Westport is an 18th-century planned town laid out for the Browne family of Westport House.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.34.2] Westport's Octagon and the Mall, along the Carrowbeg River, show its planned layout.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.34.3] Ballina lies on the River Moy; Tuam is the seat of the Catholic Archdiocese of Tuam.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.34.4] Ballinasloe, Co. Galway, and Boyle, near Lough Key Forest Park, are small towns, for example serving their local areas.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.34.5] The towns form a weak urban hierarchy (the ranking of settlements by size and services): few large towns and no large city beyond Galway.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.b] The West has a small share of the State's people, and they are spread very unevenly. Exams ask you to account for the distribution, which means explaining each pattern with a reason.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.0] The region had about 486,000 people in Census 2022, about 9% of the State's 5.1 million.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.1] Galway (including Galway city) about 278,000, Mayo about 138,000 and Roscommon about 70,000 in Census 2022.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.2] Mayo has about 25 people per km² (area about 5,590 km², Ireland's third-largest county); Roscommon has under 30 per km².
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.3] The State average is about 73 per km², and Co. Dublin has about 1,600 per km², which shows how thinly the West is settled.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.4] Concentration in Galway city and its suburbs and satellite towns, where jobs, services and transport are.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.5] Moderate densities on the east Galway, Roscommon and east Mayo lowlands and in the towns, where land and roads are better.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.6] Very low densities on the uplands and blanket bogs of west Mayo (Erris) and Connemara, where soils are poor, slopes steep and the land exposed.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.35.7] Coastal and island settlement, linked to fishing and the Gaeltacht, as on the Aran Islands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.36.b] Population dynamics means how a population changes over time. The West's history of loss is now partly reversed in places.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.36.0] The Great Famine of the late 1840s hit the West hard, because many people depended on small farms.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.36.1] A long history of emigration has left populations far below pre-Famine levels, so the West has not recovered its earlier numbers.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.36.2] Continuing out-migration of young adults to Dublin and abroad, known as brain drain (the loss of educated, skilled people).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.36.3] An ageing population with fewer births and many empty houses, because the young have left.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.36.4] Recent growth from return migration (emigrants coming home), immigration and the expansion of Galway city.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.36.5] The population of every county grew between 2016 and 2022 (Census 2022).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.37.b] Rural settlement in the West has changed from old clustered and scattered forms to housing strung along roads. Rural life has also declined as people have left.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.37.0] Traditional dispersed settlement (houses spread out across the land) and clachans, small clusters of houses shared by families under rundale.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.37.1] Later ribbon development (houses in a line along roads) and one-off housing, because roads give access to services.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.37.2] Rural decline, which follows from out-migration: depopulation, an ageing population, and closures of schools, post offices and Garda stations.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.37.3] Island depopulation, especially on the Aran Islands, where fewer people stay.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.38.b] Governments and the EU have launched programmes to support rural areas. Some invest in places that have lost population and some bring in jobs and services.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.38.0] CLÁR programme (2001): investment in depopulated rural areas, to slow the decline.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.38.1] LEADER, the EU rural development programme, which funds local projects.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.38.2] National Broadband Plan: contract signed in 2019 with National Broadband Ireland, to bring broadband to rural areas.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.38.3] Our Rural Future (2021): rural development policy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.38.4] Remote-working hubs and digital hubs bring work to rural towns, so people can work there without commuting to the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.38.5] The Western Development Commission covers seven counties, including Galway, Mayo and Roscommon, which is wider than the three-county region used here.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.38.6] The Congested Districts Board of 1891 was an earlier response, and Údarás na Gaeltachta supports Gaeltacht areas by giving grants to firms.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.39.b] The West holds Ireland's largest Gaeltacht areas, the districts where Irish is the main community language.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.39.0] The Galway Gaeltacht is the largest; it includes Connemara and the Aran Islands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.39.1] The Mayo Gaeltacht: Erris (Iorras), Achill (Acaill) and Tourmakeady (Tuar Mhic Éadaigh).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.39.2] Roscommon has no Gaeltacht area, so Irish-speaking districts lie only in Galway and Mayo.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.39.3] The Census 2022 Gaeltacht population was about 106,000.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.40.b] Fewer people now use Irish every day, even inside the Gaeltacht. Several forces push the language back.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.40.0] About 20,000 people speak Irish daily outside the education system in the Gaeltacht; about 72,000 do so nationally (Census 2022).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.40.1] Emigration of young speakers from the Gaeltacht, which removes the next generation of speakers.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.40.2] English-language media and English spoken in the home, which reduce daily use of Irish.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.40.3] Tourism and the in-migration of English speakers into Gaeltacht areas, which makes English the common language locally.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.b] Institutions and strategies try to protect Irish in the Gaeltacht. They include State bodies, media, laws and plans, schooling and housing.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.0] Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980), head office at Na Forbacha, Co. Galway, is the State body responsible for the Gaeltacht's development.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.1] TG4, launched in 1996 as Teilifís na Gaeilge, with its headquarters at Baile na hAbhann, Co. Galway.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.2] RTÉ Raidió na Gaeltachta, from 1972, with its headquarters at Casla (Costelloe), Co. Galway.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.3] Gaeltacht Act 2012: language-planning areas.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.4] 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language, 2010–2030.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.5] Irish-medium schools (where lessons are taught in Irish) and summer colleges, so young people use Irish regularly.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.6] Planning conditions that reserve new houses for Irish speakers, so that new housing goes to Irish speakers.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.41.7] Údarás jobs to keep young people in the Gaeltacht, which counters emigration.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.42.b] The West is strongly Roman Catholic, and its religion shows in pilgrimage sites and old church centres. Religion also draws tourists.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.42.0] The West has a higher Roman Catholic share than Dublin; nationally, about 69% of people are Roman Catholic and about 14% have no religion (Census 2022).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.42.1] For example, Knock Shrine, Co. Mayo: an apparition was reported in 1879; Pope John Paul II visited in 1979 and Pope Francis in 2018.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.42.2] Croagh Patrick pilgrimage on Reek Sunday, the last Sunday in July, for example, draws pilgrims to the mountain.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.42.3] Ballintubber Abbey, Co. Mayo, was founded in 1216, and Mass has been celebrated there continuously since.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.42.4] Tuam is the seat of the Catholic Archdiocese of Tuam, so it is a church centre for the region.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.42.5] Religious practice and vocations (people entering the priesthood) are declining, but religion remains important in community life.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s4: 4. Socio-economic regions
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.b] Geographers group places into regions by how wealthy and developed they are, not just by landscape. A socio-economic region is defined by its level of wealth and development, judged by several indicators at once, because no single figure tells the whole story.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.0] The indicators include income or GDP per head (the value of what an area produces, divided by its population), unemployment, the mix of primary, secondary and tertiary jobs, migration, age structure, education and services.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.1] Primary jobs take materials from the land and sea (farming, fishing, forestry, mining); secondary jobs make goods in factories; tertiary jobs provide services. The mix of these three is a clue to how developed a region is.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.2] The core–periphery idea: wealth, people and investment (money put into businesses and infrastructure) concentrate in a core, while the periphery (the outer, distant area) loses people and capital to it.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.3] Cumulative causation means each advantage in the core attracts more, so the gap tends to widen unless governments or the EU intervene.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.b] A core region has a strong, growing economy, high incomes, and a concentration of population, investment and decision-making. Everything else about it follows from that economic strength.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.0] It has high GDP per head and low unemployment, because jobs are plentiful.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.1] It has a large tertiary (services) sector, including finance, company headquarters and high-tech industry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.2] Most foreign investment goes to the core, because firms from abroad want its markets, workers and transport links.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.3] Net in-migration (more people arriving than leaving) of young, educated workers gives it a young population, because they move to where the jobs are.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.4] It has dense, well-developed infrastructure (the transport networks that move people and goods): motorways, airports, ports and rail lines.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.5] It has universities and research institutions, which supply skilled workers, and usually the capital city with the seat of government.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.6] Causes: accessibility, a large market and labour force, an early start, government and capital functions, and agglomeration, where firms attract more firms to the same place because they gain from being near suppliers, customers and workers.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.7] Problems of success: congestion, high house prices, urban sprawl (the city spreading outwards over farmland) and pressure on services.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.b] Three examples show what a core looks like at national and European scale. Each is a place where population, government and business cluster.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.0] Greater Dublin Area: Dublin, Kildare, Meath and Wicklow. County Dublin had about 1.45 million people in Census 2022, when the State's population was about 5.1 million.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.1] Dublin, the capital, has the seat of government, Ireland's main port (Dublin Port) and busiest airport (Dublin Airport). Its docklands host the European headquarters of many technology firms, e.g. Google. There is more on this in the Irish Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.2] Paris Basin, France: Île-de-France, the region around Paris at the basin's core, has about 12 million people. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.3] The Blue Banana: the curved belt of Europe's main core, running from south-east England and London through the Benelux countries (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg) and the Rhine valley to northern Italy (Milan).
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.b] A peripheral region is distant, physically or economically, from the core, with a weaker economy and fewer opportunities. Its problems tend to feed each other, so people leave and the region falls further behind.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.0] Remote location and poor access to markets and services, so it is costly to sell goods or reach shops, hospitals and schools.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.1] Difficult physical conditions: mountains, high rainfall, poor soils and a short growing season, which limit what farmers can produce.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.2] Dependence on primary activity (taking materials from land and sea): small farms, fishing and forestry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.3] Low incomes and higher unemployment, so people look elsewhere for work, or underemployment (people working fewer hours or in lower-skilled jobs than they want).
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.4] Net out-migration of young adults, so the people left behind are older and rural populations fall.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.5] Fewer services, and small, scattered settlements, which means services costly to provide.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.b] Because the periphery tends to fall further behind, governments and the EU act to slow the loss of people and jobs.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.0] EU regional and cohesion funds (money meant to narrow the gap between richer and poorer regions) and state agencies channel investment into the periphery.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.1] Better roads and broadband, and decentralisation of government offices (moving them out of the capital), help peripheral areas keep people and jobs.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.2] Tourism builds on the scenery and culture of peripheral areas, so it brings jobs without needing a nearby core.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.3] There is more on each example in the case-study chapters.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.b] These four examples are far from their country's or Europe's core, in the west, south and far north.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.0] West of Ireland: the Northern and Western regional assembly area. Co. Leitrim has the smallest population of any Irish county.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.1] The Mezzogiorno, southern Italy. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.2] Northern Norway: Arctic, remote and reliant on fishing.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.3] North-west Scotland: the Highlands and Islands.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.b] A less-developed region is defined by how far its development falls below an average, and the EU uses a precise cut-off to decide which regions get the most help.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.0] A less-developed region is one whose level of development is well below the national or EU average.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.1] In EU regional policy, a less-developed region has GDP per head below 75% of the EU average, and receives the most EU aid.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.2] Most less-developed regions in Europe are also peripheral: the Mezzogiorno, and much of eastern Europe such as Romania and Bulgaria.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.3] Peripheral is not the same as less-developed, because a peripheral region can lie in a rich country, such as the West of Ireland or northern Norway.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.4] Ireland was a single Objective 1 region (the category receiving the highest level of EU aid) until 1999. From 2000 to 2006 only the BMW (Border, Midland and Western) region kept Objective 1 status.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.5] At a global scale, less-developed regions are developing countries. India is the continental example. There is more on India in its own chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.6] The Mezzogiorno's development history began with the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno (Fund for the South) in 1950.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.b] A region of industrial decline grew on heavy, traditional industry and has since lost much of it. The loss then spreads through the local economy.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.0] Its traditional industries include coal mining, iron and steel, shipbuilding and textiles.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.1] Causes: coal seams exhausted or too costly to work; cheaper imports and competition from newly industrialised countries (countries that built up their factory industry more recently, often with lower costs).
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.2] Further causes: coal replaced by oil and gas; out-of-date plant (old machinery and factories); and automation (machines doing work people once did), which needs fewer workers.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.3] Effects: high unemployment, especially among older male workers; derelict factories, mines and spoil heaps (waste rock dumped from mining); and polluted land.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.4] Further effects: out-migration, falling services, and a poor image that deters new investment.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.5] Responses: retraining; clearing and cleaning derelict land; attracting new light industry and services; universities and technology parks; reuse of industrial heritage for tourism and culture; EU funds.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.b] Most examples are old coal and steel regions of north-west Europe. The Irish examples are port and shipbuilding areas.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.0] Sambre–Meuse valley, Wallonia, Belgium: coal mining and steel along the Sambre and Meuse, e.g. Charleroi on the Sambre and Liège on the Meuse.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.1] Ruhr, Germany (North Rhine-Westphalia): Europe's largest coal and steel region, so its decline was large, with cities such as Essen, Dortmund and Duisburg.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.2] Prosper-Haniel in Bottrop, the last hard-coal mine in Germany, closed in 2018. The Zollverein coal mine complex in Essen became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001 and is now a cultural centre, an example of reusing industrial heritage.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.3] South Wales coalfield, including the Rhondda valleys: coal mining collapsed in the late 20th century.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.4] Nord–Pas-de-Calais, France, is a further European example.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.5] Belfast, for example: the Harland and Wolff shipyard, which built the Titanic (launched 1911), declined greatly in the late 20th century.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.6] Dublin docklands: port jobs fell with containerisation (goods shipped in standard metal containers, which need far fewer dock workers). The International Financial Services Centre (IFSC), established in 1987 on derelict dockland, regenerated the area.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.7] There is more on Irish economic evidence in the Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Economic Activities chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.b] The clearest difference between the two region types is the kind of work people do. Compare them sector by sector: primary, secondary and tertiary.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.0] Primary, for example: large, commercial, efficient farms on good land in the core; small, fragmented farms on poor land in the periphery, with more fishing and forestry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.1] Secondary: concentrations of manufacturing and multinational investment (factories owned by firms based in other countries) in the core; few, small and often grant-aided (helped by state money) factories in the periphery.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.2] Tertiary: finance, headquarters, government, universities, and major ports and airports in the core; tourism and public services in the periphery.
