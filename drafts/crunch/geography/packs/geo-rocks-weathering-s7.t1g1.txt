TARGET geo-rocks-weathering-s7: 7. Human interaction: mass movement
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.b] Mass movement is the downhill movement of soil and rock under gravity. People can make it happen more often and faster, or reduce it, depending on how they treat slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.0] Humans make mass movement more frequent and faster in three ways: by removing the vegetation that binds soil and absorbs water, by adding water or weight to slopes, and by removing support at the base of slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.1] Managing slopes can reduce the risk, because the same three factors can be reversed: keep vegetation, keep water out and keep the base supported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.2] Removing vegetation also exposes soil to rain splash (raindrops knocking soil particles loose), sheet wash (thin water flow over the whole surface), gullying (water cutting channels) and wind, which strip it away. Slopes then become prone to creep, slides and mudflows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.0.3] Answers may cover soil erosion as well as mass movement, provided the link to slopes and gravity is made.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.b] Overgrazing means keeping more animals on land than it can feed. It removes the plant cover that holds a slope together, so it is one of the three human activities the syllabus names.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.0] Too many animals for the land's carrying capacity (the number it can support without damage) eat the vegetation down to the roots and trample the ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.1] Hooves compact the soil and break the surface of peat, leaving bare patches.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.2] Without roots, soil and peat lose cohesion (the way the particles stick together). Rain runs off the compacted surface or saturates the broken peat, so the ground is both weaker and wetter.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.3] Sheet and gully erosion increase, soil creep (slow downhill movement of soil) speeds up as terracettes (small step-like ridges on a slope) deepen, and peat slides occur.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.1.4] Sediment from the eroded slopes silts up rivers and damages fish spawning beds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.b] In Ireland, overgrazing occurred on the hill commonage of the west, where grazing land is shared. A payment system encouraged farmers to keep far more sheep than the hills could carry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.0] From the 1980s, EU (CAP) headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage (shared mountain grazing) in western Ireland, including Connemara, Co. Galway, and the mountains of Co. Mayo, Co. Donegal and Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.1] Overgrazing stripped heather and grass and exposed and eroded blanket peat (a thick layer of peat covering upland ground). Silt then entered rivers, harming salmon and trout spawning grounds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.2] Response 1: payments were changed from per-head to per-area, so farmers no longer gained by adding animals.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.3] Response 2: Commonage Framework Plans, from the early 2000s, set lower stocking rates (fewer animals per area).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.2.4] Response 3: REPS (the Rural Environment Protection Scheme, introduced in 1994) paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.3.b] Each management method tackles the cause of overgrazing: too many animals, or animals staying too long in one place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.3.0] Destocking: reducing the number of animals to the land's carrying capacity, so the vegetation is no longer eaten down to the roots.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.3.1] Rotational grazing, moving animals between areas so that vegetation can recover.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.3.2] Fencing off degraded ground to let vegetation recover, because the roots can then bind the soil again.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.b] Overcropping means growing crops on the same land too intensively, so the soil is worn out. Weak, bare soil is then easily carried away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.0] The same land is cropped year after year without fallow periods (rest years with no crop) or fertiliser. Soil nutrients and organic matter are used up, and soil structure breaks down.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.1] Crops grow poorly, the ground is left bare between harvests, and marginal (poor-quality) and sloping land is ploughed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.2] Loose, dry, structureless soil is washed downslope by rain or blown away by wind, forming rills (tiny channels) and gullies (larger, deeper channels).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.4.3] On slopes, ploughing up and down the slope channels water and soil downhill, because the furrows act like drains.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.b] The Dust Bowl shows how ploughing up natural cover and then meeting a drought can strip a whole region of its soil.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.0] On the Great Plains of the USA, including Oklahoma and Kansas, natural grassland was ploughed up for wheat in the 1930s.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.1] Severe drought then left bare, dry soil, which wind erosion removed in huge dust storms, because there were no grass roots to hold it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.2] Many farming families were forced to leave, because the land could no longer grow crops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.5.3] The US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935. Shelter belts of trees (rows of trees that break the wind), contour ploughing (ploughing along the slope) and crop rotation (changing the crop grown each year) were promoted.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.b] The Sahel is an international example in which overgrazing and overcropping act together, with fuelwood cutting added.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.0] The Sahel is the semi-arid (very dry) belt along the southern edge of the Sahara.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.1] Population growth led to more cattle, goats and sheep concentrated around wells and boreholes, so the land around the water was grazed bare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.2] Population growth also led to shorter fallow periods and more cash crops (crops grown to sell), which is overcropping, and to more fuelwood cutting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.6.3] With droughts, notably in the 1970s and 1980s, the bare soil was eroded by wind and by the heavy rain of the short wet season. This process is called desertification (land turning into desert).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.b] Management either keeps the soil fed and covered, or stops water and wind from carrying it away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.0] Crop rotation and fallow periods, so that soil nutrients recover.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.1] Contour ploughing, ploughing along the slope rather than up and down it, so furrows hold water instead of channelling it downhill.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.2] Strip cropping (growing crops in alternate strips), terracing of steep slopes (cutting level steps into them), and shelter belts against wind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.3] Mulching (covering the soil with plant material) and cover crops keep the soil covered between harvests.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.7.4] Example: the rice terraces of Banaue, Luzon, Philippines, show terracing of steep slopes that is over 2,000 years old.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.b] Deforestation is the clearing of forest. Trees hold a slope together and manage the water that falls on it, so removing them makes slopes much less stable.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.0] Trees are removed for farmland, timber, fuelwood, roads and building, so forest is replaced by bare or thinly covered ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.1] Tree roots bind soil and anchor it to bedrock. The canopy breaks the force of rain, and trees take up water (interception, where leaves catch rain, and transpiration, where plants release water vapour).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.2] Without trees, more rain reaches and saturates the soil. Roots rot and no longer hold the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.8.3] Landslides and mudflows become more frequent, especially in heavy tropical rain. More sediment and faster run-off reach rivers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.b] Two international examples show how deforested slopes turn heavy rain into deadly floods and mudflows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.0] Haiti has lost almost all its natural forest, mainly for farmland and charcoal. Neighbouring Dominican Republic has far more forest cover.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.1] In September 2004 Tropical Storm Jeanne caused floods and mudslides off the bare hills around Gonaïves, killing about 3,000 people.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.2] Hurricane Mitch, October 1998: torrential rain over Honduras and Nicaragua caused floods and landslides, made worse by deforested slopes. About 11,000 people were killed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.9.3] A mudflow from the Casita volcano, Nicaragua, buried villages during Hurricane Mitch.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.10.b] Ireland has little woodland left after centuries of clearance, and modern forestry can still destabilise upland slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.10.0] Historic forest clearance left Ireland with very little woodland. About 1% of Ireland was forested around 1900.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.10.1] Forest cover is now about 11%, so it has risen since 1900, mostly in conifer plantations.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.10.2] Clear-felling (cutting down all the trees in an area at once) of conifer plantations on upland peat, and building forestry and wind-farm roads, can destabilise peat slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.11.b] These methods keep tree roots and canopy in place on the slopes that need them most.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.11.0] Afforestation (planting trees where there were none) and reforestation (replanting where trees were cut) of steep slopes, so roots bind the soil again.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.11.1] Buffer strips of trees beside rivers, so that roots hold the banks and trap sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.11.2] Selective felling (removing only some trees) instead of clear-felling, and restricting felling on steep or peaty slopes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.b] Building on upland blanket bog can cause peat slides, because the work disturbs a wet, weak slope. Exams accept this as a human cause of mass movement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.0] Wind farms and roads on upland blanket bog involve excavation, dumping of spoil (waste material dug out) on the bog surface and interference with drainage.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.1] Derrybrien, Slieve Aughty Mountains, Co. Galway, 16 October 2003: during construction of a wind farm on a forested blanket-bog hillside, a peat slide of about 450,000 cubic metres moved into the Owendalulleegh River, killing large numbers of fish.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.2] In 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland, a lump sum of €5 million plus €15,000 a day, because no proper environmental impact assessment (a study of likely damage before building) had been carried out.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.12.3] Meenbog wind farm, near Ballybofey, Co. Donegal, November 2020: a peat slide during construction flowed into the Mourne Beg River and crossed into Northern Ireland, polluting a river system important for salmon.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.b] Anything that steepens a slope, loads it, wets it or removes its support raises the risk of mass movement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.0] Road and rail cuttings steepen slopes or remove support at their base, so the slope above is left unsupported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.1] Building weight on top of a slope adds to the downslope force.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.2] Leaking pipes, drainage and irrigation saturate slopes, because extra water adds weight and reduces friction between particles.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.3] Reservoirs saturate valley sides. Vajont is the example.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.13.4] Mining spoil heaps (piles of waste rock) are unstable. Aberfan is the example.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.b] Slope risk can be cut by physically strengthening slopes, and by planning so that people avoid or are warned about the danger.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.0] Slope engineering: retaining walls, gabions (wire cages of stones), rock bolts, wire netting and rock-catch fences on cliffs, all of which hold or catch loose material.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.1] Regrading slopes to a gentler angle, and drainage pipes to lower the water content.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.2] Planning: hazard mapping, avoiding building on unstable slopes, and environmental impact assessments before building on peat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s7.14.3] Monitoring and warning systems, so that people can leave before a slope fails.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s5: 5. Mass movement (mass wasting) processes and the factors governing them
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.b] Slopes are never completely still. Mass movement is the way weathered material shifts downhill, and everything else in this subsection (the processes, the factors and the case studies) is a variation on it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.0] Mass movement (also called mass wasting) is the downslope movement of weathered material, such as regolith (the loose broken rock and soil that covers solid rock), soil, rock, mud, peat or snow, under the influence of gravity.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.1] No transporting agent such as a river, glacier or wind is needed, because gravity does the work. Weathering (the breakdown of rock where it lies) supplies the material that moves.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.2] Gravity pulls material downslope. Friction (grip between surfaces), cohesion (particles sticking together) and plant roots resist it. Movement happens when the downslope force exceeds the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.3] Processes are classed, so that they can be compared, by speed (slow or fast), by water content (dry or wet) and by type of movement (heave, slide, flow or fall).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.b] Mass movement is not even across the landscape or through the year. It varies in space and in time, which is the syllabus idea of processes differing in intensity (how strong) and frequency (how often).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.0] In space, it is most intense on steep, wet and unvegetated slopes, because steepness increases the pull downslope while water and bare ground reduce the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.1] In time, its frequency rises after heavy rain, snowmelt, earthquakes and seasonal freeze-thaw (the repeated freezing and thawing of water in rock cracks).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.b] The slowest mass movement, moving soil by a few millimetres to centimetres a year. It is too slow to watch, so you recognise it from its effects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.0] Soil particles are lifted at right angles to the slope by wetting (swelling) or by freezing (frost heave, the upward push of freezing ground).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.1] On drying or thawing, the particles drop vertically under gravity, so each cycle moves them a little further downslope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.2] Evidence: terracettes (small steps on grassy slopes), bent tree trunks, tilted fences, walls and poles, soil piled up behind walls, and cracked roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.3] Diagram: draw the zigzag path of a particle, lifted at right angles to the slope and dropped vertically.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.b] Solifluction is a slow flow of waterlogged soil in cold regions near ice. It works because frozen ground below stops water draining away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.0] In periglacial areas (cold areas bordering ice sheets and glaciers) the surface layer thaws in summer above permanently frozen ground, called permafrost.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.1] The saturated layer cannot drain, because the frozen ground beneath is impermeable, so it flows slowly downhill, forming lobes (tongue-shaped bulges) and terraces.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.2] Solifluction was widespread in Ireland at the end of the last glaciation, when the climate was periglacial.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.b] Rockfall and landslide both move solid rock quickly. The difference is that rockfall is a free fall from a cliff, while a landslide is a slide along a plane.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.0] Rockfall: blocks loosened by freeze-thaw, or undercut by the sea or a river (worn away at the base), fall freely from a steep cliff. They collect as scree (also called talus) at the foot.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.1] Landslide (rockslide): a mass of rock or debris slides suddenly along a flat plane, such as a bedding plane (a layer boundary in sedimentary rock) or joint (a crack) sloping in the same direction as the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.2] Landslides often follow heavy rain, which lubricates the plane so the rock slips more easily, or an earthquake, which shakes it loose.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.b] A slump is a fast slide in which the moving mass turns as it goes, because the surface it slides on is curved rather than flat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.0] Saturated material, often clay over or under permeable rock (rock that lets water through), slides along a curved (concave) slip plane and rotates backward.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.1] The slump leaves a crescent-shaped scar (scarp) at the top, a back-tilted block and a bulging toe at the foot.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.2] Slumps are common on clay cliffs undercut by the sea, because the sea removes the support at the base, for example on the Antrim coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.3] A slump moves on a curved plane. A landslide moves on a flat plane.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.b] These three fast flows each carry a wet or loose mass downhill: mud, volcanic mud, or snow and ice. All are triggered by a sudden supply of water or by disturbance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.0] Mudflow: fine material (clay, silt, weathered rock or volcanic ash) saturated by heavy rain or snowmelt flows rapidly downhill like a liquid, often along valleys. It can bury settlements.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.1] Lahar: a volcanic mudflow of ash and water, from rain or from melted snow and ice on the volcano. It can travel very fast and far. The word is from Indonesia.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.2] Avalanche: a sudden, rapid slide of snow and ice down a steep mountain slope, triggered by heavy snowfall, rapid thaw or disturbance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.3] Ice avalanches occur when part of a glacier collapses, such as at Marmolada in 2022.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.b] A bog burst is a fast mass movement of peat, common in the uplands of Ireland. Peat (partly rotted plant matter that builds up in waterlogged ground) on a slope fills with water and then slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.0] Blanket bog (a thick layer of peat covering a hillside) on a slope becomes saturated after prolonged or intense rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.1] The peat gains weight, and its base loses grip on the underlying mineral soil or rock. The peat bursts and flows downhill as a black slurry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.2] A bog burst blocks rivers, kills fish, and destroys roads, bridges and land.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.3] Bog bursts are common in the uplands of western and north-western Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.b] Whether a slope moves, and how fast, depends on a set of factors. The first three are the slope angle, the water in the ground and what the slope is made of.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.0] Gradient (slope angle): the steeper the slope, the greater the downslope pull of gravity and the faster the movement. Gentle slopes favour creep; steep slopes favour falls and slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.1] Water content: some water adds cohesion, but saturation fills the pores (the tiny gaps between particles), adds weight, reduces friction and acts as a lubricant, so material flows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.2] Heavy or prolonged rain and snowmelt trigger most fast movements, because they saturate the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.3] Clay becomes slippery and plastic (easily moulded) when wet. Jointed or bedded rock dipping towards the slope slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.4] Permeable rock over impermeable clay or shale (rock that water cannot pass through) concentrates water at the junction, as on the Antrim coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.b] The remaining factors either hold a slope together or remove what supports it. Some are natural, some come from the climate, and one is human.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.0] Vegetation: roots bind soil and plants absorb water, so bare slopes move more easily.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.1] Climate and weathering: freeze-thaw loosens rock, heavy seasonal rainfall adds water, and periglacial climates produce solifluction.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.2] Undercutting: erosion by the sea or a river at the base of a slope removes support, so the material above is left unsupported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.3] Loading: extra weight at the top of a slope, from rock, buildings or spoil (waste rock), increases the downslope force.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.4] Tectonic activity: earthquakes and volcanic eruptions shake loose material and trigger landslides and lahars.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.5] Human activity also affects slope stability. There is more on this in Human interaction: mass movement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.b] The Antrim coast is an Irish example of landslides and slumps. Its layers of rock, hard on top of soft, explain why the slopes fail.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.0] Location: the Antrim coast between Larne and Cushendall, along the Antrim Coast Road (A2).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.1] Geology: hard basalt lies on chalk, which lies on soft Lias clay with other mudstones.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.2] Rainwater soaks through the jointed basalt and chalk and saturates the clay, which becomes slippery and plastic.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.3] The rock above slumps and rotates seaward in rotational slides, and clay flows out as mudflows. These have repeatedly blocked the coast road.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.4] Garron Point is a classic landslip site on this coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.5] Responses, so that the road stays open: engineers have built retaining walls and drainage, and fitted rock-catch fences and netting to protect the road.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.11.b] Two Irish bog bursts show how rain-soaked peat can fail, one recent and one historic.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.11.0] Pollatomish (Dooncarton Mountain), Co. Mayo, 19 September 2003: an intense thunderstorm with very heavy rain triggered about 40 landslides and peat slides in a few hours.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.11.1] At Pollatomish, houses, roads, a bridge and a graveyard were damaged, and several families were evacuated.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.11.2] Knocknageeha, near Rathmore, Co. Kerry, December 1896: a bog burst swept away a house, killing a family of eight. It is the classic Irish bog burst.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.b] Italy gives two international examples: mudflows after heavy rain at Sarno, and a landslide into a reservoir at Vajont.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.0] Sarno and neighbouring towns, Campania, southern Italy, 5-6 May 1998: after prolonged heavy rain, loose volcanic ash and soil on steep limestone slopes became saturated and slid off as fast mudflows into the towns below. About 160 people were killed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.1] Vajont Dam, northern Italy, 9 October 1963: a huge landslide from Monte Toc slid into the reservoir (the lake held back by the dam). The wave it sent over the dam destroyed the town of Longarone, killing about 2,000 people. The dam itself survived.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.2] Weak clay layers in the valley side, and the filling of the reservoir which saturated the slope, caused the Vajont slide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.b] South America gives a lahar triggered by a volcano and mudslides triggered by torrential rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.0] Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia, 13 November 1985: a small eruption melted part of the summit ice cap. Lahars rushed down river valleys and buried the town of Armero, killing about 23,000 people.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.1] Warnings had been given, but Armero was not evacuated.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.2] Vargas state, on the coast of Venezuela, December 1999: days of torrential rain triggered mudslides and debris flows on the steep mountains above coastal towns, killing thousands of people.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.b] Recent glacier collapses in the Alps are mass movements of ice, rock and mud. They show glacier retreat acting as a new cause.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.0] Blatten, Lötschental, canton of Valais, Switzerland, 28 May 2025: the Birch Glacier collapsed, sending a huge mass of ice, rock and mud onto the village and burying most of it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.1] The village had been evacuated days earlier, after geologists detected rockfalls onto the glacier.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.2] The debris blocked the River Lonza, forming a lake upstream, which means a flood risk.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.3] Marmolada glacier, Dolomites, Italy, 3 July 2022: during a heatwave a large section of the glacier collapsed as an ice avalanche, killing 11 people.
