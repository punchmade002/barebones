TARGET geo-economic-development-s4: 4. Global issues and a justice perspective
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.b] Global issues are problems that cross borders and affect the whole world, such as poverty, inequality, trade rules, debt, climate change and migration. A justice perspective asks whether the distribution of wealth and power is fair, who decides, and who benefits. It treats development as a matter of rights, not charity.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.0] The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948, includes rights to food, health, education and decent work.
[geo-economic-development-s4.0.1] Poverty denies these rights, so a justice perspective treats poverty as a rights failure rather than a matter of charity. In other words, people are owed these things, not given them as a favour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.b] Justice questions start from the scale of the problem. The figures below show how many people are extremely poor, where they live, and how unevenly wealth is shared.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.0] About 700 million people lived in extreme poverty in 2022, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa (the part of Africa south of the Sahara). The World Bank, the UN-linked development lender, measures extreme poverty as living on under 2.15 US dollars a day (2017 prices).
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.1] The share of the world's population in extreme poverty fell from over a third in 1990 to under a tenth by 2019, so the trend is strongly downward even though the numbers remain large.
[geo-economic-development-s4.1.2] The richest 10% own about 76% of global wealth, while the poorest half own about 2% (World Inequality Report 2022). This shows how unequal the sharing of wealth is.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.b] The UN has set global targets to measure progress against poverty and injustice. The first set ran from 2000 to 2015 and the second runs to 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.0] The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000-2015) had eight goals. The target of halving the share of people in extreme poverty from 1990 was met by 2010, five years early, largely thanks to China.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.1] The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were adopted by the UN in September 2015. They have 17 goals, to be met by 2030.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.2] The SDGs target poverty (SDG 1), hunger (SDG 2), quality education (SDG 4), gender equality (SDG 5), reduced inequalities (SDG 10) and climate action (SDG 13). Other targets cover health, clean water and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.2.3] The world is off track to meet most SDG targets by 2030, so shortfalls remain alongside the progress.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.b] Trade rules and prices can leave poor countries poorer. Many depend on selling raw materials, and the biggest profits are made further along the chain, in rich countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.0] Many developing countries depend on exporting a few primary commodities (raw materials such as coffee, cocoa, cotton and minerals), whose prices are low and unstable. This makes their income hard to plan around.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.1] The terms of trade (the amount of imports a country's exports can buy) tend to move against these countries. Their exports buy fewer imports over time, while they import manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.2] Most of the final price of coffee, chocolate or clothing goes to processors, brands and retailers in rich countries. The grower, at the start of the value chain (the steps from raw material to finished product), receives a small share.
[geo-economic-development-s4.3.3] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials. This discourages poor countries from processing their own crops, so they stay stuck exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.b] Rich countries also help their own farmers with money, and this can push down the prices poor farmers receive. Cotton is the clearest example.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.0] Rich-country farm subsidies (government payments to farmers), such as US cotton subsidies and the EU's Common Agricultural Policy in the past, let surpluses be sold cheaply abroad. This is called dumping, and it undercuts poor farmers who cannot match the low price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.1] The Cotton-4 West African producers, Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad and Mali, were harmed by these subsidies.
[geo-economic-development-s4.4.2] The WTO (World Trade Organization, the body that rules on trade disputes) ruled in Brazil's favour against US cotton subsidies in 2004-05.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.b] Governments and trade bodies have tried to make trade fairer for poor countries, with mixed success. Fair trade is a third response, because it works through consumers rather than governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.0] The WTO Doha Development Round (a set of global trade talks meant to help developing countries) was launched in 2001 but has stalled without agreement.
[geo-economic-development-s4.5.1] The EU's 'Everything But Arms' scheme (2001) gives the least developed countries duty-free (no tariffs) and quota-free (no limit on amounts) access to the EU for all products except arms and ammunition.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.b] Fair trade is a trading model in which producers receive guaranteed terms and the Fairtrade Mark identifies certified products. It tries to give growers a bigger and steadier share than the normal market does.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.0] The Fairtrade Minimum Price covers the cost of sustainable production. It is a guaranteed floor, not a guaranteed higher price: when the market price is higher, producers receive the market price plus the premium.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.1] The Fairtrade Premium is paid to producer co-operatives (groups of farmers who work together and share the benefits) for community projects such as schools, clinics and clean water.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.2] Fair trade also requires long-term contracts, decent working conditions, no child labour, environmental standards and democratically run co-operatives.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.3] Fair trade covers coffee, tea, cocoa, bananas, sugar and cotton, for example, which are the kinds of crops whose growers get a small share of the price.
[geo-economic-development-s4.6.4] The first fair trade label, Max Havelaar, was launched in the Netherlands in 1988 for Mexican coffee. Fairtrade International now coordinates the Fairtrade Mark.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.b] Ireland has its own fair trade links, but fair trade has weaknesses that an evaluation should include.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.0] Fairtrade Ireland (Fairtrade Mark Ireland) licenses the Fairtrade Mark in Ireland, meaning it gives permission for products to carry it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.1] Irish towns and schools can apply for Fairtrade status. Fairtrade Fortnight takes place each year in late February and March.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.2] Limit: fair trade makes up only a tiny share of world trade, so it reaches few producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.3] Limit: certified products can cost consumers more, and certification is expensive for small producers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.7.4] Limit: fair trade does not change the trade rules that cause unfair trade in the first place, so it treats the symptoms rather than the cause.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.b] In the 1970s and 1980s, cheap lending turned into a debt crisis for many developing countries. Each step below made repayment harder.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.0] 1. When oil prices rose sharply, oil-exporting countries deposited their surplus money in banks. Banks lent these deposits, called petrodollars, to developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.1] 2. Interest rates then rose sharply and commodity prices fell. Borrowers earned less from their exports while owing more, so many could not afford repayments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.2] 3. Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts, starting the Latin American debt crisis.
[geo-economic-development-s4.8.3] 4. Many African and Latin American countries could not repay their debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.b] Heavy debt took money away from the people it was meant to help, and in some cases the people repaying had never benefited from the loan.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.0] Governments spent more on debt repayments than on health and education, so public services suffered.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.1] Countries took new loans to repay old ones, so the debt grew.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.2] The 1980s 'lost decade' hit Latin America and Africa hard, because debt blocked progress in those years.
[geo-economic-development-s4.9.3] Some debts were 'odious': dictators borrowed the money and stole or wasted it, yet the people were made to repay it. This is the clearest justice argument against debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.b] Lenders did not simply hand over money. The IMF (International Monetary Fund) and the World Bank attached conditions to their loans, which is different from tied aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.0] IMF and World Bank loans came with conditions. These cut public spending, introduced school and health fees, privatised state firms (sold them to private owners), removed food subsidies, devalued the currency (lowered its value against others) and opened markets to imports.
[geo-economic-development-s4.10.1] Structural adjustment programmes, as these packages of conditions were called, were criticised for hurting the poorest people most. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.b] Campaigns and lenders eventually cancelled some debt for the poorest countries, with conditions. Relief had real results, but debt is building up again.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.0] The Jubilee 2000 campaign pressed for debt cancellation by the millennium, so that poor countries could stop repaying old debts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.1] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative was launched in 1996 by the IMF and World Bank.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.2] The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005) followed the G8 summit (a meeting of leaders of major rich countries) at Gleneagles, Scotland, in July 2005. It was backed by the Make Poverty History campaign and the Live 8 concerts.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.3] More than 30 countries, most in Africa, have received debt relief under HIPC, on condition that the savings go to poverty reduction.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.4] Relief has funded results such as free primary education in some relieved countries, so more children are in school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.11.5] Debt is rising again in the 2020s, so relief has not ended the problem.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.b] Aid is help given to poorer countries. It comes in different forms, depending on its purpose, who gives it and how it is channelled.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.0] Emergency (humanitarian) aid responds to disasters and famines, so it is short-term and urgent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.1] Long-term development aid funds projects that build lasting capacity, such as a country's ability to meet its own needs.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.2] Bilateral aid passes from one government to another, so the donor government decides directly where it goes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.3] Multilateral aid is channelled through bodies such as the UN, the World Bank and the EU, so several donors pool their money.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.4] NGO (voluntary) aid is given by non-governmental organisations, which are charities independent of governments.
[geo-economic-development-s4.12.5] Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, so it benefits the donor as well as the receiver.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.b] The UN has set a target for how much rich countries should give, and most do not meet it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.0] The UN target, set in 1970, is that rich countries give 0.7% of their gross national income (the total income earned by a country's people and firms) as official development aid.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.1] Norway, Sweden, Luxembourg and Denmark have reached or exceeded the target in recent years, so only a few countries reach it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.13.2] Ireland has not yet reached the target (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.b] Aid is neither all good nor all bad. A balanced answer gives both sides, with reasons, and ends with what better aid looks like.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.0] Helps: aid saves lives in emergencies and funds schools, clinics, wells and vaccination programmes.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.1] Helps: aid builds roads and infrastructure, trains teachers, nurses and farmers, and supports women's groups and small businesses.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.2] Hinders: tied aid benefits donor firms and may supply unsuitable technology, because the receiver must buy what the donor sells.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.3] Hinders: corrupt elites can steal aid, and food aid can undercut local farmers, who cannot compete with free food.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.4] Hinders: aid can create dependency instead of self-reliance, and loans add to debt.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.5] Hinders: donors' political or strategic interests decide who gets aid, rather than need.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.6] Hinders: large prestige projects, such as dams, can displace people.
[geo-economic-development-s4.14.7] Better aid is untied (not required to be spent in the donor country), targeted at the poorest, and designed with local participation and ownership. It supports self-help and co-operatives and is transparent.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.b] Ireland gives aid through its government programme and through voluntary organisations, many of them founded in response to particular crises.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.0] Irish Aid is Ireland's official aid programme, part of the Department of Foreign Affairs. Its bilateral programme (government to government) began in 1974.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.1] Long-term partner countries include Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia in sub-Saharan Africa, and Vietnam.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.2] Ireland's aid is untied. 'A Better World' (2019), Ireland's international development policy, focuses on the furthest behind, gender equality, climate and governance.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.3] Concern Worldwide was founded in 1968 in response to the famine in Biafra during Nigeria's civil war.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.4] Trócaire was founded in 1973 by the Irish Catholic bishops. Its Lenten 'Trócaire box' raises funds, and it campaigns on human rights and climate.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.5] GOAL was founded in 1977 by John O'Shea.
[geo-economic-development-s4.15.6] Live Aid concerts in July 1985, organised by Bob Geldof, responded to the 1984-85 Ethiopian famine.
[geo-economic-development-s4.16.b] Ireland's past gives it a reason to care about hunger elsewhere.
[geo-economic-development-s4.16.0] The Great Famine (1845-49) killed about 1 million people and led about 1 million to emigrate. This history is one reason for Irish solidarity (support and fellow feeling) with developing countries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.b] Multinationals (companies operating in many countries) move work to where labour is cheapest. This brings jobs, but it can also bring exploitation.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.0] Multinationals locate labour-intensive work (work needing many workers), such as clothing, electronics assembly and mining, in low-wage countries, because wage costs are lowest there.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.1] Benefits for workers include jobs, wages higher than farm work, and skills.
[geo-economic-development-s4.17.2] Exploitation includes very low wages, long hours, unsafe factories, banned trade unions (organisations of workers) and child labour.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.b] The collapse of Rana Plaza shows what can happen when global brands rely on cheap suppliers whose safety standards are not checked.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.0] Rana Plaza, an eight-storey building in Savar near Dhaka, housed garment factories. It collapsed on 24 April 2013, killing about 1,130 people and injuring about 2,500.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.1] Cracks had appeared the day before, but workers were ordered back in.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.2] The factories supplied Western brands, including Primark, which was founded in Dublin in 1969 as Penneys. Primark paid compensation to the victims.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.3] The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh was signed in May 2013 by global brands and trade unions, and eventually more than 200 brands joined. It provided independent factory inspections.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.4] Garments make up over four-fifths of Bangladesh's exports (2020s), so the country depends heavily on this industry.
[geo-economic-development-s4.18.5] The case shows how brands profit from low-cost factories while the safety of workers depends on local suppliers.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.b] Child labour is one of the worst forms of exploitation, and it appears in the supply chains of everyday products.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.0] About 160 million children were in child labour worldwide in 2020 (ILO and UNICEF), about 70% of them in agriculture.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.1] Child labour occurs in cocoa farming and in mining, for example, so everyday products such as chocolate and batteries can be linked to it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.2] The DR Congo produces about 70% of the world's cobalt, which is used in rechargeable batteries.
[geo-economic-development-s4.19.3] Amnesty International (2016) documented child labour in the artisanal (hand-dug) cobalt mines of the DR Congo.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.b] Exploitation also involves land. Poor countries and poor communities can lose the land they depend on.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.0] Foreign companies and governments buy or lease large areas of farmland in poor countries, a practice called land grabbing.
[geo-economic-development-s4.20.1] Indigenous peoples (the original inhabitants of a region) lose land to mining, ranching and logging, as in the Amazon example in the Brazil section of Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.b] Pressure to improve conditions comes from consumers, from the law and from workers themselves.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.0] Ethical consumer campaigns and company codes of conduct (a company's own rules for how suppliers must treat workers) put pressure on brands.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.1] The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, adopted in 2024, requires very large companies to identify and address human-rights and environmental harm in their supply chains.
[geo-economic-development-s4.21.2] Trade unions represent workers and campaign for safer conditions and better wages.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.b] Climate change is an injustice because the countries that caused it least suffer most from it.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.0] The poorest countries have contributed least to greenhouse gas emissions (the gases that cause global warming) but suffer most from climate change.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.1] Bangladesh is a low-lying delta vulnerable to cyclones, floods and sea-level rise, because so much of its land is barely above the sea.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.2] Small island states such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives are threatened by sea-level rise, because their land is low.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.3] Sub-Saharan Africa has produced only a small share of historical emissions, yet its dry regions suffer drought.
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.4] Responses include climate finance (money from rich countries to help poorer ones cope) and the Loss and Damage Fund, agreed at COP27 in Sharm el-Sheikh, Egypt (2022) and made operational at COP28 in Dubai (2023).
[geo-economic-development-s4.22.5] The Mary Robinson Foundation - Climate Justice was set up in 2010 by Mary Robinson, who was President of Ireland from 1990 to 1997 and UN High Commissioner for Human Rights from 1997 to 2002.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.b] A justice evaluation weighs real progress against the problems that remain.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.0] Extreme poverty has fallen greatly since 1990, especially in China. Child mortality has fallen, and more children attend school.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.1] Sub-Saharan Africa lags behind other regions.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.2] Inequality within and between countries is high, so gains are not shared equally.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.3] Conflict, such as the war in Sudan, and climate change are reversing gains. There is more on this in Case study of a developing economy.
[geo-economic-development-s4.23.4] The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 caused the first rise in global extreme poverty in about a generation (World Bank).
[geo-economic-development-s4.24.b] Justice is not only for governments. Individuals and Ireland can act.
[geo-economic-development-s4.24.0] Individuals can buy fair trade, campaign, and support NGOs. Ireland can press governments on aid, trade and debt policy.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-globalisation-s3: 3. Patterns in world trade
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.b] Trade is the buying and selling of goods and services between countries. Exports leave a country and imports arrive in it, and data questions use these words constantly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.0] Visible trade is goods that can be seen and counted, such as cars, food and computer chips.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.1] Invisible trade is services, which cannot be held or counted in the same way, such as software, financial services, tourism and aircraft leasing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.2] Balance of trade: the value of exports minus the value of imports, which gives one number that shows whether a country sells more than it buys.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.3] A trade surplus means exports are greater than imports; a trade deficit means imports are greater than exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.b] The balance of trade is only part of the picture of a country's dealings with the world. The balance of payments is the wider record.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.0] Balance of payments: the record of all money flows between a country and the rest of the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.1] It includes trade in goods and services, plus investment flows (money put into businesses abroad), profits sent home, borrowing and transfers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.2] It is a wider measure than the balance of trade, which records only exports and imports, so the two are not the same thing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.b] A country that keeps buying more from abroad than it sells pays a price at home, while exports bring benefits. Each effect follows from where the money goes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.0] A persistent trade deficit (imports greater than exports year after year) means money flows out of the country to pay for the extra imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.1] The country may have to borrow or sell assets (things it owns, such as businesses or property) to pay for those imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.2] Home industries and jobs can be lost as cheaper imports replace domestic production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.3] The currency (the country's money) may weaken when a country persistently imports more than it exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.4] Exports bring jobs, tax revenue, foreign earnings and economic growth, because foreign buyers pay money into the country.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.b] A country that sells to many different countries is safer than one that sells mainly to one. This is the advantage of many export partners.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.0] A country with many export partners is less dependent on any one market.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.1] A recession (a fall in economic activity), tariff or crisis in one country, such as the US tariffs imposed in 2025, then does less damage to its overall trade, because the other markets keep buying.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.b] Governments can open their markets to imports or restrict them. Protectionism is the restricting approach, and international bodies try to set common rules.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.0] Protectionism restricts imports to protect home industries from foreign competition, so home producers face less pressure from cheaper foreign goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.1] Barriers to trade include tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on the quantity imported), subsidies (payments that help home producers compete with imports), and regulations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.2] Trade blocs (groups of countries that agree trade rules among themselves) remove barriers between their own members, so members trade with each other more easily than with outsiders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.3] GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade) was signed in 1947 to reduce tariffs between countries, so goods cost less to trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.4] The World Trade Organisation (WTO) was set up on 1 January 1995, with headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland; it has over 160 member countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.5] The WTO sets the rules of world trade and settles disputes between members, so countries have a common referee.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.6] Fair trade gives producers in developing countries a guaranteed minimum price and a premium (an extra payment), shown by the Fairtrade mark.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.b] World trade has grown much faster than world output since 1950, and most countries now depend on imports and exports. Several changes made this possible.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.0] Falling trade barriers, through GATT and the WTO, made it cheaper to trade across borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.1] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard-sized steel boxes) began with the first container ship voyage, the Ideal X of Malcolm McLean, USA, in 1956; containers made loading and shipping faster and cheaper.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.2] Shanghai, China, has been the world's busiest container port since 2010.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.3] Air freight and information and communication technology (ICT) speed up trade in high-value goods and services, for example by moving them quickly and sharing information instantly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.4] Multinational companies (MNCs, firms that operate in many countries) trade components between their own plants in different countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.5] The entry of China and Eastern Europe into world trade added large new producers and markets, so there were more goods to sell and more buyers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.b] The three triad areas and the rest of the world are linked by shipping routes and by products whose parts come from many countries. Because they are so linked, a disruption in one place spreads worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.0] One product links many countries: the iPhone and Intel chip supply chains (the series of countries and firms that make a product's parts and assemble it) each pass through several countries. There is more on this in One multinational company (MNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.1] The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, and the Panama Canal, opened in 1914, are chokepoints (narrow passages that much shipping must use) on the shipping routes linking the triad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.2] In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given blocked the Suez Canal for about six days, holding up hundreds of ships.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.3] From late 2023 attacks on shipping in the Red Sea forced many ships to sail around the Cape of Good Hope, Africa.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.4] The 2008-09 global financial crisis caused world trade to fall sharply in 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.5] COVID-19 (2020-21) disrupted supply chains, for example in the 2021 chip shortage.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.b] Most of the world's trade and production is concentrated in three areas. The trading triad is North America, Europe and the Pacific Rim, the three core economic areas of the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.0] The triad produces most of the world's manufactured goods, such as cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.1] It holds the headquarters of most multinational companies (MNCs), so company decisions are made there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.2] Most world trade takes place among the three areas, and most foreign direct investment (money that firms invest in businesses and factories in other countries) flows between them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.b] The USA is the triad's North American member and the dominant economy within it. Its trade is shaped by its size, its trade bloc and its use of tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.0] The USA is the world's largest economy and its largest importer, so its demand matters to exporters everywhere.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.1] It has had a trade deficit in goods every year since the mid-1970s, which means it imports more goods than it exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.2] NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement: USA, Canada, Mexico) took effect on 1 January 1994.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.3] USMCA (United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement) replaced NAFTA on 1 July 2020, so the three countries kept a trade bloc.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.4] Mexico overtook China in 2023 as the largest source of US imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.5] From 2018 the USA put tariffs (taxes on imports) on many Chinese goods in the US-China trade war.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.6] In 2025 the USA raised tariffs on imports from most countries, which raised the price of goods entering the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.b] Europe is one of the three triad areas, and it trades as a bloc. The EU is a union of countries that trade freely with one another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.0] The EU is a single market (members trade as if in one market) of 27 member states, since the UK left the EU.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.1] The single market began on 1 January 1993, removing barriers to trade between members, so goods move freely between them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.2] The EU is the largest trading bloc by value, with strong trade with the USA and China, for example in goods sold between the blocs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.3] The euro, the shared currency, was introduced in 1999; euro notes and coins followed in 2002.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.4] In July 2025 the EU and USA agreed a 15% US tariff on most EU goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.b] In the triad, the Pacific Rim means mainly East and South-East Asia, together with Australia. India is not part of it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.0] Members include, for example, Japan, China, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, the ASEAN countries and Australia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.1] ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) was founded in 1967 as a group of South-East Asian countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.2] APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) was founded in 1989.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.3] RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership) has 15 Asia-Pacific members, including China, Japan, South Korea, Australia and the ASEAN members.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.4] RCEP was signed in November 2020 and came into force from 2022; its members hold about 30% of the world's population and GDP (the total value of goods and services a country produces in a year).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.5] The Asian Tigers are South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, fast-growing Asian economies. China and Japan are not Tigers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.b] Japan is a Pacific Rim economy that grew after the Second World War by exporting manufactured goods. Its story shows how a country with few raw materials can grow by selling manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.0] After the Second World War Japan's growth was based on exporting manufactured goods, mainly cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.1] Japan has few raw materials, so it must import most of its oil, coal and iron ore.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.2] Japan was the world's second-largest economy from the late 1960s until China overtook it in 2010; Germany overtook Japan in 2023.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.3] Toyota, based in Toyota City, uses just-in-time production, which delivers parts as they are needed rather than storing them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.4] Other major Japanese companies are, for example, Honda, Sony and Nissan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.5] To avoid trade barriers, Japanese firms opened factories inside the EU and USA; Nissan's car plant at Sunderland, north-east England, opened in 1986, inside the European Economic Community (EEC, the EU's earlier name).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.6] Japan's growth has been slower since the 1990s, so it is no longer expanding as quickly as it did after the war.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.b] China moved from a closed economy to the world's largest exporter of goods, which is why it is called the workshop of the world. Government reforms opened it to trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.0] Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms, from 1978, opened China's closed economy to foreign trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.1] The first Special Economic Zones (SEZs, areas with special rules to attract foreign firms) were set up in 1980, including Shenzhen beside Hong Kong.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.2] Shenzhen is in mainland China and is not part of Hong Kong; it grew from a small town to over 17 million people (2020 census).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.3] China joined the WTO on 11 December 2001, which gave it a place under world trade rules.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.4] China has been the world's largest exporter of goods since 2009, and it overtook Japan as the second-largest economy in 2010.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.5] China runs a huge trade surplus, because it sells so much abroad; meaning its exports greatly exceed its imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.6] The Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, links China to Asia, Africa and Europe through ports, railways and roads.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.7] The trade war with the USA, begun in 2018, also affects China; the US side is in the North America notes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.b] South Korea is the standard example of a newly industrialised country (NIC, a country that has recently built a large manufacturing economy). It went from poverty to high income by selling manufactured goods abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.0] South Korea was war-damaged by the Korean War (1950-53) and was one of the world's poorest countries in the early 1960s.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.1] Under President Park Chung-hee (1961-79) it industrialised through exports and rose from poverty to high income.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.2] Its economy is dominated by chaebol, family-controlled business groups such as Samsung (founded 1938), Hyundai, LG and SK.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.3] Ulsan is home to Hyundai's shipyards, among the world's largest, and to a Hyundai car plant, as an example of an industrial city built on exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.4] South Korea is a world leader in shipbuilding, cars, electronics and memory chips, and Samsung makes smartphones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.5] South Korea is one of the Asian Tigers and joined the OECD (the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) in 1996.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.14.b] Not every region shares in the triad's trade. Some areas are left on the edges of world trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.14.0] Africa, much of Latin America and South Asia hold only a small share of world trade, mostly in raw materials. There is more on this in How the global economy is organised.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.b] An international division of labour means that countries and regions specialise in different stages of production and different types of work. The first pattern grew out of colonial rule.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.0] In the old, colonial pattern, colonies and developing countries exported raw materials and food, while Europe and later the USA manufactured goods and sold them back.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.1] Sudan's Gezira Scheme, from 1925, grew irrigated cotton for the British (Lancashire) textile mills.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.2] India exported raw cotton and tea to Britain under colonial rule, as an example of raw materials and food leaving a colony.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.3] Ghana (then the Gold Coast) exported cocoa; Cote d'Ivoire and Ghana together grow over half of the world's cocoa today, still mostly exported unprocessed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.b] From the 1960s-70s the pattern changed. Multinational companies began to split production, placing each stage where it suited them best.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.0] Multinational companies moved assembly and routine manufacturing to newly industrialised countries (NICs) and low-wage countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.1] The core (the rich, powerful economies) kept headquarters, research and development (R&D, work on new products and technology), design, finance and marketing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.2] The periphery (the poorer countries on the edge of the world economy) still supplies raw materials.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.3] As an example, Apple's packaging carried the line 'Designed by Apple in California. Assembled in China.'
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.4] Intel's R&D is at Hillsboro, Oregon; its fabs (chip factories) include Leixlip in Ireland; assembly and test take place in Malaysia and Vietnam. There is more on this in One multinational company (MNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.b] The pattern is not fixed. Services are now divided between countries, and countries move up or spread to new places.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.0] Services are now divided too: Bengaluru (Bangalore), India, is an IT services hub for firms such as Infosys, Wipro and Tata Consultancy Services, and IT and call centres are located in India.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.1] China is moving into high-value products instead of only low-cost assembly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.2] Low-cost assembly is spreading to Vietnam, Bangladesh and India, because firms look for cheaper places to assemble.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.b] Ireland is a very open economy: its exports are larger than its GDP. It has moved from supplying food to the UK to hosting high-value manufacturing and services by multinational companies (MNCs).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.0] Goods exports passed 200 billion euro in 2024 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office), a record.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.1] Most of this export value is produced by MNC plants, not by Irish-owned firms, so large exports reflect MNC production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.2] Main goods exports: pharmaceuticals and chemicals (the largest category), medical devices (a cluster at Galway, with firms such as Medtronic and Boston Scientific), computer chips and electronics, and food and drink (beef, dairy and whiskey).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.3] Main services exports: computer services (Ireland is one of the world's largest exporters of them), financial services, aircraft leasing and tourism.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.4] Irish-based lessors (companies that own aircraft and lease them to airlines) manage over half of the world's leased commercial aircraft, from Dublin and Shannon.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.5] Ireland has a large goods trade surplus, because it exports far more goods than it imports, the largest of it with the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.b] Where Irish goods go reflects who makes them. The main market is the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.0] The USA is the largest single market for Irish goods, taking about a third of them, which makes it Ireland's biggest buyer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.1] Other main markets, such as EU partners Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands, then the UK and China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.2] US pharmaceutical and technology MNCs export from their Irish plants back to the USA and worldwide, which is why the USA dominates.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.3] In early 2025 Irish exports to the USA rose sharply as US buyers stockpiled medicines (bought extra in advance) before new tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.b] Ireland's trade with the UK changed after the UK left the EU. Brexit is the usual reason to give.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.0] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition period (when the old trade rules still applied) ended on 31 December 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.1] Brexit brought customs checks (inspections of goods at the border) on trade with the UK and took the UK out of the EU single market.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.2] A weaker pound makes Irish goods dearer for UK buyers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.3] Irish firms have diversified into other markets, so they depend less on the UK.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.b] Ireland's export success depends on a narrow base, which brings risk.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.0] Ireland depends on a few US MNCs and on the US market, so a problem for those firms or that market hits Irish exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.1] US tariffs from 2025 expose Irish exports to the USA to new barriers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.b] Data-response questions give charts and tables of trade and ask you to calculate. The method is the same every time.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.0] Read values from the chart or table and state their units before calculating.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.1] Balance of trade for one country: subtract imports from exports; a negative result is a trade deficit.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.2] Difference between two countries: subtract the smaller value from the larger, so the answer is positive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.3] Total exports: add the values for every partner, so none is missed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.4] Use a bar chart to compare values between countries and a line graph to show change over time, because each suits that kind of data.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s3: 3. Environmental pollution
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.b] Pollution is a problem caused by people, and it can be small or enormous in scale. Knowing how to define it, classify it and place it on a scale lets you tackle any pollution question.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.0] Pollution is the release by human activity of substances or energy (heat, noise, light) into the environment in amounts that harm living things, health or property. Harm is what makes a release pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.1] Types of pollution: air, water (fresh and marine), land and soil, noise and visual. For example, oil in the sea is water pollution and a motorway's roar is noise pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.2] Scales: local (one river or town), national (a country's water quality) and global (the whole atmosphere or ocean). The scale tells you how many people and places are affected.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.3] Transboundary pollution (pollution that crosses national borders) means the place it harms may be far from its source. Acid rain, rain made acidic by gases from burning fuels, is the European example, taught in the note Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.b] Pollution can be traced back to its source in two ways, depending on whether it leaves from one spot or from many. This matters because a single outlet is easier to find and control than thousands of small ones.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.0] Point sources come from one identifiable place, such as a factory pipe or a sewage outfall (the pipe where sewage enters a river or the sea).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.1] Diffuse sources come from many places at once, such as runoff (rainwater washing substances off the land) from many farms, or traffic.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.2] Economic activities that pollute include agriculture, manufacturing, energy generation, transport, mining, tourism, households and urban waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.b] Global warming is the main global-scale example of pollution. It starts with a natural process that human activity has strengthened.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.0] The natural greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases (gases in the atmosphere that trap heat) let incoming short-wave solar radiation through but absorb outgoing long-wave radiation (heat given off by the earth), which keeps the earth habitable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.1] The enhanced greenhouse effect: human activity adds greenhouse gases to the atmosphere, which trap more heat and so warm the planet further.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.b] Each greenhouse gas has its own human sources, and many of them are economic activities. Knowing which activity gives which gas lets you explain the causes of warming.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.0] CO2 (carbon dioxide) comes from burning fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas), deforestation (clearing forests, which removes trees that absorb CO2) and cement production.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.1] Methane comes from cattle and sheep, rice paddies, landfill (rubbish buried in the ground) and gas leaks.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.2] Nitrous oxide comes from nitrogen fertilisers and slurry (liquid animal manure).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.3] CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons, man-made gases) and other fluorinated gases are also greenhouse gases.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.b] Scientists measure warming in several independent ways, and they all point the same way. Each measurement below is a piece of evidence you can quote.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.0] Atmospheric CO2 was about 280 ppm (parts per million) before the Industrial Revolution. It passed 400 ppm in the mid-2010s and was over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s. These readings are measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, which has recorded them since 1958.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.1] Global average surface temperature has risen by about 1.1 to 1.2 °C since the late 1800s (IPCC, the UN's climate science panel).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.2] 2024 was the warmest year on record and the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels (Copernicus, the EU's Earth observation programme).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.3] Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.4] Global sea level rose by about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.5] Glaciers (large masses of ice on land) are retreating worldwide, which is a visible sign that temperatures are rising.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.b] A warmer atmosphere changes oceans, ice, weather and ecosystems, and these changes harm people and wildlife. The effects are worst for places that are low-lying or already vulnerable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.0] Sea-level rise, from thermal expansion of seawater (water expands as it warms) and melting land ice, threatens low-lying countries and deltas: Bangladesh (the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta), the Maldives, Tuvalu and Kiribati.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.1] More intense heatwaves, droughts, floods and storms. The Sahel (the dry belt south of the Sahara) has suffered droughts.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.2] Glacier retreat threatens water supplies, because many places depend on meltwater from glaciers.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.3] Coral bleaching (corals losing their colour and dying when seas get too warm): the Great Barrier Reef, Australia, has suffered repeated mass bleaching since the late 1990s.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.4] Species shifts and extinctions, crop failures in vulnerable regions, and climate refugees (people forced to leave their homes because of climate change).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.b] Ireland is not exempt from global warming. Its temperature has risen, and the effects are felt on land, at the coast and at sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.0] Ireland's average temperature has risen by about 0.9 °C since 1900 (EPA and Met Éireann, the national weather service).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.1] Warmer temperatures bring wetter winters and more flooding, a risk of summer drought, stronger storms and coastal erosion (the sea wearing away the coast).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.2] Effects also include changes to farming and to marine species, and a threat to low-lying coastal towns.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.3] Storm Éowyn, in January 2025, caused record damage and power cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.b] Ireland's emissions come mainly from a few sectors, and farming dominates. This is why so many Irish climate measures target agriculture.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.0] Agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, the largest share, mainly methane and nitrous oxide from cattle, slurry and fertiliser.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.1] Transport, mainly fuel burned by vehicles, is the next largest source, at about a fifth of emissions (EPA, early 2020s), followed by energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.2] Ireland's emissions per person are high for an EU country, which is largely because of its large farming sector.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.b] Warming is a global problem, so governments have tried to answer it through international agreements. Each one built on the one before.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.0] The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) was agreed at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in 1992.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.1] The Kyoto Protocol, agreed in 1997 and in force from 2005, set legally binding cuts for developed countries only. The USA never ratified it (it signed but did not formally approve it), so it was not a full party.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.2] The Paris Agreement, 2015, aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C. Each country sets its own national plan (nationally determined contribution).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.3] Countries meet every year at COP meetings (conferences of the countries that signed the UNFCCC). COP30 was held at Belém, Brazil, in November 2025.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.b] The EU and Ireland have turned the global agreements into laws, targets and charges. Together they put a price on emissions and set deadlines for cutting them.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.0] The EU Emissions Trading System began in 2005. Power stations and heavy industry must hold an allowance (a permit) for each tonne of CO2 they emit, which gives them a cost reason to cut emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.1] The European Green Deal (2019) aims for a climate-neutral EU by 2050 (no net emissions). Its Fit for 55 package aims to cut net emissions by at least 55% by 2030, compared with 1990.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.2] Ireland's Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 requires a 51% cut in emissions by 2030, compared with 2018, and net zero by 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.3] Sectoral ceilings (limits on each sector's emissions, set in 2022) require agriculture to cut its emissions by 25% by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.4] An Irish carbon tax (a charge on fuels based on their CO2) was introduced in 2010 and is rising in stages towards €100 a tonne by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.5] The Climate Action Plan aims to retrofit (upgrade with insulation and heating) 500,000 homes to a B2 energy rating by 2030, and includes renewables, electric vehicles and measures for agriculture.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.b] Governments cannot cut emissions alone, because homes, transport and food are all household choices. Everyday changes add up.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.0] Improve energy efficiency and insulate homes, so less fuel is burned for heating.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.1] Use public transport, cycling and walking in place of car journeys, so fewer fossil fuels are burned.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.2] Reduce food waste, because rotting food in landfill gives off methane.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.3] Switch to renewable heating, because it does not burn fossil fuels such as oil and gas, so it gives off less CO2.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.b] Agricultural water pollution is the Irish national case study of water pollution. Nutrients meant to grow crops end up in rivers and lakes, where they set off a chain of events called eutrophication.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.0] Cause: nitrogen and phosphorus from slurry, manure and chemical fertilisers wash off fields into rivers and lakes, especially after heavy rain or when spread on wet or frozen ground. Silage effluent (liquid from stored grass) and sewage from towns and septic tanks (private sewage tanks) add more.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.1] Eutrophication (water becoming overloaded with nutrients): the nutrients feed rapid algal growth, forming algal blooms (dense layers of algae).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.2] When the algae die, bacteria decomposing them use up the dissolved oxygen, so fish and other life suffocate in fish kills.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.3] The water turns green and smells. Some blue-green algae are toxic.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.b] Polluted water harms wildlife first, and then the people and businesses that rely on clean water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.0] Loss of fish and other wildlife, such as in a fish kill, and damage to angling and tourism, because few people visit or fish in green, smelly water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.1] Drinking water needs costly treatment, and nitrates (nitrogen compounds from fertiliser) contaminate groundwater (water held underground).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.2] Coastal waters, including estuaries (where rivers meet the sea), are affected by algal mats, because rivers carry the nutrients downstream.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.b] The EPA, which monitors water quality, shows that pollution is a national problem and not only a local one. Farming is the main pressure.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.0] An EPA assessment for 2016 to 2021 found that only about half of Ireland's rivers, lakes and estuaries were in good or high ecological status.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.1] Agriculture is the most common pressure on water quality, followed by urban wastewater (sewage from towns), because fertiliser and slurry wash into streams.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.2] Milk quotas (limits on how much milk farms could produce) were abolished on 1 April 2015. The dairy herd then grew strongly, especially in the south and east, and raised nitrogen levels there.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.b] Lough Neagh is a real example of eutrophication, and the 2023 bloom shows the processes above in action. Treat it as a single event, and do not claim it has been solved.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.0] Lough Neagh, Northern Ireland, is the largest lake on the island of Ireland. In summer 2023 a huge toxic blue-green algal bloom covered much of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.1] The bloom was blamed on nutrient pollution from farm runoff, sewage and septic tanks, together with warm weather and invasive zebra mussels that clear the water, letting more light reach the algae.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.2] Lough Neagh supplies a large share of Northern Ireland's drinking water, so a toxic bloom threatens a key water supply.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.b] Reducing the pollution means keeping nutrients on the land and out of water. Most of the rules limit when, where and how much farmers spread.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.0] The EU Nitrates Directive (1991) and Ireland's Nitrates Action Programme set closed periods each winter when slurry and fertiliser may not be spread, because rain would wash them straight into rivers.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.1] The Nitrates Action Programme also limits nitrogen per hectare, requires slurry storage, and requires buffer strips (strips of vegetation) beside watercourses, which trap nutrients before they reach the water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.2] The EU Water Framework Directive (2000) aims for good status for all waters, which gives countries a target to work towards.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.3] Other measures: upgrading urban wastewater treatment, EPA monitoring of water quality, and farm advisory programmes.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.16.b] Oil in the sea comes from accidents and from deliberate or careless releases. It is a global pollution problem because oil is moved and drilled for all over the world.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.16.0] Tanker accidents, drilling blow-outs (uncontrolled releases of oil from a well), illegal tank washing at sea, and pipeline leaks release oil into the sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.b] The Exxon Valdez is a tanker accident that caused huge damage to a wild coast and led to a change in law. Remember it is in Alaska, not the Gulf of Mexico.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.0] The tanker ran aground on Bligh Reef, Prince William Sound, Alaska, on 24 March 1989.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.1] About 11 million US gallons (about 37,000 tonnes) of crude oil were spilled, and about 2,000 km of coastline was oiled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.2] Hundreds of thousands of seabirds and thousands of sea otters were killed, and the herring fishery collapsed.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.3] The response was the US Oil Pollution Act 1990, which required double-hulled tankers (tankers with two layers of steel, so a hit is less likely to leak) in US waters.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.b] The Prestige shows that a tanker accident can damage the coasts of several countries and the fishing that depends on them.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.0] The single-hulled tanker Prestige broke in two and sank off Galicia, north-west Spain, in November 2002. It spilled tens of thousands of tonnes of heavy fuel oil.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.1] Oil reached the Costa da Morte and the coasts of Spain, Portugal and France. Galician shellfish and fishing industries were closed for months.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.2] The disaster sped up the EU's phase-out of single-hulled tankers, because a single hull is more likely to leak after damage.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.b] Deepwater Horizon is an example of oil pollution from drilling, and not from a tanker. It is in the Gulf of Mexico.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.0] The Deepwater Horizon rig (an offshore drilling platform), operated by BP, exploded on 20 April 2010 in the Gulf of Mexico. Eleven workers were killed.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.1] About 4.9 million barrels of oil were spilled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.b] Whatever the cause, oil spills have similar effects, and the same responses are used in each. Know the effects first, then the clean-up and the prevention.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.0] Effects: oiled seabirds and mammals, smothered shorelines, fishing bans and lost tourism, because beaches and catches are unusable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.1] Clean-up: booms (floating barriers) to contain the oil, skimmers (machines that scoop oil off the surface) to remove it, and dispersants (chemicals) to break it up.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.2] Prevention: double-hulled tankers, and routing ships away from coasts, so that any accident happens far from the shore.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.b] Plastic is cheap and lasts a very long time, which is why it is a problem once it is thrown away. It pollutes at local and global scale.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.0] Causes: single-use packaging that is thrown away after one use, litter, illegal dumping and landfill (burying rubbish in the ground).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.1] Plastics take hundreds of years to break down, and they fragment into microplastics (tiny pieces of plastic).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.2] Effects: litter in towns and countryside, and wildlife entangled in or eating plastic, which can injure or kill animals.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.3] Ocean garbage patches: the Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a large area of the North Pacific where floating plastic collects in an ocean gyre (a large circling ocean current).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.4] Microplastics enter food chains, as animals eat them. Landfill produces leachate (polluted liquid that drains from rubbish) and methane.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.b] Ireland has tried to cut plastic and waste by putting a price on it, by rewarding returns, and by ranking how waste should be handled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.0] The plastic bag levy (a charge on each bag) was introduced in March 2002 at 15 cent a bag, raised to 22 cent in 2007. Plastic bag use fell by over 90% almost at once, and the money funds environmental projects.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.1] The Deposit Return Scheme (Re-turn) began on 1 February 2024 for plastic drinks bottles and cans.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.2] The waste hierarchy ranks reduce, reuse, recycle, recover and dispose. The EPA licenses waste facilities.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.3] The Dublin Waste-to-Energy incinerator at Poolbeg (a plant that burns waste to make energy) has operated since 2017.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.b] Ozone depletion is a different problem from global warming, with a different cause and a different treaty. It is also a success story.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.0] CFCs, from aerosols, fridges and air conditioning, destroy stratospheric ozone (the ozone in the upper atmosphere), which absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.1] British Antarctic Survey scientists reported the Antarctic ozone hole (a thin area in the ozone layer over Antarctica) in 1985.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.2] More ultraviolet radiation causes skin cancer and eye cataracts, and damages plankton and crops.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.3] The Montreal Protocol, agreed in 1987, phased out CFCs. Every UN member state has ratified it, and it is often cited as the most successful global environmental agreement.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.4] The Antarctic ozone layer is expected to recover to 1980 levels around the 2060s (UN assessment, 2023).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.b] Pollution also occurs closer to home, from traffic, old mines and large structures. Each of these is a local example.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.0] Air pollution in towns: traffic produces nitrogen dioxide and fine particles. Home burning of solid fuel adds smoke, which is why smoke-free zones exist in some towns (see the note Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources for smoke-free zones).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.1] Mining pollution: acid mine drainage (acidic water flowing out of old mines) from old copper mines at Avoca, Co. Wicklow, discolours and pollutes the Avoca River.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.2] Noise and visual pollution (unwanted sound and ugly views) from airports, motorways, quarries, wind farms and pylons.
