[geo-option-geoecology-s5.0.b] A biome (a large natural community of plants and animals, shaped by climate) has a natural state. People have changed almost every one, and the exam asks how.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.0.0] Natural (climax) vegetation is what would grow if the land were left undisturbed. Very little of the earth's land remains untouched, so most biomes now show some human influence.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.0.1] Humans alter biomes by removing, replacing or degrading vegetation, changing soils and water, introducing or removing species, and polluting.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.0.2] The effects reach every part of the system, because the parts are linked: climate, soil, plants, animals and people all change together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.1.b] Before people changed it, Ireland's natural vegetation was forest. The forest spread as the ice retreated.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.1.0] After the last glaciation (the last ice age) ended about 10,000 years ago, Ireland was colonised first by birch and hazel, then by pine, oak, elm and ash.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.1.1] By about 6,000 years ago forest covered almost all of the island. This was the temperate deciduous forest biome (broadleaved trees that lose their leaves in winter).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.2.b] The first big change came when people stopped hunting and gathering and began to farm. Farming needs open land, so forest had to go.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.2.0] Mesolithic hunter-gatherers (the Middle Stone Age, people who lived by hunting and gathering) had little impact on the forest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.2.1] Farming (the Neolithic, the New Stone Age) reached Ireland about 6,000 years ago, about 4000 BC. Farmers cleared forest with stone axes and fire to grow cereals and graze animals. This was the start of human alteration of the Irish biome.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.2.2] A sharp fall in elm pollen (found in layers of old soil and bog) around this time, the elm decline, is seen across Ireland and Britain. Clearance by farmers and elm disease are both thought to have contributed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.3.b] The Céide Fields show what clearance did to Irish soils. Farmland created by clearance was later buried by bog.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.3.0] Neolithic farmers built stone-walled fields here, about 5,500 years ago. The fields are among the oldest known field systems in the world.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.3.1] Blanket bog (a thick peat cover over wide areas of upland and west-coast land) later covered the fields. Forest clearance, a wetter climate and leaching (nutrients washed out of the soil by rain) turned the forest soils into bog, and peat grew over the walls.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.3.2] A local teacher, Patrick Caulfield, found the stone walls under the bog while cutting turf in the 1930s. His son, the archaeologist Séamas Caulfield, later studied the site.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.b] Clearance went on for thousands of years, but the greatest loss of Irish forest came much later, under conquest and trade.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.0] Earlier clearance continued through the Bronze and Iron Ages and in early Christian and medieval farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.1] The great clearance came in the 16th and 17th centuries, during the Tudor conquest and plantations. Woods were felled for timber exports, shipbuilding, barrel staves and charcoal for ironworks.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.2] Woods were also felled to remove cover used by Irish resistance, because forest gave fighters places to hide.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.3] By 1900 Ireland was among the least wooded countries in Europe, with about 1% of the island forested.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.4.4] Forest cover is now about 11%, mostly conifer plantations (cone-bearing evergreen trees grown in rows), so most new forest is not the original woodland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.5.b] Clearance did not just remove trees. It changed the land cover, the wildlife and the soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.5.0] Native deciduous forest was replaced by farmland, hedgerows, bog and grassland, which is why little of the original forest remains.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.5.1] Woodland species declined because their habitat was lost. The wolf was hunted to extinction, the last one generally said to have been killed in the late 1700s.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.5.2] Soils under heath (open land of low shrubs such as heather) were leached and podzolised (made acidic and infertile, with a pale layer where nutrients have washed out), and blanket bog spread across the uplands.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.6.b] Ireland has tried to bring trees back, but the new forests are not the old ones.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.6.0] State afforestation (planting trees on land that had none) has mostly used fast-growing, non-native conifers, chiefly Sitka spruce (native to western North America), because they grow quickly for timber.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.6.1] Sitka plantations form a new, species-poor habitat, with few kinds of plant and animal, and acidify soils and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.6.2] Native woodland survives in remnants, such as the oakwoods of Killarney National Park, Co. Kerry, and the Reenadinna yew wood on the Muckross peninsula, a rare pure yew wood.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.b] Forests around the Mediterranean Sea have been cut since ancient times, so the damage there is a clear European example.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.0] Deforestation since ancient times, for shipbuilding, fuel, farming and goat grazing, degraded the evergreen oak and pine forest to maquis (dense scrub) and garrigue (low, open scrub on thin soils).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.1] Soil erosion on the cleared slopes followed, because tree roots no longer held the soil. There is more on this in A Peripheral European Region: the Mezzogiorno.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.2] The cedars of Lebanon, prized by the ancient Egyptians and Phoenicians for shipbuilding and temples, survive only in small groves, such as the Cedars of God grove, a UNESCO World Heritage site.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.7.3] Global example, Iceland: birch woodland was cleared and grazed after settlement. There is more on this in Human interference with soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.8.b] The Amazon in Brazil is the main case study for felling (cutting down) tropical rainforest. Satellites measure how fast it is being cleared, and the rate has risen and fallen sharply.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.8.0] Brazil's space agency, INPE, measures annual deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon by satellite (the PRODES programme).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.8.1] Clearance peaked at about 27,800 km² in 2004, fell to about 4,600 km² in 2012, rose to about 13,000 km² in 2021, and fell again from 2023 under renewed enforcement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.8.2] Close to a fifth of the original Amazon forest has been cleared, so a large share of the biome is already gone.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.b] Forest is cleared to make money from the land. Farming and timber are the first group of causes, and cattle ranching is the biggest.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.0] Cattle ranching is the largest single cause and accounts for most of the cleared land in the Brazilian Amazon. It is the cause, not soya or logging, to name first.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.1] Soya farming, for example, spread north from Mato Grosso. Much of the soya is exported as animal feed, including to Europe and China, which links the forest to distant demand.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.2] Logging of hardwoods: selective logging (taking only chosen valuable trees) opens roads and gaps in the canopy that lead to further clearance.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.9.3] Government policy and debt, together with global demand for beef, soya, timber and minerals, drive clearance.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.b] Big building projects open the forest up. Roads bring settlers, mines need power and fuel, and dams flood land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.0] Road building and planned settlement: the Trans-Amazonian Highway (BR-230), begun in the early 1970s by the military government, opened the Amazon to settlers from the poor, drought-prone north-east.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.1] BR-364 into Rondônia, paved in the 1980s with World Bank support (the Polonoroeste programme), led to a fishbone pattern of clearings along its side roads, which shows up on satellite images.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.2] Mining: Carajás, Pará state, is one of the world's largest iron-ore mines, operated by Vale and opened in the 1980s. A railway carries the ore to the coast.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.3] Charcoal-fuelled pig-iron smelters (furnaces making crude iron) near Carajás used forest wood, so mining cleared forest beyond the mine itself.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.4] Illegal gold mining (garimpo) on Yanomami land pollutes rivers with mercury.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.5] Hydroelectric dams: the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River, Pará, completed in 2019, is one of the world's largest hydroelectric schemes by capacity. It flooded forest and land, diverted the river past the Volta Grande (Big Bend), and displaced thousands of people.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.10.6] The Kayapó and other indigenous groups strongly opposed Belo Monte. There is more on river impacts in Human interaction: river processes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.b] Removing the trees affects the animals, the soil, the water cycle and the climate. The effects feed into each other.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.0] Loss of habitat and species, many of them endemic to the region (found nowhere else), so animals that depend on the forest lose their homes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.1] Fragmentation of the remaining forest (breaking it into patches) creates edge effects, as the forest edge is drier, hotter and more exposed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.2] The nutrient cycle is broken: cleared latosols (the deep, nutrient-poor red tropical soils) lose their fertility within a few years, and pasture is abandoned.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.3] Soil erosion and a hard laterite crust (an iron-rich layer that bakes hard) form on the exposed land, and rivers silt up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.4] Less evapotranspiration (water given off by plants into the air) means less rainfall downwind and longer dry seasons, and fires become more frequent.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.5] The forest stores huge amounts of carbon; burning and decay release it to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.11.6] Scientists, notably Carlos Nobre and Thomas Lovejoy, have warned that losing about 20 to 25% of the forest could push parts of the Amazon past a tipping point (a threshold after which change cannot easily be reversed) towards degraded savanna.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.12.b] People live in the rainforest, and clearance harms indigenous peoples (the original inhabitants) through loss of land, disease and conflict.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.12.0] Indigenous peoples lose their land, suffer disease, and face violence and conflict with ranchers and miners, for example the Yanomami and the gold miners.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.12.1] Chico Mendes, a rubber tapper and union leader who campaigned to protect the forest, was murdered by a rancher's family at Xapuri, Acre, on 22 December 1988.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.12.2] In 2023 the Brazilian government declared a public health emergency in Yanomami territory after illegal gold mining caused malnutrition and disease.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.b] Brazil has laws, markets and monitoring to slow clearance. How well they work has changed with national policy.

[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.1] The Amazon Soy Moratorium (2006): major traders agreed not to buy soya grown on newly deforested Amazon land, which removes the market for that soya.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.2] The Amazon Fund (2008), with Norway the largest donor, pays for forest protection, so money from abroad supports enforcement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.3] INPE's PRODES annual figures and DETER near-real-time alerts guide enforcement by IBAMA, the federal environment agency, so illegal clearing can be found and acted on.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.4] Protected areas, indigenous territories and extractive reserves, where local people harvest rubber and nuts sustainably, were set up after Chico Mendes's death; the Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve in Acre is one example.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.13.5] Evaluation: the falls and rises in clearance show that protection depends on political will and enforcement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.b] Rainforest in Borneo and Sumatra is a second example. Here the main driver is palm oil, a vegetable oil from oil palm trees.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.0] Indonesia and Malaysia together produce about 85% of the world's palm oil, so these two countries dominate world supply.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.1] Lowland rainforest and peat swamp forest (waterlogged forest growing on thick peat) in Borneo and Sumatra are cleared for oil palm plantations and timber.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.2] Drained and burned peat causes regional haze (smoke pollution), notably in 1997 and 2015, affecting Singapore and Malaysia.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.14.3] The Bornean orangutan was listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN in 2016, making it the flagship threatened species (the animal that stands for the wider loss) of this clearance.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.15.b] European consumers drive some of the clearance, and the EU has begun to act on it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.15.0] Europe's demand for beef, soya (as animal feed), timber and palm oil drives felling.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.15.1] The EU Deforestation Regulation (adopted 2023) will require firms selling beef, soya, palm oil, cocoa, coffee, rubber and timber in the EU to prove they were not grown on land deforested after 2020. Its application has been delayed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.16.b] Intensive farming tries to get as much food as possible from each patch of land. This changes the habitats on and around the farm.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.16.0] Intensive farming uses high inputs of capital (money), machinery, fertiliser, pesticides and water to get maximum output per hectare.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.16.1] It creates monocultures (one crop grown over a large area), larger fields and drained wetlands, so habitats become fewer and simpler.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.17.b] Irish farming has intensified in both dairying (milk production) and tillage (growing crops on ploughed land). The changes removed habitats and polluted water.

[geo-option-geoecology-s5.18.b] Heavy use of fertiliser and slurry (liquid animal manure) affects water and the atmosphere. Policies now try to reduce it and help wildlife.

[geo-option-geoecology-s5.19.0] The Common Agricultural Policy began in 1962. Guaranteed prices encouraged farmers to remove hedgerows, enlarge fields and drain wetlands, notably in the arable east of England (East Anglia).
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.19.1] Food surpluses, the butter mountains and wine lakes, followed in the 1980s, because guaranteed prices encouraged farmers to produce more than was needed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.19.2] Later reforms, from 1992 onwards, began paying farmers for environmental farming.

[geo-option-geoecology-s5.21.6] The Kok-Aral Dam (2005), built by Kazakhstan with World Bank funding, raised the level of the small North Aral Sea, and fishing has partly recovered there.

[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.3] Effects on lakes include fish being killed and aluminium being released into the water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.22.4] The Black Triangle, where the borders of Germany, Poland and the Czech Republic meet, had lignite-burning power stations, so large amounts of acid gases were released (lignite is a low-grade, dirty brown coal).

[geo-option-geoecology-s5.25.0] The Athabasca oil sands, around Fort McMurray in northern Alberta, Canada, are mined by surface (open-pit) methods that strip away boreal forest (northern coniferous forest) and muskeg (peatland) to reach bitumen-soaked sand.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.25.1] The mining produces large tailings ponds (ponds of polluted waste water) and high greenhouse gas emissions per barrel.
[geo-option-geoecology-s5.25.2] Companies must reclaim mined land (restore it), but little of it has been certified as reclaimed.

[geo-option-geoecology-s5.28.b] Industry affects biomes beyond the factory site, through the atmosphere and through the spread of towns and factories.

CANDIDATES geo-option-geoecology-s3: 3. Human interference with soils
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.0.b] Soil is the thin layer on which almost all land plants and farming depend. People can protect it or damage it, and the damage is hard to undo.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.0.0] Soil forms very slowly but can be lost quickly, so on a human timescale it is a non-renewable resource: once it is gone, it will not be replaced within our lifetimes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.0.1] The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO, the UN food agency) has stated that it can take up to 1,000 years to form 1 cm of soil. This was quoted for the International Year of Soils, 2015.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.0.2] Human activity can degrade soil, which is a negative impact, or conserve and improve it, which is a positive impact.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.b] Overcropping happens when farmers take more from the soil than they give back. It changes the soil's characteristics (its nutrients, humus, structure and water-holding ability) in a series of steps.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.0] Overcropping is growing crops on the same land too intensively, without fallow (leaving land unplanted to recover), rotation (changing the crop each year) or enough organic matter returned, so the soil is exhausted.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.1] Nutrients are removed faster than they are replaced, so humus content (the dark, decayed organic matter in soil) falls and crumb structure (the small stable clumps of soil particles) breaks down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.2] The soil becomes dusty or compacted, holds less water, and is easily eroded by wind and rain, so yields fall.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.3] Monoculture, one crop grown year after year, takes the same nutrients each time and encourages pests.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.1.4] In the Sahel, cash crops (crops grown for sale) and shorter fallow periods exhaust soils.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.b] The Dust Bowl shows how ploughing up natural vegetation, followed by drought, can strip away a whole region's soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.0] Place and time: the Great Plains of the USA, including Oklahoma and Kansas, in the 1930s.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.1] The natural grassland was ploughed up for wheat, which removed the roots that had held the soil together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.2] Severe drought then left bare, dry soil that strong winds blew away in huge dust storms. Many farming families were forced to leave.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.3] The prairie soils are chernozems (deep, dark, fertile grassland soils).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.2.4] Response: the US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935. Shelter belts of trees, contour ploughing and crop rotation were promoted.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.b] Overgrazing damages soil by removing the plant cover that protects it. Animals add a second problem by trampling the ground.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.0] Overgrazing is keeping more animals on land than its vegetation can support, which exceeds its carrying capacity (the number of animals the land can feed in the long term).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.1] Vegetation is eaten down and roots die, so the soil is exposed to rain-splash (the impact of raindrops that knocks soil particles loose) and wind.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.2] Trampling compacts the soil. On wet Irish ground this is called poaching. It reduces infiltration (water soaking in) and increases runoff (water flowing over the surface).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.3] Sheet, rill and gully erosion (washing away of a thin layer, then small channels, then deep channels) remove topsoil and humus. Less litter (fallen leaves and plant remains) means less humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.4] On peat, bare surfaces erode quickly, because loose, light peat is easily washed and blown away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.3.5] International examples: in the Sahel, livestock concentrated around wells, and in Iceland, sheep grazing prevented birch regrowth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.b] A change in farm payments, not a change in the land, caused overgrazing of Irish hills. Commonage is hill land that farmers use together for grazing.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.0] From the 1980s, EU (CAP) headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in the west of Ireland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.1] The affected areas include Connemara, Co. Galway, and the mountains of Co. Mayo, Co. Donegal and Co. Kerry.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.2] Overgrazing stripped heather and grass, exposed and eroded blanket peat (a thick peat layer covering hills), and sent silt into rivers.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.3] The silt harmed salmon and trout spawning grounds, because it smothers the gravel where the fish lay eggs.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.4] Response: payments changed from per-head to per-area. Commonage Framework Plans, from the early 2000s, set lower stocking rates (fewer animals per hectare).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.4.5] Response: REPS, the Rural Environment Protection Scheme introduced in 1994, paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.b] Desertification is often misunderstood. It is about land losing its productive soil, and it has both natural and human causes that usually work together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.0] Desertification is land degradation in arid (very dry), semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas, resulting from climatic variations and human activities.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.1] It is the degradation of land on desert margins, not the advance of sand dunes from an existing desert.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.2] Physical causes are drought, unreliable rainfall, high temperatures and evaporation.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.3] Human causes are population growth, overgrazing, overcropping, cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood, and poorly managed irrigation causing salinisation (salts building up in the soil as irrigation water evaporates).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.5.4] The two usually act together. Human pressure leaves the soil bare, then drought and storms remove it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.b] Desertified land loses the qualities that make soil useful, and the people who farm it lose their living.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.0] Loss of topsoil and humus, and lower fertility, because the nutrient-rich surface layer is the part that is removed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.1] Surface crusting, so rain runs off rather than soaking in, and water retention is reduced.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.2] Wind-blown sand and dust, because bare, dry soil has nothing to hold its particles down.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.3] Salt crusts where irrigation is poorly managed (salinisation). In extreme cases the ground becomes bare and sterile.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.6.4] Human effects: falling yields, food shortages and famine, loss of livestock, and migration to cities and abroad.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.7.b] The Sahel is the main example of desertification. Its climate explains why its soils are so vulnerable.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.7.0] The Sahel is the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara. It stretches across Africa from Senegal and Mauritania in the west, through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad, to Sudan.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.7.1] Annual rainfall is roughly 200 to 600 mm. It falls in one short summer wet season, when the ITCZ, the rain belt, moves north.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.7.2] Rainfall is highly unreliable from year to year, so a run of dry years can strip the plant cover.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.b] In the Sahel, human pressure and drought combined: people left the soil bare, then drought and heavy rain removed it.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.0] Population growth led to more cattle, goats and sheep concentrated around wells and boreholes (drilled water sources), shorter fallow periods, more cash crops and more fuelwood cutting.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.1] Cash crops such as groundnuts (peanuts) in Senegal and cotton took the best land, pushing food crops and herders onto marginal land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.2] Nomadic herders, whose movements once let pasture recover, became more settled, so land around their camps was grazed continuously.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.8.3] Droughts, notably in the 1970s and 1980s, left bare soil that was eroded by wind and by the heavy rain of the short wet season.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.9.b] The Sahel shows the soil damage of desertification and its effect on water supply.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.9.0] The soil effects are loss of topsoil and humus, lower fertility, surface crusting, reduced water retention, wind-blown sand and dust, and salt crusts where irrigation is poor.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.9.1] Lake Chad, on the Sahel's border of Chad, Niger, Nigeria and Cameroon, has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s, through drought and water use for irrigation. A widely quoted UN figure gives a shrinkage of about 90%.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.10.b] Because desertification crosses borders, governments have tried to respond together.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.10.0] The UN Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD) was adopted in 1994. 17 June is World Day to Combat Desertification.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.10.1] The Great Green Wall was launched by the African Union in 2007. It is a planned band of restored land about 8,000 km long across Africa, from Senegal to Djibouti, aiming to restore 100 million hectares of degraded land by 2030.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.10.2] Progress has been slow and well short of target. The project has shifted from a line of planted trees towards a mosaic of local land-restoration projects.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.11.b] Some of the most effective responses come from farmers using low-cost methods that fit dry soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.11.0] In Burkina Faso, in the Yatenga region of northern Burkina Faso, farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo revived the traditional zaï technique.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.11.1] Zaï are small pits dug in hard, crusted soil and filled with manure or compost. They catch rainwater and concentrate nutrients around the seed.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.11.2] Farmers also built stone lines, or bunds, along the contours (lines of equal height) to slow runoff and trap soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.12.b] In Niger, farmers rebuilt tree cover without planting. They simply protected what was already growing.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.12.0] In Niger, farmer-managed natural regeneration (FMNR) means farmers protect and prune tree shoots that regrow from old stumps and roots in their fields.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.12.1] FMNR has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, especially in the Maradi and Zinder regions.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.12.2] Trees shade the soil, cut wind speed and add leaf litter. Species such as Faidherbia albida also add nitrogen.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.13.b] Comparing the Sahel's responses shows what works.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.13.0] Local methods have restored land at scale in Niger, but the Great Green Wall has been slow to meet its targets.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.13.1] Conservation works best when farmers gain from it and take part, as the Niger FMNR and the Burren results-based payments show.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.b] Beyond overcropping, overgrazing and desertification, several other human activities damage soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.0] Deforestation exposes soil to erosion and stops the supply of litter, which is the source of humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.1] Heavy machinery and livestock on wet ground compact the soil and destroy its structure.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.2] Excess fertiliser and slurry let nitrates and phosphates wash out of the soil into rivers and lakes, causing eutrophication (nutrient enrichment that makes algae overgrow). Some fertilisers also acidify the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.3] Salinisation and waterlogging (soil saturated with water) occur under badly managed irrigation. Canal irrigation in Punjab and Haryana, India, has caused both.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.4] Peat extraction removes an entire soil, as in Irish bogs, because the peat itself is the soil being cut away.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.14.5] Urban sprawl and roads seal soil under concrete and tarmac, so it can no longer grow plants or absorb rain.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.b] On slopes, water and wind carry soil away. These methods slow the water or wind so the soil stays in place.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.0] Contour ploughing means ploughing across a slope along the contours, so that furrows trap water instead of channelling it downhill.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.1] Terracing cuts steep slopes into level steps, such as the rice terraces of Banaue, Luzon, Philippines, which are over 2,000 years old.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.2] Strip cropping alternates strips of crops and grass. The grass traps soil washed or blown from the cropped strip.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.15.3] Shelter belts and windbreaks of trees and hedges cut wind speed and reduce wind erosion.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.16.b] These methods keep nutrients and humus in the soil and keep the surface covered.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.16.0] Crop rotation, fallow, cover crops and green manure, such as clover, restore nutrients and humus.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.16.1] Adding manure and compost builds up humus, so the soil holds more nutrients and water.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.16.2] Reduced or zero tillage (little or no ploughing), and mulching, leave crop residue on the surface to protect the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.17.b] Protecting soil from grazing animals means letting plants recover and using roots to hold the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.17.0] Controlled stocking (limiting animal numbers), rotational grazing (moving animals between fields), fencing and reseeding allow vegetation to recover.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.17.1] Afforestation (planting trees where there were none) and replanting bind the soil with roots.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.18.b] Dry regions need methods that capture scarce rain and protect soil from wind.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.18.0] Stone lines (bunds) along contours, zaï planting pits, and farmer-managed natural regeneration of trees are the dryland methods used in the Sahel, because they capture scarce rain and protect soil from wind.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.b] Conservation protects soil that already exists. These methods go further and improve poor soils, or even create soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.0] Liming raises the pH of acid soils, so they become less acidic and suit more crops.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.1] Field drainage and arterial drainage improve gleys (waterlogged soils). The Arterial Drainage Act, 1945, began state schemes to deepen and straighten Irish rivers to drain wet farmland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.2] Deep ploughing breaks an iron pan (a hard layer in the soil), as in podzols.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.3] Irrigation with proper drainage avoids salinisation, because the drainage carries away the salts instead of letting them build up.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.19.4] Building soil where none existed: on the bare limestone of the Aran Islands, Co. Galway, islanders layered sand and seaweed, and later manure, on the rock inside stone-walled fields.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.20.b] Government schemes pay farmers to protect soil, and EU rules limit pollution from farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.20.0] REPS was followed by GLAS (Green, Low-Carbon, Agri-Environment Scheme), from 2015, and ACRES (Agri-Climate Rural Environment Scheme), from 2023.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.20.1] The EU Nitrates Directive (1991) limits how much organic nitrogen can be spread per hectare and bans spreading slurry during a closed period in winter.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.b] The Burren is the syllabus's Irish example of human interference with soil. It is thought that its bare rock is partly a result of early farming.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.0] The Burren, in north-west Co. Clare, lies on Carboniferous limestone, with extensive limestone pavement of clints (blocks) and grikes (gaps between them).
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.1] Its soils are thin, dark, lime-rich rendzinas, found mainly in the grikes and hollows.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.2] Plants from Arctic-alpine and Mediterranean environments grow side by side, e.g. spring gentian and mountain avens.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.3] Poulnabrone, a Neolithic portal tomb (dolmen) over 5,000 years old, is evidence of early farming communities.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.4] It is thought that the Burren once had thicker soil and woodland cover. Clearance and grazing by early farmers, followed by erosion of soil into the grikes and underground, helped expose the bare pavement.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.21.5] The Burren was also glaciated.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.b] On the Burren, grazing is part of the solution. The problem today is too little grazing.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.0] Winterage means cattle are grazed on the upland limestone in winter, because the rock stores heat and the ground is dry.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.1] Grazing keeps grass and scrub down and maintains the flower-rich grassland.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.2] From the late 20th century fewer farmers grazed the uplands in winter, because of farm changes, supplementary feeding and labour shortage.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.3] Hazel and blackthorn scrub (low woody bushes) then spread over grassland and pavement, because nothing grazed it back.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.22.4] On the Burren the main threat is undergrazing, not overgrazing.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.b] Conservation projects on the Burren led to a payment system that rewards farmers for the quality of their land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.0] BurrenLIFE (2005 to 2010) was an EU LIFE-funded project led by Dr Brendan Dunford, with local farmers, the National Parks and Wildlife Service and Teagasc.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.1] It tested scrub removal, better feeding systems and grazing plans.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.2] It was followed by the Burren Farming for Conservation Programme (2010 to 2015) and the Burren Programme (from 2016), funded by the Department of Agriculture.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.3] These pay farmers by results. Each field is scored for habitat quality, e.g. on a scale of 1 to 10, and a higher score earns a higher payment, so farmers decide how best to manage their land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.23.4] The model has been copied in other Irish agri-environment schemes.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.b] Iceland shows how settlers can strip a landscape of trees. Their cutting and their sheep removed the cover that protected the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.0] Iceland was settled by Norse people from about AD 874.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.1] At settlement, birch woodland and scrub covered a large part of the island, an estimated quarter or more. Today native birch woodland covers only about 1 to 2%.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.2] Settlers cut and burned birch for fuel, for charcoal used in iron working, and for grazing land.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.24.3] They kept large numbers of sheep, which grazed the year round and prevented regrowth.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.b] Iceland's soil and climate made the damage worse, because the soil is easily blown away and plants recover slowly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.0] Iceland's soils are andosols formed from volcanic ash. They are light, loose and low in clay.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.1] Once the vegetation cover is broken, they are easily blown away by strong winds and washed out by rain.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.2] Erosion escarpments (rofabörð in Icelandic) are steep banks of soil being eaten away from their edges.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.3] Volcanic eruptions add ash that smothers vegetation, so plant cover is lost again after each eruption.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.25.4] A cold climate and a short growing season mean vegetation recovers very slowly.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.b] Iceland also shows the positive side: over a century of work to hold and rebuild its soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.0] The Soil Conservation Service of Iceland (Landgræðslan, now part of Land and Forest Iceland) was founded in 1907. It is one of the oldest soil conservation services in the world.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.1] Methods include fencing off eroding land to keep sheep out, and sowing lyme grass (Leymus arenarius) to stabilise drifting sand.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.2] Other methods are sowing grasses with fertiliser from the air, and replanting birch.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.3] Nootka lupin fixes nitrogen (turns nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use) and binds soil, but it has become an invasive plant that crowds out native species, so its use is controversial.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.4] Farmer-led reclamation projects, called Farmers Heal the Land, began in 1990.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.5] Sheep numbers have fallen from their peak in the late 1970s, which means less grazing pressure on the soil.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.6] Iceland shows both sides: over a century of positive impact through conservation and reclamation, after centuries of negative impact through deforestation, overgrazing and erosion.
[geo-option-geoecology-s3.26.7] Positive and negative impacts can occur on the same land at different times.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s2: 2. Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.b] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas formed from ancient living things) supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy (the energy taken straight from nature before conversion) in the 2020s. Their production is concentrated in a few places, so most countries must import them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.0] The Middle East (e.g. Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq) holds about half the world's proven oil reserves (oil known to be recoverable). Venezuela has the largest proven reserves of any single country.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.1] The USA has been the world's largest oil producer since the late 2010s, through shale oil and gas (trapped in rock and released by fracking, which fractures the rock with pressurised liquid).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.2] Russia is one of the largest gas exporters. Qatar is a leading exporter of liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so that ships can carry it).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.3] China produces and consumes about half the world's coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.4] Fossil fuels are traded over long distances by tanker, pipeline and LNG ship, so importers depend on exporters for their energy security (a steady, affordable supply of energy).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.b] Energy use is very uneven across the world. It follows wealth and industry, and the pattern is shifting towards Asia.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.0] Developed countries use far more energy per person than developing countries. Demand is now growing fastest in Asia, notably China and India, because their economies and populations are growing.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.1] The USA, Canada and Australia use many times more energy per person than India or most of sub-Saharan Africa.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.2] China has been the world's largest CO2 (carbon dioxide) emitter in total since the mid-2000s, but the USA emits far more CO2 per person.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.b] Burning a fossil fuel releases the carbon and impurities locked inside it. Each pollutant causes a different problem, so keep them apart.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.0] Carbon dioxide (CO2) is the main greenhouse gas released, and it drives global warming.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.1] Sulphur dioxide (SO2), mainly from coal and heavy oil (thick, impure oil), is a cause of acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.2] Nitrogen oxides (NOx), mainly from vehicles and power stations, are also a cause of acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.3] Smoke, fine particles (soot, tiny solid specks that can be breathed deep into the lungs) and carbon monoxide are released as well.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.b] The greenhouse effect is the natural trapping of heat by gases in the atmosphere. Burning fossil fuels adds extra gas to it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.0] CO2 traps heat in the atmosphere, so extra CO2 from burning fossil fuels enhances the greenhouse effect and causes global warming. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.b] Acid rain is a transboundary problem (one that crosses national borders): the countries that cause it are often not the ones that suffer.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.0] Normal rain is slightly acidic, at about pH 5.6, because CO2 dissolves in it naturally. Acid rain is rain with a pH below 5.6, not below pH 7.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.1] SO2 and NOx dissolve in water vapour to form sulphuric and nitric acids. These fall as wet deposition in rain, or as dry deposition of acidic particles and gases.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.2] Sources include coal-fired power stations, heavy industry and vehicles, because all of them burn fuels that contain sulphur or release nitrogen oxides.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.3] Emissions from Britain and Germany were carried by the prevailing south-westerly winds (the usual wind direction) to Norway and Sweden, which is why Scandinavia suffered from other countries' pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.4] The Black Triangle, the border region of former East Germany, Poland and Czechia, had lignite-burning power stations (lignite is a low-grade, smoky coal) that caused severe forest death in the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.b] Acid rain harms living things, buildings and people. The damage can appear far from the source, because the wind carries the pollution across borders.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.0] Forest damage: needle and leaf loss, and nutrients leached (washed out) from the soil. German forests, such as the Black Forest, were reported dying in the 1980s (Waldsterben, meaning forest death).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.1] Lake acidification: thousands of Scandinavian lakes were acidified and lost their fish. Aluminium released from the soil by the acid kills fish.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.2] Limestone and marble buildings and statues dissolve, through the same process as the chemical weathering of limestone (carbonation, where weak acid reacts with the rock).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.3] Corrosion of metal, for example, and respiratory (breathing) problems in people.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.b] Because acid rain crosses borders, responses had to be international as well as national. They work either by cutting emissions or by repairing the damage.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.0] The 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution, signed at UNECE (the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe) in Geneva, was an international agreement on air pollution that crosses borders.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.1] EU directives (laws binding on member states) limit emissions from large power stations.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.2] Flue-gas desulphurisation (scrubbers, which remove SO2 from the smoke before it leaves the chimney) is used at power stations. Moneypoint, Co. Clare, opened in 1985 and had scrubbers fitted in the late 2000s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.3] Low-sulphur fuels cut SO2 at source. Catalytic converters (exhaust devices that reduce NOx) became compulsory on new petrol cars in the EU in 1993.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.4] Sweden and Norway have limed acidified lakes (added lime to neutralise the acid) since the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.5] Countries switched from coal to gas and renewables, which release little or no SO2.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.6] Outcome: European SO2 emissions have fallen by well over three-quarters since 1990, and many lakes are recovering.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.b] Smog is smoke mixed with fog. It builds up when smoke from home fires cannot escape upwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.0] Burning coal and turf in homes releases smoke and fine particles, because the fuel burns incompletely in an open fire.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.1] In calm, cold winter weather an anticyclone (a high-pressure system with settled air) creates a temperature inversion, where a layer of warm air sits above cold air near the ground.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.2] The inversion traps the smoke near the ground, because the warm layer stops it rising and blowing away. This forms smog.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.3] Smog causes bronchitis, asthma, heart and lung disease, and extra deaths, because the smoke and fine particles are breathed in.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.b] Smog led governments to ban smoky fuels in city areas. London's disaster in 1952 was the earlier precedent, and Dublin's ban is the Irish case study.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.0] London's Great Smog, in December 1952, caused about 4,000 deaths at the time. Later estimates are far higher.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.1] The Great Smog led to the UK Clean Air Act 1956, which created smokeless zones (areas where smoky fuel may not be burned).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.2] Dublin suffered severe winter smogs in the early 1980s from household coal burning, which led to the ban.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.3] The sale of smoky (bituminous) coal was banned in Dublin from 1 September 1990. Black smoke levels fell by about 70%.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.4] The ban covered smoky coal only, so smokeless fuel and turf could still be burned in Dublin at first.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.5] A study published in The Lancet in 2002 estimated that the ban was linked to about 350 fewer deaths a year in Dublin from respiratory and heart disease.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.6] The ban was extended to Cork in 1995, and in stages to other cities and large towns as low-smoke zones. National rules from 2022 further restrict the sale of smoky solid fuels and wet wood.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.b] Beyond the air pollutants, getting and using fossil fuels harms land, water and rivers.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.0] Oil spills during transport, for example from tankers, pollute the sea and coasts. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.1] Landscape damage comes from coal mining and peat cutting, which scar and strip the land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.2] Thermal pollution of rivers is caused by the cooling water of power stations, which is returned to the river warmer than it was taken.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.b] Wind turns turbine blades that drive a generator. Turbines are built onshore on land and offshore at sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.0] Ireland: wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s, from onshore farms such as Galway Wind Park, Connemara (2017), and from Arklow Bank Wind Park, off Co. Wicklow (2004), Ireland's first offshore wind farm.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.1] Denmark: wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.2] Advantage: strong, frequent Atlantic winds give Ireland a good wind resource, and wind is the main renewable source in Irish electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.3] Disadvantage: output varies with the wind, and local residents object on grounds of noise, shadow flicker (flickering shadows cast by moving blades) and visual impact.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.4] Disadvantage: Derrybrien, Co. Galway, suffered a peat slide of about 450,000 m³ on 16 October 2003 during construction of a wind farm on bog. The EU Court of Justice fined Ireland in 2019 for the lack of a proper environmental impact assessment (a study of the likely harm before building).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.b] Hydroelectric power uses falling water to turn turbines, through three main methods.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.0] Dams and reservoirs release stored water through turbines at a controlled rate.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.1] Run-of-river schemes use the river's natural flow, so they need little or no stored water.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.2] Pumped storage pumps water uphill at times of low demand and releases it at peak times, so it acts like a store of energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.3] Ireland: Ardnacrusha on the River Shannon (Shannon Scheme, opened 1929, built by Siemens) and Turlough Hill, Co. Wicklow (1974), the country's pumped-storage station. Poulaphouca on the Liffey, Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid on the Lee, and Cathaleen's Fall at Ballyshannon on the Erne also generate power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.4] Advantage: output can be raised quickly at peak times (when demand is highest), as at Turlough Hill.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.5] Disadvantage: Ireland has little hydro potential left for large new schemes.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.6] Norway: hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of its electricity, for example.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.7] Three Gorges Dam, Yangtze River, China: the world's largest power station by installed capacity. It was completed in 2006, fully operational in 2012, and over one million people were relocated. It gives flood control downstream but traps sediment (silt that would otherwise travel downriver).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.b] Photovoltaic panels convert sunlight into electricity, and solar thermal panels use sunlight to heat water.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.0] Ireland: solar power has grown rapidly since the early 2020s, through large solar farms on farmland and panels on rooftops. SEAI (Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland) grants help homes to install panels.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.1] Disadvantage: Ireland's cloud cover and short winter days limit output, because panels need strong daylight.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.b] Waves and tides drive turbines or barrages (dams built across an estuary). The three technologies are different, so do not mix them up.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.0] Wave: the Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is a wave test site.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.1] Tidal barrage: La Rance, Brittany, France, a barrage across the Rance estuary, opened in 1966. It was one of the first and largest tidal power stations.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.2] Tidal stream: SeaGen in Strangford Lough, Co. Down, was installed in 2008 as the world's first commercial-scale tidal-stream turbine, using the fast tidal flow through the narrows. It has since been removed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.3] Advantage: tides are predictable, because they follow the moon's cycle. Disadvantages: high cost and marine impacts (harm to sea life and habitats).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.b] Geothermal energy uses heat from the Earth's interior, to heat water or to generate steam.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.0] Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (a plate boundary where the crust is thin) over a hotspot (a rising plume of hot rock). The Hellisheiði power station generates electricity from geothermal water, and about 9 in 10 Icelandic homes are heated by it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.1] Ireland uses low-temperature heat pumps only, which draw modest heat from the ground.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.2] Advantage: output does not depend on the weather. Disadvantage: high-temperature sources are found only where the crust is hot, as in Iceland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.b] Biomass is organic material (from plants or animal waste) burned or converted to energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.0] Sources: wood chip, energy crops such as willow and miscanthus, and biogas from slurry and food waste produced by anaerobic digestion (rotting without oxygen).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.1] Biofuels (fuels made from plants) are blended into petrol and diesel, by law in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.2] Edenderry, Co. Offaly, was converted from peat to biomass, an example of a power station switching fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.3] Advantage: carbon-neutral in principle, because the crops absorb as much CO2 as they release when burned. Disadvantage: energy crops compete with food production for land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.b] Nuclear power uses fission, the splitting of uranium atoms, to release heat that makes steam to drive turbines. Nuclear is not renewable.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.0] France: nuclear power supplies about two-thirds of its electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.1] Advantages: low carbon emissions, so little CO2, plus reliable and large output.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.2] Disadvantages, such as radioactive waste, accident risk, very high cost, and the cost of decommissioning (safely shutting down and dismantling) old reactors.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.3] Chernobyl, Ukraine (then USSR), 26 April 1986: a reactor exploded, and radioactive fallout spread across Europe, including Ireland. A 30 km exclusion zone remains.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.4] Fukushima Daiichi, Japan, 11 March 2011: an earthquake and tsunami knocked out cooling and caused meltdowns.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.b] Ireland has never had a nuclear power station. A combination of law, public opinion and practical limits explains why.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.0] The Electricity Regulation Act 1999 bans the use of nuclear fission to generate electricity in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.1] The ESB (Electricity Supply Board) planned a nuclear power station at Carnsore Point, Co. Wexford, in the 1970s. It was abandoned after large public protests and was never built.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.2] Other reasons: Ireland's small electricity grid, the high cost, the problem of waste disposal, and fears raised by Chernobyl.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.3] Sellafield, Cumbria, England, a nuclear reprocessing site on the Irish Sea coast, has discharges that Irish governments have long objected to, which adds to Irish unease about nuclear power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.b] Renewable energy comes from sources that are naturally replaced. These advantages apply to wind, hydro, solar, wave, tidal and geothermal power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.0] Renewables are inexhaustible, so supply is secure: the sun, wind, water and tides will not run out.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.1] They produce little or no CO2, SO2 or NOx, so they cause less global warming and acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.2] They reduce the fossil-fuel import bill and improve energy security.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.3] They create jobs, often in rural areas.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.4] Once a plant is built, the fuel is free.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.5] They help meet EU and national targets and avoid penalties for missing them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.b] Renewables also have costs and limits, and these explain why fossil fuels are still widely used.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.0] Renewables are intermittent (not constant), because wind and sun vary, so backup or storage is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.1] The initial cost of building a plant is high.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.2] Turbines and dams have visual impact, and cause noise and local objections.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.3] Remote sites need grid connections (cables linking them to the electricity network).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.4] Some habitats are damaged: birds are killed by turbines, and dams flood valleys. Peat slides can also happen at wind farms on bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.b] Ireland imports a large share of its energy, so renewable energy matters for both its environment and its economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.0] Ireland imported about three-quarters of its energy in the early 2020s, at a cost of billions of euro a year. Renewables reduce this import bill and the exposure to price shocks (sudden rises in world fuel prices).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.1] Ireland must meet its 2030 targets for renewable electricity and emissions. The Climate Action Plan aims for 80% renewable electricity by 2030, and missing EU targets exposes Ireland to the cost of buying compliance.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.2] Energy security improves through interconnectors (cables that link electricity grids): the East-West Interconnector to Wales (2012) and the Celtic Interconnector to France (due in the late 2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.3] Renewables provide jobs and income in rural areas, because wind farms, solar farms and dams are built there.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.4] Green power attracts multinational companies that want renewable electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.5] Cleaner air and water follow from burning less fossil fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.b] Map questions ask you to choose and justify a wind-farm site on an Ordnance Survey map, and to explain how conflict may arise. Match each feature of the site to a need of the wind farm.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.0] A wind farm needs a high, exposed upland or coastal site with strong winds, because output depends on wind speed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.1] It should be away from houses, to limit noise and shadow flicker.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.2] It needs an access road close by for construction, and a connection close to the electricity grid, by transmission line (the high-voltage cable that carries power).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.3] It should avoid protected areas and deep peat, because peat can slide when disturbed. It is best placed on land of low farming value.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.4] Conflict arises when a site lies near homes or in a protected or scenic area, so the site must balance wind strength against these constraints.
