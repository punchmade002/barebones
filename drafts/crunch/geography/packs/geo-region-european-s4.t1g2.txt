[geo-region-european-s4.0.b] The Gaeltacht is the area where Irish is the community language, the language people use in everyday life. Its extent has shrunk as speakers have declined, and the decline began in the 1800s.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.0] Census 1851 (a count of the whole population) was the first census to count Irish speakers. It found about 1.5 million Irish speakers, about 23% of the population, and Irish-speaking areas then covered much of the west and south.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.1] The Great Famine (1845–49) and emigration (people leaving the country) hit the poor, western, Irish-speaking areas hardest, so the Irish-speaking population fell fastest where Irish was strongest.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.2] National schools, set up in 1831, taught through English, so children learned to read and write in a language that was not their home language.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.3] English became the language of law, trade, politics, the Church and emigration. Parents saw English as the route to work, so many chose not to pass Irish on to their children.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.4] Census 1891 shows the result: about 680,000 Irish speakers, about 14% of the population, down from about 23% in 1851.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.b] From the 1890s people tried to reverse the decline. The new state then gave the Gaeltacht its first official boundaries, and later redrew them.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.0] The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge), a revival movement, founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill, promoted the language, to stop its decline.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.1] The new state's language policy made Irish compulsory in schools and required for some public jobs, so that the state itself supported the language.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.2] Coimisiún na Gaeltachta (the commission set up to define the Gaeltacht) reported in 1926 and defined two types of Gaeltacht. Fíor-Ghaeltacht ('true' Gaeltacht): districts where 80% or more spoke Irish.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.3] Breac-Ghaeltacht ('partly' Gaeltacht): districts with 25–79% Irish speakers. The 1926 definitions together covered a large area across many western and southern counties.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.4] The Gaeltacht Areas Order 1956 (a government order, not a new law) shrank the official Gaeltacht to areas where Irish was still the community language, because Irish had weakened in the Breac-Ghaeltacht districts.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.5] The Department of the Gaeltacht (Roinn na Gaeltachta) was set up in 1956. Gaeltarra Éireann, the Gaeltacht industry body, followed in 1957. Later small extensions were made by order.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.b] Today's Gaeltacht lies in seven counties, and one of them, Meath, is an unusual case because its Gaeltacht was planned by the state.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.0] Gaeltacht areas lie in seven counties: Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford and Meath.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.1] The Meath Gaeltacht was built by planned colonies (settlements created by the state, not areas where Irish had survived): Ráth Chairn, settled in 1935 by families moved from Connemara, and Baile Ghib, settled in 1937.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.2] Galway (Conamara) has the largest Gaeltacht population; Donegal is second.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.3] Census 2022: the Gaeltacht population was about 106,000, and about 20,000 Gaeltacht residents speak Irish daily outside the education system, so only a minority use it as an everyday language.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.4] Nationally (Census 2022): about 1.87 million people (about 40%) said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.b] The state and communities have built institutions to protect Irish, from development agencies to broadcasting and official recognition.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.0] Údarás na Gaeltachta, the Gaeltacht development authority, was set up in 1980 to replace Gaeltarra Éireann. It creates jobs so people can stay in the Gaeltacht. The Gaoth Dobhair (Gweedore) industrial estate in Co. Donegal is an example of Gaeltacht industry.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.1] Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972. TG4 (originally Teilifís na Gaeilge) launched on 31 October 1996. Both give Irish a presence in the media.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.2] The Official Languages Act 2003 (amended 2021) gives Irish official status, which means public bodies must provide services in it.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.3] Irish became an official EU language in 2007 and gained full working-language status on 1 January 2022.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.4] The 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language, a long-term state plan, covers 2010 to 2030.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.5] Gaeltacht summer colleges give young people intensive immersion in Irish, so they use the language all day.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.b] The 2012 Act changed how the Gaeltacht is defined. Instead of a fixed map, boundaries now depend on how much Irish is actually spoken.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.0] The Gaeltacht Act 2012 defined the Gaeltacht by language use rather than by a fixed map, so boundaries now depend on language use.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.1] Gaeltacht Language Planning Areas: 26, so the Gaeltacht is now a set of planning units, each needing a language plan (a local plan to strengthen the use of Irish).
[geo-region-european-s4.4.2] Gaeltacht Service Towns, and Irish Language Networks outside the Gaeltacht, extend Irish use beyond the core areas.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.b] Even with these supports, the use of Irish as a daily language in the Gaeltacht has kept falling. A 2007 study set out how much daily use is needed to keep it alive.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.0] The Comprehensive Linguistic Study of the Use of Irish in the Gaeltacht (2007) found that Irish is viable as a community language only where at least 67% of people speak it daily.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.1] Its 2015 update warned that Irish could cease to be the community language in most Gaeltacht districts within about ten years.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.2] In-migration of English speakers (people moving in from outside) weakens Irish, because English becomes the language of daily life in the area.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.3] Suburban growth, such as Galway city's spread west, brings the Gaeltacht edge into contact with English-speaking suburbs.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.4] Tourism, emigration of young people and English-language media add further pressure on Irish as the language of the community.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.b] Responses target the pressures above, by keeping Irish speakers in the Gaeltacht and giving them reasons and ways to use the language.

CANDIDATES geo-region-ireland-s1: 1. A Core Irish Region
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.b] The Greater Dublin Area (GDA) is the core region of Ireland's east coast, facing Britain and Europe across the Irish Sea. Knowing exactly which counties it includes is the starting point for every question on it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.0] The GDA is Dublin City and County Dublin, plus Counties Meath, Kildare and Wicklow. Louth, including Drogheda, is not part of it, so do not count it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.1] Dublin City, Fingal, South Dublin and Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown are the four local authorities (the councils that run local services) of Dublin; the single Co. Dublin county council was split into the last three in 1994.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.b] A core region is one that concentrates people, wealth and services, in contrast to peripheral regions (remote areas with fewer of these). The GDA fits this description in several ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.0] Its large, dense and growing population is the largest in the State, which gives firms customers and workers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.1] It holds most of the State's wealth, jobs, multinational investment, government and services, which means firms and workers want to be there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.2] It has the best infrastructure in the State, including the main port, airport and motorway network, so goods and people move easily.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.3] It attracts in-migrants (people moving in) from the rest of Ireland and from abroad.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.4] Cumulative causation (growth that feeds more growth): growth attracts firms and workers, which attracts further investment and widens the gap with peripheral regions such as the West.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.2.b] A sketch map of the region is set as a question, and it is marked on a handful of simple features. Draw these, each clearly labelled.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.2.0] Outline the four counties: Dublin and Wicklow on the coast, Meath to the north (with a short coastline south of the Boyne estuary), Kildare to the west.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.2.1] Draw the River Liffey from the Wicklow Mountains, through Kildare, to Dublin Bay.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.2.2] Draw the River Boyne through Trim and Navan, reaching the sea at Drogheda, just outside the GDA.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.2.3] Mark Dublin city, and the towns of Navan, Naas and Bray.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.2.4] Show relief (the height and shape of the land): the Wicklow Mountains in the south and the lowland plain of Meath and Kildare, so the map shows both upland and lowland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.b] The GDA has a cool temperate oceanic climate (mild and wet because of the nearby ocean), with prevailing south-westerly winds bringing moist Atlantic air. Because of where the mountains sit, the east is drier than the west.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.0] Rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of high ground): the Wicklow Mountains and the uplands further west force moist air to drop its rain on the western slopes, so the east is the driest part of Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.1] Rainfall in the east is about 750–1,000 mm a year, so it is much drier than the west; Dublin Airport records about 750 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.2] By contrast, the west receives 1,000–1,250 mm a year and some mountains exceed 2,000 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.3] The south-east is the sunniest part of Ireland, which adds to the east's value for crops.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.4] Mean January temperatures in Ireland are about 4–7 °C and mean July temperatures about 14–16 °C, so winters are mild and summers cool.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.5] Being farther from the Atlantic than the west gives a slightly greater temperature range: warmer summers, colder winters and more frost.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.6] Heavier rain and snow fall on the Wicklow uplands than on the lowlands, because rising air cools and drops its moisture.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.b] Climate decides what the land can be used for. The dry east and the wet uplands are used in different ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.0] Effect on farming: the dry, relatively sunny lowlands suit tillage (growing crops such as cereals and vegetables) and horticulture (growing vegetables, fruit and flowers).

[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.0] Dublin was founded as a Viking settlement in 841, which makes it a very old city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.1] Norman rule expanded the town in medieval times.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.2] The Wide Streets Commissioners, set up in 1757, widened and laid out Georgian streets; squares such as Merrion Square were developed by private estates.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.3] Georgian Dublin grew into a planned capital with wide streets and squares.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.b] A question on how one urban area developed asks for factors. For Dublin, these are site, route focus, port, capital-city functions, industry and planning.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.0] Site: Dublin grew at the lowest bridging point of the Liffey (the lowest place the river could be crossed by a bridge), sheltered by Dublin Bay.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.1] Route focus: roads and routeways radiate from the city, linking it to the rest of the region, so people and goods pass through it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.2] Port: the harbour on the Liffey gave the settlement a trading role, so the city grew by trade.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.3] Capital-city functions: national government, administration and services concentrate in the city, which creates jobs.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.4] Industry: brewing and food processing grew near the port, because ships brought in materials and carried out products.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.5] Planning: the Wide Streets Commissioners, the Wright Report and the Docklands Development Authority shaped the city's growth.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.b] In the 20th century the city spread outward, while its inner areas were rebuilt. Planning and regeneration (renewing run-down areas) drove both changes.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.0] Suburban expansion spread the city outward during the 20th century, because more people needed housing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.1] The Wright Report, 1967, proposed new towns at Tallaght, Clondalkin and Lucan.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.2] Ballymun high-rise flats, built in the 1960s, were demolished during regeneration from the 2000s.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.3] Temple Bar was regenerated in the 1990s as a cultural quarter, so a run-down inner-city area gained new uses.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.4] The Docklands and IFSC renewed the inner city, drawing offices and tech firms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.5] Commuter towns have expanded: Navan and Ashbourne (Meath); Naas, Newbridge and Maynooth (Kildare); Bray and Greystones (Wicklow).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.b] Rapid growth has left Dublin with problems that affect both the city and the commuter towns around it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.0] Urban sprawl spreads low-density housing into farmland and commuter towns.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.1] A housing shortage pushes up rents and house prices, because demand is greater than supply.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.2] Homelessness is a serious problem in the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.3] Commuting traffic causes congestion on the city's roads, because so many workers travel in at the same time.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.4] Growing demand puts pressure on the water supply.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.b] In the West, rural areas are losing people. In the counties around Dublin the opposite is true: the countryside is under pressure from the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.0] Rural areas of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow are under urban pressure, not decline.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.1] Commuter villages grow as city workers move out to live in the countryside, for example in Meath and Kildare.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.2] One-off houses (single houses built in the countryside) and urban-generated housing (housing built for people who work in the city) are built in rural areas.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.3] Rising land prices lead to the loss of farmland to housing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.4] Growing populations need new schools and services in rural towns and villages, so rural areas are building rather than closing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.5] Contrast: the West faces rural decline and depopulation.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.b] English is the everyday language of the GDA, but Irish and many other languages are also spoken there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.0] English is the dominant language of the GDA, so most daily life is in English.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.1] Nationally, about 1.9 million people (about 40%) said they could speak Irish in Census 2022; about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.2] Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools) are growing in number in the region, so more children are learning through Irish.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.3] Ráth Chairn, Co. Meath, is a Gaeltacht (an area where Irish is the community language) founded in 1935 by families resettled from Connemara by the Land Commission.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.4] Immigration has made the region multilingual; Polish is the most common foreign language spoken at home in Ireland (Census 2022).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.b] The GDA is mostly Roman Catholic, as is Ireland as a whole, but Dublin is less Catholic than the national figure.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.0] Nationally, about 69% of people are Roman Catholic and about 14% have no religion (Census 2022).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.1] Dublin has a lower Catholic share and a higher no-religion share than the national figure, so it is more secular than the State as a whole.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.2] The Church of Ireland has a historic presence, shown by Christ Church and St Patrick's cathedrals in Dublin.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.3] Immigration has added Muslim, Orthodox and other communities; the Islamic Cultural Centre of Ireland is in Clonskeagh.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.4] Secularisation (the decline in the role of religion in society) has weakened the role of organised religion in the city.
