TARGET geo-economic-environment-s4: 4. Sustainable economic development
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.b] Economic activity such as farming, fishing, mining and industry always affects the environment. Sustainable development is the framework within which that environmental impact is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.0] The Brundtland Report, "Our Common Future" (UN World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987), defined it as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.1] It has three pillars: environmental protection, economic growth and social fairness. Sustainable development balances all three rather than maximising one, so a plan that boosts profit but wrecks the environment is not sustainable.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.b] Five working rules turn the definition into practice. Each one protects the environment so that it can keep supporting people in the future.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.0] Renewable resources (ones that regrow or refill naturally) such as fish, forests and water should be used no faster than they regenerate, otherwise they are used up.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.1] Non-renewable resources (ones that cannot be replaced once used) should be used efficiently and recycled, so that they last longer.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.2] Pollution should be kept within what the environment can absorb, so that air, water and soil are not overloaded.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.3] The polluter pays: those who cause environmental damage bear the cost of it, which gives them a reason to cause less.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.4] The precautionary principle: act to prevent serious harm even before the harm is proven, because waiting for proof may be too late.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.b] Sustainable development became an international aim, with world meetings setting out plans for governments to follow.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.0] The Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro (1992) was a later, separate event from the Brundtland Report. It produced Agenda 21, an action plan for sustainable development.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.1] The UN Sustainable Development Goals were adopted in 2015: 17 goals with a target year of 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.b] In Ireland, the main body that controls the environmental impact of industry is the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). It was created by law and works independently of the government departments it advises.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.0] It was set up under the Environmental Protection Agency Act 1992, and the EPA began work in 1993.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.1] It is an independent public body (a State organisation not run by a government minister) with headquarters at Johnstown Castle, Co. Wexford.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.b] The EPA's most important power is licensing. Activities with a high risk of pollution may not operate unless the EPA allows it and sets the conditions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.0] Large industries need an EPA licence: pharmaceutical and chemical plants, power stations, intensive pig and poultry farms, cement works and mines. Waste facilities also need one.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.1] The licence sets emission limits for the activity, which means the maximum pollution it may release into air, water or land.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.2] Integrated pollution control means one licence covers air, water, waste and noise, instead of separate permits for each. This lets the EPA look at the whole impact of a site at once.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.b] To control pollution, the EPA first has to measure it. It tracks the state of the environment and reports what it finds.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.0] The EPA monitors water quality in rivers, lakes, estuaries (where rivers meet the sea), groundwater (water held underground in rock and soil) and bathing waters.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.1] It runs a national air quality network and produces a greenhouse gas inventory (a count of emissions of gases that cause climate change) and projections of future emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.2] Drinking water is supplied by Uisce Éireann.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.3] The State of the Environment report is published every four years, so the public and government can see whether things are improving.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.4] The EPA advises government and the public and funds environmental research.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.5] The EPA took over radiological protection in 2014, including the control of radon (a natural radioactive gas that can seep up from the ground).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.b] A licence is only useful if it is obeyed, so the EPA checks and punishes breaches.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.0] Inspections check that licensed activities keep within their emission limits.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.1] The Office of Environmental Enforcement, set up within the EPA in 2003, prosecutes polluters (takes them to court).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.2] The EPA oversees the environmental work carried out by local authorities (the county and city councils).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.b] The EPA cannot solve every problem alone. Some pollution is hard to see, track or assign to one source.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.0] The EPA cannot inspect everywhere, so some breaches go unchecked.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.1] Diffuse pollution (pollution that comes from many scattered sources rather than one pipe or chimney) from thousands of farms is hard to police.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.2] Local authorities and other bodies share responsibility for environmental control, so the EPA is one part of a wider system.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.b] Control is best done before a project starts. An environmental impact assessment is the study that looks at a project's likely effects in advance.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.0] An environmental impact assessment is a study, required before planning permission is decided for large projects, that predicts a project's effects on the environment and how to reduce them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.1] The developer's report (the developer is the company or body proposing the project) was called an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS). It is now an Environmental Impact Assessment Report (EIAR).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.b] Environmental impact assessment is not optional for big projects. It comes from EU law and is built into Irish planning rules.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.0] The EU Environmental Impact Assessment Directive, 1985, is applied in Irish planning law.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.1] Assessment is required for projects above set sizes, such as motorways, power stations, wind farms, mines and quarries, large intensive farms, pipelines, ports and airports. Small projects below the set sizes do not need one.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.b] An EIAR has to describe the project, the place it will be built and every kind of effect it could have, then say how harm will be reduced.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.0] A description of the project and of the existing environment (the baseline, meaning the condition of the site before anything is built, which later effects are measured against).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.1] Likely effects on people, plants and animals, soil, water, air, climate, landscape, archaeology and heritage, traffic and noise.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.2] Alternatives considered, such as other designs or sites, and the mitigation measures (steps to reduce or prevent harm) proposed to reduce effects.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.3] A non-technical summary for the public, so ordinary people can understand the report.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.4] The developer writes the report, often with consultants. Neither the EPA nor the planning authority writes it.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.b] The assessment follows a set order. It ends with a decision-maker who weighs the report against what the public and experts say.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.0] 1. The developer prepares the EIAR.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.1] 2. The public and bodies such as the National Parks and Wildlife Service make submissions (written comments and objections).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.2] 3. The planning authority or An Coimisiún Pleanála decides whether to grant permission, often with conditions. The board was renamed in 2025 from An Bord Pleanála, the national planning appeals board.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.b] Short questions ask why environmental impact studies are needed. There are four reasons, and Ireland's experience at Derrybrien shows what happens when the law is not followed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.0] They prevent damage before it happens, which is cheaper than paying for clean-up afterwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.1] They require developers to consider alternatives, so a less harmful design or site may be chosen.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.2] They give the public a say in decisions, through submissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.3] They are a legal requirement.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.4] At Derrybrien, Co. Galway, a peat slide on 16 October 2003 occurred during wind-farm construction. In 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland a lump sum of €5 million plus €15,000 a day because no proper environmental impact assessment had been carried out.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.b] Fish are a renewable resource, but only if people leave enough of them to breed. This idea is the basis of sustainable fishing.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.0] Fish are renewable only while catches stay below the rate at which the stock (the population of one kind of fish) breeds and grows.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.1] The maximum sustainable yield is the largest catch that can be taken year after year without reducing the stock.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.b] Overfishing means taking fish faster than stocks can replace themselves. Several causes push fleets to do this.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.0] Modern technology, including sonar (finds fish using sound), satellite navigation, factory ships (which process the catch at sea) and huge nets, lets fleets find and take fish faster than stocks can recover.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.1] Too many boats chase the same stock, so the catch is shared out among more boats and each one fishes harder.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.2] Subsidies (payments from government) keep fleets fishing when stocks are falling.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.3] Young fish are caught before they have had the chance to breed, so the stock cannot renew itself.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.4] Discards: dead or unwanted fish are thrown back into the sea, which wastes the catch.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.5] Illegal fishing takes fish outside the quota system (the limits on how much each country or boat may catch).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.b] The Grand Banks is the classic example of a fishery destroyed by overfishing. It shows how both fish stocks and jobs can be lost.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.0] The Grand Banks, off Newfoundland, Canada, were one of the world's richest cod fisheries for centuries.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.1] Catches rose sharply with factory trawlers (large boats that fish with nets and process the catch on board) from the 1960s, and the stock collapsed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.2] Canada declared a moratorium (ban) on northern cod fishing on 2 July 1992.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.3] About 30,000 people in Newfoundland lost their jobs, the largest single lay-off in Canadian history.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.4] The stock has recovered only slowly, and partly, in the decades since.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.b] North Sea herring is a second example, with a happier ending. It shows that a stock can recover when fishing is stopped and then tightly limited.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.0] North Sea herring was overfished in the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.1] The fishery was closed from 1977 to the early 1980s, which gave the stock time to breed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.2] The stock recovered after the fishery reopened under strict quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.b] In EU waters, fish stocks are managed through the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP). Here it matters as a conservation tool that tries to keep catches sustainable.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.0] The EU Common Fisheries Policy, 1983, sets total allowable catches (TACs, the maximum amount of each stock that may be caught) and divides them into national quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.1] Conservation rules include minimum mesh sizes (so small fish escape the net), minimum landing sizes, closed areas and seasons, and vessel decommissioning (scrapping boats to reduce fleet size).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.2] The CFP reform of 2013 aims to fish at maximum sustainable yield and introduces a landing obligation that ends discards, phased in from 2015 to 2019.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.3] Monitoring uses satellite tracking, the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority (set up in 2007) and fishery protection patrols by the Irish Naval Service.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.4] Ireland's exclusive economic zone (the sea area over which a country controls resources) extends 200 nautical miles from the coast, within which these rules apply.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.5] Further measures include marine protected areas (areas of sea where fishing is restricted) and aquaculture (fish farming) as an alternative to wild catch.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.b] Fishing matters to Irish coastal communities, which depend on the fish stocks around the coast for their living.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.0] Mackerel is the most valuable Irish catch, landed mainly at Killybegs, Co. Donegal, Ireland's largest fishing port.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.1] Killybegs is the base of the pelagic fleet (boats that fish species living in open water, not on the seabed) that catches mackerel and herring.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.2] Ireland has six State Fishery Harbour Centres: Killybegs, Castletownbere, Dingle, Rossaveel, Dunmore East and Howth.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.3] Irish fishing communities depend on these stocks, so conservation limits cause hardship. There is more on this in Conflicts between economic interests and environmental interests.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.b] Mining that happened before modern controls can go on polluting for generations. Avoca is the Irish example.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.0] Copper mining at Avoca, Co. Wicklow, over centuries left acid mine drainage (acidic water that runs out of old mine workings) when mining ended.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.1] The acid drainage still discolours and pollutes the Avoca River long after the mines closed, which shows why closure plans are needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.b] Today a mine has to work within licence rules. Tara Mines shows what controlled mining looks like.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath, is an underground zinc and lead mine, Europe's largest zinc mine, producing since 1977 and owned by Boliden.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.1] It operates under an EPA licence, which sets limits on its emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.2] Tailings (the waste left after the metal has been extracted) are stored in a lined tailings facility, so they do not leak into the ground, and the water is monitored.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.3] Restoration plans are in place for the site.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.b] A responsible mine plans for its end before it opens. Lisheen shows rehabilitation (restoring a site to a safe, useful condition) after closure.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.0] Lisheen, Co. Tipperary, a zinc and lead mine, closed in 2015 and the site has been rehabilitated.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.1] A new mine needs an environmental impact assessment report (EIAR) and a closure and restoration plan backed by a financial bond (money set aside in advance) before it opens, so the clean-up is paid for even if the company fails.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.b] Peat was long treated as a fuel to be dug out. The scale of that harvesting damaged Ireland's bogs.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.0] Bord na Móna, founded in 1946, carried out large-scale industrial harvesting of peat for power stations and fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.1] Harvesting removed raised bogs (domed peatlands formed over thousands of years), released the carbon they had stored and destroyed their habitats.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.b] Peat harvesting has ended, and the focus has moved to repairing the damage and finding new uses for the land.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.0] The Lanesborough and Shannonbridge peat stations closed in December 2020, and Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.1] Cutaway bogs (bogs where the peat has been removed) are being rehabilitated and rewetted, meaning water is returned to them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.2] Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly: a cutaway bog turned into parkland, wetlands and trails.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.3] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo: the peat station closed in 2003 and wind farms now stand on the cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.4] A just transition (a change that is fair to the workers affected) is needed for Midland workers who lost jobs in peat.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.b] The Aral Sea shows how diverting water for farming can destroy a whole environment and the livelihoods that depended on it.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.0] The Amu Darya and Syr Darya, which feed the Aral Sea in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, were diverted from the 1960s to irrigate (water by channels) cotton in the Soviet Union.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.1] The sea shrank to a fraction of its size and split into separate lakes, and its salinity (salt content) rose.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.2] The fishing industry collapsed, and ports such as Moynaq (Uzbekistan) were left stranded far from the water.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.3] Dust storms spread salt and pesticides from the exposed seabed, harming local health.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.4] The Kok-Aral dam (Kazakhstan, 2005) raised the level of the small northern sea, and fishing there has partly revived.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.25.b] Burning fossil fuels pollutes the air and adds to climate change, so a sustainable approach changes the energy source.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.25.0] Switching to renewable energy (energy from sources that do not run out, such as wind) reduces the environmental impact of power generation. There is more on this in Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.b] Farming can be paid and encouraged to protect the environment, not just to produce food.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.0] Agri-environment schemes pay farmers for conservation: REPS (Rural Environment Protection Scheme, 1994), now succeeded by ACRES.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.1] Origin Green, Bord Bia's sustainability programme for Irish food and drink, was launched in 2012.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.2] Nitrates rules limit the amount of nitrogen fertiliser and manure that farms can apply, because the excess can run off and pollute water.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.3] BurrenLIFE (2005 to 2010) paid Burren farmers to manage species-rich limestone grassland through winter grazing (winterage); it continued as the Burren Programme.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.4] Results-based farming in the Burren rewards farmers for measured environmental outcomes, not just for following set practices.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.b] Forests and tourist sites can be used without being destroyed if how they are used is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.0] Forests are replanted after felling, using mixed native broadleaves (a variety of native trees such as oak, rather than one species).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.1] Forests are not planted on deep peat.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.2] Visitor limits and ecotourism (tourism designed to protect nature) reduce pressure on fragile sites.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.3] At Skellig Michael, Co. Kerry, access is controlled through licensed boats and visitor limits.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.b] The best way to deal with waste is to create less of it, which is why there is an order of priority.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.0] Reduce, reuse and recycle, in that order of priority.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.1] Levies (charges) and deposit schemes (a refundable payment on containers) give people and businesses an incentive to reduce waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.2] A circular economy keeps products and materials in use for as long as possible, instead of using them once and throwing them away.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.b] The polluter pays principle is put into effect through taxes and charges that put a price on pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.0] Irish carbon tax, introduced in 2010.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.1] Plastic bag levy, introduced in March 2002, a charge on plastic bags, so shoppers have a reason to use fewer.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.2] Landfill levy, charged on waste sent to landfill, so dumping costs more than reducing waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.3] EU Emissions Trading System, from 2005: companies pay for the emissions they release.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.b] Governments have set targets for cutting emissions, and the future of sustainable development depends on meeting them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.0] The EU and Ireland aim for climate neutrality by 2050 (releasing no more greenhouse gases than are removed).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.1] Ireland's Climate Act 2021 sets a 51% cut in emissions by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.2] The Climate Action Plan sets 80% renewable electricity by 2030 and a retrofit (upgrading insulation and heating) of 500,000 homes to B2 energy rating by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.3] Offshore wind, electric vehicles, public transport and the circular economy are the main routes to lower emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.4] Cutting agricultural emissions, restoring bogs and planting forests as carbon sinks (places that absorb and store carbon).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.b] Meeting these targets will not be easy. Several obstacles stand in the way.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.0] Cost: clean technology, retrofitting and rehabilitation are expensive.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.1] Short-term economic pressure favours quick profit over long-term protection.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.2] Jobs in traditional industries, such as peat and fishing, are at risk.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.3] Global cooperation is needed, because pollution and climate change do not stop at borders.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.46.b] Spain is the European example of mass tourism on a large scale. Its growth had specific causes.

[geo-economic-activities-s1.50.b] Industries do not last forever. Industrial decline is a downward trend, and it leaves visible signs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.50.0] Industrial decline is the long-term fall in the number of firms and jobs in a traditional industry or region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.50.1] Evidence: plant closures, derelict sites and buildings, high unemployment, emigration (out-migration), falling population in industrial towns, and social problems.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.b] Irish industry declined for several reasons. Each reason is illustrated by the dated examples that follow.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.0] The end of protection and the opening to free trade exposed small, inefficient protected firms, through the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement (1965) and EEC membership (1973).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.1] Competition from low-wage countries, because they can sell more cheaply.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.2] Recessions (periods when the economy shrinks), such as those of the 1980s and 2008.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.3] New technology replacing labour, so fewer workers are needed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.4] EU policy reform, such as the reform of the EU sugar regime.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.5] Corporate decisions to move production to lower-cost locations, as with Dell in Limerick. There is more on this in the Irish examples blocks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.b] Cork lost several large employers in the 1980s, during a recession.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.0] The Dunlop tyre factory in Cork closed in 1983.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.1] Ford's Marina plant in Cork closed in 1984; Ford's Cork company was founded in 1917.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.2] Verolme Cork Dockyard at Rushbrooke closed in 1984.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.b] Further closures followed in the 2000s, each with a different cause. Dates matter here.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.0] Irish Steel (later Ispat) closed on Haulbowline Island, Cork Harbour, in 2001; the site needed a costly clean-up of contaminated waste.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.1] Irish Sugar (Greencore) closed its Carlow beet factory in 2005 and its Mallow factory in 2006, ending Irish sugar production after EU sugar-regime reform.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.2] Waterford Crystal's Kilbarry factory closed in January 2009 when the company went into receivership (when an outside manager takes control of a company that cannot pay its debts).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.3] Dell opened at Raheen, Limerick, in 1990; in January 2009 it announced that manufacturing would move to Łódź, Poland, with the loss of about 1,900 jobs, while sales and service stayed in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.b] Energy industries also declined as Ireland moved away from fossil fuels (fuels such as coal and peat, formed from ancient plants).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.0] Moneypoint, Co. Clare, a coal-fired power station built in the 1980s, was Ireland's last coal-burning power station.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.1] Coal burning at Moneypoint ended in 2025, so Ireland no longer burns coal for power, part of the move away from fossil fuels that also closed the peat stations.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.b] When industries close, the damage spreads from the workers to the whole town.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.0] Unemployment reached about 17% in the mid-1980s.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.1] Heavy emigration in the 1980s drew skilled people away from the affected towns, because there were no jobs for them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.2] Derelict docklands and empty factories were left in city and town centres.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.3] Local towns lost skills and spending as jobs disappeared.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.b] Governments and agencies tried to replace lost jobs and reuse empty sites.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.0] Replacement investment: the IDA and Enterprise Ireland sought new firms to replace lost jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.1] Regeneration (rebuilding and renewing run-down areas) of the Dublin Docklands and the IFSC. There is more on this in the IFSC blocks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.2] Regeneration of Cork Docklands, through a city plan to redevelop the old port quays.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.3] Heritage reuse: a smaller House of Waterford Crystal factory and visitor centre opened in Waterford city centre (the Mall) in 2010.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.4] Retraining for displaced workers, so they can move into new jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.5] The EU Just Transition Fund supports the Midlands peat region, because peat jobs have ended.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.57.b] Ireland is not alone in losing traditional industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.57.0] There is more on coalfield and steel regions of decline elsewhere in Europe in the Socio-economic regions notes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.b] Economic activity is unevenly spread across Ireland. Some regions have far more jobs and income than others.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.0] The Greater Dublin Area had about 2.1 million people in Census 2022, about 40% of the State's population, and holds most multinational and service jobs, along with Cork, Galway and Limerick.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.1] The Border, Midlands and West have more small farms, fewer FDI jobs and lower incomes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.2] The Border, Midland and Western (BMW) region was designated for EU funding in 1999 (Objective 1 for 2000–2006); the rest of the State was the Southern and Eastern region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.3] The National Planning Framework (Project Ireland 2040, 2018) aims for Galway, Cork, Limerick and Waterford to each grow by at least 50% by 2040, to balance growth away from Dublin.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-eu-s3: 3. How the EU influences the Irish economy
[geo-economic-eu-s3.0.b] Every member state pays into the EU budget and receives money back. Whether a country gets out more than it puts in (a net recipient) or puts in more than it gets (a net contributor) shows how its place in the EU has changed.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.0.0] Ireland was a net recipient of EU budget money from joining on 1 January 1973 until about 2014, receiving over €40 billion more than it paid in, mostly through the CAP (the Common Agricultural Policy, the EU's farm policy).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.0.1] Ireland became a net contributor in 2014, the first time since joining, because its economic growth meant it was now a richer member paying more in than it received.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.1.b] Membership brings Ireland benefits and costs. Exams ask for one or two advantages, so learn the short list and the matching costs.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.1.0] Advantages: access to the single market (the EU area of about 450 million people in 2024, where goods and services move freely), foreign direct investment (FDI, money invested in Irish operations by overseas firms), and CAP payments.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.1.1] More advantages: structural funds (EU money for poorer regions and for people) that pay for infrastructure, social and equality laws, and free movement to work and study anywhere in the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.1.2] Costs: loss of control over some policies, such as fishing and farm prices, because these are set at EU level rather than by Ireland alone.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.1.3] Further costs: net contributions to the EU budget since 2014, and competition from lower-cost members, whose firms and workers can sell and work in Ireland.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.1.4] FDI examples are Intel, Leixlip (since 1989) and Apple, Cork (since 1980). Ireland's 2010 EU/IMF bailout of about €85 billion may be named as an EU-related support.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.1.5] There is more on this in The complexity of regions (ii), which covers the effects of enlargement (the EU taking in new member states).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.2.b] The Common Agricultural Policy was launched in 1962 to run farming for all EU members as one system.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.2.0] Its aims, set out in the Treaty of Rome (the founding treaty of the EEC, the forerunner of the EU), were to raise farm productivity, give farmers a fair standard of living, stabilise markets, secure food supplies and provide reasonable prices for consumers.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.2.1] The CAP was once the largest part of the EU budget. It still takes about a third of the budget (2021-27), which shows how central farming remains to EU spending.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.3.b] To meet its aims, the early CAP guaranteed farmers a price for what they produced. This worked too well for producers and created problems for everyone else.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.3.0] Guaranteed (intervention) prices were set above world levels, so farmers were sure of a good price, and intervention buying (the EU buying up produce) took up surpluses that the market could not absorb.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.3.1] Export subsidies (payments to sell abroad) and import levies (taxes on food imported from outside the EU) supported the system by keeping EU prices above world prices.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.3.2] Results: overproduction, shown by the butter mountains and wine lakes of the 1970s and 1980s (huge stored surpluses), high cost, and dumping of surpluses on world markets (selling them abroad cheaply).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.4.b] Ireland joined in 1973, so its farmers suddenly gained access to CAP guaranteed prices. For Irish farming this was a major boost.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.4.0] Farm prices and incomes rose sharply, because Irish farmers now received the higher EU guaranteed prices.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.4.1] A guaranteed market opened for beef and dairy, so farmers knew they could sell what they produced.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.4.2] Farms modernised with machinery, fertiliser, silage (stored grass for winter feed), land drainage and farm buildings, in what is remembered as the golden age of the 1970s.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.b] Because the CAP caused surpluses, the EU limited how much milk each farm could produce. Irish dairying was held back until the limit was removed.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.0] Milk quotas, introduced in 1984, capped each farm's milk output to cut surpluses. They held back Irish dairying for 30 years.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.1] The quotas were abolished on 1 April 2015; the last quota year ended on 31 March 2015.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.2] Once the cap went, output rose: Irish milk output went from about 5.5 billion litres in 2014 to about 8.3 billion litres in 2020.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.3] From 2013 to 2023 dairy cow numbers rose about 41% and milk output about 56% (EPA, from CSO data).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.4] Expansion was strongest in the south, especially Munster.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.b] Surpluses and high costs forced the CAP to change. Each reform moved support away from guaranteed prices and towards payments tied to other goals.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.0] The MacSharry reform of 1992, led by Ray MacSharry, EU Agriculture Commissioner from Sligo, replaced price cuts with direct payments (money paid straight to farmers) and introduced set-aside (paying farmers to leave some land unused to cut output).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.1] Agenda 2000 made rural development (support for the wider countryside, not only farm output) the second pillar of the CAP.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.2] The Fischler reform, agreed in 2003, decoupled payments from production, so farmers were no longer paid according to how much they produced.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.3] The Single Farm Payment began in 2005 and was replaced by the Basic Payment Scheme from 2015.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.4] Cross-compliance links payments to environmental and animal-welfare rules, so a farmer who breaks the rules can lose payment.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.b] The current CAP period is run in Ireland through a national plan that puts a large share of the money towards the environment.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.0] Ireland's CAP Strategic Plan (its national plan for the CAP) is worth about €9.8 billion from EU and national funds, with about 45% for environmental and climate measures.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.1] Basic Income Support for Sustainability (BISS, the main direct payment to farmers) is worth about €3.6 billion. The eco-scheme (a payment for environmentally friendly farming) is worth about €1.5 billion, 25% of direct payments.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.2] Convergence (moving payments towards the national average) raises every payment entitlement to at least 85% of the national average.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.3] Payments for young farmers and the ACRES agri-environment scheme (paying farmers for farming that protects nature) are included in the plan.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.4] Areas of Natural Constraint (ANC) payments support farms on disadvantaged land, where farming is harder because of natural conditions.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.8.b] Under the CAP's second pillar, money also goes to protecting the environment and keeping the countryside economically alive, not just to farm output.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.8.0] REPS, the Rural Environment Protection Scheme, ran from 1994 to 2009 as an agri-environment scheme, paying farmers to farm in ways that protect the environment.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.8.1] LEADER, from 1991, funds local rural enterprise such as farm diversification (farmers earning from new activities), rural tourism and crafts.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.8.2] Grants support forestry and young farmers, such as those taking over family farms.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.9.b] Exams ask you to name types of farm and may ask about the number and size of farms. The Census of Agriculture 2020 (CSO) gives the picture.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.9.0] The Census of Agriculture 2020 counted 135,037 farms, down about 3% on 2010, with an average size of about 33 hectares.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.9.1] On a third (33%) of farms the holder was aged 65 or over; only 7% of holders were under 35, which shows the farming population is ageing.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.9.2] The main farm types are dairy, cattle (beef) rearing, sheep, tillage (growing crops) and mixed. Specialist beef is the most common type.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.b] For Ireland the CAP has brought security and support to farming and the countryside. These points suit questions on the positive effects of EU policies.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.0] Stable farm incomes and a guaranteed market, so farmers can plan with confidence.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.1] Modernisation of farms and food quality standards, so Irish produce meets EU rules and sells in the single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.2] Environmental schemes and support for rural communities, such as LEADER and REPS, so the countryside keeps people and income.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.3] Dependence on direct payments is high for many beef and sheep farms, which is a limit as well as a support, because those farms struggle if payments change.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.b] The same policy also has costs. To get full marks, pair each positive effect with a negative one.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.0] Payments have historically favoured bigger farmers, so most of the money went to a minority.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.1] Dependence on subsidies (payments from the state or EU) and heavy bureaucracy and inspections.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.2] Intensification (farming more intensively, with more animals and inputs) has damaged water quality through nitrates and raised greenhouse gas emissions as the dairy herd grew.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.3] The number of farms has fallen and farmers are ageing; small western farms remain uneconomic, meaning they cannot make a viable living.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.4] Trade deals such as EU-Mercosur, which concluded in December 2024 and was opposed by Irish farm organisations, are feared to undercut Irish beef.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.12.b] Intensive farming puts nitrogen into water, so the EU limits it. This shows EU environmental rules shaping Irish farming.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.12.0] The Nitrates Directive of 1991 limits the organic nitrogen (for example from slurry and manure) that can be spread per hectare.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.12.1] Ireland has a derogation (a permitted exception) that allows higher stocking (more animals per hectare) on intensive farms, conditional on water quality. EU environmental rules therefore shape farming directly.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.13.b] The Common Fisheries Policy treats EU seas as shared resources. This suited countries with big fleets more than Ireland.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.13.0] The Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) was agreed in full in 1983. It creates a common pond of EU waters, giving all members' fleets equal access beyond coastal limits.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.13.1] Its aim is to conserve fish stocks (the numbers of fish in the sea) for the long term.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.13.2] Ireland had to share its rich waters with much larger fleets, notably those of Spain, France and the Netherlands. Spain and Portugal joined in 1986.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.14.b] The CFP controls how much fish can be taken and how, through quotas and rules on fishing effort.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.14.0] Total Allowable Catches (TACs, the maximum amount of each species that can be caught) are set each December by EU fisheries ministers and divided into national quotas using fixed shares.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.14.1] A coastal band of 12 nautical miles is reserved mainly for local boats.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.14.2] Days-at-sea limits, mesh sizes (net hole size, so young fish escape), closed areas and closed seasons control fishing effort.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.14.3] The Hague Preferences, agreed in 1976, give Ireland a smaller cut when TACs fall, in recognition of its coastal communities' dependence on fishing.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.15.b] Irish fishing is centred on a small number of ports on the coast, and a state agency supports it.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.15.0] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland's largest fishing port by volume and value, specialising in pelagic fish (species living near the surface or mid-water): mackerel, herring and blue whiting.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.15.1] The other Fishery Harbour Centres are Castletownbere (Co. Cork), Ros an Mhíl (Rossaveel, Co. Galway), Dingle (Co. Kerry), Howth (Co. Dublin) and Dunmore East (Co. Waterford).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.15.2] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM) is the state seafood development agency.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.15.3] Processing and fishing jobs sustain peripheral coastal areas, which have few other sources of employment.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.16.b] For Ireland the CFP has brought funding, protection for stocks and market access.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.16.0] EU funding for boats, harbours and processing plants, now through the European Maritime, Fisheries and Aquaculture Fund.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.16.1] Conservation aims protect stocks over the long term, so there are fish left for future catches.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.16.2] Access to EU seafood markets, so Irish fish can be sold across the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.16.3] The Hague Preferences protect Ireland's share when quotas are cut.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.b] The CFP has costs for Ireland as well as benefits, because its quota share is small and stocks have not always been protected.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.0] Ireland's quota share is small relative to the size and richness of its waters.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.1] Overfishing and stock collapses have occurred despite the CFP, so its conservation aim has not always been met.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.2] Discards, the throwing back of dead fish, were common until the landing obligation, which wasted fish.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.3] Decommissioning of boats (paying owners to take boats out of service) and job losses in coastal communities.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.4] Quota cuts under Brexit.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.18.b] The 2013 reform tackled two weaknesses: wasteful discarding and overfishing.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.18.0] The reform was agreed in 2013. It introduced a landing obligation, which bans discarding, phased in between 2015 and 2019.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.18.1] It aims to fish at maximum sustainable yield, the largest catch that can be taken without running down stocks.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.18.2] There is more on this in Conflicts between economic interests and environmental interests, which covers depletion of fish stocks as an economic and environmental conflict.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.b] Brexit (the UK's leaving of the EU) hit Irish fishing hard because EU fishing quota was handed to the UK.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.0] The EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement from 1 January 2021 transferred EU quota to the UK.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.1] Ireland lost about 26% of its mackerel quota. The government's Seafood Task Force valued the annual quota loss at about €43 million by 2026.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.2] Irish ministers said Ireland's losses were about double those of other EU fleets in proportion.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.3] The Brexit Adjustment Reserve (an EU fund to help countries hit by Brexit) gave Ireland about €1 billion, the largest share of the EU-wide reserve.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.4] A €25.6 million pelagic support scheme was funded from the reserve, and a voluntary permanent cessation (decommissioning) scheme ran in 2022-23.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.20.b] The EU has rich and poor regions, and Ireland began as one of the poor ones. Regional funds exist to narrow that gap.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.20.0] The EU has regional inequality, and Ireland was a poor, peripheral member in 1973, far from the EU's economic centre.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.20.1] The European Regional Development Fund (ERDF) was set up in 1975 to fund infrastructure, enterprise, research and innovation in poorer regions.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.20.2] The Structural Funds (ERDF, the European Social Fund and the farm guidance fund) doubled after the 1988-89 reform, to prepare poorer regions for the single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.21.b] Poorer regions got the strongest support. Ireland qualified for two major schemes, one for its regions and one for the state as a whole.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.21.0] Ireland was a single Objective 1 region (the EU's poorest category) from 1989 to 1999, because its income per head was below 75% of the EU average. National Development Plans were co-financed by the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.21.1] The Cohesion Fund began in 1993-94 under the Maastricht Treaty. It serves states with income below 90% of the EU average, namely Ireland, Spain, Portugal and Greece, and funds transport and environmental projects.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.21.2] Ireland stopped qualifying for the Cohesion Fund as its income rose in the 2000s.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.22.b] By 2000 parts of Ireland had grown richer, so the country was split in two to keep some EU funding for the poorer part.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.22.0] Ireland was split into two regions. The Border, Midland and Western (BMW) region kept Objective 1 status.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.22.1] The Southern and Eastern region was in transition (being phased out of top-level support), not Objective 1.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.23.b] Regional funds paid for infrastructure and enterprise, the things poorer regions lacked.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.23.0] Roads, such as inter-urban motorways and bypasses, and public transport.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.23.1] Water and wastewater treatment, ports, airports and broadband.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.23.2] Research centres and enterprise support, such as help for new firms, which builds jobs in poorer regions.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.23.3] Better access for peripheral regions, because new roads and ports link them to markets, attraction of foreign direct investment, and a contribution to the Celtic Tiger (Ireland's rapid economic boom).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.24.b] In the current period, the money is allocated by region and each region's classification depends on its income relative to the EU average.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.24.0] ERDF regional programmes are run by the regional assemblies. Two regional programmes are worth about €853 million in total, of which about €396 million comes from the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.24.1] The Northern and Western Region was reclassified from a more developed to a transition region, because its income relative to the EU average fell.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.24.2] The Southern and Eastern and Midland regions remain more developed regions.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.24.3] Three regional assemblies have operated since 2015, so each region has its own body to run its programme.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.25.b] Besides the ERDF, other EU funds target particular parts of Ireland.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.25.0] PEACE PLUS (2021-27) and INTERREG support the border region. There is more on this in The complexity of regions (i).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.25.1] The Just Transition Fund (2021-27) targets the Midlands peat region after Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting for electricity, because jobs there depended on peat.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.26.b] The funds achieved a lot, but not everything. A balanced answer gives both sides.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.26.0] The funds helped Ireland converge with EU incomes and modernised its infrastructure.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.26.1] The gap between the Dublin-centred east and the Northern and Western region has persisted.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.26.2] As a wealthy member, Ireland now receives far less, and EU regional funds go mainly to eastern Europe, an effect of enlargement.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.27.b] Where regional funds target places, social funding targets people: their jobs, skills and inclusion.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.27.0] The ESF was set up by the Treaty of Rome in 1957 and is the oldest structural fund.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.27.1] It improves employment, skills and social inclusion (making sure disadvantaged groups are not left out of work and education).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.28.b] In Ireland ESF money has paid for training and education, particularly for people who found it hard to get work.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.28.0] The ESF co-financed training through FÁS (1988-2013), replaced in 2013 by SOLAS and 16 Education and Training Boards.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.28.1] Youthreach, begun in 1989, helps early school-leavers. Post-Leaving Cert courses, apprenticeships and back-to-education schemes are also co-financed.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.28.2] Programmes target the long-term unemployed, people with disabilities, Travellers and women returning to work.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.28.3] The skilled workforce created this way helped attract foreign direct investment.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.29.b] The ESF is now called ESF+ and in Ireland it is delivered through one programme, paid for by both the EU and the Irish state.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.29.0] The Employment, Inclusion, Skills and Training (EIST) programme for 2021-2027 is worth about €1.08 billion: about €508 million from the EU and about €573 million national.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.29.1] It was launched in March 2023, so the 2021-2027 period began delivery only partway through.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.29.2] Priorities: about €500 million for social inclusion, about €280 million for upskilling and lifelong learning, and over €180 million for employment, mainly of young people and the long-term unemployed.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.30.b] Global trade can move jobs abroad. The EGF helps workers who lose their jobs this way to retrain.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.30.0] The EGF was set up in 2007 to retrain workers made redundant by shifts in global trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.30.1] In 2009 the EU approved about €14.8 million to help about 2,400 former Dell and supplier workers in the Limerick area. Dell had announced in January 2009 that it was moving manufacturing from Limerick to Łódź in Poland.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.30.2] Waterford Crystal workers also received EGF support, as another example of jobs lost to global trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.31.b] The EU also shapes Irish society through laws. Irish equality and workplace rights follow EEC and EU directives (laws that member states must put into their own law).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.31.0] The civil service marriage bar (the rule forcing women to leave their jobs on marrying) ended in 1973. Irish equality laws followed EEC directives: the Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 and the Employment Equality Act 1977.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.31.1] The Organisation of Working Time Act 1997, which applied the EU Working Time Directive, set an average maximum working week of 48 hours.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.31.2] EU directives also set rules on health and safety, maternity and parental leave, and the rights of part-time workers.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.32.b] EU membership also gives citizens freedom to move and study across member states.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.32.0] Irish people can live and work anywhere in the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.32.1] The Erasmus student exchange programme, from 1987, lets students study abroad.
