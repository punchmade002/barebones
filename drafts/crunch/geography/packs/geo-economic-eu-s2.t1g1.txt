TARGET geo-economic-eu-s2: 2. Irish trading patterns with the EU
[geo-economic-eu-s2.0.b] Irish trading patterns changed enormously after 1973, so you need to know the starting point. Before Ireland joined the EEC (the European Economic Community, the forerunner of the EU), its trade depended on one neighbour, the UK.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.0.0] Before 1973 Irish trade depended on the UK. In 1960 about three-quarters (74%) of Irish exports went to the UK, mainly live cattle and other farm produce, so Ireland sold mostly raw farm output to a single customer.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.0.1] The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement, signed in 1965, governed trade with the UK (free trade means few or no tariffs, the taxes charged on imports).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.0.2] Protectionism, meaning taxes and limits on imports to shield home industry, was dismantled from the late 1950s, which opened the Irish economy to outside trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.1.b] Membership of the EEC gave Ireland a way to trade beyond the UK. The decision was put to the people first.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.1.0] Ireland joined the EEC on 1 January 1973, together with the UK and Denmark.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.1.1] A referendum (a public vote on one question) on 10 May 1972 returned about 83% Yes, which shows strong public support for joining.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.2.b] Diversification means spreading sales across more customers instead of relying on one. After 1973 Ireland did this, and the UK's role shrank.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.2.0] The UK's share of Irish exports fell from about three-quarters in 1960 to a small fraction today. In February 2024 Great Britain took about 8% of Irish goods exports in that month alone, which is under a tenth.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.2.1] As the UK's share fell, the rest of the EU and the USA became far more important, so Ireland is no longer tied to a single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.3.b] It is not only where Ireland sells that changed but what it sells. Exports moved from raw farm produce to goods and services of much higher value.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.3.0] Exports moved from mainly agricultural goods and live animals to high-value manufactured goods (goods that sell for a lot relative to their size). Pharmaceuticals and chemicals, medical devices, and computers and electronics grew strongly in the 1990s and 2000s.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.3.1] Processed food and drink replaced live animals as the main farm exports, and services (such as software and finance, rather than physical goods) became a larger part of the economy's trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.3.2] Exports far exceed imports. Ireland has a very large goods trade surplus (exports worth more than imports), mainly because foreign multinationals (companies that operate in several countries) export most of what they make in Ireland.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.4.b] Use 2024 as the main current year: it is a normal year, unlike 2025, when exports surged. These three figures show the size of Ireland's trade and its surplus.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.4.0] Goods exports were about €224 billion in 2024, a record and up about 14% on 2023.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.4.1] Goods imports were about €135 billion in 2024, giving a goods surplus (exports minus imports) of about €89 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.5.b] A trading bloc is a group of countries that trade freely together. Ireland sells most to two destinations, the EU 27 (the 27 member states) as a bloc and the USA as a single country.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.5.0] The EU 27 took about €88 billion of Irish goods exports in 2024, about 40%, making it the largest bloc.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.5.1] The USA took about €73 billion, about one-third of exports, and is Ireland's largest single export market, ahead of any one EU country.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.5.2] The main EU markets in 2024 were the Netherlands (about €23 billion), Germany (about €20 billion), Belgium (about €17 billion) and then France.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.6.b] Imports are the goods Ireland buys from abroad. The EU is again the biggest source, but the pattern is more spread out than for exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.6.0] The EU 27 supplied about €50 billion of Irish imports in 2024, about 37%, making it the largest bloc.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.6.1] Asia supplied about €31 billion, the USA about €23 billion and the UK about €22 billion. The USA and the UK are the largest single countries.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.6.2] Asia, especially China, is growing as a source of imports.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.7.b] A few product groups make up most of what Ireland sells abroad. One of them dominates.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.7.0] Medical and pharmaceutical products are the largest export group, at about €100 billion in 2024 (about 45% of goods exports), roughly half the total.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.7.1] Organic chemicals, medical devices, and food and drink follow as the other main exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.8.b] What Ireland buys reflects what it lacks and what its industries and consumers need.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.8.0] Fuels, because Ireland imports most of its energy.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.8.1] Machinery and transport equipment, including aircraft for the leasing industry (firms that buy aircraft and rent them to airlines).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.8.2] Chemicals, consumer goods and food.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.b] 2025 was an unusual year. Preliminary CSO (Central Statistics Office) figures show a record, driven by firms moving exports forward to beat US tariffs (taxes on imported goods).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.0] Preliminary CSO figures show goods exports of about €260 billion in 2025, up about 16% and a record. Pharmaceutical exports to the USA were brought forward (front-loaded) before threatened US tariffs.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.1] Medical and pharmaceutical products were about €139 billion in 2025, about 53% of goods exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.2] The USA took about 43% of exports in 2025. Imports were about €144 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.3] Exports to the USA fell sharply at the end of 2025. The EU-USA deal of July 2025 capped US tariffs on most EU goods, including pharmaceuticals, at 15%.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.9.4] The surge shows how dependent the totals are on one product and one market, so 2024 is the better guide to a typical year.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.10.b] Ireland sells services as well as goods. Many are sold by multinationals based in Dublin, and much of this trade goes to EU customers.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.10.0] Ireland is a major exporter of services, especially computer and software services sold by multinationals such as Google, Meta and Microsoft, whose European headquarters are in Dublin.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.10.1] Financial services are based at the International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) in Dublin Docklands, which opened in 1987. Aircraft leasing is another major service.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.10.2] Much of this services trade is sold into EU markets from an Irish base.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.b] Farming is still central to Irish trade. Ireland produces far more food than it needs, so the surplus must be sold abroad.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.0] Ireland exports about 90% of its beef and dairy output, and produces far more food than its population eats.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.1] Food, drink and horticulture (growing fruit, vegetables and flowers) exports were about €17 billion in 2024, a record and up about 5% (Bord Bia, the Irish food board).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.2] Of these exports, about 35% (about €5.9 billion) went to the EU and about €5.2 billion to markets outside the EU and UK; the UK took the remainder, about a third.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.11.3] Dairy, at about €6.3 billion, was the largest category.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.b] Food exports show how Ireland sells to both the EU and the UK, and Irish brands carry the products into those markets.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.0] The UK remains the largest single market for Irish food, though not for Irish goods overall.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.1] Meat and livestock exports in 2024 went about 44% to the EU and about 42% to the UK, so they are split almost equally.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.2] Kerrygold butter, marketed by Ornua (formerly the Irish Dairy Board), sells in Germany, one of its biggest markets, and other EU countries.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.12.3] Irish beef is sold through EU retailers, and Irish whiskey and Baileys are sold internationally.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.b] Several features of EU membership make the EU the natural market for Irish firms. Each one lowers the cost or risk of selling there.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.0] Single market access: the single market lets goods move tariff-free and barrier-free among member states, giving Irish firms access to about 450 million consumers in 2024.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.1] The euro: Ireland adopted it in 1999 and notes and coins followed in 2002. Trade with eurozone countries (those using the euro) carries no exchange-rate risk, because there is no currency to change.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.2] Sterling (the British pound) fell sharply after the UK referendum of 23 June 2016, which hurt Irish food exporters to the UK because sterling earnings bought fewer euros. This makes UK trade riskier.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.13.3] CAP (the Common Agricultural Policy) access and common standards opened EU food markets to Irish farm produce. There is more on this in How the EU influences the Irish economy.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.14.b] Foreign direct investment (FDI) means a foreign company setting up operations in Ireland. It is another reason Irish trade with the EU is so large.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.14.0] US and other multinationals locate in Ireland to supply the EU market from inside it. Examples include Intel at Leixlip, Co. Kildare (since 1989), Apple in Cork (since 1980) and Pfizer.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.14.1] Ireland attracts them with an English-speaking, educated workforce, a corporation tax (tax on company profits) rate of 12.5%, and support from IDA Ireland, the state agency that attracts foreign investment.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.14.2] From 2024 firms with turnover over €750 million pay a minimum tax of 15%. There is more on the multinationals in The Global Economy notes.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.15.b] Ireland's position shapes how goods leave and arrive. Being an island makes sea links the main route to Europe.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.15.0] Ireland is an island on the periphery (the edge) of Europe, so most trade goes by sea.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.15.1] Dublin Port is Ireland's largest port. Rosslare Europort in Co. Wexford is the main port for direct ferries to continental Europe. Cork and Shannon Foynes also handle trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.15.2] High-value, light goods such as pharmaceuticals and electronics go by air.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.b] Brexit, the UK's departure from the EU, changed the rules for Ireland's trade with its nearest neighbour. It pushed Irish trade further towards the rest of the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.0] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020. The transition ended on 31 December 2020, and the Trade and Cooperation Agreement has applied since 1 January 2021.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.1] Trade with Great Britain now crosses an EU external border, with customs declarations (forms listing goods crossing the border) and checks on food and animals.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.2] The UK landbridge, trucks crossing Britain to reach the continent, became slower. From 2 January 2021 DFDS ran a direct Rosslare-Dunkirk freight ferry, six times a week. By April 2021 there were about 36 direct weekly sailings from Rosslare to continental Europe.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.3] Rosslare also has direct sailings to Cherbourg, Saint-Malo and Roscoff in France, and to Bilbao in Spain.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.4] Imports from Great Britain fell after 2021 as some importers switched to EU suppliers. Cross-border trade with Northern Ireland grew, because Northern Ireland stays in the EU single market for goods under the Windsor Framework of February 2023. There is more on this in The complexity of regions (i).
[geo-economic-eu-s2.16.5] Brexit raised the share of Irish trade with the rest of the EU and reduced Ireland's dependence on the UK further.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.17.b] The pattern has weaknesses as well as strengths. Concentrating trade in a few places leaves Ireland exposed.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.17.0] Over-reliance on a few multinationals, one sector (pharmaceuticals) and one market (the USA) leaves exports exposed to US tariffs and to corporate decisions, such as a firm moving production.
[geo-economic-eu-s2.17.1] Ireland's peripheral location adds transport costs, so sea links to the continent are vital.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-eu-s1: 1. EU trading patterns
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.b] A trading bloc is a group of countries that remove trade barriers between themselves and act together in trade with the rest of the world. The EU is the biggest example, and its two founding trade tools are a customs union and the single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.0] The EU accounted for about 13% of world goods trade in 2024, second to China in goods. Counting goods and services together, the EU is the world's largest trader, with China and the USA as the other two of the three largest.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.1] Customs union: completed on 1 July 1968 (as the EEC, the European Economic Community, the EU's earlier name), it removes tariffs (taxes on imports) between members and applies a common external tariff to imports from outside the EU, so every member charges the same rate.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.b] The single market, in force from 1 January 1993 and set up by the Single European Act of 1986, goes beyond the customs union. A customs union only deals with tariffs, whereas the single market also tackles every other obstacle to trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.0] It guarantees the four freedoms: free movement of goods, services, capital (money and investment) and people.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.1] It removes non-tariff barriers (obstacles to trade other than taxes): border checks, different national product standards and national preference in public contracts (governments favouring their own firms).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.2] Common standards, such as the CE mark (a label showing a product meets EU rules), replace those barriers, so one product can be sold in every member state.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.3] The single market extends beyond the 27 members: the European Economic Area (EEA) brings in Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein, while Switzerland, which is in neither the EU nor the EEA, trades under bilateral agreements (separate deals made with the EU).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.b] Trade questions use a small set of terms, and examiners expect you to define them precisely.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.0] Imports are goods or services bought from abroad; exports are goods or services sold abroad.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.1] Visible trade is trade in goods, things you can see and touch; invisible trade is trade in services, such as finance, tourism and transport.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.2] Balance of trade: the value of exports minus the value of imports. A positive balance is a surplus (the country sells more than it buys); a negative balance is a deficit (it buys more than it sells).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.3] A tariff is a tax on imports; a quota is a limit on the quantity of imports allowed. Both make imports harder to sell.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.4] Intra-EU (internal) trade is trade between member states; extra-EU (external) trade is trade with countries outside the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.b] Most member states trade mainly with each other, because the single market makes it the easiest and cheapest option. The share, though, varies a great deal from country to country.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.0] In 2024 intra-EU goods exports were about €4.0 trillion, against about €2.6 trillion of extra-EU goods exports. So about three-fifths (about 60%) of members' goods exports stayed within the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.1] Germany is by far the largest intra-EU trader, with intra-EU goods exports of about €845 billion in 2024; Cyprus is the smallest, at about €1 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.2] The core of the EU, the Blue Banana (a curved band of dense industry and population) from south-east England through the Benelux countries and the Rhine valley to northern Italy, together with the Paris Basin, dominates internal trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.3] Peripheral members (those on the edge of the EU, far from the core), such as Cyprus and Greece, trade much less within the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.4] Eastern members depend most on intra-EU trade. Intra-EU trade was about 81% of Czechia's goods trade in 2024, compared with about 30% for Cyprus, because of their proximity to Germany and their integration into its supply chains (the linked steps in making a product, spread across countries).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.b] The goods that cross borders inside the EU are mostly high-value manufactures. The pattern of trade takes three forms: swapping similar goods, linked production chains, and swapping different goods.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.0] Manufactured goods dominate: machinery, vehicles and parts, and chemicals and pharmaceuticals. Food and farm produce come next, and services such as finance, tourism and transport add to the total.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.1] Intra-industry trade: members swap similar goods, such as cars for cars, and components cross borders several times in supply chains before the finished product is sold.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.2] Since the 2004 enlargement the car industry has linked Germany with Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary and Poland, where assembly plants supply the German-centred industry. Volkswagen took over Škoda (Czechia) in stages from 1991 and has fully owned it since 2000.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.3] Slovakia makes more cars per head of population than any other country.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.4] Complementary trade (members swapping goods the other lacks): greenhouse fruit and vegetables from Almería in southern Spain are trucked north to northern Europe, while northern manufactured goods flow south and east.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.b] Some trade figures look larger than the country's own economy would suggest, because goods pass through its ports on the way to somewhere else.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.0] Rotterdam in the Netherlands is Europe's largest port and Antwerp in Belgium is the second largest; both sit at the mouth of the Rhine-Meuse-Scheldt delta, where the Rhine reaches the sea.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.1] Goods entering and leaving the single market through these ports are counted as trade with the Netherlands and Belgium, which gives both countries very large trade totals.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.2] The Rotterdam effect: goods recorded as trade with the Netherlands may be going on to other member states, so the figure overstates what the Netherlands itself buys or sells.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.b] Trade inside the EU is high because several things lower its costs and widen its market. Exams ask you to explain these factors, so give each with its reason.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.0] Removal of tariffs and border controls: the customs union, the single market and Schengen border-free travel for most members cut costs and delays.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.1] The euro: 21 countries used it from 1 January 2026, with Bulgaria the newest. Trade among them has no exchange costs or exchange-rate risk, and prices are easy to compare.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.2] The euro is not the currency of the whole EU, which has 27 members. The euro's history is in The complexity of regions (i).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.3] Proximity and transport: short distances, dense motorway and rail networks, the Rhine (navigable to Basel in Switzerland), the Channel Tunnel (opened 1994) and the Øresund Bridge between Copenhagen and Malmö (opened 2000).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.4] EU-funded Trans-European Transport Networks (TEN-T) also add links, so goods move quickly and cheaply between member states.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.5] Common policies: the CAP (Common Agricultural Policy, the EU's farm policy) and common food standards make farm and food trade easy within the EU. There is more on the CAP in How the EU influences the Irish economy.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.b] The size of the market and changes in membership also shape internal trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.0] A large, wealthy market: about 450 million people in 2024, with high incomes and broadly similar tastes, so a product made for one country sells easily in others.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.1] Enlargement widened the single market: ten countries joined in 2004, Bulgaria and Romania in 2007 and Croatia in 2013. New members opened new markets and lower-cost production sites in the east.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.2] Brexit moved the UK from internal to external partner. The UK left on 31 January 2020; the Trade and Cooperation Agreement has applied since 1 January 2021, with zero tariffs and zero quotas on qualifying goods (those meeting rules of origin).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.3] Trade with the UK nonetheless became more costly because of customs declarations, checks and rules-of-origin paperwork.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.b] In trade with the outside world the EU does not act as 27 separate countries. Its common trade policy gives it a single negotiating position.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.0] The European Commission (the EU's executive body) negotiates trade for all 27 members under the Common Commercial Policy, so the EU speaks with one voice in the World Trade Organization (WTO, the body that sets global trade rules).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.1] All members apply the same common external tariff, so the EU bargains as one bloc rather than as 27 separate countries, which gives it more weight.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.b] Outside the EU, a handful of big economies take most of the EU's goods and supply most of its imports. The EU sells more to them than it buys overall.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.0] Export partners for extra-EU goods: the USA is the largest at about 21%, then the UK at about 13%, China at about 8%, Switzerland at about 7-8% and Turkey at about 4%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.1] Import partners for extra-EU goods: China is the largest at about a fifth (about 20-21%), then the USA at about 13-14%, the UK at about 7%, Switzerland at about 6% and Turkey at about 4%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.2] Overall, extra-EU goods exports were about €2.58 trillion and imports about €2.43 trillion, a goods surplus (exports exceed imports) of about €150 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.3] The EU had a surplus with the USA and a large deficit with China, meaning it bought far more from China than it sold there. It also had a surplus with the UK.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.b] The EU sells high-value manufactured goods and buys energy, raw materials and cheaper manufactured goods, because it has little energy of its own.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.0] Exports: machinery and vehicles were about €1.0 trillion (about 39% of extra-EU exports); chemicals and related products, including pharmaceuticals, were about €560 billion (about 22%). Food, drink and services are also exported.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.1] Imports: energy products, mainly oil and gas, were about 15% of EU imports by value in 2024, down from about 23% in 2022.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.2] Imports also include raw materials, consumer electronics and other manufactured goods from China and Asia, and tropical foods such as coffee, cocoa and bananas, which cannot be grown in the EU's climate.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.b] Political events change trade patterns quickly, as the change in energy imports shows.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.0] Russia was a major gas supplier before its full-scale invasion of Ukraine began on 24 February 2022.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.1] Russian gas fell from about 45% of EU gas imports in 2021 to about 19% in 2024, through sanctions (penalties that restrict trade with a country) and the REPowerEU plan (the EU's plan to cut energy dependence on Russia, launched May 2022).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.2] The USA supplied almost half of EU liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so ships can carry it) imports in 2024, replacing much of the lost Russian gas.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.3] The EU plans to end Russian gas imports by the end of 2027.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.b] The EU widens its external trade by signing agreements with other countries. It also gives poorer countries easier access to its market, though critics say its past policies hurt them.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.0] Trade agreements widen external trade: CETA with Canada has been provisionally applied (in use before final approval) since September 2017; the EU-Japan Economic Partnership Agreement has been in force since February 2019; the EU-South Korea agreement has applied since 2011.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.1] EU-Mercosur negotiations with Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay concluded in December 2024. Irish farm organisations strongly opposed the deal because of competition from South American beef.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.2] The Lomé Convention (1975, with African, Caribbean and Pacific (ACP) countries) and the Cotonou Agreement (2000) gave preferential access (easier, lower-tariff entry to the EU market) to poorer countries.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.3] Everything But Arms (2001) gives the least developed countries duty-free, quota-free access for all goods except arms.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.4] Critics argue that tariffs on processed goods (tariff escalation, where the tariff rises as a product is processed further) and past subsidised farm exports (sold cheaply with EU government support) harmed producers in the developing world.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.b] The EU also uses tariffs to protect its industries, and it is on the receiving end of other countries' tariffs. In each case it acts as a single bloc.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.0] The EU-USA agreement of July 2025 capped the US tariff on most EU goods at 15%, including pharmaceuticals; Irish pharmaceutical exports to the USA are among the goods it covers. Steel and aluminium remained at 50%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.1] EU definitive duties (lasting tariffs, not temporary ones) on Chinese-made electric cars, from October 2024, reached up to about 35%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.2] In both disputes the EU acted as one bloc, so one set of duties or one deal applies to all 27 members.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.b] Several factors explain why the EU trades with the outside world as it does. Exams ask you to explain them after describing the patterns.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.0] The size and wealth of the single market gives the EU bargaining power in trade negotiations, because other countries want access to its consumers.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.1] High-tech, high-value industry makes manufactured goods the main exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.2] A lack of energy and some raw materials makes the EU dependent on imports, especially of fuels.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.3] Historical and colonial links, including former colonies in Africa, the Caribbean and the Pacific and the Commonwealth ties of the former UK member, shaped the preferential agreements.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.4] Globalisation (the growing worldwide linking of economies): low-cost manufacturing in China and Asia draws in imports.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.5] The trading triad is the three main trading regions, the USA, the EU and the Pacific Rim (the countries around the Pacific Ocean). There is more on the trading triad in Patterns in world trade, in The Global Economy.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.6] Politics redirects trade: sanctions on Russia, US tariff disputes and Brexit each changed the direction of trade flows.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.b] Exam data questions give export and import shares for each partner. Use the invented values below only to practise the method.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.0] Identify the largest partner: the biggest slice or the highest value.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.1] Calculate a missing share: subtract the known shares from 100. Illustrative: exports to Country A 25% and Country B 15% leave 100 minus 25 minus 15 = 60% for all other partners.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.2] Compare exports and imports with one partner. If the value sent to it is higher than the value received, the EU has a surplus with that partner; if it is lower, a deficit.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-activities-s1: 1. Case study of a developed economy: Ireland
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.b] Economic activities (the ways people earn a living) are grouped into three sectors by what they do with raw materials or with people's needs. Knowing which sector a job belongs to is the first step in describing any economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.0] Primary activity is extracting raw materials from nature, such as farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying and energy extraction. Nothing is made yet, so the resource is simply taken from the land or sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.1] Secondary activity is processing raw materials or assembling components (parts) into goods, such as manufacturing, food processing and construction.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.2] Tertiary activity is services, which provide help or access rather than goods, such as retail, banking, tourism, teaching, health, transport and IT (information technology).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.3] Classifying jobs: a miner or forester is primary; a builder is secondary, because construction is secondary; a teacher, banker or tour guide is tertiary.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.4] Some writers add a quaternary sector for research and information, but the three-sector model is used here.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.b] The share of workers in each sector changes as a country becomes richer. Ireland shows the end result of that shift.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.0] As an economy develops, employment moves from primary to secondary and then to tertiary activity. This is why primary employment falls as countries develop.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.1] Services employ about three-quarters of Irish workers (2020s), which means Ireland is a tertiary economy; agriculture, forestry and fishing employ about 4–5%.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.2] Census 2022 counted a State population of about 5.1 million; the CSO (Central Statistics Office) Labour Force Survey (2024) recorded over 2.7 million people in employment.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.3] In a developing economy, by contrast, primary employment is high. There is more on this in the Economic Development notes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.b] For its first decades as an independent State, Ireland tried to shelter its own firms from foreign competition. This is the starting point of the timeline of how Ireland became a developed economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.0] The Control of Manufactures Acts, 1932 and 1934, restricted foreign ownership of Irish firms, so industry stayed in Irish hands.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.1] High tariffs (taxes on imported goods) protected small Irish firms serving the home market, so they had little pressure to become efficient or to export.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.2] The 1950s brought high unemployment and heavy emigration (people leaving the country to live abroad), which showed that protection had not created enough jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.b] From the late 1950s Ireland reversed its policy and opened up to trade and foreign companies. Each step below was meant to bring in jobs and exports.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.0] The Industrial Development Authority (IDA) was set up in 1949 to attract industry, so Ireland had an agency for winning new firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.1] Export Profits Tax Relief, 1956, removed tax on profits from exports, so firms had a reason to sell abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.2] T.K. Whitaker's Economic Development and the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958) set the policy of free trade, foreign investment and export-led growth (growth driven by selling abroad), carried out under Seán Lemass as Taoiseach from 1959.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.3] The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement (1965) reduced trade barriers with Britain, Ireland's nearest and biggest market.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.4] Ireland joined the EEC (European Economic Community, the forerunner of the EU) on 1 January 1973, together with the UK and Denmark.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.b] Attracting foreign firms also needed people with the right skills. Free schooling was the policy that built them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.0] Free second-level education was announced in 1966 by Donogh O'Malley and introduced in 1967.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.1] A better-educated workforce made Ireland attractive to firms that needed skilled labour in the decades that followed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.2] The IDA became an independent agency under the Industrial Development Act 1969, so it could pursue foreign investment in its own right.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.b] Later steps tied Ireland more closely to Europe and set the tax rates that attract multinational companies. Corporation tax is the tax a company pays on its profits.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.0] The EU Single Market began in 1993, giving Irish firms and foreign investors access to the wider EU market without trade barriers.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.1] The euro was adopted in 1999, with notes and coins introduced in 2002, which means Irish firms trade with the eurozone (the countries using the euro) without exchange-rate costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.2] Corporation tax on trading profits was 12.5% from 2003. This low rate is a main reason multinationals locate in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.3] From 2024 very large multinational groups (global turnover over €750 million) pay a 15% minimum rate under the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) agreement.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.b] The Celtic Tiger is the name for Ireland's period of very rapid growth. It followed from the earlier policies of free trade, investment and education.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.0] Rapid growth from the mid-1990s to 2007 was driven first by exports and foreign direct investment (FDI, money invested by foreign firms in Irish plants), especially from US technology and pharmaceutical firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.1] A property and construction boom added to growth later in the period, because rising prices encouraged more building.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.2] Unemployment fell to about 4–5% by the mid-2000s, because so many new jobs were created.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.3] Net immigration (more people arriving than leaving) replaced emigration, with large inflows from the new EU states after the 2004 enlargement.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.b] The property boom ended in a crash that put Irish banks in danger. The State stepped in to protect them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.0] The property bubble (a rise in prices that could not last) burst in 2007–2008, leaving banks with large bad loans (loans unlikely to be repaid) secured on property that had lost value.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.1] On 30 September 2008 the government guaranteed Irish bank liabilities, promising that the banks' debts would be covered.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.2] Anglo Irish Bank was nationalised (taken into State ownership) in January 2009.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.3] The National Asset Management Agency (NAMA), set up in 2009, took over property loans from the banks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.b] The cost of rescuing the banks left the State unable to borrow normally, so it needed outside help. The crash also hit jobs and population.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.0] An EU–IMF (International Monetary Fund) programme was agreed in November 2010, providing €67.5 billion in external loans.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.1] Ireland left the programme in December 2013, which means the State could borrow on normal markets again.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.2] Unemployment peaked at about 16% in early 2012, so net emigration resumed from 2009 to 2014.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.3] Government debt peaked at about 120% of GDP (the value of everything produced in the country in a year) in 2012: the state borrowed to rescue the banks while its tax base (the income and spending that can be taxed) collapsed with the economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.b] After 2013 the economy recovered strongly. Jobs returned and public finances improved.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.0] Unemployment was about 4–5% in 2023–2024, near full employment (almost everyone who wants work has it).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.1] Budget surpluses (tax income greater than spending) in the 2020s came largely from corporation tax paid by a small number of large multinationals, so public finances depend on them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.2] COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020–2021 hit tourism, hospitality and retail hardest.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.b] When most people who want work have it, the whole economy gains, not only the workers. Each benefit below follows from people earning and spending.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.0] Employed people pay income tax, and their spending pays VAT (value-added tax, a tax on goods and services), so tax revenue rises.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.1] Welfare spending falls because fewer people need unemployment payments, so the State has more to spend elsewhere.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.2] Consumer spending by employed people supports jobs in other sectors, such as shops and cafes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.3] Less emigration keeps skilled people in the economy, so firms can find the workers they need.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.4] Government revenue can pay for investment in infrastructure (roads, rail, utilities) and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.b] Because so many multinationals are based in Ireland, their profits can distort the national figures. This makes GDP a poor guide to how well Irish people are doing.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.0] In 2015 Irish GDP grew by about 26% after multinationals moved intellectual property assets (such as patents and software rights) to Ireland; the effect was nicknamed 'leprechaun economics'.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.1] Because GDP is inflated by these profits, the CSO introduced modified gross national income (GNI*) in 2017 to strip out their effects.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.b] Farming is Ireland's main primary activity, and its pattern follows the climate. Mild, wet weather suits grass, so animals matter more than crops.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.0] The Census of Agriculture 2020 counted about 135,000 farms, averaging about 33 hectares.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.1] Grass grows for most of the year because winters are mild and rain is reliable, so grass-based livestock farming dominates.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.2] Pastoral farming is livestock farming: beef and dairy cattle, sheep and pigs. The national cattle herd is about 7 million (2020s), which is why beef and dairy cattle are central to farming.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.3] Arable (tillage) farming grows crops: barley is the largest tillage crop, with wheat, oats and potatoes also grown.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.4] Tillage is concentrated in the south and east, in Counties Wexford, Carlow, Kildare, Meath and Cork.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.b] Livestock farming earns money abroad, but a large herd also damages the environment. Exams ask for a benefit and a problem.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.0] EU milk quotas (limits on how much milk each country could produce) ended on 1 April 2015, and dairy cow numbers and milk output rose strongly afterwards.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.1] About 90% of Irish beef and dairy output is exported (Bord Bia, 2020s).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.2] A large cattle herd brings exports, food-processing jobs and incomes to rural areas.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.3] Agriculture is the largest single source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions (gases that warm the atmosphere), about 38% (EPA, 2022 data), with methane from livestock a main part of this.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.4] Nitrate and phosphate runoff (fertiliser and slurry washed from fields) pollutes rivers and lakes, and livestock release ammonia.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.b] Agri-food means food and drink made from farm produce. Irish brands turn farm output into exports, and exams ask for one food and one drink product.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.0] Kerrygold butter is exported by Ornua, the dairy co-operatives' export company; the brand was launched in 1962.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.1] Kerry Group (headquarters Tralee, Co. Kerry) and Glanbia (headquarters Kilkenny) are major food companies, such as those that process Irish farm output.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.2] Tayto crisps were first made in 1954 by Joe 'Spud' Murphy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.3] Bord Bia, the Irish food board, promotes Irish food, so its exports are better known abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.4] Guinness is made at St James's Gate Brewery, Dublin, a site dating from 1759.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.5] Jameson Irish whiskey is made at Midleton Distillery, Co. Cork; its producer, Irish Distillers, is owned by Pernod Ricard.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.6] Baileys Irish Cream was launched in 1974.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.b] Ireland's geography gives it good conditions for fishing. Several harbours are officially designated as fishing centres.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.0] Ireland has a long, indented coastline with sheltered harbours, so boats can land fish safely, and a wide continental shelf (shallow seabed around the coast where fish feed).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.1] Plankton-rich waters and the North Atlantic Drift (a warm ocean current) support productive stocks, especially pelagic (surface-swimming) fish such as mackerel.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.2] Six State Fishery Harbour Centres: Killybegs (Co. Donegal), Castletownbere (Co. Cork), Dingle (Co. Kerry), Rossaveel (Co. Galway), Dunmore East (Co. Waterford) and Howth (Co. Dublin).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.3] Killybegs is Ireland's largest fishing port and lands mainly pelagic fish, such as mackerel.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.4] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM) is the Irish sea fisheries board, so it is the State body for the industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.b] Irish fishing is limited by EU rules, but it still matters to the coastal places that depend on it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.0] The EU Common Fisheries Policy (1983) sets fishing quotas (limits on the catch) for member states, so Irish boats cannot catch as much as they wish.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.1] The 2020 EU–UK Brexit trade agreement reduced Irish quotas, which means Irish fishers may catch less.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.2] Mackerel is the most valuable species landed in Ireland, which is why pelagic waters matter so much.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.3] Fishing earns export income, provides jobs in remote coastal communities and supports fish processing.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.b] Ireland has little forest, which limits how much wood can be processed. This also explains why the wood industry employs few people.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.0] Forest cover is about 11% (2020s), among the lowest in the EU, so there is little timber to process.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.1] Coillte, the State forestry company, was set up in 1988; Sitka spruce is the main plantation tree.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.2] Wood processing employs few people because the forest area is small, plantations are young, sawmills and board mills (which make boards from wood) are highly mechanised (done by machines), and timber is partly exported unprocessed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.3] Coillte's board mills are Medite, making MDF (medium-density fibreboard) at Clonmel, Co. Tipperary, and SmartPly, making oriented strand board at Belview, Co. Kilkenny, on Waterford Port.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.b] Ireland also takes metal and energy from the ground. The examples below are the named ones to know, one for each.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath, is Europe's largest zinc mine; it opened in 1977 and is owned by Boliden. Production was paused from July 2023 and restarted at the end of 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.1] The Corrib gas field off Co. Mayo first flowed in December 2015, so it is Ireland's example of gas extraction.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.2] ESB's peat-fired power stations at Shannonbridge (West Offaly) and Lanesborough (Lough Ree) closed in December 2020.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.3] Bord na Móna's peat harvesting for electricity has ended, because the peat stations it supplied have closed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.b] Manufacturing in Ireland is driven by foreign companies. The reasons they come are also the answer to 'factors that attract industry to Ireland'.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.0] Foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment by foreign companies that set up or expand plants and offices in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.1] IDA client companies, mostly foreign-owned, employ about 300,000 people (2023), which shows how much jobs depend on FDI.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.2] Low corporation tax, IDA grants and serviced sites (land already supplied with roads, power and water) attract firms to Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.3] A young, educated, English-speaking workforce gives firms access to skilled labour.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.4] Access to the EU single market and clusters of similar firms add to the attraction, since suppliers and skills are nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.5] Ireland's time zone, between the USA and Asia, suits firms that serve both.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.20.b] Pharmaceuticals (medicines) and chemicals are the biggest manufacturing sector in Ireland. Cork Harbour is the main centre.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.20.0] Cork Harbour, especially Ringaskiddy and Little Island, is one of the world's largest concentrations of pharmaceutical and chemical plants.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.20.1] Pfizer, Janssen (Johnson & Johnson) and Novartis have plants at Ringaskiddy; Eli Lilly is at Kinsale, Co. Cork; Pfizer also has a site at Grange Castle, Clondalkin, Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.20.2] Pharmaceuticals and chemicals are Ireland's largest goods exports, about half or more of the total (2020s).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.b] Medical technology and electronics are the other main manufacturing sectors, each centred on named places.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.0] Galway is one of Europe's leading medical-technology clusters: Boston Scientific opened there in 1994 and Medtronic is at Parkmore.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.1] Intel at Leixlip, Co. Kildare, opened in 1989 and is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, worth billions of euro.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.2] Fab 34 at Leixlip opened in 2023 and makes advanced chips using extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography (printing very fine circuits with a short-wavelength light).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.3] Apple's European headquarters at Hollyhill, Cork, opened in 1980 and employs about 6,000 staff (2020s), so it is a major employer in the city.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.b] Not all Irish industry is foreign-owned. Indigenous (Irish-owned) firms get State support, and some have become multinationals themselves.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.0] Enterprise Ireland, set up in 1998, supports Irish-owned firms, so they can grow as well as the foreign ones.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.1] Irish-founded firms that grew into multinationals include CRH (building materials) and Smurfit Kappa (paper packaging), which merged with WestRock in 2024 as Smurfit Westrock.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.2] Ryanair, an Irish-founded airline, is a tertiary firm because it provides a transport service.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.3] Ireland has a dual economy: a highly productive foreign-owned sector sits beside a smaller, lower-productivity Irish-owned sector.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.b] Ireland's industrial success rests on a few foreign firms and favourable conditions. Each challenge below puts one of those at risk.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.0] Dependence on a few large US multinationals means that a single firm's decisions can change output and jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.1] The 15% global minimum tax for very large groups reduces the advantage of Ireland's low rate. There is more on this in the block on the Single Market, euro and corporation tax.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.2] In September 2024 the EU Court of Justice ruled that Apple must pay Ireland about €13 billion plus interest in back taxes; the case had been begun by the European Commission in 2016.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.3] US trade and tax policy changes: the USA raised tariffs on EU goods in 2025.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.4] Brexit: the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020 and the transition ended on 31 December 2020, changing trade, supply chains and access to the UK market.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.5] High housing and living costs make it hard to attract staff, because workers cannot afford to live near the jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.6] Electricity grid and water constraints, and high energy costs, limit expansion.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.7] Competition from lower-cost countries, because they can make goods more cheaply.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.b] A footloose industry is not tied to a location by raw materials or power, so it can locate almost anywhere. This freedom is why governments compete to attract it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.0] Footloose firms choose places with skilled labour, good communications, quality of life and government incentives, because none of these is fixed by nature.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.1] Services such as software, internet and data processing are the most footloose, since their product is information that needs no bulky raw materials.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.b] The Dublin region is Ireland's main example of an area dominated by services and footloose industry. Its best-known cluster is Silicon Docks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.0] Silicon Docks is the Grand Canal Dock area, south of the River Liffey, where digital firms cluster.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.1] Microsoft has been in Ireland since 1985; Google has had its European headquarters in Dublin since 2003, at Barrow Street on Grand Canal Dock.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.2] Meta (Facebook), LinkedIn and Salesforce also have large Dublin offices.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.3] The Dublin Docklands Development Authority, set up in 1997, was created to plan and regenerate the docklands.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.b] Service firms are footloose, so they go where conditions suit them. Dublin offers several at once.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.0] Skilled graduates come from Trinity College Dublin, UCD, DCU, TU Dublin and Maynooth University.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.1] Firms recruit staff from across the EU for offices serving Europe, so language and skills are not limited to Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.2] Dublin Airport, with over 30 million passengers a year (2023), and fibre connectivity (high-speed internet cables) link the region to the world.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.3] A low tax rate and government incentives attract firms, because they cut costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.4] Agglomeration: firms gain from being close to each other, sharing skills and suppliers, so one firm attracts others.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.b] Clustering brings both gains and strains to the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.0] The cluster provides high-paid jobs in offices and related services.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.1] Demand for offices and housing has pushed rents up, because supply cannot keep pace.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.2] The concentration of firms and workers adds to congestion (crowded roads and transport) in Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.b] Data centres are buildings full of computers that store and process internet data. They are a newer footloose service, and they put heavy demand on the electricity supply.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.0] Data centres used about a fifth (about 21%) of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO), and most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.1] A cool climate reduces cooling costs, so running the machines is cheaper; fibre links connect them to global networks; firms cluster near each other; and tax is favourable.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.2] The problem is electricity demand: EirGrid (the national electricity grid operator) stated in 2022 that it would not connect new data centres in the Dublin region until about 2028.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.b] Shannon was an early planned zone for footloose industry. Its duty-free shop (1947) came before the Free Zone (1959).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.0] Brendan O'Regan opened the world's first duty-free airport shop at Shannon in 1947.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.1] The Shannon Free Airport Development Company (SFADCo) was set up in 1959, and the Shannon Free Zone that year offered firms tax relief and duty-free import of components, which cut their costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.2] Aircraft leasing (renting planes to airlines) grew from Shannon after Guinness Peat Aviation (GPA) was founded there in 1975 by Tony Ryan, who co-founded Ryanair in the 1980s.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.3] Ireland now manages about half of the world's leased commercial aircraft (2020s, approximately).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.b] Silicon Valley is the original region of footloose high-technology and service industry. It is the global comparison for Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.0] It covers the Santa Clara Valley and the southern San Francisco Bay Area; the name comes from the silicon used in microchips.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.1] Stanford University at Palo Alto encouraged spin-off firms (new firms started by its staff and graduates) and set up Stanford Research Park in 1951.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.2] Hewlett-Packard was founded in a Palo Alto garage in 1939; Intel was founded in Mountain View in 1968 and is now headquartered in Santa Clara.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.3] Apple is based in Cupertino and Google in Mountain View, which shows how the valley hosts the biggest firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.4] Problems: very high house prices and rents, traffic congestion, and homelessness alongside great wealth.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.31.b] Financial services are a highly footloose, high-value tertiary activity. They deal in money rather than goods.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.31.0] They include banking, insurance, fund administration, asset management (looking after investments for clients), aircraft leasing, payments and fintech (technology used to deliver financial services).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.32.b] The International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) is Ireland's main financial services case study. It was set up to bring in finance and to revive a run-down area.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.32.0] The IFSC was set up in 1987 by the Charles Haughey government on the derelict Custom House Docks, on the north bank of the Liffey.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.32.1] Approved firms paid a 10% corporation tax rate, so the IFSC was cheaper than elsewhere, later replaced by the 12.5% national rate.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.32.2] Firms were attracted by the low tax rate, government incentives, an educated English-speaking workforce, EU access, the time zone, and the chance to regenerate a declining dock area.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.33.b] The IFSC grew into a major international centre and changed the area around it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.33.0] The IFSC became one of the world's largest centres for investment funds (pooled savings invested for clients); Irish-domiciled funds held over €3 trillion in assets (2020s, Central Bank of Ireland).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.33.1] Aircraft leasing is a second specialism. There is more on this in the block on the Shannon Free Zone and aircraft leasing.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.33.2] Effects: high-value jobs and the regeneration of the north docklands, served by the Luas Red Line from 2004, with new housing nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.34.b] Finance brings risks as well as gains, but Brexit also created an opening for Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.34.0] The 2008 banking crisis damaged confidence in Irish finance, and the Central Bank of Ireland regulates the sector.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.34.1] After the 2016 Brexit vote, Bank of America and Barclays moved their main EU operations to Dublin, an opportunity for Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.35.b] London is the older and larger financial centre against which Dublin can be compared.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.35.0] The City of London (the Square Mile) was deregulated (its rules relaxed) by the 'Big Bang' of the London Stock Exchange in October 1986.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.35.1] Canary Wharf, built on derelict London docks from the late 1980s, became a second financial district.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.35.2] After Brexit, some banks moved EU operations from London to Dublin, Frankfurt and Paris.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.b] Tourism is a tertiary activity, and whether an area develops it depends on a mix of natural and human factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.0] Physical attractions: climate, coast, scenery, mountains and lakes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.1] Cultural and historic attractions: heritage sites, language, music, festivals and sport.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.2] Access: airports, ports, roads and low-cost airlines.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.3] Accommodation and services, and marketing by government agencies.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.4] Cost and exchange rates, and safety and political stability.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.b] Tourism brings overseas visitors and supports jobs. State agencies develop it and market it abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.0] About 11 million overseas visits were made to the Republic of Ireland in 2019 (CSO).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.1] Tourism supported about a quarter of a million jobs in 2019 (industry estimate), which means it is a major employer.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.2] Britain, the USA and continental Europe (Germany, France) are the main source markets, so most visitors come from these.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.3] Fáilte Ireland, set up in 2003, develops tourism in the Republic; Tourism Ireland, an all-island marketing body created under the Good Friday Agreement (1998), markets the island abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.4] The Wild Atlantic Way, launched in 2014, runs about 2,500 km from Inishowen to Kinsale; Ireland's Ancient East was launched in 2015.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.38.b] Tourism depends on people being free to travel, so it is exposed to sudden shocks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.38.0] Overseas visits collapsed in 2020–2021 during COVID-19 travel restrictions, because people could not travel.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.38.1] Job losses, closed hotels and lost revenue hit remote areas that depend on visitors.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.39.b] Visitors arrive by air or sea, so access is a main factor in tourism. Exams ask you to name an airport and a ferry port.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.39.0] International airports: Dublin, Shannon, Cork, Ireland West Airport Knock (opened 1986) and Kerry Airport at Farranfore.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.39.1] Ferry ports: Dublin Port (to Holyhead), Rosslare Europort (to Fishguard and Pembroke, and since Brexit more direct routes to France) and Port of Cork at Ringaskiddy (to Roscoff, France).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.39.2] Ryanair, Europe's largest airline by passengers (2020s), is headquartered in Swords, Co. Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.40.b] More overseas visitors arrive on the east coast than anywhere else. The reasons all come back to Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.40.0] Dublin Airport and Dublin Port are the main entry points for overseas visitors.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-activities-s1: 1. Case study of a developed economy: Ireland
[geo-economic-activities-s1.40.1] Dublin is the capital, with the most attractions, hotels and events.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.40.2] Short trips, such as city breaks, suit visitors who arrive in Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.40.3] Good motorway and rail links radiate from Dublin, so visitors can reach other places easily.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.41.b] These are named east-coast attractions you can use when asked for two Irish tourist attractions.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.41.0] The Guinness Storehouse, opened in 2000, is Ireland's most visited fee-paying attraction.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.41.1] The Book of Kells is at Trinity College Dublin; Dublin Zoo is in the Phoenix Park.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.41.2] Brú na Bóinne (Newgrange), Co. Meath, has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site (a place listed by the United Nations as of world importance) since 1993.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.41.3] The Glendalough monastic site and Powerscourt are both in Co. Wicklow.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.42.b] The west and north coasts have scenic attractions for visitors.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.42.0] The Cliffs of Moher, Co. Clare, are part of the Burren and Cliffs of Moher UNESCO Global Geopark.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.42.1] Kylemore Abbey is in Connemara, for example as a west-coast attraction.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.42.2] The Giant's Causeway, Co. Antrim, in Northern Ireland, has been a UNESCO site since 1986.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.43.b] Co. Kerry, centred on Killarney and the Ring of Kerry, is a mass-tourism region, meaning large numbers of visitors come there. Its scenery, heritage and access combine to draw them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.43.0] Killarney National Park, created in 1932 from the Muckross estate, was Ireland's first national park; the estate was given to the State by the Bourn Vincent family.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.43.1] Attractions include Muckross House, the Lakes of Killarney, Torc Waterfall, the Gap of Dunloe and Ross Castle.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.43.2] Carrauntoohil (1,038 m), in the Macgillycuddy's Reeks, is Ireland's highest mountain.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.43.3] The Ring of Kerry is a circular route of about 180 km around the Iveragh Peninsula.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.43.4] Skellig Michael, an early Christian monastery on a rocky island, has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1996 and was a film location for Star Wars: The Force Awakens (2015) and The Last Jedi (2017).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.43.5] The Dingle Peninsula, with the Slea Head drive and the Dingle Gaeltacht (an Irish-speaking area), adds to the county's attractions.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.44.b] Kerry has attracted visitors for a long time, and better transport helped.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.44.0] Tourism in Killarney dates from the 18th and 19th centuries.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.44.1] The railway reached Killarney in 1853, bringing more visitors by making travel easier.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.44.2] Queen Victoria visited Killarney in 1861, which helped publicise it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.45.b] Tourism changes a region in good and bad ways. Exams ask for positive and negative impacts in a named region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.45.0] Positive: jobs and income in a peripheral region (one far from the main centres), services kept open in rural areas, and heritage protected through tourism income.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.45.1] Negative: seasonal jobs, with work concentrated in the summer months.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.45.2] Negative: traffic and coach congestion on the narrow roads of the Ring of Kerry.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.45.3] Negative: erosion on the Carrauntoohil paths and litter on popular trails.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.45.4] Negative: holiday homes push up house prices and make affordable housing scarce for local workers.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.46.b] Spain is the European example of mass tourism on a large scale. Its growth had specific causes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.46.0] Spain received about 84 million international tourists in 2019 and about 94 million in 2024, a record (INE, the Spanish statistics office).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.46.1] Tourism provides over a tenth of Spain's GDP (2020s, approximate).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.46.2] Mass tourism grew from the 1960s under the Franco regime, which promoted it with the 'Spain is different' slogan to earn foreign currency.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.46.3] Causes: sun and beaches; package holidays and jet aircraft; cheap flights; low costs; and proximity to northern Europe.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.47.b] Spain's Mediterranean coasts and islands hold the main resorts.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.47.0] Benidorm, on the Costa Blanca, was a small fishing village transformed in the 1950s and 1960s by mayor Pedro Zaragoza's plan for high-rise buildings close to the beach.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.47.1] The Gran Hotel Bali (186 m, opened 2002) was then Europe's tallest hotel.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.47.2] The Costa del Sol has Málaga airport, so visitors arrive by air at Torremolinos and Marbella.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.47.3] The Balearic Islands, in the Mediterranean, include Mallorca (Palma, Magaluf) and Ibiza.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.48.b] Mass tourism brought money to Spain, but it also caused problems, now met with protest.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.48.0] Effects: jobs, foreign earnings and infrastructure, such as airports and roads.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.48.1] Problems: 'ribbon development' (a strip of concrete buildings along the coast), and high-rise overdevelopment.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.48.2] Water shortages: Catalonia declared a drought emergency in early 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.48.3] Low-paid seasonal jobs, and rising rents as short-term tourist lets spread.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.48.4] Large anti-overtourism protests took place in 2024 in Barcelona, Mallorca and the Canary Islands.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.49.b] Governments in Spain have started to limit the effects of tourism.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.49.0] The Balearic and Catalan governments charge tourist taxes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.49.1] Barcelona plans to end licences for about 10,000 tourist apartments by 2028.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.50.b] Industries do not last forever. Industrial decline is a downward trend, and it leaves visible signs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.50.0] Industrial decline is the long-term fall in the number of firms and jobs in a traditional industry or region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.50.1] Evidence: plant closures, derelict sites and buildings, high unemployment, emigration (out-migration), falling population in industrial towns, and social problems.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.b] Irish industry declined for several reasons. Each reason is illustrated by the dated examples that follow.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.0] The end of protection and the opening to free trade exposed small, inefficient protected firms, through the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement (1965) and EEC membership (1973).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.1] Competition from low-wage countries, because they can sell more cheaply.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.2] Recessions (periods when the economy shrinks), such as those of the 1980s and 2008.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.3] New technology replacing labour, so fewer workers are needed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.4] EU policy reform, such as the reform of the EU sugar regime.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.51.5] Corporate decisions to move production to lower-cost locations, as with Dell in Limerick. There is more on this in the Irish examples blocks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.b] Cork lost several large employers in the 1980s, during a recession.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.0] The Dunlop tyre factory in Cork closed in 1983.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.1] Ford's Marina plant in Cork closed in 1984; Ford's Cork company was founded in 1917.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.52.2] Verolme Cork Dockyard at Rushbrooke closed in 1984.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.b] Further closures followed in the 2000s, each with a different cause. Dates matter here.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.0] Irish Steel (later Ispat) closed on Haulbowline Island, Cork Harbour, in 2001; the site needed a costly clean-up of contaminated waste.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.1] Irish Sugar (Greencore) closed its Carlow beet factory in 2005 and its Mallow factory in 2006, ending Irish sugar production after EU sugar-regime reform.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.2] Waterford Crystal's Kilbarry factory closed in January 2009 when the company went into receivership (when an outside manager takes control of a company that cannot pay its debts).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.53.3] Dell opened at Raheen, Limerick, in 1990; in January 2009 it announced that manufacturing would move to Łódź, Poland, with the loss of about 1,900 jobs, while sales and service stayed in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.b] Energy industries also declined as Ireland moved away from fossil fuels (fuels such as coal and peat, formed from ancient plants).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.0] Moneypoint, Co. Clare, a coal-fired power station built in the 1980s, was Ireland's last coal-burning power station.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.54.1] Coal burning at Moneypoint ended in 2025, so Ireland no longer burns coal for power, part of the move away from fossil fuels that also closed the peat stations.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.b] When industries close, the damage spreads from the workers to the whole town.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.0] Unemployment reached about 17% in the mid-1980s.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.1] Heavy emigration in the 1980s drew skilled people away from the affected towns, because there were no jobs for them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.2] Derelict docklands and empty factories were left in city and town centres.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.55.3] Local towns lost skills and spending as jobs disappeared.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.b] Governments and agencies tried to replace lost jobs and reuse empty sites.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.0] Replacement investment: the IDA and Enterprise Ireland sought new firms to replace lost jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.1] Regeneration (rebuilding and renewing run-down areas) of the Dublin Docklands and the IFSC. There is more on this in the IFSC blocks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.2] Regeneration of Cork Docklands, through a city plan to redevelop the old port quays.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.3] Heritage reuse: a smaller House of Waterford Crystal factory and visitor centre opened in Waterford city centre (the Mall) in 2010.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.4] Retraining for displaced workers, so they can move into new jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.56.5] The EU Just Transition Fund supports the Midlands peat region, because peat jobs have ended.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.57.b] Ireland is not alone in losing traditional industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.57.0] There is more on coalfield and steel regions of decline elsewhere in Europe in the Socio-economic regions notes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.b] Economic activity is unevenly spread across Ireland. Some regions have far more jobs and income than others.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.0] The Greater Dublin Area had about 2.1 million people in Census 2022, about 40% of the State's population, and holds most multinational and service jobs, along with Cork, Galway and Limerick.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.1] The Border, Midlands and West have more small farms, fewer FDI jobs and lower incomes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.2] The Border, Midland and Western (BMW) region was designated for EU funding in 1999 (Objective 1 for 2000–2006); the rest of the State was the Southern and Eastern region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.58.3] The National Planning Framework (Project Ireland 2040, 2018) aims for Galway, Cork, Limerick and Waterford to each grow by at least 50% by 2040, to balance growth away from Dublin.
