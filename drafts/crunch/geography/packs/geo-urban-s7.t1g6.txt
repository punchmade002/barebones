TARGET geo-urban-s7: 7. Developing-world cities
[geo-urban-s7.0.b] Cities in the developing world (poorer countries, mostly in Asia, Africa and Latin America) now account for most of the world's urban growth. The UN figures below show how big the shift is.
[geo-urban-s7.0.0] Most of the world's urban growth now takes place in developing countries in Asia, Africa and Latin America, so the fastest-growing cities are no longer in Europe or North America.
[geo-urban-s7.0.1] About 30% of the world's population was urban (living in towns and cities) in 1950 and about 55% in 2018 (UN, World Urbanization Prospects 2018).
[geo-urban-s7.0.2] Over half of the world's population has been urban since about 2007, so the world passed the point where city dwellers outnumber rural dwellers.
[geo-urban-s7.0.3] The UN projects that about 68% of the world's population will be urban by 2050.
[geo-urban-s7.0.4] Close to 90% of the increase to 2050 is expected in Asia and Africa; India, China and Nigeria together account for over a third of it.
[geo-urban-s7.0.5] Megacities, with over 10 million people, are a growing feature of urbanisation in the developing world.
[geo-urban-s7.0.6] UN-Habitat (the UN agency for human settlements) estimates that over 1 billion people live in slums or informal settlements, meaning crowded housing built without legal permission or services.
[geo-urban-s7.1.b] Cities grow in two ways: more babies than deaths, and more people arriving than leaving. In developing countries both are strong at once. There is more on migration theory in the Rural-to-urban migration in developed and developing regions note.
[geo-urban-s7.1.0] Natural increase (births minus deaths): the urban population is young, birth rates are high and death rates have fallen, so the city grows even without newcomers.
[geo-urban-s7.1.1] Push factors (things that drive people out of the countryside): rural poverty, landlessness, small farms, mechanisation (machines replacing farm workers), drought and crop failure, lack of services, conflict and natural disasters.
[geo-urban-s7.1.2] Pull factors (things that draw people to the city): jobs in the formal and informal sectors, higher wages, schools and hospitals, perceived opportunities, and family already living in the city.
[geo-urban-s7.1.3] Primate cities (a country's largest city, far bigger than any other) attract investment and government spending, which reinforces their pull on migrants.
[geo-urban-s7.2.b] Migrants arrive faster than the city can build homes, and most have little money. They build their own shelter wherever they can, which creates the first and most visible problem of rapid growth.
[geo-urban-s7.2.0] Migrants with little money and no legal land settle in shanty towns, slums and informal settlements, called bustees, favelas, chawls or jhopadpattis depending on the country.
[geo-urban-s7.2.1] These settlements are built on marginal land (land nobody else wants because it is unsafe or unsuitable): river banks, steep slopes, beside railways, rubbish dumps and floodplains.
[geo-urban-s7.2.2] Tenure (the legal right to occupy land) is insecure, so residents can be evicted and have no legal right to the land they occupy.
[geo-urban-s7.2.3] Overcrowding is severe, and some people have no shelter at all, living as homeless pavement dwellers.
[geo-urban-s7.3.b] Informal settlements are built without services, and this directly harms health. Sanitation means the safe disposal of human waste and dirty water.
[geo-urban-s7.3.0] Without piped water or sewers, settlements rely on open drains and shared toilets, so waste stays close to where people live and cook.
[geo-urban-s7.3.1] Contaminated water and waste spread water-borne disease (illness carried in dirty water): cholera, typhoid and dysentery.
[geo-urban-s7.4.b] There are not enough regular jobs for all the migrants, so many make a living outside the formal economy. The informal sector means work that is unregistered, untaxed and unprotected.
[geo-urban-s7.4.0] Many residents work in the informal sector as street vendors, rickshaw pullers, waste pickers and home workers.
[geo-urban-s7.4.1] Informal work offers no job security and low pay; child labour is part of informal employment.
[geo-urban-s7.4.2] Underemployment means that people have work but not enough of it to earn a living, so they are counted as working yet remain poor.
[geo-urban-s7.5.b] Road and rail systems were built for much smaller populations. When the city expands faster than its transport, movement slows for everyone.
[geo-urban-s7.5.0] Road networks cannot cope with the growth in traffic, so journeys take longer and congestion (traffic jams) becomes permanent.
[geo-urban-s7.5.1] Trains and buses are overcrowded at peak times, when most people travel to and from work.
[geo-urban-s7.5.2] Road deaths are a serious problem in the city, because crowded roads mix heavy traffic with pedestrians.
[geo-urban-s7.6.b] Crowded cities with weak services release waste into the air, rivers and streets faster than it can be removed.
[geo-urban-s7.6.0] Air pollution comes from traffic, industry and cooking fuels burned in crowded homes.
[geo-urban-s7.6.1] Untreated sewage and industrial waste are discharged into rivers, because there are no treatment plants to clean them first.
[geo-urban-s7.6.2] Rubbish is not collected in many areas, so it builds up in streets and drains.
[geo-urban-s7.7.b] Poor people settle on the land most exposed to danger, so natural hazards hit informal settlements hardest.
[geo-urban-s7.7.0] Monsoon (seasonal heavy rain) or tropical downpours flood low-lying, paved and badly drained land, where settlements are often built; paving stops rain soaking into the ground and poor drains cannot carry it away.
[geo-urban-s7.7.1] Hillside slums are at risk of landslides, and crowded, poorly built homes are at risk of fire.
[geo-urban-s7.8.b] Rapid growth also produces social strains, which weak city government is too stretched to manage.
[geo-urban-s7.8.0] Crime and drugs are social problems, and street children live and work on the streets.
[geo-urban-s7.8.1] Rich and poor districts sit side by side, so inequality is visible within the city.
[geo-urban-s7.8.2] Local government is weak, so it struggles to provide services.
[geo-urban-s7.9.b] The first response to slums is often to remove them. This is the least successful of the solutions.
[geo-urban-s7.9.0] Slum clearance removes informal settlements by demolition and eviction (forcing residents out).
[geo-urban-s7.9.1] It usually fails, because residents are moved further out or into new slums and the underlying problems (poverty and lack of housing) remain.
[geo-urban-s7.10.b] Instead of building homes for people, the authorities prepare land and let residents do the building. No named scheme is needed for this general solution.
[geo-urban-s7.10.0] In a site-and-service scheme the authorities provide serviced plots with water, drains and power.
[geo-urban-s7.10.1] Residents build their own homes on these plots, which is known as aided self-help (the authorities help with the services, the residents do the building).
[geo-urban-s7.11.b] Upgrading improves a slum where it stands rather than removing it, so nobody has to move.
[geo-urban-s7.11.0] Lanes are paved, water taps, toilets, drains and electricity are installed, and residents are given secure tenure (a legal right to stay).
[geo-urban-s7.11.1] Because the community stays in place, people can improve their own homes over time, as they no longer fear eviction.
[geo-urban-s7.12.b] Another approach is to build new towns beside the city so that growth goes there instead of into the crowded centre.
[geo-urban-s7.12.0] Planned satellite towns and new towns (towns built beside a large city, with planned housing, jobs and services) divert growth away from the overcrowded core.
[geo-urban-s7.12.1] Examples are given in the Mumbai case study (Navi Mumbai) and the Kolkata case study (Salt Lake and New Town).
[geo-urban-s7.13.b] Cities cannot solve congestion just by building more roads, so they invest in public transport that carries many people at once.
[geo-urban-s7.13.0] Metros (underground or elevated urban railways), suburban rail and bus rapid transit (buses with their own lanes and fast boarding, working like a train system) move large numbers of people without adding more cars to the roads.
[geo-urban-s7.13.1] Curitiba in Brazil is the leading example of bus rapid transit, described below.
[geo-urban-s7.14.b] Curitiba, in southern Brazil, shows how a cheap, well-planned bus system can ease traffic in a developing-world city.
[geo-urban-s7.14.0] Bus rapid transit began in Curitiba in 1974 under architect-mayor Jaime Lerner.
[geo-urban-s7.14.1] Dedicated bus lanes and raised tube stations allow fast boarding, which reduces car use because the bus is quicker than sitting in traffic.
[geo-urban-s7.14.2] Recyclable waste could be exchanged for food or bus tokens, which tackled rubbish and helped poorer residents at the same time.
[geo-urban-s7.14.3] The model has been copied worldwide, for example in Bogotá's TransMilenio (2000).
[geo-urban-s7.15.b] Some solutions act on the push factors in the countryside, so fewer people leave for the city in the first place.
[geo-urban-s7.15.0] Rural development, land reform (giving landless farmers land) and jobs in smaller cities give people reasons to stay in rural areas.
[geo-urban-s7.15.1] NGOs (non-governmental organisations, i.e. charities) and community groups also act on these problems, as the Missionaries of Charity do in Kolkata.
[geo-urban-s7.16.b] Kolkata (called Calcutta until 2001) is the main developing-world case study. Its site and history explain why it became one of India's largest cities.
[geo-urban-s7.16.0] Kolkata lies on the Hooghly river near the Bay of Bengal, on low-lying delta land (flat land built up by river deposits), which is why it floods easily.
[geo-urban-s7.16.1] It was founded in 1690 by the British East India Company (Job Charnock).
[geo-urban-s7.16.2] It developed as a port on the Hooghly, with jute mills (factories making cloth and sacks from the jute plant); Howrah Bridge across the river opened in 1943.
[geo-urban-s7.16.3] It was the capital of British India until 1911, so government jobs and trade drew people to it.
[geo-urban-s7.16.4] The city was renamed from Calcutta to Kolkata in 2001.
[geo-urban-s7.17.b] Kolkata grew because colonial rule built an economy that drew people in, and because disasters and wars then pushed more people towards it.
[geo-urban-s7.17.0] It was a colonial port and administrative capital, and its jute and engineering industries attracted migrants from Bihar, Odisha and rural Bengal.
[geo-urban-s7.17.1] The Bengal famine of 1943 drove millions of rural people towards the city.
[geo-urban-s7.17.2] Partition in 1947 (the division of British India into India and Pakistan) brought refugees, and the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War brought a further wave.
[geo-urban-s7.17.3] Natural increase adds to the population, and the city has regional primacy in eastern India, meaning it dominates the region's economy and services.
[geo-urban-s7.17.4] Census 2011 gave an urban agglomeration (the built-up city and its suburbs) of about 14.1 million people.
[geo-urban-s7.18.b] Migrants arriving faster than housing could be built ended up in slums or on the streets.
[geo-urban-s7.18.0] Bustees (slums) house many of the city's poorest people, in overcrowded conditions, because they cannot afford other housing.
[geo-urban-s7.18.1] Pavement dwellers have no shelter and live on the streets.
[geo-urban-s7.18.2] Census 2011 counted about 65 million people in slums across India; this is an India-wide figure, not a Kolkata figure.
[geo-urban-s7.19.b] Kolkata's economy weakened just as migrants kept arriving, so there were too few jobs.
[geo-urban-s7.19.0] Partition cut the city off from the jute-growing lands that became East Pakistan, so the economy declined because the jute mills lost their raw material.
[geo-urban-s7.19.1] Jobs did not keep pace with migrants, so many people depend on informal work.
[geo-urban-s7.19.2] Informal work includes hand-pulled rickshaws; Kolkata is one of the few cities in the world where these still operate.
[geo-urban-s7.20.b] Services and infrastructure (the basic systems a city runs on) were never built for so many people, and the city's low-lying site adds to the problems.
[geo-urban-s7.20.0] Water supply and sewerage (the system of pipes carrying away waste water) are poor, so many residents lack safe water and proper sanitation.
[geo-urban-s7.20.1] Monsoon rain floods the low-lying streets of the city.
[geo-urban-s7.20.2] Air pollution and traffic congestion add to the city's environmental problems.
[geo-urban-s7.21.b] Kolkata's planners, governments and charities have tried housing, transport, new towns and environmental solutions. Together these form the solutions part of the case study.
[geo-urban-s7.21.0] The Calcutta Metropolitan Development Authority, the city's planning body, was set up in 1970.
[geo-urban-s7.21.1] From the 1970s its bustee improvement programme provided water taps, toilets, paved lanes and drains.
[geo-urban-s7.21.2] Kolkata Metro, India's first, opened in 1984, which eases congestion by moving people underground.
[geo-urban-s7.21.3] Salt Lake City (Bidhannagar) is a planned township built from the 1960s on reclaimed wetland (wetland drained and filled to make building land) east of the city; its Sector V is an IT hub.
[geo-urban-s7.21.4] New Town (Rajarhat) was planned from the late 1990s, with IT parks (business parks for information technology firms).
[geo-urban-s7.21.5] The East Kolkata Wetlands were designated a Ramsar wetland of international importance in 2002; their fish ponds and fields use and naturally treat much of the city's sewage.
[geo-urban-s7.21.6] Mother Teresa founded the Missionaries of Charity in the city in 1950 and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1979.
[geo-urban-s7.22.b] To judge the responses, ask who benefited and what is left unsolved.
[geo-urban-s7.22.0] Bustee upgrading improved conditions for many residents, but slums remain.
[geo-urban-s7.22.1] The new towns serve the middle classes more than the poor.
[geo-urban-s7.23.b] Mumbai (called Bombay until 1995) is the second Indian case study. Its natural harbour and its role as a trading centre made it a magnet for migrants.
[geo-urban-s7.23.0] Mumbai was originally seven islands, joined together by land reclamation (building up land from the sea), with a deep, sheltered natural harbour.
[geo-urban-s7.23.1] It was a British East India Company port, and cotton mills were built there from the 19th century.
[geo-urban-s7.23.2] Mumbai is India's financial capital, not its political capital; it houses the Reserve Bank of India headquarters and the Bombay Stock Exchange, founded in 1875, Asia's oldest.
[geo-urban-s7.23.3] It is the centre of the Bollywood film industry, which is another source of jobs that attracts migrants.
[geo-urban-s7.23.4] The city was renamed from Bombay in 1995.
[geo-urban-s7.23.5] Migrants come from across India, including Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, drawn by industry and jobs.
[geo-urban-s7.23.6] Census 2011 gave a Greater Mumbai urban agglomeration (the city plus its built-up suburbs) of about 18.4 million people.
[geo-urban-s7.24.b] Mumbai is built on a narrow strip of land, so space is short and housing is expensive. Many people end up in slums.
[geo-urban-s7.24.0] Dharavi is one of Asia's largest slums, in central Mumbai, with many small home-based industries such as recycling, leather and pottery.
[geo-urban-s7.24.1] Land and housing costs are very high on the narrow peninsula (a strip of land almost surrounded by water), which limits the space available for housing.
[geo-urban-s7.25.b] Dense population puts pressure on transport and services, and heavy monsoon rain can overwhelm the city.
[geo-urban-s7.25.0] The suburban railway carries several million passengers a day, and its trains are overcrowded.
[geo-urban-s7.25.1] Road congestion adds to the pressure on the transport network.
[geo-urban-s7.25.2] On 26 July 2005 about 944 mm of rain fell in one day at Santacruz, flooding the city.
[geo-urban-s7.25.3] Water supply and sanitation cannot keep pace with the dense population.
[geo-urban-s7.26.b] Mumbai's old industrial base collapsed in the 1980s, which changed where people worked.
[geo-urban-s7.26.0] The Great Bombay textile strike of 1982 led to the closure of most cotton mills.
[geo-urban-s7.26.1] Mill land was later redeveloped, so the city's industrial base changed, for example away from cotton.
[geo-urban-s7.27.b] Mumbai's planners tried to relieve the crowded island city by building a second city across the water.
[geo-urban-s7.27.0] Navi Mumbai is a planned twin city across the harbour, begun in the 1970s by CIDCO (City and Industrial Development Corporation of Maharashtra, set up 1970).
[geo-urban-s7.27.1] It is designed to take population and industry away from the crowded core of Mumbai.
[geo-urban-s7.28.b] Rehabilitation means rehousing slum dwellers in new buildings, with private developers paying for it.
[geo-urban-s7.28.0] The Slum Rehabilitation Authority, set up in 1995, lets developers rehouse slum dwellers free in new blocks and sell the remaining flats, so the developers pay for it from those sales.
[geo-urban-s7.28.1] The Dharavi Redevelopment Project was awarded to the Adani Group in 2022, as a plan to rebuild the slum, for example with new housing.
[geo-urban-s7.29.b] New rail and road links ease pressure on Mumbai's transport and tie it to Navi Mumbai.
[geo-urban-s7.29.0] Mumbai Metro Line 1 opened in 2014, so some commuters have an alternative to the overcrowded roads and trains.
[geo-urban-s7.29.1] The Bandra–Worli Sea Link opened in 2009, a road bridge over the sea that shortens journeys across the city.
[geo-urban-s7.29.2] The Mumbai Trans Harbour Link (Atal Setu), opened in January 2024, is India's longest sea bridge and links Mumbai to Navi Mumbai.
[geo-urban-s7.30.b] Rehousing gave people new homes but has drawbacks.
[geo-urban-s7.30.0] Rehousing flats under the Slum Rehabilitation Authority are criticised for poor design.
[geo-urban-s7.30.1] Rehousing is also criticised for breaking up Dharavi's home-based industries, which depended on the slum's layout and community for their work.
[geo-urban-s7.31.b] São Paulo is the Latin American case study. It is not Brazil's capital (that is Brasília) and it is not on the coast.
[geo-urban-s7.31.0] São Paulo lies on a plateau inland from the port of Santos, not on the coast.
[geo-urban-s7.31.1] It was founded on 25 January 1554 by Jesuit missionaries, long before its coffee and industrial growth.
[geo-urban-s7.31.2] Santos is now Latin America's busiest container port, which is why trade and growth gathered inland at São Paulo.
[geo-urban-s7.32.b] Coffee, immigration and industry each brought a new wave of people to São Paulo.
[geo-urban-s7.32.0] Coffee wealth in the 19th century was exported through Santos and carried by the Santos–Jundiaí railway, opened in 1867.
[geo-urban-s7.32.1] Immigration from Europe, especially Italy, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.
[geo-urban-s7.32.2] Japanese immigration began in 1908 with the ship Kasato Maru; the city has the largest Japanese community outside Japan, centred on the Liberdade district.
[geo-urban-s7.32.3] From the 1950s São Paulo became Brazil's industrial heartland; car plants, including Volkswagen, opened in the ABC region (Santo André, São Bernardo do Campo and São Caetano do Sul).
[geo-urban-s7.32.4] Migrants from the drought-prone North-East of Brazil, known as nordestinos, moved to the city to work in its industries.
[geo-urban-s7.33.b] Be clear whether a figure refers to the city or the wider metropolitan region, because the two are very different.
[geo-urban-s7.33.0] IBGE (the Brazilian statistics agency) Census 2022 gave the city of São Paulo about 11.5 million people and the metropolitan region about 21 million.
[geo-urban-s7.33.1] São Paulo is the largest city in South America and in the Southern Hemisphere.
[geo-urban-s7.33.2] Brazil is over 80% urban, so São Paulo's growth is part of a country-wide shift.
[geo-urban-s7.34.b] São Paulo's wealth sits right beside poverty, and informal housing (favelas) is the clearest sign of this.
[geo-urban-s7.34.0] Paraisópolis favela borders the wealthy district of Morumbi, so extreme inequality is visible from one street to the next.
[geo-urban-s7.34.1] Heliópolis is São Paulo's largest favela.
[geo-urban-s7.34.2] Crime is a problem in the city, including in its favelas, so inequality has social effects as well as housing ones.
[geo-urban-s7.35.b] A very large metropolitan region puts heavy pressure on its roads, air and rivers.
[geo-urban-s7.35.0] Traffic congestion is severe across the metropolitan region.
[geo-urban-s7.35.1] Air pollution from vehicles and industry adds to the city's environmental problems.
[geo-urban-s7.35.2] The Tietê river flows through the city and floods in heavy summer rain.
[geo-urban-s7.36.b] Unusually, São Paulo's problem was too little water, not too much.
[geo-urban-s7.36.0] The Cantareira reservoir system (a group of reservoirs) is the main source of the city's water.
[geo-urban-s7.36.1] During the 2014–2015 drought the Cantareira fell to very low levels, and water rationing (limiting how much water people receive) was introduced.
[geo-urban-s7.37.b] The authorities have tried traffic limits, new transport and new housing.
[geo-urban-s7.37.0] Rodízio, from 1997: cars are banned from central São Paulo at rush hours on one weekday, according to the last digit of the number plate, which cuts the number of cars on the road each day.
[geo-urban-s7.37.1] The São Paulo Metro opened its first line in 1974, so that people can travel without using the congested roads.
[geo-urban-s7.37.2] The Rodoanel Mário Covas ring road encircles the metropolitan region, so traffic can bypass the centre; its first section opened in 2002.
[geo-urban-s7.37.3] Projeto Cingapura (1990s) replaced some favela shacks with apartment blocks on the same sites.
[geo-urban-s7.37.4] Minha Casa, Minha Vida, a national subsidised housing programme (the state pays part of the cost), was launched in 2009.
[geo-urban-s7.38.b] The measures have made a difference, but not enough.
[geo-urban-s7.38.0] Favela upgrading and transport improvements have helped.
[geo-urban-s7.38.1] Favelas and traffic congestion persist, because growth has outpaced the measures taken.

CANDIDATES geo-region-continental-s2: 2. Brazil
[geo-region-continental-s2.39.b] The Cerrado has a rainy summer and a dry winter, and farmers have fitted two crops into the rains. This doubles what each field yields in a year.
[geo-region-continental-s2.39.0] Soya is planted with the first rains, about October, because the seeds need moist soil to germinate.
[geo-region-continental-s2.39.1] The soya is harvested about January to February, which clears the field while the rains are still falling.
[geo-region-continental-s2.39.2] A second crop of maize, called the safrinha (Portuguese for 'little harvest'), is then planted in the same rainy season, so the same land gives two crops a year.
[geo-region-continental-s2.39.3] The dry winter, about May to September, is the harvest and rest period. The calendar works only because the rain is reliable in summer.
[geo-region-continental-s2.40.b] Coffee made 19th-century São Paulo rich, but frost pushed the crop north. Its history links climate, soil, labour and ports.
[geo-region-continental-s2.40.0] The coffee boom in São Paulo state in the 19th century was worked first by enslaved Africans and then by European immigrants, and the beans were exported through the port of Santos.
[geo-region-continental-s2.40.1] The 'black frost' of July 1975 destroyed most coffee trees in Paraná, because coffee bushes die in hard frost. The risk of frost shaped where coffee is now grown.
[geo-region-continental-s2.40.2] Minas Gerais is now the leading coffee state, and Brazil remains the world's largest coffee producer, with about a third of world output.
[geo-region-continental-s2.41.b] Within a few decades Brazil turned acidic, infertile savanna into the world's biggest soya belt. Science, flat land and cheap land made it possible.
[geo-region-continental-s2.41.0] Cerrado soils are acidic and high in aluminium, so for a long time they were thought useless for crops. Adding crushed limestone (liming) and fertiliser made them productive.
[geo-region-continental-s2.41.1] EMBRAPA, the state agricultural research body founded in 1973, bred tropical soya varieties that grow well in the heat and day length of the Cerrado.
[geo-region-continental-s2.41.2] The land is cheap and flat, which suits large machines, and the summer rain is reliable, so two crops a year are possible.
[geo-region-continental-s2.41.3] Mato Grosso is Brazil's leading soya state, and China buys most of Brazil's soya exports (2020s), which gives farmers a huge market.
[geo-region-continental-s2.42.b] Sugar was Brazil's first great export crop and is now also a fuel. The story shows how a colonial crop became a modern energy industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.42.0] Colonial sugar plantations grew on the dark, fertile massapê soils of the Northeast coast, using enslaved labour.
[geo-region-continental-s2.42.1] Today São Paulo state is the leading sugar-cane state, and Brazil is the world's largest producer of sugar cane.
[geo-region-continental-s2.42.2] Proálcool (the National Alcohol Programme, 1975) was launched after the oil crisis so that Brazil could make fuel from cane instead of importing oil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.42.3] Flex-fuel cars, sold from 2003, run on ethanol, petrol or any mix, so drivers can choose whichever is cheaper.
[geo-region-continental-s2.43.b] Brazil has more cattle than people, and ranching is one of its biggest land uses. It brings export income but also forest loss.
[geo-region-continental-s2.43.0] Brazil has one of the world's largest cattle herds, with more cattle than people (2020s), and it is the largest exporter of beef.
[geo-region-continental-s2.43.1] Extensive ranching takes place on the Cerrado, in the Pantanal wetland and on cleared Amazon land, because cattle need large areas of cheap grass.
[geo-region-continental-s2.43.2] Cattle ranching is the largest single cause of Amazon clearance, as ranchers burn forest to make pasture.
[geo-region-continental-s2.43.3] Gaúchos (cattle herders) work the grasslands of the Pampa in Rio Grande do Sul in the far south.
[geo-region-continental-s2.44.b] In a dry region, a river can turn desert-like land into export farmland. The São Francisco does this for the Northeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.44.0] Petrolina (Pernambuco) and Juazeiro (Bahia) face each other across the São Francisco River and export grapes and mangoes.
[geo-region-continental-s2.44.1] Water comes from the river and its dams, such as Sobradinho, whose lake is one of the world's largest artificial lakes by area.
[geo-region-continental-s2.44.2] Irrigation turns the semi-arid sertão, where rain is low and unreliable, into reliable farmland, and the sunny climate suits fruit.
[geo-region-continental-s2.44.3] Where irrigation water evaporates it can leave salt in the soil (salinisation), which is a risk for these farms.
[geo-region-continental-s2.45.b] Brazil's land, climate and rivers give it natural advantages that few countries can match.
[geo-region-continental-s2.45.0] A huge area of flat land makes it easy to use big machines and to expand farms.
[geo-region-continental-s2.45.1] Tropical temperatures allow two or three crops a year, because there is no cold season to stop growth in most regions.
[geo-region-continental-s2.45.2] Heavy rainfall over most of the country supplies crops without irrigation.
[geo-region-continental-s2.45.3] Fertile soils, such as terra roxa on the Paraná Plateau basalt and massapê on the Northeast coast, give high yields for coffee and sugar.
[geo-region-continental-s2.45.4] Rivers provide water for irrigation, for example the São Francisco in the Northeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.46.b] Climate and soil also hold farming back in many areas. Each problem has a cause in the physical geography.
[geo-region-continental-s2.46.0] Latosols (the red-yellow Amazon and Cerrado soils) are leached by heat and rain and are acidic and low in nutrients, so crops fail without lime and fertiliser.
[geo-region-continental-s2.46.1] Drought in the sertão (rain about 250 to 800 mm a year, varying greatly) makes farming unreliable and drives people away.

[geo-region-continental-s2.46.3] Erosion after clearance strips thin soil once trees no longer hold it, and pests and diseases thrive in the warm, humid tropics.
[geo-region-continental-s2.47.b] Land ownership shapes who farms and who gains. Brazil's is among the most unequal in the world.
[geo-region-continental-s2.47.0] A latifúndio is a very large estate; a minifúndio is a tiny plot. Huge estates sit beside tiny plots, so most farmland is held by few owners.

[geo-region-continental-s2.47.2] Family farms grow most of the food eaten at home, while big agribusiness exports, so the biggest earners are not the main food growers.
[geo-region-continental-s2.47.3] Credit and technology favour large farms, so small farmers struggle to buy machines and inputs.
[geo-region-continental-s2.47.4] High transport costs to the ports cut profits, especially for farms far inland.
[geo-region-continental-s2.48.b] Where crops can be sold, and what the state does, decide what farmers grow.
[geo-region-continental-s2.48.0] Export markets such as China and the EU buy soya, beef and coffee and set demand and prices.
[geo-region-continental-s2.48.1] Mercosur (founded 1991 by the Treaty of Asunción: Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay) gives Brazilian farm goods a regional market.
[geo-region-continental-s2.48.2] EMBRAPA research improves seeds, soils and methods, so yields keep rising.
[geo-region-continental-s2.48.3] The Amazon soy moratorium (2006) is an agreement by traders not to buy soya grown on newly cleared Amazon land.
[geo-region-continental-s2.48.4] Farm credit for family farms helps small producers buy seed and equipment.
[geo-region-continental-s2.49.b] Brazil's farming success has costs, from violence to dependence on world prices.
[geo-region-continental-s2.49.0] Land conflict and violence are common on the Amazon frontier, where ranchers, farmers and settlers fight over land.
[geo-region-continental-s2.49.1] Deforestation for pasture and soya clears forest, which brings international criticism.
[geo-region-continental-s2.49.2] Heavy pesticide use pollutes land and water and affects the health of workers.
[geo-region-continental-s2.49.3] Dependence on world commodity prices means that when the price of soya or coffee falls, farm incomes and export earnings fall too.
[geo-region-continental-s2.50.b] The Amazon forest gives timber and other products. Some are taken sustainably and some are not.
[geo-region-continental-s2.50.0] Amazon timber is heavily logged, much of it illegally. Mahogany was one of the most heavily logged species.
[geo-region-continental-s2.50.1] Brazil nuts are collected from wild trees and rubber is tapped from standing forest, so these products can be harvested without clearing it.
[geo-region-continental-s2.50.2] The rubber boom, about 1879 to 1912, was centred on Manaus and built its wealth, for example the Teatro Amazonas opera house (1896).
[geo-region-continental-s2.50.3] The boom collapsed because Henry Wickham took rubber seeds from the Amazon to Britain in 1876, and plantations in British Asia (Malaya) later undercut wild Amazon rubber on price.
[geo-region-continental-s2.51.b] Extractive reserves let local people earn a living from the forest while keeping it standing.
[geo-region-continental-s2.51.0] Chico Mendes was a rubber tapper and union leader who campaigned to protect the forest and the people who tapped it.
[geo-region-continental-s2.51.1] He was murdered at Xapuri, Acre, on 22 December 1988, which brought world attention to the fight over the Amazon.
[geo-region-continental-s2.51.2] The Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve in Acre was created in 1990. In reserves like it, local people harvest forest products, such as rubber and nuts, without clearing the forest.
[geo-region-continental-s2.52.b] Fast-growing plantations meet industrial demand, and satellite figures show how fast natural forest has been lost.
[geo-region-continental-s2.52.0] Eucalyptus plantations in the Southeast and South supply pulp and paper, because eucalyptus grows fast in the warm, wet climate.
[geo-region-continental-s2.52.1] Brazil is the world's largest exporter of pulp (2020s).
[geo-region-continental-s2.52.2] PRODES (the satellite measure of Amazon clearance) recorded about 27,800 km² cleared in 2004, about 4,600 km² in 2012 and about 13,000 km² in 2021.
[geo-region-continental-s2.52.3] About a fifth of the Brazilian Amazon's original forest has been cleared.
[geo-region-continental-s2.52.4] The Forest Code says Amazon landholders must keep 80% of their land under forest, and the Amazon Fund (2008), paid for mainly by Norway and also Germany, finances protection.
[geo-region-continental-s2.53.b] Brazil has a very long coast but a small fishing industry, and the reason is in the ocean.
[geo-region-continental-s2.53.0] The warm Brazil Current flows south along the coast and is low in plankton, so there is little food for fish and fishing is small for so long a coastline (about 7,400 km).
[geo-region-continental-s2.53.1] Artisanal (small-boat) fishing is the main sea fishing on the Northeast coast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.53.2] Amazon river fisheries catch pirarucu (arapaima, one of the largest freshwater fish) and tambaqui.
[geo-region-continental-s2.53.3] Aquaculture is growing: tilapia is the main farmed fish, and shrimp farms are found in Ceará and Rio Grande do Norte in the Northeast.
[geo-region-continental-s2.54.b] Brazil has huge mineral wealth, especially iron ore, and the mines shape regions.
[geo-region-continental-s2.54.0] Carajás in Pará, discovered in 1967 and run by Vale, is the world's largest iron-ore mine. The ore travels about 890 km by the Carajás Railway to the port of São Luís in Maranhão.
[geo-region-continental-s2.54.1] The Iron Quadrangle (Quadrilátero Ferrífero) in Minas Gerais is another iron-ore region, and the 18th-century gold rush happened around Ouro Preto.
[geo-region-continental-s2.54.2] Bauxite (the ore of aluminium) is mined at Trombetas in Pará.
[geo-region-continental-s2.54.3] Brazil produces most of the world's niobium, used in steel alloys, at Araxá in Minas Gerais.
[geo-region-continental-s2.55.b] Mining brings export income, but accidents and illegal mines cause deaths and lasting pollution.
[geo-region-continental-s2.55.0] A tailings dam stores the waste mud from a mine. At Mariana, Minas Gerais, on 5 November 2015 the Fundão dam (Samarco, owned by Vale and BHP) collapsed, killing 19 people, and mud polluted the Rio Doce all the way to the Atlantic.
[geo-region-continental-s2.55.1] At Brumadinho, Minas Gerais, on 25 January 2019 a Vale tailings dam collapsed, with about 270 deaths.
[geo-region-continental-s2.55.2] Illegal gold miners (garimpeiros) in Yanomami territory in Roraima pollute rivers with mercury, which poisons fish and people.
[geo-region-continental-s2.55.3] A health emergency was declared among the Yanomami in 2023 because of illegal mining.
[geo-region-continental-s2.56.b] Brazil's rain-fed rivers give it one of the cleanest electricity supplies of any large economy.
[geo-region-continental-s2.56.0] Hydroelectricity supplies about three-fifths of Brazil's electricity, because heavy rain on the plateau feeds fast-flowing rivers.
[geo-region-continental-s2.56.1] Renewables (hydro, wind, solar and biomass) give about 85 to 90% of electricity (early 2020s), so Brazil's electricity is among the cleanest of large economies.
[geo-region-continental-s2.56.2] Wind farms in Bahia and Rio Grande do Norte, and solar, are growing fast in the Northeast, where winds and sun are strong.
[geo-region-continental-s2.57.b] From a state-owned company to deep-sea discoveries, oil has made Brazil a major producer.
[geo-region-continental-s2.57.0] Petrobras was founded in 1953 under the slogan 'the oil is ours', as a state company to control Brazil's oil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.57.1] The Campos Basin, off Rio de Janeiro state, has been producing from the 1970s.
[geo-region-continental-s2.57.2] Pre-salt fields in the Santos Basin were discovered in 2006 to 2007. They lie up to about 300 km offshore, several kilometres below the sea surface and under a thick layer of salt, so drilling is hard and costly.
[geo-region-continental-s2.57.3] The pre-salt fields now supply most of Brazil's oil, and Brazil is one of the world's ten largest oil producers (2020s).
[geo-region-continental-s2.58.b] Brazil also gets energy from crops and a little from uranium.
[geo-region-continental-s2.58.0] Ethanol from sugar cane is a transport fuel, used in flex-fuel cars, which cuts Brazil's need for imported oil.
[geo-region-continental-s2.58.1] Angra dos Reis, in Rio de Janeiro state, has Brazil's nuclear power station, a small share of its supply.
[geo-region-continental-s2.59.b] Brazil's early economy sent raw goods abroad, so manufacturing started late.
[geo-region-continental-s2.59.0] Brazilwood, sugar, gold, rubber and coffee were exported raw, so little manufacturing developed before 1900.
[geo-region-continental-s2.59.1] Coffee profits later financed São Paulo's first factories, which is why São Paulo became the industrial centre.
[geo-region-continental-s2.60.b] From the 1930s the state decided to build industry behind tariff walls rather than import factory goods.
[geo-region-continental-s2.60.0] Import substitution industrialisation (ISI) means making goods at home that were once imported, protecting the new factories with tariffs.
[geo-region-continental-s2.60.1] Under Getúlio Vargas (in power 1930 to 1945 and 1951 to 1954) the state built heavy industry: the CSN steelworks at Volta Redonda began production in 1946, and Petrobras was founded in 1953.
[geo-region-continental-s2.60.2] Under Juscelino Kubitschek (president 1956 to 1961), whose slogan was 'fifty years of progress in five', foreign car makers were invited into the ABC region (Santo André, São Bernardo do Campo and São Caetano do Sul) near São Paulo. Volkswagen's São Bernardo plant opened in 1959.
[geo-region-continental-s2.61.b] Rule by the generals mixed fast growth with huge state projects and then a debt crisis.
[geo-region-continental-s2.61.0] Military rule lasted from 1964 to 1985, and the 'Brazilian miracle', about 1968 to 1973, was a period of very fast growth.
[geo-region-continental-s2.61.1] State projects included Itaipu, Carajás and the Trans-Amazonian Highway (begun 1970, opened 1972).
[geo-region-continental-s2.61.2] Embraer was founded in 1969 at São José dos Campos and is now the world's third-largest maker of commercial jets.
[geo-region-continental-s2.61.3] The 1980s brought a debt crisis and hyperinflation (prices rising very fast).
[geo-region-continental-s2.62.b] From the 1990s Brazil ended hyperinflation and opened its economy.
[geo-region-continental-s2.62.0] The Real Plan (1994) ended hyperinflation, which gave firms stable prices for investing.
[geo-region-continental-s2.62.1] Privatisation sold state firms: Embraer in 1994 and Vale in 1997.
[geo-region-continental-s2.62.2] Mercosur (1991) opened a regional market for Brazilian manufactured goods.
[geo-region-continental-s2.63.b] Industry grows where its inputs are, and Brazil has many raw materials near its industry. This is one of the factors examiners ask for.
[geo-region-continental-s2.63.0] Iron ore from Minas Gerais and Carajás feeds the steel industry, for example the CSN works at Volta Redonda.
[geo-region-continental-s2.63.1] Sugar cane is turned into ethanol.
[geo-region-continental-s2.63.2] Soya and meat supply food processing, and JBS is the world's largest meat-processing company.
[geo-region-continental-s2.63.3] Eucalyptus supplies the pulp and paper industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.63.4] Oil supplies petrochemicals, so oil fields lead to refining and chemical plants.
[geo-region-continental-s2.64.b] Factories need power, and cheap hydroelectricity has pulled industry to certain places.
[geo-region-continental-s2.64.0] Itaipu, on the Paraná and shared with Paraguay, supplies the Southeast industrial core with cheap hydroelectricity.
[geo-region-continental-s2.64.1] Tucuruí Dam on the Tocantins in Pará (1984) powers aluminium smelting in the Amazon, a very energy-hungry industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.64.2] Rain on the plateau feeds the rivers, so cheap power is available at low cost to industry.
[geo-region-continental-s2.65.b] Industry needs workers, and Brazil's population has supplied both cheap labour and skills.
[geo-region-continental-s2.65.0] A large, low-cost workforce keeps wages low for factories, which attracts foreign firms.
[geo-region-continental-s2.65.1] European and Japanese immigrants brought skills to São Paulo and the South.
[geo-region-continental-s2.65.2] Migrants from the Northeast came to work in the ABC factories.
[geo-region-continental-s2.65.3] Skilled engineers at São José dos Campos support aerospace, so Embraer grew there.
[geo-region-continental-s2.66.b] Factories locate close to buyers, and Brazil has a big home market.
[geo-region-continental-s2.66.0] A domestic market of over 200 million people, with a growing middle class, buys cars, goods and food.
[geo-region-continental-s2.66.1] Mercosur (the trade bloc of Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay) opens neighbouring markets.
[geo-region-continental-s2.66.2] Export markets take iron ore, jets, meat and other goods.

[geo-region-continental-s2.67.1] The port of Santos, the largest in Latin America, exports and imports for São Paulo.
