TARGET geo-option-culture-identity-s1: 1. Racial groupings
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.0.b] Geographers can group a population by physical indicators (race) or by cultural indicators (ethnicity). The two ideas are easy to mix up, so keep them separate from the start.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.0.0] Race: a grouping of people by inherited physical traits such as skin colour, hair type and facial features. It describes the body, not the way of life.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.0.1] Ethnic group: a group that shares culture, ancestry, language, religion or history and is seen as distinct both by its members and by others. It describes shared culture, not appearance.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.0.2] The syllabus treats race as one part of ethnicity, so race is one of several ethnic indicators. Language, religion and nationality (citizenship of a state) are the other cultural indicators, covered in the notes Language as a cultural indicator, Religion as a cultural indicator and Nationality and the nation state.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.1.b] Modern science has changed how race is understood. Biologists now see it as a weak way of dividing people, yet it still shapes the human map because people have acted on it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.1.0] All humans belong to one species, and people share about 99.9% of their DNA (the genetic code in every cell), so the differences between groups are tiny.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.1.1] There is more genetic variation within any so-called race than between races, which is why the groupings do not mark real biological divisions.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.1.2] Physical traits such as skin colour change gradually across space (these gradual changes are called clines) and have no sharp boundaries, so there is no point where one group clearly stops and another starts.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.1.3] Race is therefore largely a social classification (one made by societies, not by biology)
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.1.4] It still matters geographically because people, laws and states have acted on it through segregation (forcing groups to live apart), apartheid (South Africa's system of legal racial separation) and genocide (the deliberate killing of a whole group of people).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.2.b] Older textbooks sorted humanity into four racial groupings. Biologists now reject this scheme, and it is presented here only as a historical, outdated classification.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.2.0] Caucasoid: textbooks linked this grouping with lighter skin, straight or wavy hair and a narrow nose.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.2.1] Mongoloid: textbooks linked this grouping with straight dark hair, an eyelid fold (epicanthic fold) and a broad face.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.2.2] Negroid: textbooks linked this grouping with dark skin, tightly curled hair and a broad nose.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.2.3] Australoid: textbooks linked this grouping with dark skin, wavy hair and a broad nose.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.2.4] None of these traits makes one group superior to another, and the classification says nothing about ability or culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.3.b] Differences in appearance are not random. They built up as human populations adapted to the places they lived in.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.3.0] Physical traits developed through adaptation to environment over thousands of years.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.3.1] Skin pigmentation (melanin, the dark pigment in skin) increases towards the tropics, where ultraviolet radiation (the harmful part of sunlight) is strongest, so darker skin gives more protection there.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.3.2] Lighter skin at high latitudes (places far from the equator) allows the body to make vitamin D from less sunlight, because skin needs sunlight to produce it.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.4.b] Before European colonisation (European powers taking over and settling other lands), and before about 1500, the main racial groupings were largely separated by oceans, deserts and mountains. This long isolation kept each grouping mostly in its own region.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.4.0] Caucasoid peoples, such as Europeans, North Africans, Middle Eastern and South Asian peoples.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.4.1] Mongoloid peoples: East Asians, Central Asians, the Inuit (the Arctic peoples) and the native peoples of the Americas.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.4.2] Negroid peoples, such as the peoples of sub-Saharan Africa (Africa south of the Sahara Desert).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.4.3] Australoid peoples: Aboriginal Australians and Melanesians (the peoples of the islands of Melanesia in the south-west Pacific).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.5.b] Colonialism (rule over other lands by a foreign power) and migration since 1500 overturned the old pattern, so most large countries are now multi-racial. The Americas show this most clearly.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.5.0] Columbus reached the Americas in 1492. Disease (e.g. smallpox), war and forced labour caused the native population to collapse in the following century.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.5.1] Spanish and Portuguese settlers produced racially mixed populations. Mestizo means European and Native American ancestry; mulatto means European and African ancestry. Mexico's population is mainly mestizo, so it is an example of racial mixing.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.5.2] About 12.5 million Africans were shipped across the Atlantic as slaves between the 16th and 19th centuries.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.5.3] Most went to Brazil and the Caribbean; far fewer went to the American South. Brazil received about 4-5 million Africans, more than any other country.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.5.4] Brazil abolished slavery on 13 May 1888, the last country in the Americas to do so. The USA abolished it by the 13th Amendment (1865), after the Civil War (1861-65).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.5.5] The legacy is large Black populations and racial inequality across the Americas, which is why the slave trade still shapes the region.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.b] In settler colonies, newcomers from Britain took over land where Indigenous peoples already lived. The result was lasting change to who lived there and how they were treated.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.0] The First Fleet of British settlers and convicts arrived at Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.1] The Immigration Restriction Act 1901 began the White Australia Policy (a policy of keeping non-White immigrants out), which was dismantled between 1966 and 1973.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.2] Stolen Generations: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children were removed from their families by governments, roughly from 1910 to the 1970s.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.3] In the 1967 referendum (a national vote on one question) about 91% voted to count Aboriginal people in the census (the official population count) and let the federal government make laws for them.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.4] Prime Minister Kevin Rudd made a national apology on 13 February 2008.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.5] Census 2021 shows, for example, that about 3.2% of Australians identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander, and over a quarter of Australians were born overseas.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.6] The Indigenous Voice to Parliament referendum of 14 October 2023 was rejected, with about 60% voting No.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.6.7] New Zealand: the Treaty of Waitangi between the British Crown and Māori chiefs was signed on 6 February 1840. Māori are about one in six of New Zealand's population.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.7.b] The USA also moved its native peoples off their land. This changed where Native Americans live today.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.7.0] The Indian Removal Act 1830 led to the forced removal of the Cherokee in 1838, known as the Trail of Tears.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.7.1] Many Native Americans now live on reservations, a legacy of the removals (areas of land set aside for Native American peoples).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.8.b] When slavery ended, colonial planters still needed workers. Britain moved people within its own empire instead, which created new Indian communities far from India.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.8.0] After slavery was abolished, Britain shipped indentured Indian labourers (workers bound by contract to work for a set period) to sugar colonies: to the Caribbean (Trinidad, Guyana), to Fiji (1879-1916), to Mauritius, and to Natal in South Africa (from 1860).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.8.1] People of Indian descent are a large share of the population in Trinidad, Guyana, Fiji and Mauritius.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.8.2] These movements created Indian communities in each destination, which is why Indian descent is common there.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.9.b] Colonial rulers did not only move people. They could also fix labels onto groups, and in Rwanda those labels later mattered terribly.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.9.0] Belgium ruled Rwanda after the First World War.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.9.1] From 1933 Belgian identity cards recorded each person as Hutu, Tutsi or Twa, so the labels became fixed and official.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.9.2] The 1994 genocide, in which Hutu extremists killed mostly Tutsi, is set out in the racial conflict blocks below.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.10.b] After empires ended, migration reversed direction. People from former colonies moved to the colonial power (the mother country), which made European cities multi-racial.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.10.0] After 1945 people from former colonies moved to the mother country. Caribbean and South Asian migrants came to Britain.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.10.1] The Empire Windrush brought several hundred Caribbean migrants to Tilbury, near London, on 22 June 1948; post-war Caribbean migrants are known as the Windrush generation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.10.2] Later migration from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh also brought South Asian communities to Britain.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.10.3] Algerians and other North and West Africans moved to France, and Indonesians and Surinamese moved to the Netherlands.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.10.4] This migration created multi-racial European cities, because settled communities stayed and grew.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.11.b] Migration within a country can also change its racial pattern. In the USA, a huge movement of African Americans changed two regions at once.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.11.0] About 6 million African Americans moved from the rural South to cities of the North and West, such as Chicago, Detroit and New York, between about 1910 and 1970.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.11.1] Segregation and poverty pushed them out of the South, and industrial jobs pulled them to northern and western cities.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.11.2] The movement changed the racial make-up of both regions and created segregated inner-city neighbourhoods.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.12.b] Some European countries invited workers from abroad to fill jobs. Many of these workers stayed, which added new ethnic groups to the population.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.12.0] West Germany signed a recruitment agreement with Turkey on 30 October 1961.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.12.1] Guest workers (Gastarbeiter, the German word for them) were expected to return home, but many settled with their families, so Germany now has a large population of Turkish origin.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.12.2] Belgium signed labour agreements with Morocco and Turkey in 1964.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.12.3] North Africans also came to France and Belgium as labour migrants.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.13.b] People who move are not all moving for the same reason, and the law treats the groups differently. Learn the four terms and how they differ.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.13.0] Refugee: a person outside their own country who cannot return because of a well-founded fear of persecution (serious mistreatment) based on race, religion, nationality, political opinion or membership of a social group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.13.1] The 1951 UN Refugee Convention, with its 1967 Protocol, defines a refugee.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.13.2] Asylum seeker: a person who has applied for refugee status and is waiting for a decision. An asylum seeker becomes a refugee only if the claim is accepted.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.13.3] Economic migrant: a person who moves mainly for work or a better income.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.13.4] Internally displaced person: a person forced from home who is still inside their own country.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.13.5] UNHCR (the UN refugee agency) figures: over 120 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide in 2024.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.14.b] The Syrian civil war is the main example of refugees changing the population of other countries, including in Europe.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.14.0] The Syrian civil war from 2011 made over 6 million Syrians refugees, most of them in neighbouring countries.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.14.1] Turkey hosted about 3.5 million of them, the largest number of any country.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.14.2] In 2015 over 1 million refugees and migrants reached Europe across the Mediterranean, many travelling on to Germany.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.14.3] Germany received about 1 million asylum seekers in 2015-16.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.14.4] The Assad government (Syria's government during the war) fell on 8 December 2024, and some refugees began to return.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.15.b] The Rohingya show how losing citizenship can lead to mass flight.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.15.0] The Rohingya are a Muslim minority in Myanmar, made stateless (belonging to no country as a citizen) by Myanmar's 1982 citizenship law.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.15.1] From August 2017 over 700,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh, mostly to camps near Cox's Bazar.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.16.b] Ireland moved from a largely White, Irish, Catholic population to a multi-ethnic one. These are the figures to use for Ireland as the national example. The causes and patterns of Irish migration are covered in the note Changing migration patterns in Ireland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.16.0] Census 2022: about 5.15 million people lived in Ireland, and about 20% (over 1 million) were born outside Ireland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.16.1] About 12% were non-Irish citizens, the largest groups being Polish and UK citizens.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.16.2] About three-quarters described their ethnicity as White Irish, so about a quarter did not.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.16.3] Polish is the most common language spoken at home after English and Irish, reflecting the large Polish-citizen group (Census 2016 and 2022).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.16.4] Irish Travellers: about 33,000 people (Census 2022), under 1% of the population. The State formally recognised them as an ethnic minority on 1 March 2017.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.16.5] Citizenship referendum, June 2004 (about 79% Yes): ended the automatic right to Irish citizenship for every child born in Ireland.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.16.6] Ukrainians came to Ireland under the EU Temporary Protection Directive (an EU rule giving people fleeing war the right to stay), activated in March 2022 after Russia's invasion. Over 100,000 arrived.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.17.b] Ireland's system for people seeking protection is part of the syllabus's national setting. Direct Provision and the right to work are the main points.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.17.0] Direct Provision, which gives asylum seekers State accommodation and a weekly allowance while their claims are decided, began in 2000 and was widely criticised for long stays in shared centres.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.17.1] A 2017 Supreme Court ruling found the total ban on asylum seekers working unconstitutional; from 2018 they gained a limited right to work.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.17.2] More than 13,000 people applied for international protection (the formal term for asking for refugee status) in Ireland in each of 2022 and 2023.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.17.3] Irish and EU migration policy is covered in the note Migration policy in Ireland and the EU.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.18.b] Migrants change the place they move to, in ways that can help and in ways that can cause conflict. Keep your answer to the racial and ethnic pattern.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.18.0] The population becomes more diverse, for example with new places of worship, shops and food.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.18.1] Ethnic neighbourhoods form as migrants cluster near others from their own group.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.18.2] Migrants supply labour to the receiving economy, so they fill jobs.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.18.3] Discrimination, segregation and tension can follow. On 23 November 2023 a riot broke out in Dublin city centre after a stabbing on Parnell Square, fuelled by anti-immigrant agitation online.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.19.b] The place migrants leave is changed too, in two ways.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.19.0] The source area loses young adults, who are the most likely to migrate, so fewer workers are left.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.19.1] Migrants send remittances, money that supports families who stay behind.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.20.b] A multi-racial society is one in which two or more racial groups make up significant shares of the population. Colonialism and migration have made this the normal pattern in large countries.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.20.0] Examples include the USA, Brazil, South Africa, the UK and Ireland today.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.20.1] Racial mixing, for example in Brazil: people of different racial backgrounds have children together, producing mixed-race populations.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.21.b] The USA is a multi-racial society whose census shows growing Hispanic and mixed populations. Two images describe how its groups live together.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.21.0] US Census 2020: White (alone) about 62%; Hispanic or Latino (of any race) about 19%; Black about 12%; Asian about 6%.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.21.1] About 10% identified with two or more races, more than double the 2010 figure. Hispanics are the largest minority, so they make up about 19% of the population.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.21.2] Melting pot: minority groups assimilate (adopt the majority's way of life) into one common national culture.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.21.3] Salad bowl: groups keep distinct identities while sharing one nation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.22.b] Brazil is the main example of racial mixing in this option. Its population grew from three starting groups, then added later immigrants.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.22.0] Brazil's population descends from Portuguese colonists, enslaved Africans and Indigenous peoples, with later Italian, German, Japanese and Arab immigrants.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.22.1] Brazil has the largest Japanese-descended population outside Japan, mainly in São Paulo state, which shows the effect of later immigrants.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.22.2] Census 2022: pardo (mixed race) about 45%, White (branco) about 44%, Black (preto) about 10%, Indigenous under 1%.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.22.3] Census 2022 was the first census since 1991 in which pardo was the largest group, so mixed race is now the biggest category.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.22.4] The ideal of racial democracy claims that mixing has removed racial division, but White Brazilians on average remain wealthier.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.23.b] The UK became multi-racial mainly through post-war migration from Commonwealth countries (former British colonies). Its census and the Windrush scandal show the result.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.23.0] Census 2021 (England and Wales): White about 82%; Asian or Asian British about 9%; Black about 4%; Mixed about 3%.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.23.1] The Windrush scandal (2018): some long-settled Caribbean-born residents were wrongly detained, denied services or deported because they lacked documents.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.24.b] Governments can respond to diversity in different ways. Multiculturalism and assimilation are the two policy approaches you should be able to compare.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.24.0] Multiculturalism: a policy of accepting and supporting distinct cultures within one society, as in Canada, which adopted an official multiculturalism policy in 1971, and Australia.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.24.1] Assimilation: minorities adopt the majority culture. France's model expects integration into one secular French identity and does not collect ethnic statistics in its census.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.24.2] Arguments for multiculturalism: enrichment, labour supply and tolerance.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.24.3] Arguments against multiculturalism: segregation, parallel societies and backlash, including far-right politics.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.25.b] Racial conflict (violent or serious conflict between racial or ethnic groups) has several causes.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.25.0] Competition for jobs and housing.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.25.1] Segregation, which separates groups and limits contact between them, so suspicion can grow.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.25.2] Unequal power inherited from colonialism and slavery.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.25.3] Racist ideologies, and fear of newcomers.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.26.b] South Africa is the main case study of racial conflict. Apartheid was a system of laws that separated people by race and gave power to the White minority.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.26.0] Apartheid was introduced by the National Party after it won the 1948 election.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.26.1] The Population Registration Act 1950 classified everyone as White, Black (African), Coloured (mixed race) or Indian/Asian.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.26.2] The Group Areas Act 1950 assigned residential areas by race, so it enforced segregation.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.26.3] Homelands, known as Bantustans, were set up for Black South Africans, so that Black South Africans were kept in separate areas.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.26.4] District Six, Cape Town, was declared a White area in 1966. About 60,000 residents were forcibly removed and their homes bulldozed.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.27.b] Resistance and protest grew against apartheid and eventually ended it, but its effects have not disappeared.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.27.0] Sharpeville, 21 March 1960: police killed 69 protesters, which shows the violence used against resistance.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.27.1] Soweto uprising, 16 June 1976: students protested against being taught through Afrikaans (the language of many White South Africans).
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.27.2] Nelson Mandela was released on 11 February 1990 after 27 years in prison. The first all-race election, on 27 April 1994, made him President.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.27.3] The Truth and Reconciliation Commission began work in 1996, so that the wrongs of apartheid could be faced.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.27.4] South Africa Census 2022: Black African about 81%, Coloured about 8%, White about 7%, Indian/Asian about 3%.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.27.5] Wealth and land ownership remain highly unequal by race, so the legacy of apartheid continues.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.28.b] After slavery ended, the American South kept racial separation in law. The civil rights movement challenged it and won new laws, but tension has continued.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.28.0] Jim Crow laws enforced segregation in the southern states after the Civil War.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.28.1] Brown v Board of Education (1954): the Supreme Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional, so separate schools by race were not allowed.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.28.2] The Montgomery bus boycott (1955) began after Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.28.3] The Civil Rights Act 1964 and the Voting Rights Act 1965 followed, because of the movement's pressure.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.28.4] Martin Luther King, a leader of the civil rights movement, was assassinated on 4 April 1968.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.28.5] George Floyd was killed by a Minneapolis police officer on 25 May 2020, leading to worldwide Black Lives Matter protests. The movement itself began in 2013.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.29.b] Nazi Germany shows racist ideology being turned into law and then into genocide.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.29.0] The Nuremberg Laws (1935) stripped Jews of citizenship and banned marriage between Jews and other Germans.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.29.1] Racist ideology was turned into law and then into genocide, so the Nuremberg Laws were a step towards the Holocaust.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.29.2] About 6 million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust. Roma were also targeted for extermination.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.30.b] Rwanda is a genocide linked to the colonial labels described earlier, when Belgian identity cards fixed Hutu and Tutsi identities.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.30.0] From 7 April 1994, Hutu extremists killed about 800,000 people, mostly Tutsi, in about 100 days.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.31.b] Racial tension in Europe has also shown itself in riots in cities, in Britain and France.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.31.0] Notting Hill, London, 1958, for example: white mobs attacked Caribbean residents.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.31.1] Brixton, London, April 1981: clashes between mainly Black youths and police.
[geo-option-culture-identity-s1.31.2] France, October-November 2005: riots spread through the suburbs (banlieues) of Paris and other cities after two teenagers died fleeing police in Clichy-sous-Bois. A state of emergency was declared.

CANDIDATES geo-population-migration-s3: 3. Changing migration patterns in Ireland
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.b] Migration has its own vocabulary, and examiners expect you to use it precisely. These terms let you describe who moves, where, and why.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.0] Migration is a permanent or long-term change of residence, so a holiday or a short work trip does not count.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.1] Emigration is leaving a country; immigration is arriving in one. Net migration is immigrants minus emigrants, so it is positive when more people arrive than leave and negative when more leave than arrive.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.2] Internal migration moves people within a country; international migration crosses borders, so it is the kind that meets immigration laws.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.3] Voluntary migration is a free choice; forced migration is driven by conflict or persecution (being treated badly or harmed because of who you are or what you believe), so people have little or no choice.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.4] Economic migrants move for work; refugees and asylum seekers (people who ask another country for protection) flee persecution. Asylum policy is in Migration policy in Ireland and the EU.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.5] Return migration is emigrants coming back to their home country, such as Irish emigrants returning in the 1970s.
[geo-population-migration-s3.0.6] Chain migration is when migrants follow family or friends who have already moved, because those contacts offer information, a place to stay and help finding work.
[geo-population-migration-s3.1.b] People move because something drives them away from home (a push factor) or something attracts them to another place (a pull factor). Ireland has examples of both.
[geo-population-migration-s3.1.0] Push factors in Ireland: unemployment, low wages, emigration of young people in recession (a period when the economy shrinks and jobs are lost) and the decline of farm labouring (paid work on other people's farms), which left rural workers without jobs.
[geo-population-migration-s3.1.1] Pull factors in Ireland: jobs in the Celtic Tiger economy (the period of rapid growth from the mid-1990s), high wages in the UK, the USA and Australia, and the availability of work permits (official permission to take a job in a country).
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.b] Even when push and pull factors favour a move, obstacles can stop or reduce it. These barriers are also called intervening obstacles.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.0] Distance and the cost of travel, because a longer journey costs more money and time.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.1] Immigration laws, visas (permits to enter or stay) and quotas (limits on how many people may enter).
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.2] Language, because it is hard to work or settle where you cannot communicate.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.3] Family ties and age: people with family at home, or older people, are less willing or able to move.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.4] Lack of information, because people will not move to a place they know little about.
[geo-population-migration-s3.2.5] Cultural difference, such as unfamiliar customs, which makes settling in harder.
[geo-population-migration-s3.3.b] Geographers have turned patterns in migration into simple models. Each gives a short rule you can use to explain why and how people move.
[geo-population-migration-s3.3.0] Ravenstein's laws of migration say that most migrants move short distances, in steps (for example from a village to a town and then to a city).
[geo-population-migration-s3.3.1] Ravenstein also said that each migration stream has a counter-stream (people moving the opposite way), that young adults are most likely to migrate, and that cities attract migrants.
[geo-population-migration-s3.3.2] Lee's push-pull model says migration is driven by push and pull factors and reduced by intervening obstacles (the barriers to migration listed above).
[geo-population-migration-s3.4.b] Migrants are not a random sample of the population. Certain groups move far more than others, which shapes both the place they leave and the place they reach.
[geo-population-migration-s3.4.0] Migration is age-selective: mostly young adults move, because they are free to take jobs and have fewest ties.
[geo-population-migration-s3.4.1] In the 1950s more women than men left some rural areas.
[geo-population-migration-s3.5.b] Emigration has shaped Irish population for well over a century. The Great Famine (a disaster in the 1840s when the potato crop failed and many starved) began a long decline in numbers.

[geo-population-migration-s3.6.5] Most went to Britain: London, Birmingham, Manchester, Coventry and Liverpool.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.6] The Commission on Emigration and Other Population Problems reported in 1954.
[geo-population-migration-s3.6.7] Impact: Ireland, as donor region, lost young workers and its rural population declined.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.b] Government policy changed direction around 1958, turning towards economic growth and foreign investment. As jobs appeared, emigration fell and, for the first time since the Famine, more people arrived than left.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.0] Economic Development by T. K. Whitaker and the First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958, cut emigration.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.1] The IDA (Industrial Development Authority), set up in 1949, was given a central role in attracting foreign investment from 1969.

[geo-population-migration-s3.7.3] Ireland joined the EEC (the European Economic Community, forerunner of the EU) on 1 January 1973.
[geo-population-migration-s3.7.4] State population rose from about 3.0 million (Census 1971) to about 3.4 million (Census 1981).
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.b] The gains of the 1970s did not last. A weak economy in the 1980s sent many people abroad again.
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.0] Causes: recession, high unemployment and high taxes.
[geo-population-migration-s3.8.1] Net emigration of over 200,000 between 1981 and 1991, including many graduates. This loss of educated people is the so-called brain drain.

[geo-population-migration-s3.8.4] The State's population fell slightly between Census 1986 and Census 1991; both were about 3.5 million.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.b] From the mid-1990s a booming economy, nicknamed the Celtic Tiger, reversed the old pattern. Ireland now attracted workers from many countries.

[geo-population-migration-s3.9.2] Ireland, the UK and Sweden were the only existing members to open their labour markets immediately, so citizens of the new members could work here without delay.

[geo-population-migration-s3.9.4] Census 2006 State population was about 4.2 million.
[geo-population-migration-s3.9.5] Ireland became a country of immigration, meaning it now received more people than it sent abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s3.10.b] The Celtic Tiger ended abruptly in 2008, and emigration returned. Many of those leaving were the young and skilled.
[geo-population-migration-s3.10.0] Causes: the property crash, the banking crisis, which began in 2008, unemployment, and the EU-IMF bailout (emergency loans from the EU and International Monetary Fund) agreed in November 2010.

[geo-population-migration-s3.11.b] As the economy recovered, the flow reversed once more. Net immigration returned in the mid-2010s and continued.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.0] Causes: economic recovery, job growth in multinationals and services, returning Irish emigrants, and non-EU skilled workers.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.1] Ukrainians came under EU temporary protection after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022; over 100,000 people from Ukraine came to Ireland by 2024.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.2] International protection applicants (people applying for asylum) also arrived.
[geo-population-migration-s3.11.3] Housing pressure, because more people needing homes added to demand for housing.
[geo-population-migration-s3.12.b] Population change has two parts: natural increase (births minus deaths) and net migration. Comparing them shows which one drove growth.
[geo-population-migration-s3.12.0] Net migration of about 190,000 between 2016 and 2022 was larger than natural increase (about 170,000), so migration was the bigger source of population growth.
[geo-population-migration-s3.13.b] Decades of immigration have made Ireland's population much more varied. The 2022 census gives the size of the immigrant population and its main nationalities.
[geo-population-migration-s3.13.0] About 630,000 non-Irish nationals (about 12% of the population), plus about 170,000 dual Irish citizens (people who hold Irish and another citizenship).
[geo-population-migration-s3.13.1] Just over 1 million people (about one in five) were born outside Ireland.
[geo-population-migration-s3.13.2] Largest non-Irish groups: Polish about 94,000; UK about 83,000; Indian about 45,000; Romanian about 43,000; Lithuanian about 31,000; Brazilian about 27,000.
[geo-population-migration-s3.13.3] Polish is the most common language other than English and Irish spoken at home (Census 2016 and 2022).
[geo-population-migration-s3.14.b] Centuries of emigration have left Irish people spread around the world. This scattered population is called the diaspora.
[geo-population-migration-s3.14.0] Irish communities and people of Irish descent live in Britain, the USA and Australia.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.b] A donor region is a place that loses people through emigration. Losing so many people, mostly young, damaged Irish communities.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.0] Loss of young workers and skills (brain drain), and of the State's investment in their education, because the country paid to train people who then worked abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.1] Ageing rural population and an imbalanced age structure, because the young left and the old stayed.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.2] Rural decline: closed schools, fewer marriages and depopulated parishes.
[geo-population-migration-s3.15.3] Lower birth rates, because fewer young adults remained to have children, and family separation.
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.b] Emigration was not all loss. It also brought some benefits to Ireland.
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.0] Emigrant remittances (money sent home by emigrants to their families).
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.1] Less pressure on jobs and welfare, because fewer people were looking for work or needing support.
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.2] Emigrants returned with skills, capital (money to invest) and experience in the 1970s and 1990s.
[geo-population-migration-s3.16.3] Diaspora links support trade, tourism and investment.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.b] A receiver region is a place that gains people through immigration. Immigration since the 1990s has brought Ireland a range of benefits.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.0] Fills labour shortages in construction, health care, hospitality and technology.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.1] Young workers pay taxes and offset ageing, because they balance a population with many older people.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.2] New skills and enterprise (business start-ups), because migrants bring training and ideas from abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.3] Cultural diversity, such as food, sport, languages and religions.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.4] Keeps rural schools and parishes open, because newcomers add children and residents.
[geo-population-migration-s3.17.5] Boosts population growth, as net migration was the larger component of growth in 2016-2022.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.b] Rapid immigration also creates problems. Services and communities have to adjust to more people.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.0] Pressure on housing, schools and health services, because more people share the same supply.
[geo-population-migration-s3.18.1] Exploitation of some migrant workers, such as being badly treated or underpaid.

[geo-population-migration-s3.19.0] Economic recovery and job creation, which drew workers.
[geo-population-migration-s3.19.1] Multinational investment, as large foreign-owned companies created jobs.
[geo-population-migration-s3.19.2] EU free movement, which lets citizens of EU countries live and work in Ireland.

[geo-population-migration-s3.19.4] Ireland is English-speaking and in the EU.

[geo-population-migration-s3.20.0] The recession, the collapse of construction and rising unemployment, which removed the jobs that had attracted migrants.
[geo-population-migration-s3.20.1] Many EU accession migrants (those from the countries that joined in 2004) returned home.

CANDIDATES geo-population-migration-s5: 5. Ethnic, racial and religious issues arising from migration
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.b] When people move, they carry their culture, appearance and beliefs with them. These three categories overlap but are not the same, and migration can bring all three into contact in one place.
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.0] An ethnic group is a group of people who share culture, language, ancestry, traditions or religion, which is why members often feel they belong to the same community.
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.1] A race is a group classed by physical traits such as skin colour. Race is a social idea with no biological basis for ranking people, so no race is naturally better than another.
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.2] Religion is a shared system of belief and practice. It can cut across ethnic and racial groups, so people of different ethnic groups or races may share one religion.
[geo-population-migration-s5.0.3] A multi-ethnic society contains several ethnic groups living together. A multicultural society goes further: it is one in which those groups keep distinct cultural practices.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.b] When migrants and the host population meet, the result can be hostility or acceptance. These terms name the attitudes, the settlement patterns and the policies that governments use in response.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.0] Racism is prejudice (an unfair opinion formed in advance) or discrimination (unfair treatment) based on race or ethnicity.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.1] Xenophobia is fear or hostility towards foreigners. Islamophobia is prejudice against Muslims, so it targets people because of their religion.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.2] Segregation is the clustering of migrants in particular areas. It happens either by choice, for support, cheap housing and chain migration (earlier migrants helping relatives and neighbours to follow them), or because discrimination forces migrants there.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.3] Assimilation is the adoption of the majority culture by migrants, so their own culture is gradually given up.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.4] Integration is the joining of migrants to society while they keep some of their own culture.
[geo-population-migration-s5.1.5] Multiculturalism is the acceptance of several cultures as equal within one society, so no single culture is treated as the norm.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.b] Tensions rarely come from difference alone. They arise from circumstances, discrimination and politics, and the same general mechanisms appear in each of the case studies below.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.0] Competition for jobs, housing and services, especially in recessions (periods when the economy shrinks and jobs are scarce), and fear that migrants will undercut wages by working for less.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.1] Visible difference, such as skin colour or dress like the hijab (the headscarf worn by some Muslim women), makes minorities easy targets of prejudice.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.2] Religious practice in public space can clash with secular (non-religious) or majority-religion norms. Examples are places of worship, dress, halal food (food prepared according to Islamic rules), holy days and faith schools.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.3] Residential segregation and concentration in deprived areas reinforce poverty and separation, because people who live apart have fewer chances to mix at school or work.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.4] Language barriers in schools, work and services make it harder for migrants to take part fully.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.5] Political exploitation: far-right and anti-immigration parties and movements, and misinformation on social media, turn worries into hostility.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.6] Second-generation issues: children born in the host country may face discrimination and conflict over identity, since they belong to two cultures.
[geo-population-migration-s5.2.7] Positive outcomes include cultural enrichment through food, music, sport and festivals, new places of worship, intercultural schools and mixed marriages.
[geo-population-migration-s5.3.b] Ireland moved from a mostly white, Catholic society to a diverse one from the 1990s. The census shows both the new nationalities and the shift in religion.
[geo-population-migration-s5.3.0] Census 2022: about one in five people (just over 1 million) were born outside Ireland, and about 630,000 were non-Irish nationals (about 12%).
[geo-population-migration-s5.3.1] The largest non-Irish groups are Polish, UK, Indian, Romanian, Lithuanian and Brazilian.
[geo-population-migration-s5.3.2] Census 2022: about 69% of people were Roman Catholic, down from about 78% in 2016, so the majority religion is shrinking in share.
[geo-population-migration-s5.3.3] Census 2022: about 14% had no religion, up from about 10% in 2016.
[geo-population-migration-s5.3.4] Census 2022 counted about 82,000 Muslims, about 100,000 Orthodox Christians and about 33,000 Hindus, showing growth in religions that were once rare in Ireland.
[geo-population-migration-s5.3.5] The Islamic Cultural Centre of Ireland is in Clonskeagh, Dublin, an example of a new place of worship and community centre.
[geo-population-migration-s5.3.6] About 9 in 10 primary schools are under Catholic patronage (the Catholic Church runs them), so religion once decided many school admissions.
[geo-population-migration-s5.4.b] Rapid change brought hostility as well as welcome. Several incidents show how housing pressure and anti-immigration feeling can turn to protest and violence.
[geo-population-migration-s5.4.0] East Wall, Dublin: protests from November 2022 against housing international protection applicants (people seeking asylum) in a former office building.
[geo-population-migration-s5.4.1] Dublin riot, 23 November 2023: after a knife attack on children outside a school on Parnell Square, buses, a Luas tram (Dublin's light-rail line) and shops were burned and looted.
[geo-population-migration-s5.4.2] Arson attacks (deliberate fires) on several buildings proposed for asylum accommodation (2023 to 2024).
[geo-population-migration-s5.4.3] Racism and hate crime (crime motivated by hatred of a group) accompany these tensions, and the protests show how hostility can turn to violence.
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.b] Irish policy combines equality law, an integration strategy and anti-racism measures, with specific reforms to schools and recognition of Travellers. Each tackles an issue raised in the earlier blocks.
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.0] The Employment Equality Act 1998 and the Equal Status Act 2000 prohibit discrimination on nine grounds, including race, religion and membership of the Traveller community.
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.1] The Prohibition of Incitement to Hatred Act 1989 is an earlier law against hatred, so Ireland had some protection before the recent migration.
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.2] The Irish Human Rights and Equality Commission was established in 2014 to promote and protect human rights and equality.
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.3] The Migrant Integration Strategy ran from 2017 to 2020, aiming to help migrants join Irish society.
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.4] The National Action Plan Against Racism was published in March 2023.
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.5] The Criminal Justice (Hate Offences) Act 2024 gives tougher sentences for crimes motivated by hatred of a protected characteristic (such as race or religion).
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.6] The Education (Admission to Schools) Act 2018 removed the baptism barrier, so most Catholic primary schools can no longer use religion to decide admissions. Educate Together, a multi-denominational school body founded in 1984, runs schools open to all faiths and none.
[geo-population-migration-s5.5.7] Irish Travellers were formally recognised by the State as an ethnic minority on 1 March 2017.
[geo-population-migration-s5.6.b] Judging Irish policy means asking whether the laws have worked in practice. The Traveller community also belongs in this discussion, but not as migrants.
[geo-population-migration-s5.6.0] The legal framework is strong, but the 2022 to 2023 protests show that hostility persists.
[geo-population-migration-s5.6.1] Direct provision (the system of housing asylum applicants in centres while their claims are processed) isolates applicants in rural centres, and this works against integration. There is more on this in Migration policy in Ireland and the EU.
[geo-population-migration-s5.6.2] The Traveller community is an indigenous Irish ethnic minority, not a migrant group, so it is included here only as part of Irish ethnic policy.
[geo-population-migration-s5.6.3] About 33,000 Travellers were counted in Census 2022.
[geo-population-migration-s5.7.b] France has a large Muslim population and a strict model of secularism, and it follows an assimilation approach. Its laws on religious dress show how the model works in practice.
[geo-population-migration-s5.7.0] Post-war immigrant labour came from Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia and Portugal. Large-scale Algerian migration followed the Algerian War of Independence (1954 to 1962) and Algeria's independence in 1962.

[geo-population-migration-s5.8.2] The Charlie Hebdo attack in January 2015 and the Paris attacks of 13 November 2015 (130 killed) were followed by rising Islamophobia.
[geo-population-migration-s5.8.3] The National Front, a far-right party founded in 1972 by Jean-Marie Le Pen, was renamed the National Rally (Rassemblement National) in 2018 under Marine Le Pen.
[geo-population-migration-s5.9.b] Germany recruited workers to rebuild its economy, expecting them to leave. Many stayed, and the question of their citizenship shaped integration for decades.

[geo-population-migration-s5.9.6] The Alternative für Deutschland (AfD), founded in 2013, grew after the 2015 refugee arrivals on an anti-immigration platform, so it is an example of political exploitation of migration.
[geo-population-migration-s5.10.b] Caribbean migrants arrived in Britain from 1948. Their arrival shows racism in housing and work, and how policy later had to respond.

[geo-population-migration-s5.11.b] The Plantation was a historic migration whose religious legacy still divides Northern Ireland. Here it is studied only as a migration and its religious legacy.
[geo-population-migration-s5.11.0] The Plantation of Ulster began in 1609, after the Flight of the Earls (1607). Land taken from Catholic Irish was settled by Protestant colonists from Scotland and England.

[geo-population-migration-s5.12.b] The Roma are an example of an ethnic minority that has been on the move for centuries and has often been excluded wherever it settles.

[geo-population-migration-s5.13.b] The case studies show three different responses to migrant minorities. Comparing them is a good way to finish an answer on government policy.
[geo-population-migration-s5.13.0] France's assimilation model treats all citizens alike and collects no official ethnic statistics.
[geo-population-migration-s5.13.1] Ireland's approach rests on equality law and an integration strategy.
[geo-population-migration-s5.13.2] Germany moved from excluding guest workers from citizenship to citizenship reform in 2000 and 2024.
