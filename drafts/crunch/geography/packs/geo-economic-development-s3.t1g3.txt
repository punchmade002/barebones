[geo-economic-development-s3.0.b] Colonialism is the control of one territory and its people by a foreign power. The colonising country rules the colony (the territory it controls), settles it and/or exploits its resources for its own benefit, and that purpose shaped the colony's economy.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.0] Imperialism is the wider policy of building an empire, that is, a group of territories ruled from one centre. Colonialism is how that policy works on the ground in each territory.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.1] The main European colonial powers were Britain, France, Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands, Belgium and Germany.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.2] The Americas were colonised first, then India and Asia, and then Africa, so the periods of colonial rule differ from region to region.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.3] Africa was divided among European powers in the Scramble for Africa after the Berlin Conference (1884–85), which set the rules for European claims. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia in Africa were still independent of European rule.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.4] Colonies supplied cheap raw materials (unprocessed goods) such as cotton, sugar, rubber and minerals to the colonial power, and bought its manufactured goods. This made the colony a supplier and a market rather than a manufacturer.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.5] Local industries that competed with these imports were destroyed, so the colony lost its own manufacturing.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.6] Plantations (large farms growing one crop for sale) and mines used forced or slave labour, which kept the cost of raw materials low.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.7] Food crops were replaced by cash crops (crops grown for sale and export, not for local food), a pattern called monoculture, meaning growing mainly one crop.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.b] Colonial powers built transport and institutions to serve their own needs, and independent states inherited the results. The legacy (what was left behind) was economic, political and social.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.0] Railways and roads ran from the interior to the ports so that raw materials could be exported. They were not built to link regions with each other, so movement between regions stayed poor.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.1] Ports and capitals grew into the main cities, so development was concentrated on the coast.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.2] Positive legacies are debated: railways, ports, some irrigation, legal and administrative systems, and a world language. Present both sides in an answer.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.3] Most of this infrastructure was built to serve the colonial power rather than the colony's own people, which is why its benefits are debated.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.4] Artificial borders drawn in Europe split some ethnic groups and grouped rival ones together, which created tension inside the new states.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.5] The colonial power's policy of divide and rule (keeping a population weak by setting its groups against one another) set groups against one another.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.6] European languages and religions spread, education reached only a small elite, racial hierarchies (rankings of people by race) were enforced, and land was owned by a few.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.7] Weak institutions (governments, courts and civil services) were handed over at independence, and conflict and coups (seizures of power by the army) followed in many new states.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.b] After independence, governments tried a sequence of strategies to make their economies work in a world market dominated by rich countries. Each country below follows some of these stages.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.0] Import substitution industrialisation: new home industries were protected from foreign competition by tariffs (taxes on imported goods), so local firms could make what had been imported.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.1] Neo-colonialism (new colonialism without direct rule): former colonies often stayed dependent on exporting a few raw materials to rich countries at low and unstable prices, and on foreign loans and companies.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.2] Debt crises in the 1980s and 1990s, when countries could not repay what they owed, led to IMF (International Monetary Fund) and World Bank structural adjustment programmes. These cut government spending, privatised state firms (sold them to private owners), opened markets to trade, and devalued the currency.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.3] Liberalisation: opening the economy to trade and foreign investment, and privatising state firms.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.4] Export diversification: moving beyond a few raw materials into manufactured and service exports, so the country is less exposed to one price.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.5] Joining the World Trade Organisation (WTO, the body that sets rules for trade between countries) and regional trading blocs (groups of countries that trade on easier terms).
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.6] Growth of services and of remittances (money sent home by emigrants).

[geo-economic-development-s3.3.2] The Rebellion of 1857 led to the Government of India Act 1858, which transferred rule from the Company to the British Crown. This began direct British rule, known as the Raj.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.3] India gained independence, so British rule ended, and was partitioned (divided into separate states) on 15 August 1947.
