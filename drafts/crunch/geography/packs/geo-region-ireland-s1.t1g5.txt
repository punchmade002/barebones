TARGET geo-region-ireland-s1 (part 1 of 2): 1. A Core Irish Region
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.b] The Greater Dublin Area (GDA) is the core region of Ireland's east coast, facing Britain and Europe across the Irish Sea. Knowing exactly which counties it includes is the starting point for every question on it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.0] The GDA is Dublin City and County Dublin, plus Counties Meath, Kildare and Wicklow. Louth, including Drogheda, is not part of it, so do not count it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.1] Dublin City, Fingal, South Dublin and Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown are the four local authorities (the councils that run local services) of Dublin; the single Co. Dublin county council was split into the last three in 1994.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.b] A core region is one that concentrates people, wealth and services, in contrast to peripheral regions (remote areas with fewer of these). The GDA fits this description in several ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.0] Its large, dense and growing population is the largest in the State, which gives firms customers and workers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.1] It holds most of the State's wealth, jobs, multinational investment, government and services, which means firms and workers want to be there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.2] It has the best infrastructure in the State, including the main port, airport and motorway network, so goods and people move easily.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.3] It attracts in-migrants (people moving in) from the rest of Ireland and from abroad.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.4] Cumulative causation (growth that feeds more growth): growth attracts firms and workers, which attracts further investment and widens the gap with peripheral regions such as the West.

[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.b] The GDA has a cool temperate oceanic climate (mild and wet because of the nearby ocean), with prevailing south-westerly winds bringing moist Atlantic air. Because of where the mountains sit, the east is drier than the west.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.0] Rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of high ground): the Wicklow Mountains and the uplands further west force moist air to drop its rain on the western slopes, so the east is the driest part of Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.1] Rainfall in the east is about 750–1,000 mm a year, so it is much drier than the west; Dublin Airport records about 750 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.2] By contrast, the west receives 1,000–1,250 mm a year and some mountains exceed 2,000 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.3] The south-east is the sunniest part of Ireland, which adds to the east's value for crops.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.4] Mean January temperatures in Ireland are about 4–7 °C and mean July temperatures about 14–16 °C, so winters are mild and summers cool.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.5] Being farther from the Atlantic than the west gives a slightly greater temperature range: warmer summers, colder winters and more frost.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.6] Heavier rain and snow fall on the Wicklow uplands than on the lowlands, because rising air cools and drops its moisture.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.b] Climate decides what the land can be used for. The dry east and the wet uplands are used in different ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.0] Effect on farming: the dry, relatively sunny lowlands suit tillage (growing crops such as cereals and vegetables) and horticulture (growing vegetables, fruit and flowers).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.1] Effect on land use: heavy upland rain supports conifer forestry and reservoirs, because the rain feeds fast-growing trees and fills water stores.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.b] Relief means the height and shape of the land. The GDA has a wide lowland to the north and west and a mountain block to the south, and each is used differently.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.0] A low-lying, gently undulating lowland covers most of Meath, Kildare and north Co. Dublin, part of the Central Lowlands.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.1] The Central Lowlands are underlain by Carboniferous limestone (a sedimentary rock), covered by glacial deposits.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.2] The Wicklow and Dublin Mountains lie to the south, with a core of the Leinster granite batholith (a huge mass of granite that cooled underground), about 400 million years old (Caledonian, the mountain-building episode that made it).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.3] Lugnaquilla, 925 m, in Co. Wicklow, is the highest peak in Leinster, so it is a good example of the region's upland relief.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.4] Kippure, about 757 m, stands on the Dublin–Wicklow border, so the mountains are close enough to the city to be used for recreation.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.5] The Great Sugar Loaf, Co. Wicklow, is a quartzite peak (quartzite is a very hard rock) that stands out from the granite.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.6] Effect: the lowland is easy to farm, build on and cross with roads and rail; the uplands suit forestry, water supply and recreation.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.b] Ice sheets and the sea have both shaped the region. These named landforms are the examples to use.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.0] The U-shaped valleys (steep-sided, flat-floored valleys) of Glendalough and Glenmalure were carved by glacial erosion, which is why their sides are steep and their floors flat.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.1] Esker Riada is a long ridge of sand and gravel deposited by glacial meltwater, which is why it is made of loose, rounded material.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.2] Howth Head is joined to the mainland by the Sutton tombolo (a bar of sand or shingle linking an island or headland to the land).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.3] North Bull Island lies in Dublin Bay, so it is an example of a coastal feature there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.b] Soil follows the rock and drainage beneath it. The lowlands have deep, fertile soils, while the granite uplands and bogs have poor ones, so soil sets how land is used.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.0] Deep, fertile brown earths (a well-developed soil rich in nutrients) on limestone boulder clay (stony clay left by ice sheets) cover Meath, Kildare and north Co. Dublin, among the best farmland in Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.1] Brown earths are well drained, which suits both tillage and grassland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.2] The Curragh, Co. Kildare, has free-draining sand and gravel from glacial outwash (material washed out from the ice by meltwater), with well-drained grassland used for horse training.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.3] The Bog of Allen, Co. Kildare, is a raised bog (a dome of peat built up from dead plants) of deep peat, which is unsuitable for farming unless drained.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.4] Blanket bog (peat covering the ground like a blanket) on granite at the Sally Gap, Co. Wicklow, is waterlogged and acidic, so it is of little use for farming.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.5] Acidic, leached podzols (grey soils with nutrients washed out of the top layer) cover the granite uplands of Wicklow, poor for farming but suitable for conifers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.6] Effect on land use: fertile lowland soils support farming; thin, acidic upland soils and bog are used for forestry, recreation and water catchment (land that collects rain for a river or reservoir).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.b] Drainage covers the rivers, canals and reservoirs of the region. In the GDA they matter because a large city needs a reliable water supply.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.0] The River Liffey rises in the Wicklow Mountains, flows through Kildare and enters the sea at Dublin Bay, so it links upland to city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.1] The Poulaphouca dam on the Liffey created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, used for hydro-electricity and for Dublin's drinking water.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.2] The Vartry Reservoir at Roundwood, Co. Wicklow, supplies Dublin with water, so the uplands serve the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.3] The River Boyne flows through Trim and Navan, Co. Meath, and reaches the sea near Drogheda, outside the GDA, so its mouth is not in the region.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.4] The smaller Dublin rivers, the Dodder and the Tolka, flow through the city to Dublin Bay, so they drain urban areas.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.5] The Royal and Grand Canals link Dublin to the River Shannon, so they connect the east coast to the interior.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.6] Uisce Éireann (Irish Water) has proposed a pipeline to bring water from the River Shannon to the Dublin region, because demand on local supplies is growing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.7] Paved surfaces in the city let rain run off quickly into rivers, raising urban flood risk.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.b] Farming does well in the GDA because physical and human advantages come together. Each factor below can be developed as a separate point in an answer.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.0] Climate: the dry, relatively sunny east suits tillage (growing crops) and horticulture (growing vegetables and similar crops).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.1] Relief: flat lowland allows large fields and mechanised farming, which cuts the cost of each crop.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.2] Soils: deep, fertile brown earths in Meath and Kildare give high-quality land for crops and grass.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.3] Market: Dublin, the largest urban area in the State, lies on the farms' doorstep, so produce has a short journey to many buyers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.4] Transport: Dublin Port and Dublin Airport export farm produce and livestock, so farmers can sell abroad.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.5] Policy and technology: EU and Irish agricultural policy and modern machinery support large, productive farms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.b] Farming here is large-scale and commercial, but each county specialises in something different, shaped by its soil, relief and nearness to Dublin.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.0] Large, commercial and highly productive farms dominate the lowlands, because the flat, fertile land suits them.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.1] Meath, for example: beef cattle fattening (raising cattle to slaughter weight) and tillage on large farms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.2] Kildare: bloodstock (breeding of racehorses) and racehorse training, centred on the Curragh racecourse and the Irish National Stud at Tully, Kildare town.

[geo-population-migration-s1.0.b] Geographers describe a population with a small set of measures. Learn each definition exactly, because every later block uses them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.0] Crude birth rate: live births per 1,000 people per year. It is 'crude' because it counts the whole population, not just women of childbearing age.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.1] Crude death rate: deaths per 1,000 people per year.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.2] Natural change: birth rate minus death rate. A positive result is natural increase (more births than deaths); a negative result is natural decrease (more deaths than births).
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.3] Net migration: immigrants (people moving in) minus emigrants (people moving out). It is positive when more people arrive than leave.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.4] Total fertility rate: the average number of children per woman, which shows family size better than the crude birth rate.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.5] Replacement level: a total fertility rate of about 2.1 children per woman in developed countries, the level at which a population replaces itself without migration.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.6] Infant mortality rate: deaths of children under one year old per 1,000 live births.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.7] Life expectancy: the average number of years a newborn is expected to live.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.b] A population can only change in two ways: people are born or die, and people move in or out. The census, a regular count of everyone, shows which of the two drove the change.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.0] Population change equals natural change plus net migration. In any period between two censuses (an inter-censal period), the larger of the two components explains the growth or decline.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.1] The census is a count of every person on one night. In Ireland it is held every five years and run by the Central Statistics Office (CSO).
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.2] The Irish census records age, sex, place of residence, birthplace, nationality, religion, ethnicity, Irish language, housing, work and commuting.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.3] Census 2016 to Census 2022: the population grew by about 360,000 (about 7.6%). Natural increase contributed about 170,000 and net migration about 190,000, so net migration was the slightly larger component.

[geo-population-migration-s1.3.0] Planning schools, hospitals and housing: counts of children, patients and households show where services are needed.

[geo-population-migration-s1.4.b] Two ideas describe where people live. They are different, so keep them apart: distribution is where people live. Density is the number of people per square kilometre of land.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.0] Distribution is the spread of people across an area, so a map can show crowded and empty places. Density turns that into a number you can compare.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.1] Density = population ÷ area in km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.2] Illustrative example (invented figures): a region of 400 km² with 120,000 people has a density of 120,000 ÷ 400 = 300 people per km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.b] Physical factors are features of the natural environment. They decide how easy it is to farm, build and live in a place.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.0] Relief (the shape and height of the land): lowlands attract more people than high mountains, which are costly to farm and build on.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.1] Climate: temperate climates (mild, without extremes) support dense settlement; places that are too hot, too cold or too dry have fewer people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.2] Soils and water supply: fertile soils and reliable water support farming and settlement.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.3] Natural resources: coalfields drew industrial populations in the 19th century, because mines and factories needed workers close by.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.b] Human factors are choices and developments made by people. They work alongside the physical factors to decide where populations cluster.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.0] Economic opportunity: jobs attract people, and regions with few jobs lose their young people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.1] Transport routes: roads, rail lines and navigable water (rivers deep enough for boats) attract settlement along them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.2] Cities and ports: cities and ports offer jobs and services, so they draw large populations.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.3] History: colonial ports and plantations (large farms for cash crops, set up by colonial powers) shaped where people settled.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.4] Government policy: policy can direct population into or out of particular areas.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.b] Low-density areas have few people per square kilometre. The reasons are mostly the opposite of the factors that attract people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.0] High, steep or cold land is difficult to farm, build on and reach.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.1] Deserts and dense forest offer little water, farmland or access.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.2] Poor soils and remoteness limit farming, and remote areas lack markets and services.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.3] Few jobs cause young people to leave, so the population falls and density drops.

[geo-population-migration-s1.18.1] Examples: Ireland and the USA. Ireland is in stage 4.
