[geo-region-continental-s1.1.5] Industrial region as an outlined area: the Chota Nagpur Plateau and Damodar valley, north-east of the Deccan around Jamshedpur, or Mumbai–Pune.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.b] India's climate is dominated by the monsoon, which divides the year into three seasons. Understanding the monsoon explains most of India's farming, rivers and hazards.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.0] India has a tropical monsoon climate. A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind direction, not simply a wet period, so the winds blow from a different direction in summer and in winter.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.1] The word monsoon comes from the Arabic word for season.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.2] Hot season: March to May, with the hottest conditions on the northern plains, because they are far inland and the land heats strongly before the rains.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.3] Wet south-west monsoon: June to September, bringing most of the year's rain, because moist winds from the ocean blow onto the land.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.4] Cool dry season: October to February, when most of India is dry because the winds blow off the land.
[geo-region-continental-s1.2.5] The monsoon retreats in October and November, marking the change between the wet and dry seasons.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.b] The summer monsoon is driven by heating of the land and by the movement of global wind belts. Follow the steps in order, because each one causes the next.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.0] 1. Intense heating of the land in early summer creates low pressure (rising warm air) over north-west India and the Tibetan Plateau, which draws in air from the cooler ocean.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.1] 2. The Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ, the belt near the equator where trade winds from both hemispheres meet) shifts north over India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.2] 3. South-east trade winds (steady winds blowing towards the equator) cross the equator and are deflected by the Coriolis force (the effect of the Earth's spin, which bends winds) into south-west winds.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.3] 4. These winds pick up moisture over the Indian Ocean and reach the land as the monsoon.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.4] 5. The subtropical westerly jet stream (a fast, high-altitude river of air) moves north of the Himalayas in early summer, and the monsoon arrives suddenly, a 'burst'.
[geo-region-continental-s1.3.5] 6. The jet stream returns south of the Himalayas in winter, when the monsoon withdraws.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.b] The monsoon does not arrive everywhere at once. It sweeps north in two branches, which hit different parts of India first.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.0] The south-west monsoon normally reaches Kerala around 1 June and covers all of India by mid-July.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.1] It brings about three-quarters of India's annual rainfall, falling mainly in June to September, so the other eight months share the remaining quarter.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.2] The Arabian Sea branch strikes the Western Ghats (the mountain range along the west coast) first, dropping heavy rain on the windward side, the side facing the wind.
[geo-region-continental-s1.4.3] The Bay of Bengal branch strikes the north-east hills, then is turned west along the Ganges plain by the Himalayas, bringing rain eastwards and along the plain.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.b] Rainfall in India depends on where the monsoon winds meet high ground. Mountains force moist air up, making one side very wet and the other dry.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.0] Relief (orographic) rain: moist air is forced up the windward slopes of the Western Ghats, cools and drops its rain, giving heavy rain on the Konkan and Malabar coasts.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.1] The Deccan Plateau behind the Western Ghats lies in their rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of mountains, where the air has already lost its moisture) and is much drier.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.2] Mawsynram and Cherrapunji, in the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, are among the wettest places on earth, due to relief rain from the Bay of Bengal branch.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.3] Rainfall falls off westwards along the Ganges plain: Kolkata in the east is wetter than Delhi in the north-west, because the winds lose moisture as they travel inland.
[geo-region-continental-s1.5.4] The Thar Desert in Rajasthan is the driest region, because the Arabian Sea branch passes parallel to the Aravalli Range and has no barrier to force it upwards.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.b] In winter the wind pattern reverses and India is mostly dry, but there are two regional exceptions. Both matter for farming.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.0] High pressure over Central Asia gives north-east winds that blow off the land, so most of India is dry in winter, because air from land carries little moisture.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.1] The Himalayas shelter India from cold air from Central Asia.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.2] Exception: the Tamil Nadu (Coromandel) coast receives most of its rain from the north-east monsoon, as these winds pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal.
[geo-region-continental-s1.6.3] Western disturbances (depressions from the west, areas of low pressure that move in) bring light winter rain to the north-west, which is important for wheat.
[geo-region-continental-s1.7.b] India lies mostly in the tropics, so heat is the norm. Only height above sea level and the season change it much.
[geo-region-continental-s1.7.0] The hot season brings high temperatures to the northern plains, often above 40°C, with hot, dry winds called loo.
[geo-region-continental-s1.7.1] The south stays hot all year.
[geo-region-continental-s1.7.2] The Himalayas are cold, so temperatures fall with altitude (height above sea level).
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.b] The monsoon is not reliable, and both too little and too much rain cause disasters. Climate change is adding to the risk.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.0] A late, weak or broken monsoon (one with long dry breaks) causes drought, because crops and reservoirs depend on its rain.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.1] A heavy monsoon causes floods, because rivers receive more water than their channels can carry.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.2] Tropical cyclones (violent rotating storms that form over warm seas) strike the east coast, mainly in the pre-monsoon and retreating-monsoon periods.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.3] Odisha super cyclone, October 1999: about 10,000 deaths, an example of how deadly a storm on the east coast can be.
[geo-region-continental-s1.8.4] Climate change is making the monsoon more erratic (so drought and flood risks both rise) and heatwaves more severe.
[geo-region-continental-s1.9.b] Because most Indians depend on farming, the timing and strength of the monsoon shape the whole economy.
[geo-region-continental-s1.9.0] Its timing decides the farming calendar, so a late monsoon delays sowing, with kharif crops (sown with the monsoon) and rabi crops (sown after it), which are covered under the farming calendar below.
[geo-region-continental-s1.9.1] It refills reservoirs and groundwater, which supply irrigation and drinking water.
[geo-region-continental-s1.9.2] Because farming is a large part of the economy, a poor monsoon affects GDP (the total value of what the country produces).
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.b] Soil type follows the parent material (the rock or sediment the soil forms from) and the climate. Alluvial soil is India's most extensive soil type.
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.0] Alluvial soils (soils made of silt and sand laid down by rivers) are the most extensive and most fertile, found on the Indo-Gangetic Plain and the eastern deltas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.1] They are deposited by Himalayan rivers, which carry silt down from the mountains, forming newer khadar (floodplain) soils and older bhangar (higher terraces).
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.2] Desert soils in the Thar, Rajasthan, are sandy, so they hold little water, low in humus (decayed plant matter that feeds soil) and sometimes saline (salty).
[geo-region-continental-s1.10.3] Desert soils are productive only where canal irrigation reaches them: the Indira Gandhi Canal, Rajasthan, brings water from Punjab's rivers into the Thar Desert.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.b] Black soils form on volcanic rock and are among the best soils of the Deccan for one particular crop.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.0] Black soils (regur, or black cotton soil) are weathered (broken down) from the basalt lava (dark volcanic rock) of the Deccan Traps in north-west Deccan, Maharashtra.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.1] They are clay-rich, so they hold moisture well.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.2] They crack deeply when dry, a feature sometimes called self-ploughing, because the cracks let air and water into the soil.
[geo-region-continental-s1.11.3] They are ideal for cotton, the main crop on the Deccan Traps, because cotton needs soil that keeps moisture.
[geo-region-continental-s1.12.b] Red soils form on old, hard rock and are poorer than black soils.
[geo-region-continental-s1.12.0] Red soils are weathered from ancient crystalline rocks (hard rocks made of interlocking crystals, here granite and gneiss) of the south and east Deccan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.12.1] They are red because of iron oxide (rust), and are low in nitrogen and humus.
[geo-region-continental-s1.12.2] They support millets (small-seeded drought-tolerant cereals), groundnuts and pulses (beans, peas and lentils), and give better yields with fertiliser and irrigation.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.b] Laterite forms where heat and heavy rain wash goodness out of the ground. It is a poor soil for farming, though it has a use in building.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.0] Laterite forms under high temperatures and heavy rainfall, which leach out (wash away) silica and nutrients, leaving iron and aluminium oxides behind.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.1] Laterite is found on the Western Ghats and in Kerala.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.2] It is infertile because the leaching removed the nutrients, but with fertiliser it supports tea, coffee and cashew.
[geo-region-continental-s1.13.3] It hardens when exposed to air, so it is cut into building bricks.
[geo-region-continental-s1.14.b] On steep Himalayan slopes soil cannot build up, so farming depends on cutting flat steps into the hillside.
[geo-region-continental-s1.14.0] Mountain soils on the Himalayan slopes are thin and immature (not yet developed into deep, layered soil).
[geo-region-continental-s1.14.1] On the terraces (flat steps cut into the slopes, which give level ground on steep land) tea and fruit are grown.
[geo-region-continental-s1.15.b] Farming and the monsoon together damage India's soils in three main ways.
[geo-region-continental-s1.15.0] Erosion (the wearing away of soil): monsoon downpours and deforestation create gullies and ravines, such as the Chambal ravines of Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.15.1] Salinisation (salt build-up in the soil) and waterlogging (soil saturated with water) occur under canal irrigation in Punjab and Haryana.
[geo-region-continental-s1.15.2] Nutrient exhaustion results from intensive Green Revolution farming, which takes crop after crop from the same land.
[geo-region-continental-s1.16.b] Two Indian soils show how rock type and weathering link to soil.
[geo-region-continental-s1.16.0] Basalt weathers to black soil, so black soils are tied to soils to the Deccan Traps, the thick basalt layers covered under the relief notes below.
[geo-region-continental-s1.16.1] Laterite is the product of intense chemical weathering (rock broken down by chemical reactions) in hot, wet conditions.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.b] Relief means the shape and height of the land. India has three main relief divisions, with the Thar Desert, the coastal plains and the islands added to them.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.0] The Himalayas lie to the north, forming India's mountain wall.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.1] The Northern Plains, the Indo-Gangetic Plain, lie in front of the Himalayas, where the rivers spread their silt.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.2] The Peninsular Plateau, the Deccan, forms the southern peninsula.
[geo-region-continental-s1.17.3] Coastal plains and islands lie along the coasts.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.b] The Himalayas form India's northern wall. They are the youngest and highest of its landforms.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.0] The Himalayas are young fold mountains (formed when rock layers are crumpled up by collision) formed by the collision of the Indian (Indo-Australian) plate with the Eurasian plate. The collision began about 50 million years ago and continues.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.1] Three parallel ranges run west to east: the Greater Himalaya (Himadri), the Lesser Himalaya and the Shiwaliks.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.2] Kangchenjunga, 8,586 m, on the India (Sikkim)–Nepal border, is India's highest peak and the world's third highest.
[geo-region-continental-s1.18.3] Mount Everest (8,849 m) is on the Nepal–China border, not in India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.b] The Himalayas shape India's climate, rivers and settlement. Their effects are both helpful and harmful.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.0] The mountains are a barrier to cold air from Central Asia.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.1] They trigger monsoon rain, as the monsoon is forced upwards by the mountain wall.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.2] Perennial rivers (rivers that flow all year) fed by snow melt rise in the Himalayas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.3] The rivers have hydroelectric potential (power from falling water) because they drop steeply.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.4] The mountains are a barrier to transport and are prone to earthquakes and landslides.
[geo-region-continental-s1.19.5] Settlement is sparse because of the steep, cold terrain, which makes farming and building hard.
[geo-region-continental-s1.20.b] South of the Himalayas lies the flattest and most useful land in India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.20.0] The Northern Plains are vast, flat and low.
[geo-region-continental-s1.20.1] They are built from alluvium (silt and sand laid down by rivers) deposited by the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra river systems in a trough (a long hollow) in front of the Himalayas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.20.2] They are the agricultural and population heartland of India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.b] The Deccan is an old, stable upland that covers most of southern India. Part of it is covered by lava from ancient eruptions.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.0] The Deccan Plateau is very old, made of hard crystalline rock, so it forms a stable shield (a block of ancient rock that is no longer folded or moved much).
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.1] It was once part of the ancient Gondwanaland landmass, the supercontinent that later broke apart.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.2] The plateau tilts from west to east, so most of its rivers flow east.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.3] The Chota Nagpur Plateau in the north-east of the plateau is rich in minerals, such as coal and iron ore.
[geo-region-continental-s1.21.4] The Deccan Traps are thick layers of basalt from huge fissure eruptions (lava pouring from long cracks) about 66 million years ago, covering much of the north-west Deccan (the source of the black soils).
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.b] Two mountain ranges edge the Deccan Plateau, one on each coast. They differ in height and continuity.
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.0] The Western Ghats, also called the Sahyadri, form a steep, continuous escarpment (a long steep slope) along the west coast.
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.1] The Western Ghats are a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2012) for their biodiversity (the variety of living things).
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.2] Anamudi, 2,695 m, in Kerala, is the highest peak in India south of the Himalayas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.22.3] The Eastern Ghats are lower and broken into separate hills, because east-flowing rivers cut through them.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.b] Between the plains and the plateau, and in the north-west, are older ranges that also shape drainage and rainfall.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.0] The Vindhya and Satpura ranges separate the plains from the Deccan, forming a divide between north and south.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.1] The Narmada flows west between the Vindhya and Satpura ranges.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.2] The Aravalli Range, Rajasthan, is among the oldest fold mountains on earth.
[geo-region-continental-s1.23.3] The Thar Desert lies west of the Aravalli Range, on the dry side where the monsoon winds are not forced upwards.
[geo-region-continental-s1.24.b] Narrow plains run along both coasts, but the west and east differ in width and in what the rivers have built.
[geo-region-continental-s1.24.0] The narrow western plain includes the Konkan coast (Maharashtra, Goa) in the north and the Malabar coast (Kerala) in the south, with backwaters and lagoons (shallow coastal waters partly cut off from the sea).
[geo-region-continental-s1.24.1] The wider eastern plain is the Coromandel coast (Tamil Nadu), with large river deltas, because the Deccan's rivers flow east and deposit silt there.
[geo-region-continental-s1.25.b] Each relief division supports a different way of life, which is why people and industry are spread unevenly.
[geo-region-continental-s1.25.0] The plains support dense population, intensive farming and easy transport, because the land is flat and fertile.
[geo-region-continental-s1.25.1] The plateau is rich in minerals, so it supports industry.
[geo-region-continental-s1.25.2] The mountains support tourism and hydroelectricity, with sparse settlement because the land is steep and cold.
[geo-region-continental-s1.26.b] India's rivers fall into two groups. The Himalayan and peninsular rivers differ in their source, flow and seasonal regime (how their flow changes through the year).
[geo-region-continental-s1.26.0] Himalayan rivers are perennial, because they are fed by snow and glacier melt plus monsoon rain, and are large, with high flows in summer.
[geo-region-continental-s1.26.1] Peninsular rivers are seasonal, because they are rain-fed, so they are low in the dry season, and shorter.
[geo-region-continental-s1.26.2] Peninsular rivers flow in older, harder rock, with fixed valleys, so they cannot easily shift course.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.b] The Ganges is India's most important river. It drains the largest basin in the country and feeds the Northern Plains.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.0] The Ganges rises as the Bhagirathi at Gaumukh, the snout of the Gangotri Glacier in Uttarakhand.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.1] It becomes the Ganga where the Bhagirathi meets the Alaknanda at Devprayag.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.2] It flows past Haridwar, Kanpur, Prayagraj (Allahabad), Varanasi and Patna.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.3] The Yamuna, a tributary (a river that joins a larger one) which flows past Delhi and Agra, joins it at Prayagraj.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.4] The river is about 2,500 km long and its basin (the area drained by it and its tributaries) covers about a quarter of India, the largest basin in India.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.5] It enters Bangladesh and joins the Brahmaputra to form the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta, the world's largest delta; the delta is shared with Bangladesh.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.6] The Sundarbans mangrove forest (trees that grow in salty coastal mud), home of the Bengal tiger, lies on the delta across India and Bangladesh.
[geo-region-continental-s1.27.7] The Hooghly, a distributary (a branch that leaves the main river), flows through Kolkata, which is why the city is a river port.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.b] The Brahmaputra drains the north-east and carries so much sediment that it floods the same area every year.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.0] The Brahmaputra rises in Tibet as the Yarlung Tsangpo.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.1] It enters India in Arunachal Pradesh and flows west through Assam.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.2] It joins the Ganges in Bangladesh.
[geo-region-continental-s1.28.3] It is braided (split into many shifting channels around sandbanks) and carries huge loads of silt, so its channels clog and it floods Assam annually.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.b] The Indus is a Himalayan river that mostly flows outside India, yet its tributaries water India's wheat belt.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.0] The Indus rises in Tibet and flows through Ladakh into Pakistan, mostly through Pakistan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.1] Its five Punjab tributaries are the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.2] Its waters irrigate India's wheat belt in the Punjab, which is why the Indus matters to India even though it flows mostly through Pakistan.
[geo-region-continental-s1.29.3] The Indus Waters Treaty with Pakistan, 1960, brokered by the World Bank, divides its waters.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.b] Because the Deccan Plateau tilts eastwards, its main rivers cross it to reach the Bay of Bengal.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.0] The Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri flow east to the Bay of Bengal.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.1] The Godavari is the longest river of peninsular India, which is the name for the southern triangle of the country.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.2] They build deltas on the Bay of Bengal coast, which are fertile rice lands because of the silt they deposit.
[geo-region-continental-s1.30.3] The Kaveri (Cauvery) water dispute is between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, two states that both want the river's water.
[geo-region-continental-s1.31.b] Two peninsular rivers run against the general eastward tilt and reach the Arabian Sea.
[geo-region-continental-s1.31.0] The Narmada and the Tapi flow west into the Arabian Sea.
[geo-region-continental-s1.31.1] They flow through rift valleys (long valleys formed where land has dropped between faults) and end in estuaries (wide river mouths affected by the tide), not deltas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.b] Rivers on the flat plains both create and destroy. Their floods bring fertile silt but also loss.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.0] Monsoon floods occur on the Ganges plain and in Bihar and Assam, because the rivers swell with monsoon rain and snow melt.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.1] Silt deposition renews soils, but floods destroy crops and homes.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.2] The Kosi, a Ganges tributary in Bihar, is called the 'Sorrow of Bihar' because of its floods and shifting course; it broke its embankment in 2008.
[geo-region-continental-s1.32.3] Rivers shift course on the flat plains, because there is little slope to hold them in one channel.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.b] People try to control the rivers so that water is available all year and floods are reduced. This brings benefits and conflict.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.0] Multipurpose dams (dams built for several uses at once) provide irrigation, hydroelectricity and flood control.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.1] Bhakra Dam on the Sutlej, Himachal Pradesh, supplies irrigation water and power to Punjab and Haryana.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.2] Sardar Sarovar Dam on the Narmada, Gujarat, brings irrigation and drinking water to Gujarat but displaced many thousands of villagers.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.3] The displacement led to the Narmada Bachao Andolan ('Save the Narmada') protest movement.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.4] Canal irrigation spreads water beyond the river valleys, so dry land far from a river can be farmed.
[geo-region-continental-s1.33.5] Water is disputed between states, as in the Kaveri dispute, and with neighbouring countries, as in the Indus Waters Treaty.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.b] The same river that millions depend on is heavily polluted, and governments have launched clean-up programmes.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.0] The Ganges is polluted by sewage, industrial waste and cremations.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.1] Ritual bathing and cremation take place along the river at Varanasi, which adds to the pollution because ashes and waste enter the water.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.2] The Ganga Action Plan was launched in 1985.
[geo-region-continental-s1.34.3] The Namami Gange programme followed in 2014.
[geo-region-continental-s1.35.b] Rivers are central to religion and daily life as well as to the economy.
[geo-region-continental-s1.35.0] The Ganges is sacred to Hindus, so millions bathe in it and visit it.
[geo-region-continental-s1.35.1] Rivers provide water for drinking, irrigation and transport, such as boats on the Ganges.
[geo-region-continental-s1.35.2] The rivers' floods deposit fertile alluvium on the plains, sustaining farming and settlement.
[geo-region-continental-s1.36.b] Agriculture is the biggest employer in India but contributes far less to the economy than its workforce suggests.
[geo-region-continental-s1.36.0] Agriculture employs the largest share of workers: in Census 2011, about 55% of workers were cultivators or agricultural labourers.
[geo-region-continental-s1.36.1] It produces a much smaller share of GDP, under a fifth in the early 2020s, which shows low productivity (little output per worker).
[geo-region-continental-s1.36.2] Its share of employment is falling, as more people move into industry and services.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.b] Indian farming ranges from tiny family plots to large commercial farms. Each type suits a particular region and climate.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.0] Subsistence farming (growing food mainly for the farmer's own family) on small plots, so little is left to sell.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.1] Intensive wet-rice (paddy) farming in the wet east, the south and the deltas, where rain and flooded fields suit rice.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.2] Commercial irrigated wheat farming (growing for sale, using canal or well water) in the north-west, in Punjab and Haryana.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.3] Plantation farming (large estates growing one crop for sale): tea in Assam and Darjeeling (West Bengal), and in the Nilgiri Hills (Tamil Nadu); coffee in Karnataka.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.4] Cash crops (crops grown to sell): cotton on black soils in Maharashtra and Gujarat, sugar cane, and jute in West Bengal.
[geo-region-continental-s1.37.5] Dairy farming, such as the co-operatives described under the White Revolution, and pastoralism (herding animals) in dry areas.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.b] The monsoon splits the farming year into two main cropping seasons, kharif and rabi.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.0] Kharif crops are sown with the monsoon in June and July, so they use its rain, and harvested in autumn: rice, cotton, maize and millets.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.1] Rabi crops are sown in October and November and harvested in March and April: wheat, mustard and gram.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.2] Rabi crops depend on irrigation and on the rain from western disturbances, because the main monsoon has ended.
[geo-region-continental-s1.38.3] Kharif means monsoon (summer) crops such as rice; rabi means winter crops such as wheat.
[geo-region-continental-s1.39.b] India's climate, soils and rivers give farmers several natural advantages.
