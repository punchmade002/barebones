TARGET geo-economic-environment-s4: 4. Sustainable economic development
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.b] Economic activity such as farming, fishing, mining and industry always affects the environment. Sustainable development is the framework within which that environmental impact is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.0] The Brundtland Report, "Our Common Future" (UN World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987), defined it as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.1] It has three pillars: environmental protection, economic growth and social fairness. Sustainable development balances all three rather than maximising one, so a plan that boosts profit but wrecks the environment is not sustainable.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.b] Five working rules turn the definition into practice. Each one protects the environment so that it can keep supporting people in the future.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.0] Renewable resources (ones that regrow or refill naturally) such as fish, forests and water should be used no faster than they regenerate, otherwise they are used up.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.1] Non-renewable resources (ones that cannot be replaced once used) should be used efficiently and recycled, so that they last longer.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.2] Pollution should be kept within what the environment can absorb, so that air, water and soil are not overloaded.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.3] The polluter pays: those who cause environmental damage bear the cost of it, which gives them a reason to cause less.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.4] The precautionary principle: act to prevent serious harm even before the harm is proven, because waiting for proof may be too late.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.b] Sustainable development became an international aim, with world meetings setting out plans for governments to follow.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.0] The Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro (1992) was a later, separate event from the Brundtland Report. It produced Agenda 21, an action plan for sustainable development.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.1] The UN Sustainable Development Goals were adopted in 2015: 17 goals with a target year of 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.b] In Ireland, the main body that controls the environmental impact of industry is the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). It was created by law and works independently of the government departments it advises.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.0] It was set up under the Environmental Protection Agency Act 1992, and the EPA began work in 1993.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.1] It is an independent public body (a State organisation not run by a government minister) with headquarters at Johnstown Castle, Co. Wexford.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.b] The EPA's most important power is licensing. Activities with a high risk of pollution may not operate unless the EPA allows it and sets the conditions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.0] Large industries need an EPA licence: pharmaceutical and chemical plants, power stations, intensive pig and poultry farms, cement works and mines. Waste facilities also need one.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.1] The licence sets emission limits for the activity, which means the maximum pollution it may release into air, water or land.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.2] Integrated pollution control means one licence covers air, water, waste and noise, instead of separate permits for each. This lets the EPA look at the whole impact of a site at once.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.b] To control pollution, the EPA first has to measure it. It tracks the state of the environment and reports what it finds.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.0] The EPA monitors water quality in rivers, lakes, estuaries (where rivers meet the sea), groundwater (water held underground in rock and soil) and bathing waters.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.1] It runs a national air quality network and produces a greenhouse gas inventory (a count of emissions of gases that cause climate change) and projections of future emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.2] Drinking water is supplied by Uisce Éireann.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.3] The State of the Environment report is published every four years, so the public and government can see whether things are improving.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.4] The EPA advises government and the public and funds environmental research.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.5] The EPA took over radiological protection in 2014, including the control of radon (a natural radioactive gas that can seep up from the ground).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.b] A licence is only useful if it is obeyed, so the EPA checks and punishes breaches.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.0] Inspections check that licensed activities keep within their emission limits.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.1] The Office of Environmental Enforcement, set up within the EPA in 2003, prosecutes polluters (takes them to court).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.2] The EPA oversees the environmental work carried out by local authorities (the county and city councils).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.b] The EPA cannot solve every problem alone. Some pollution is hard to see, track or assign to one source.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.0] The EPA cannot inspect everywhere, so some breaches go unchecked.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.1] Diffuse pollution (pollution that comes from many scattered sources rather than one pipe or chimney) from thousands of farms is hard to police.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.2] Local authorities and other bodies share responsibility for environmental control, so the EPA is one part of a wider system.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.b] Control is best done before a project starts. An environmental impact assessment is the study that looks at a project's likely effects in advance.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.0] An environmental impact assessment is a study, required before planning permission is decided for large projects, that predicts a project's effects on the environment and how to reduce them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.1] The developer's report (the developer is the company or body proposing the project) was called an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS). It is now an Environmental Impact Assessment Report (EIAR).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.b] Environmental impact assessment is not optional for big projects. It comes from EU law and is built into Irish planning rules.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.0] The EU Environmental Impact Assessment Directive, 1985, is applied in Irish planning law.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.1] Assessment is required for projects above set sizes, such as motorways, power stations, wind farms, mines and quarries, large intensive farms, pipelines, ports and airports. Small projects below the set sizes do not need one.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.b] An EIAR has to describe the project, the place it will be built and every kind of effect it could have, then say how harm will be reduced.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.0] A description of the project and of the existing environment (the baseline, meaning the condition of the site before anything is built, which later effects are measured against).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.1] Likely effects on people, plants and animals, soil, water, air, climate, landscape, archaeology and heritage, traffic and noise.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.2] Alternatives considered, such as other designs or sites, and the mitigation measures (steps to reduce or prevent harm) proposed to reduce effects.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.3] A non-technical summary for the public, so ordinary people can understand the report.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.4] The developer writes the report, often with consultants. Neither the EPA nor the planning authority writes it.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.b] The assessment follows a set order. It ends with a decision-maker who weighs the report against what the public and experts say.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.0] 1. The developer prepares the EIAR.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.1] 2. The public and bodies such as the National Parks and Wildlife Service make submissions (written comments and objections).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.2] 3. The planning authority or An Coimisiún Pleanála decides whether to grant permission, often with conditions. The board was renamed in 2025 from An Bord Pleanála, the national planning appeals board.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.b] Short questions ask why environmental impact studies are needed. There are four reasons, and Ireland's experience at Derrybrien shows what happens when the law is not followed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.0] They prevent damage before it happens, which is cheaper than paying for clean-up afterwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.1] They require developers to consider alternatives, so a less harmful design or site may be chosen.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.2] They give the public a say in decisions, through submissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.3] They are a legal requirement.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.4] At Derrybrien, Co. Galway, a peat slide on 16 October 2003 occurred during wind-farm construction. In 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland a lump sum of €5 million plus €15,000 a day because no proper environmental impact assessment had been carried out.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.b] Fish are a renewable resource, but only if people leave enough of them to breed. This idea is the basis of sustainable fishing.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.0] Fish are renewable only while catches stay below the rate at which the stock (the population of one kind of fish) breeds and grows.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.1] The maximum sustainable yield is the largest catch that can be taken year after year without reducing the stock.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.b] Overfishing means taking fish faster than stocks can replace themselves. Several causes push fleets to do this.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.0] Modern technology, including sonar (finds fish using sound), satellite navigation, factory ships (which process the catch at sea) and huge nets, lets fleets find and take fish faster than stocks can recover.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.1] Too many boats chase the same stock, so the catch is shared out among more boats and each one fishes harder.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.2] Subsidies (payments from government) keep fleets fishing when stocks are falling.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.3] Young fish are caught before they have had the chance to breed, so the stock cannot renew itself.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.4] Discards: dead or unwanted fish are thrown back into the sea, which wastes the catch.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.5] Illegal fishing takes fish outside the quota system (the limits on how much each country or boat may catch).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.b] The Grand Banks is the classic example of a fishery destroyed by overfishing. It shows how both fish stocks and jobs can be lost.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.0] The Grand Banks, off Newfoundland, Canada, were one of the world's richest cod fisheries for centuries.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.1] Catches rose sharply with factory trawlers (large boats that fish with nets and process the catch on board) from the 1960s, and the stock collapsed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.2] Canada declared a moratorium (ban) on northern cod fishing on 2 July 1992.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.3] About 30,000 people in Newfoundland lost their jobs, the largest single lay-off in Canadian history.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.4] The stock has recovered only slowly, and partly, in the decades since.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.b] North Sea herring is a second example, with a happier ending. It shows that a stock can recover when fishing is stopped and then tightly limited.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.0] North Sea herring was overfished in the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.1] The fishery was closed from 1977 to the early 1980s, which gave the stock time to breed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.2] The stock recovered after the fishery reopened under strict quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.b] In EU waters, fish stocks are managed through the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP). Here it matters as a conservation tool that tries to keep catches sustainable.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.0] The EU Common Fisheries Policy, 1983, sets total allowable catches (TACs, the maximum amount of each stock that may be caught) and divides them into national quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.1] Conservation rules include minimum mesh sizes (so small fish escape the net), minimum landing sizes, closed areas and seasons, and vessel decommissioning (scrapping boats to reduce fleet size).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.2] The CFP reform of 2013 aims to fish at maximum sustainable yield and introduces a landing obligation that ends discards, phased in from 2015 to 2019.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.3] Monitoring uses satellite tracking, the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority (set up in 2007) and fishery protection patrols by the Irish Naval Service.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.4] Ireland's exclusive economic zone (the sea area over which a country controls resources) extends 200 nautical miles from the coast, within which these rules apply.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.5] Further measures include marine protected areas (areas of sea where fishing is restricted) and aquaculture (fish farming) as an alternative to wild catch.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.b] Fishing matters to Irish coastal communities, which depend on the fish stocks around the coast for their living.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.0] Mackerel is the most valuable Irish catch, landed mainly at Killybegs, Co. Donegal, Ireland's largest fishing port.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.1] Killybegs is the base of the pelagic fleet (boats that fish species living in open water, not on the seabed) that catches mackerel and herring.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.2] Ireland has six State Fishery Harbour Centres: Killybegs, Castletownbere, Dingle, Rossaveel, Dunmore East and Howth.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.3] Irish fishing communities depend on these stocks, so conservation limits cause hardship. There is more on this in Conflicts between economic interests and environmental interests.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.b] Mining that happened before modern controls can go on polluting for generations. Avoca is the Irish example.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.0] Copper mining at Avoca, Co. Wicklow, over centuries left acid mine drainage (acidic water that runs out of old mine workings) when mining ended.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.1] The acid drainage still discolours and pollutes the Avoca River long after the mines closed, which shows why closure plans are needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.b] Today a mine has to work within licence rules. Tara Mines shows what controlled mining looks like.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath, is an underground zinc and lead mine, Europe's largest zinc mine, producing since 1977 and owned by Boliden.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.1] It operates under an EPA licence, which sets limits on its emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.2] Tailings (the waste left after the metal has been extracted) are stored in a lined tailings facility, so they do not leak into the ground, and the water is monitored.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.3] Restoration plans are in place for the site.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.b] A responsible mine plans for its end before it opens. Lisheen shows rehabilitation (restoring a site to a safe, useful condition) after closure.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.0] Lisheen, Co. Tipperary, a zinc and lead mine, closed in 2015 and the site has been rehabilitated.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.1] A new mine needs an environmental impact assessment report (EIAR) and a closure and restoration plan backed by a financial bond (money set aside in advance) before it opens, so the clean-up is paid for even if the company fails.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.b] Peat was long treated as a fuel to be dug out. The scale of that harvesting damaged Ireland's bogs.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.0] Bord na Móna, founded in 1946, carried out large-scale industrial harvesting of peat for power stations and fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.1] Harvesting removed raised bogs (domed peatlands formed over thousands of years), released the carbon they had stored and destroyed their habitats.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.b] Peat harvesting has ended, and the focus has moved to repairing the damage and finding new uses for the land.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.0] The Lanesborough and Shannonbridge peat stations closed in December 2020, and Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.1] Cutaway bogs (bogs where the peat has been removed) are being rehabilitated and rewetted, meaning water is returned to them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.2] Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly: a cutaway bog turned into parkland, wetlands and trails.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.3] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo: the peat station closed in 2003 and wind farms now stand on the cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.4] A just transition (a change that is fair to the workers affected) is needed for Midland workers who lost jobs in peat.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.b] The Aral Sea shows how diverting water for farming can destroy a whole environment and the livelihoods that depended on it.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.0] The Amu Darya and Syr Darya, which feed the Aral Sea in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, were diverted from the 1960s to irrigate (water by channels) cotton in the Soviet Union.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.1] The sea shrank to a fraction of its size and split into separate lakes, and its salinity (salt content) rose.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.2] The fishing industry collapsed, and ports such as Moynaq (Uzbekistan) were left stranded far from the water.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.3] Dust storms spread salt and pesticides from the exposed seabed, harming local health.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.4] The Kok-Aral dam (Kazakhstan, 2005) raised the level of the small northern sea, and fishing there has partly revived.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.25.b] Burning fossil fuels pollutes the air and adds to climate change, so a sustainable approach changes the energy source.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.25.0] Switching to renewable energy (energy from sources that do not run out, such as wind) reduces the environmental impact of power generation. There is more on this in Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.b] Farming can be paid and encouraged to protect the environment, not just to produce food.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.0] Agri-environment schemes pay farmers for conservation: REPS (Rural Environment Protection Scheme, 1994), now succeeded by ACRES.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.1] Origin Green, Bord Bia's sustainability programme for Irish food and drink, was launched in 2012.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.2] Nitrates rules limit the amount of nitrogen fertiliser and manure that farms can apply, because the excess can run off and pollute water.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.3] BurrenLIFE (2005 to 2010) paid Burren farmers to manage species-rich limestone grassland through winter grazing (winterage); it continued as the Burren Programme.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.4] Results-based farming in the Burren rewards farmers for measured environmental outcomes, not just for following set practices.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.b] Forests and tourist sites can be used without being destroyed if how they are used is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.0] Forests are replanted after felling, using mixed native broadleaves (a variety of native trees such as oak, rather than one species).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.1] Forests are not planted on deep peat.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.2] Visitor limits and ecotourism (tourism designed to protect nature) reduce pressure on fragile sites.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.3] At Skellig Michael, Co. Kerry, access is controlled through licensed boats and visitor limits.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.b] The best way to deal with waste is to create less of it, which is why there is an order of priority.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.0] Reduce, reuse and recycle, in that order of priority.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.1] Levies (charges) and deposit schemes (a refundable payment on containers) give people and businesses an incentive to reduce waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.2] A circular economy keeps products and materials in use for as long as possible, instead of using them once and throwing them away.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.b] The polluter pays principle is put into effect through taxes and charges that put a price on pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.0] Irish carbon tax, introduced in 2010.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.1] Plastic bag levy, introduced in March 2002, a charge on plastic bags, so shoppers have a reason to use fewer.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.2] Landfill levy, charged on waste sent to landfill, so dumping costs more than reducing waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.3] EU Emissions Trading System, from 2005: companies pay for the emissions they release.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.b] Governments have set targets for cutting emissions, and the future of sustainable development depends on meeting them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.0] The EU and Ireland aim for climate neutrality by 2050 (releasing no more greenhouse gases than are removed).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.1] Ireland's Climate Act 2021 sets a 51% cut in emissions by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.2] The Climate Action Plan sets 80% renewable electricity by 2030 and a retrofit (upgrading insulation and heating) of 500,000 homes to B2 energy rating by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.3] Offshore wind, electric vehicles, public transport and the circular economy are the main routes to lower emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.4] Cutting agricultural emissions, restoring bogs and planting forests as carbon sinks (places that absorb and store carbon).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.b] Meeting these targets will not be easy. Several obstacles stand in the way.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.0] Cost: clean technology, retrofitting and rehabilitation are expensive.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.1] Short-term economic pressure favours quick profit over long-term protection.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.2] Jobs in traditional industries, such as peat and fishing, are at risk.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.3] Global cooperation is needed, because pollution and climate change do not stop at borders.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s2: 2. Human interaction with the rock cycle
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.b] People take rock, minerals and energy out of the earth's crust, and in doing so they become part of the rock cycle. The syllabus asks you to study one of four options: mining, extraction of building materials (quarrying), oil and gas exploitation, or geothermal energy production.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.0] Extraction removes rock far faster than natural weathering (the breakdown of rock in place) and erosion (the wearing away and carrying off of rock) can, and it exposes fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.1] Extraction creates new deposits of waste, which are stored on the surface, for example spoil heaps (piles of waste rock) and tailings (fine waste left after ore is processed).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.2] People therefore act as an exogenic process (one that works on the surface of the crust from outside): removing rock is the human equivalent of erosion, and dumping waste is the human equivalent of deposition.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.b] Mining removes valuable minerals from the crust. The method depends on how deep the ore (rock containing a metal worth extracting) lies, and the ore must then be processed to separate the useful mineral from the rest.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.0] Mined materials include, for example, metal ores (zinc, lead, copper, gold), coal and salt.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.1] Ore bodies form where minerals are concentrated by igneous activity (the movement and cooling of magma) or by hot fluids moving through rock, which is why ore is found in particular places rather than everywhere.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.2] Exploration comes first, so money is not wasted on a poor site: geological surveys and test drilling show whether the ore is worth mining before a mine is developed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.3] Open-cast (open-pit) mining removes the overburden (the soil and rock lying above the ore) and works ore that lies near the surface, because digging down from above is cheapest there.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.4] Underground (shaft) mining is used because deep ore cannot be reached from the surface; it goes through shafts and tunnels; room and pillar is one underground method.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.5] Processing crushes and grinds the ore and separates the mineral from the waste rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.6] Waste rock is stored in spoil heaps, and fine waste, called tailings, is stored in tailings ponds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.b] A named case study with specific facts carries a mining answer. Learn one Irish mine, one open-pit mine abroad, and Aberfan as an example of what can go wrong with mining waste.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath: an underground zinc and lead mine, Europe's largest zinc mine, in production since 1977 and owned by the Swedish company Boliden; the ore lies in Carboniferous limestone (limestone formed in the Carboniferous period).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.1] Bingham Canyon copper mine, Utah, USA: one of the largest open-pit mines in the world.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.2] Super Pit gold mine, Kalgoorlie, Western Australia: a large open-pit gold mine.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.3] Aberfan, south Wales, 21 October 1966: a colliery (coal mine) spoil tip built over springs on a hillside collapsed into the village after heavy rain, burying Pantglas Junior School; 144 people were killed, 116 of them children.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.4] Aberfan was a flow slide of saturated waste, a mass movement process (the downhill movement of loose material under gravity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.b] Mining brings real economic benefits, but it also changes the land and water around the mine. A good answer gives both sides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.0] Landscape scars and subsidence (the sinking of the ground surface) occur above and around underground workings.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.1] Spoil heaps and tailings take up land, so they remove it from farming or wildlife.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.2] Avoca, Co. Wicklow: centuries of copper mining, now closed, left acid mine drainage (acidic water draining from old workings) that discolours and pollutes the Avoca River with heavy metals (toxic metals such as copper).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.3] Dust, noise and heavy traffic affect people living near the mine, for example on local roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.4] Positive impacts are jobs, exports and local spending, so the mine supports the local economy.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.b] Governments and mining companies try to limit the damage, both while the mine runs and after it closes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.0] Environmental licensing and monitoring control the handling of waste and the quality of water leaving the site, so pollution can be caught and stopped.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.1] Tailings ponds are lined to stop seepage (waste liquid leaking into the ground) and capped (sealed over) when they are closed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.2] Lisheen, Co. Tipperary: a zinc and lead mine that closed in 2015; the site was rehabilitated (restored to a safe, usable condition).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.b] Quarries work rock and sand close to the surface for cement, aggregate (crushed rock used in concrete), building stone and roofing. This option is called quarrying on the Ordinary Level paper, but it is the same option.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.0] Limestone is quarried, for example, for cement, aggregate and lime for agriculture.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.1] Sand and gravel come from glacial and fluvio-glacial deposits (material dropped by ice and by glacial meltwater) and are used in concrete.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.2] Granite and other dimension stone (stone cut into blocks of a set size) are cut from the rock for building.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.3] Slate is quarried for roofing, such as Valentia slate, and marble for decoration.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.b] Quarrying follows a fixed order, working from the top of the site down into the rock and then preparing the rock for sale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.0] 1. Remove the overburden: the soil and glacial till (the mixed clay and stones left by ice) above the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.1] 2. Cut benches (stepped ledges) into the rock face.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.2] 3. Drill and blast the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.3] 4. Crush and screen the rock, grading it by size.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.4] Dimension stone is cut to size rather than blasted, because blasting would shatter the blocks.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.b] Cement is one of the main uses of quarried limestone, and making it links directly back to the rock cycle through the carbon locked in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.0] Limestone is crushed and mixed with shale, then heated in a kiln (a very hot furnace) to make clinker; the clinker is ground with gypsum to make cement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.1] Heating limestone releases carbon dioxide, because calcium carbonate gives lime and carbon dioxide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.2] The carbon dioxide returns to the atmosphere carbon that had been locked in the rock for hundreds of millions of years.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.3] Irish Cement's works at Platin, near Drogheda, Co. Meath, and at Castlemungret, Co. Limerick, are both based on Carboniferous limestone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.b] Each Irish material has a place you can name. Use these in your answer in place of general statements such as a quarry in Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.0] Sand and gravel: large pits in glacial deposits around Blessington, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.1] Granite: quarried at Ballyknockan, Co. Wicklow, from the Leinster granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.2] Marble: green Connemara marble is quarried in Connemara, Co. Galway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.3] Slate: quarried on Valentia Island, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.b] A quarry sits in the countryside, so its effects fall on neighbours, farmland and water as well as on the rock itself.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.0] Permanent scars, and loss of farmland and habitat, because the ground is removed rather than just covered.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.1] Dust, noise and blasting vibration, and heavy lorries on rural roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.2] Lowering of the local water table (the level below which the ground is saturated with water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.3] Positive impacts are jobs and the materials needed for housing and roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.b] Quarries are controlled through the planning system, and the land can be given a new use once the rock is worked out.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.0] Planning permission comes with conditions on working hours, and limits on dust and noise, so neighbours are protected.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.1] Earth banks and trees screen the quarry from view, so it is less of an eyesore.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.2] Worked-out quarries are rehabilitated as lakes, wildlife habitats, landfill or amenity areas (places for public recreation).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.b] Oil and gas are fossil fuels that form from living things buried in sediment, which makes them part of the sedimentary side of the rock cycle. They only become a usable resource where the right rock layers hold them in place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.0] The remains of tiny marine organisms are buried in sea-floor sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.1] Heat and pressure over millions of years turn these remains into oil and gas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.2] Oil and gas migrate upward through porous (full of tiny holes), permeable (letting fluids pass through) reservoir rock, such as sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.3] They are trapped beneath impermeable cap rock (rock that fluids cannot pass through), such as shale, in a trap: an anticline (an upfold of rock layers), a fault (a crack along which rock has moved) or a salt dome (a rising mass of salt that pushes up the layers above).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.b] Finding and producing oil and gas takes several stages. Ireland has two gas fields to use as case studies, and Corrib shows how a project can cause conflict.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.0] Seismic surveys (which use sound waves to map rock layers) locate possible traps, and exploratory wells test them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.1] Production, which starts once a well finds oil or gas, uses production platforms or subsea wells, with pipelines to an onshore terminal.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.2] Kinsale Head gas field, off the Co. Cork coast: discovered 1971, in production from 1978; Ireland's first commercial gas field; production ended in 2020 when the field was depleted.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.3] Corrib gas field, off the coast of north-west Co. Mayo: discovered 1996, and gas first flowed in December 2015; gas is piped ashore to a processing terminal at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo, originally operated by Shell.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.4] Opposition at Corrib: the Shell to Sea campaign objected to a high-pressure raw-gas pipeline near homes, and five local men, the Rossport Five, were jailed in 2005 for refusing to obey a court order not to obstruct the pipeline work.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.b] Offshore drilling can go badly wrong. The Deepwater Horizon spill is the international example to learn, alongside the North Sea as a major producing region.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.0] The Deepwater Horizon oil rig, leased by BP in the Gulf of Mexico, exploded on 20 April 2010, killing 11 workers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.1] About 4.9 million barrels of oil spilled before the well was sealed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.2] The spill damaged, for example, fishing, tourism and wildlife along the US Gulf coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.3] The North Sea, shared by the UK and Norway, is a major oil and gas province (a region with large oil and gas reserves).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.b] Fracking is a newer way of getting gas from rock that does not flow freely, and it is controversial. Like all oil and gas use, it brings benefits and costs that you must weigh.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.0] Hydraulic fracturing (fracking) of shale (a fine-grained rock that traps gas) releases gas; concerns include groundwater pollution and induced earthquakes (quakes caused by human activity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.1] Ireland banned onshore fracking by law in 2017.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.2] Positive impacts are energy supply and security, revenue and jobs, since a country with its own gas depends less on imports.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.3] Negative impacts are oil spills, damage to marine life and fishing, and conflict with local communities.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.4] Burning fossil fuels returns stored carbon to the atmosphere, contributing to climate change.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.b] Geothermal energy is heat from inside the earth. It is available everywhere deep enough, but it is much easier to use where the heat is close to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.0] Heat comes from the earth's interior, from radioactive decay (the breakdown of unstable atoms, which gives off heat) and residual heat (heat left over from the earth's formation).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.1] Temperature rises with depth at about 25 to 30 °C per kilometre; this is the geothermal gradient.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.2] Heat is greatest near plate boundaries and hotspots (places where hot rock rises from deep in the earth), where magma (molten rock) lies close to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.b] Where magma is near the surface, the rock heats groundwater to very high temperatures. This high-temperature (high-enthalpy) system can drive power stations and heat whole towns.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.0] Groundwater is heated by hot rock near magma.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.1] Production wells bring steam and hot water to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.2] Steam drives turbines (wheels spun by the steam) to generate electricity, and hot water heats buildings through district heating (hot water piped to many buildings from one source).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.3] Cooled water is pumped back down a reinjection well, so the water supply is returned to the ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.b] Not every country has hot rock near the surface. Two other approaches use lower heat or create the conditions for heat to be collected.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.0] Ground-source heat pumps use the steady warmth of shallow ground to heat homes. This is the low-temperature type, suited to places far from plate boundaries.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.1] Enhanced geothermal systems pump water into hot dry rock (hot rock with no natural water in it) to fracture it, so that the water picks up heat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.b] Iceland is the main international case study because geothermal heat is central to its energy supply. Larderello and Wairakei add historical and international examples.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.0] Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (a plate boundary where plates move apart) above a hotspot, which gives it geothermal heat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.1] About 9 out of 10 homes in Iceland are heated by geothermal water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.2] Geothermal power provides between a quarter and a third of Iceland's electricity; most of the rest is hydro (hydroelectric power).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.3] Hellisheiði is Iceland's largest geothermal power station, and Reykjavík has a geothermal district heating system.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.4] The Blue Lagoon is a pool of warm, mineral-rich waste water from the Svartsengi power station, now a tourist attraction.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.5] Larderello, Tuscany, Italy: the world's first geothermal electricity (1904) and first commercial geothermal power station (1913).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.6] Wairakei, New Zealand: geothermal power station opened in 1958.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.b] Ireland is far from a plate boundary, where geothermal heat is greatest, so its geothermal potential is low-temperature only.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.0] Ireland is far from a plate boundary, so it has only low-temperature geothermal potential.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.1] Warm springs at Mallow, Co. Cork, show that heated groundwater exists.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.2] Ground-source heat pumps are the main use, because they need only shallow ground, for example for heating homes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.b] Geothermal energy is clean and dependable, but it can only be used in suitable places and brings some problems of its own.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.0] Advantages: renewable (it does not run out), low carbon emissions, reliable because it does not depend on the weather, and cheap to run once built.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.1] Disadvantages: it is limited to suitable locations, and drilling costs are high.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.2] Hydrogen sulphide gas is released, giving a rotten-egg smell.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.3] Minerals are deposited in pipes as the hot water cools.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.4] Basel, Switzerland: an enhanced geothermal project triggered small earthquakes in 2006 and was abandoned.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-activities-s1: 1. Case study of a developed economy: Ireland
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.b] Economic activities (the ways people earn a living) are grouped into three sectors by what they do with raw materials or with people's needs. Knowing which sector a job belongs to is the first step in describing any economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.0] Primary activity is extracting raw materials from nature, such as farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying and energy extraction. Nothing is made yet, so the resource is simply taken from the land or sea.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.1] Secondary activity is processing raw materials or assembling components (parts) into goods, such as manufacturing, food processing and construction.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.2] Tertiary activity is services, which provide help or access rather than goods, such as retail, banking, tourism, teaching, health, transport and IT (information technology).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.3] Classifying jobs: a miner or forester is primary; a builder is secondary, because construction is secondary; a teacher, banker or tour guide is tertiary.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.0.4] Some writers add a quaternary sector for research and information, but the three-sector model is used here.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.b] The share of workers in each sector changes as a country becomes richer. Ireland shows the end result of that shift.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.0] As an economy develops, employment moves from primary to secondary and then to tertiary activity. This is why primary employment falls as countries develop.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.1] Services employ about three-quarters of Irish workers (2020s), which means Ireland is a tertiary economy; agriculture, forestry and fishing employ about 4–5%.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.2] Census 2022 counted a State population of about 5.1 million; the CSO (Central Statistics Office) Labour Force Survey (2024) recorded over 2.7 million people in employment.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.1.3] In a developing economy, by contrast, primary employment is high. There is more on this in the Economic Development notes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.b] For its first decades as an independent State, Ireland tried to shelter its own firms from foreign competition. This is the starting point of the timeline of how Ireland became a developed economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.0] The Control of Manufactures Acts, 1932 and 1934, restricted foreign ownership of Irish firms, so industry stayed in Irish hands.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.1] High tariffs (taxes on imported goods) protected small Irish firms serving the home market, so they had little pressure to become efficient or to export.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.2.2] The 1950s brought high unemployment and heavy emigration (people leaving the country to live abroad), which showed that protection had not created enough jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.b] From the late 1950s Ireland reversed its policy and opened up to trade and foreign companies. Each step below was meant to bring in jobs and exports.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.0] The Industrial Development Authority (IDA) was set up in 1949 to attract industry, so Ireland had an agency for winning new firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.1] Export Profits Tax Relief, 1956, removed tax on profits from exports, so firms had a reason to sell abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.2] T.K. Whitaker's Economic Development and the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958) set the policy of free trade, foreign investment and export-led growth (growth driven by selling abroad), carried out under Seán Lemass as Taoiseach from 1959.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.3] The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement (1965) reduced trade barriers with Britain, Ireland's nearest and biggest market.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.3.4] Ireland joined the EEC (European Economic Community, the forerunner of the EU) on 1 January 1973, together with the UK and Denmark.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.b] Attracting foreign firms also needed people with the right skills. Free schooling was the policy that built them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.0] Free second-level education was announced in 1966 by Donogh O'Malley and introduced in 1967.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.1] A better-educated workforce made Ireland attractive to firms that needed skilled labour in the decades that followed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.4.2] The IDA became an independent agency under the Industrial Development Act 1969, so it could pursue foreign investment in its own right.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.b] Later steps tied Ireland more closely to Europe and set the tax rates that attract multinational companies. Corporation tax is the tax a company pays on its profits.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.0] The EU Single Market began in 1993, giving Irish firms and foreign investors access to the wider EU market without trade barriers.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.1] The euro was adopted in 1999, with notes and coins introduced in 2002, which means Irish firms trade with the eurozone (the countries using the euro) without exchange-rate costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.2] Corporation tax on trading profits was 12.5% from 2003. This low rate is a main reason multinationals locate in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.5.3] From 2024 very large multinational groups (global turnover over €750 million) pay a 15% minimum rate under the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) agreement.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.b] The Celtic Tiger is the name for Ireland's period of very rapid growth. It followed from the earlier policies of free trade, investment and education.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.0] Rapid growth from the mid-1990s to 2007 was driven first by exports and foreign direct investment (FDI, money invested by foreign firms in Irish plants), especially from US technology and pharmaceutical firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.1] A property and construction boom added to growth later in the period, because rising prices encouraged more building.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.2] Unemployment fell to about 4–5% by the mid-2000s, because so many new jobs were created.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.6.3] Net immigration (more people arriving than leaving) replaced emigration, with large inflows from the new EU states after the 2004 enlargement.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.b] The property boom ended in a crash that put Irish banks in danger. The State stepped in to protect them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.0] The property bubble (a rise in prices that could not last) burst in 2007–2008, leaving banks with large bad loans (loans unlikely to be repaid) secured on property that had lost value.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.1] On 30 September 2008 the government guaranteed Irish bank liabilities, promising that the banks' debts would be covered.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.2] Anglo Irish Bank was nationalised (taken into State ownership) in January 2009.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.7.3] The National Asset Management Agency (NAMA), set up in 2009, took over property loans from the banks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.b] The cost of rescuing the banks left the State unable to borrow normally, so it needed outside help. The crash also hit jobs and population.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.0] An EU–IMF (International Monetary Fund) programme was agreed in November 2010, providing €67.5 billion in external loans.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.1] Ireland left the programme in December 2013, which means the State could borrow on normal markets again.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.2] Unemployment peaked at about 16% in early 2012, so net emigration resumed from 2009 to 2014.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.8.3] Government debt peaked at about 120% of GDP (the value of everything produced in the country in a year) in 2012: the state borrowed to rescue the banks while its tax base (the income and spending that can be taxed) collapsed with the economy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.b] After 2013 the economy recovered strongly. Jobs returned and public finances improved.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.0] Unemployment was about 4–5% in 2023–2024, near full employment (almost everyone who wants work has it).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.1] Budget surpluses (tax income greater than spending) in the 2020s came largely from corporation tax paid by a small number of large multinationals, so public finances depend on them.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.9.2] COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020–2021 hit tourism, hospitality and retail hardest.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.b] When most people who want work have it, the whole economy gains, not only the workers. Each benefit below follows from people earning and spending.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.0] Employed people pay income tax, and their spending pays VAT (value-added tax, a tax on goods and services), so tax revenue rises.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.1] Welfare spending falls because fewer people need unemployment payments, so the State has more to spend elsewhere.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.2] Consumer spending by employed people supports jobs in other sectors, such as shops and cafes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.3] Less emigration keeps skilled people in the economy, so firms can find the workers they need.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.10.4] Government revenue can pay for investment in infrastructure (roads, rail, utilities) and services.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.b] Because so many multinationals are based in Ireland, their profits can distort the national figures. This makes GDP a poor guide to how well Irish people are doing.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.0] In 2015 Irish GDP grew by about 26% after multinationals moved intellectual property assets (such as patents and software rights) to Ireland; the effect was nicknamed 'leprechaun economics'.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.11.1] Because GDP is inflated by these profits, the CSO introduced modified gross national income (GNI*) in 2017 to strip out their effects.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.b] Farming is Ireland's main primary activity, and its pattern follows the climate. Mild, wet weather suits grass, so animals matter more than crops.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.0] The Census of Agriculture 2020 counted about 135,000 farms, averaging about 33 hectares.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.1] Grass grows for most of the year because winters are mild and rain is reliable, so grass-based livestock farming dominates.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.2] Pastoral farming is livestock farming: beef and dairy cattle, sheep and pigs. The national cattle herd is about 7 million (2020s), which is why beef and dairy cattle are central to farming.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.3] Arable (tillage) farming grows crops: barley is the largest tillage crop, with wheat, oats and potatoes also grown.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.12.4] Tillage is concentrated in the south and east, in Counties Wexford, Carlow, Kildare, Meath and Cork.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.b] Livestock farming earns money abroad, but a large herd also damages the environment. Exams ask for a benefit and a problem.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.0] EU milk quotas (limits on how much milk each country could produce) ended on 1 April 2015, and dairy cow numbers and milk output rose strongly afterwards.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.1] About 90% of Irish beef and dairy output is exported (Bord Bia, 2020s).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.2] A large cattle herd brings exports, food-processing jobs and incomes to rural areas.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.3] Agriculture is the largest single source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions (gases that warm the atmosphere), about 38% (EPA, 2022 data), with methane from livestock a main part of this.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.13.4] Nitrate and phosphate runoff (fertiliser and slurry washed from fields) pollutes rivers and lakes, and livestock release ammonia.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.b] Agri-food means food and drink made from farm produce. Irish brands turn farm output into exports, and exams ask for one food and one drink product.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.0] Kerrygold butter is exported by Ornua, the dairy co-operatives' export company; the brand was launched in 1962.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.1] Kerry Group (headquarters Tralee, Co. Kerry) and Glanbia (headquarters Kilkenny) are major food companies, such as those that process Irish farm output.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.2] Tayto crisps were first made in 1954 by Joe 'Spud' Murphy.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.3] Bord Bia, the Irish food board, promotes Irish food, so its exports are better known abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.4] Guinness is made at St James's Gate Brewery, Dublin, a site dating from 1759.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.5] Jameson Irish whiskey is made at Midleton Distillery, Co. Cork; its producer, Irish Distillers, is owned by Pernod Ricard.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.14.6] Baileys Irish Cream was launched in 1974.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.b] Ireland's geography gives it good conditions for fishing. Several harbours are officially designated as fishing centres.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.0] Ireland has a long, indented coastline with sheltered harbours, so boats can land fish safely, and a wide continental shelf (shallow seabed around the coast where fish feed).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.1] Plankton-rich waters and the North Atlantic Drift (a warm ocean current) support productive stocks, especially pelagic (surface-swimming) fish such as mackerel.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.2] Six State Fishery Harbour Centres: Killybegs (Co. Donegal), Castletownbere (Co. Cork), Dingle (Co. Kerry), Rossaveel (Co. Galway), Dunmore East (Co. Waterford) and Howth (Co. Dublin).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.3] Killybegs is Ireland's largest fishing port and lands mainly pelagic fish, such as mackerel.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.15.4] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM) is the Irish sea fisheries board, so it is the State body for the industry.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.b] Irish fishing is limited by EU rules, but it still matters to the coastal places that depend on it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.0] The EU Common Fisheries Policy (1983) sets fishing quotas (limits on the catch) for member states, so Irish boats cannot catch as much as they wish.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.1] The 2020 EU–UK Brexit trade agreement reduced Irish quotas, which means Irish fishers may catch less.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.2] Mackerel is the most valuable species landed in Ireland, which is why pelagic waters matter so much.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.16.3] Fishing earns export income, provides jobs in remote coastal communities and supports fish processing.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.b] Ireland has little forest, which limits how much wood can be processed. This also explains why the wood industry employs few people.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.0] Forest cover is about 11% (2020s), among the lowest in the EU, so there is little timber to process.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.1] Coillte, the State forestry company, was set up in 1988; Sitka spruce is the main plantation tree.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.2] Wood processing employs few people because the forest area is small, plantations are young, sawmills and board mills (which make boards from wood) are highly mechanised (done by machines), and timber is partly exported unprocessed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.17.3] Coillte's board mills are Medite, making MDF (medium-density fibreboard) at Clonmel, Co. Tipperary, and SmartPly, making oriented strand board at Belview, Co. Kilkenny, on Waterford Port.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.b] Ireland also takes metal and energy from the ground. The examples below are the named ones to know, one for each.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath, is Europe's largest zinc mine; it opened in 1977 and is owned by Boliden. Production was paused from July 2023 and restarted at the end of 2024.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.1] The Corrib gas field off Co. Mayo first flowed in December 2015, so it is Ireland's example of gas extraction.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.2] ESB's peat-fired power stations at Shannonbridge (West Offaly) and Lanesborough (Lough Ree) closed in December 2020.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.18.3] Bord na Móna's peat harvesting for electricity has ended, because the peat stations it supplied have closed.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.b] Manufacturing in Ireland is driven by foreign companies. The reasons they come are also the answer to 'factors that attract industry to Ireland'.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.0] Foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment by foreign companies that set up or expand plants and offices in Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.1] IDA client companies, mostly foreign-owned, employ about 300,000 people (2023), which shows how much jobs depend on FDI.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.2] Low corporation tax, IDA grants and serviced sites (land already supplied with roads, power and water) attract firms to Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.3] A young, educated, English-speaking workforce gives firms access to skilled labour.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.4] Access to the EU single market and clusters of similar firms add to the attraction, since suppliers and skills are nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.19.5] Ireland's time zone, between the USA and Asia, suits firms that serve both.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.20.b] Pharmaceuticals (medicines) and chemicals are the biggest manufacturing sector in Ireland. Cork Harbour is the main centre.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.20.0] Cork Harbour, especially Ringaskiddy and Little Island, is one of the world's largest concentrations of pharmaceutical and chemical plants.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.20.1] Pfizer, Janssen (Johnson & Johnson) and Novartis have plants at Ringaskiddy; Eli Lilly is at Kinsale, Co. Cork; Pfizer also has a site at Grange Castle, Clondalkin, Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.20.2] Pharmaceuticals and chemicals are Ireland's largest goods exports, about half or more of the total (2020s).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.b] Medical technology and electronics are the other main manufacturing sectors, each centred on named places.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.0] Galway is one of Europe's leading medical-technology clusters: Boston Scientific opened there in 1994 and Medtronic is at Parkmore.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.1] Intel at Leixlip, Co. Kildare, opened in 1989 and is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, worth billions of euro.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.2] Fab 34 at Leixlip opened in 2023 and makes advanced chips using extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography (printing very fine circuits with a short-wavelength light).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.21.3] Apple's European headquarters at Hollyhill, Cork, opened in 1980 and employs about 6,000 staff (2020s), so it is a major employer in the city.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.b] Not all Irish industry is foreign-owned. Indigenous (Irish-owned) firms get State support, and some have become multinationals themselves.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.0] Enterprise Ireland, set up in 1998, supports Irish-owned firms, so they can grow as well as the foreign ones.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.1] Irish-founded firms that grew into multinationals include CRH (building materials) and Smurfit Kappa (paper packaging), which merged with WestRock in 2024 as Smurfit Westrock.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.2] Ryanair, an Irish-founded airline, is a tertiary firm because it provides a transport service.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.22.3] Ireland has a dual economy: a highly productive foreign-owned sector sits beside a smaller, lower-productivity Irish-owned sector.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.b] Ireland's industrial success rests on a few foreign firms and favourable conditions. Each challenge below puts one of those at risk.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.0] Dependence on a few large US multinationals means that a single firm's decisions can change output and jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.1] The 15% global minimum tax for very large groups reduces the advantage of Ireland's low rate. There is more on this in the block on the Single Market, euro and corporation tax.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.2] In September 2024 the EU Court of Justice ruled that Apple must pay Ireland about €13 billion plus interest in back taxes; the case had been begun by the European Commission in 2016.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.3] US trade and tax policy changes: the USA raised tariffs on EU goods in 2025.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.4] Brexit: the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020 and the transition ended on 31 December 2020, changing trade, supply chains and access to the UK market.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.5] High housing and living costs make it hard to attract staff, because workers cannot afford to live near the jobs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.6] Electricity grid and water constraints, and high energy costs, limit expansion.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.23.7] Competition from lower-cost countries, because they can make goods more cheaply.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.b] A footloose industry is not tied to a location by raw materials or power, so it can locate almost anywhere. This freedom is why governments compete to attract it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.0] Footloose firms choose places with skilled labour, good communications, quality of life and government incentives, because none of these is fixed by nature.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.24.1] Services such as software, internet and data processing are the most footloose, since their product is information that needs no bulky raw materials.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.b] The Dublin region is Ireland's main example of an area dominated by services and footloose industry. Its best-known cluster is Silicon Docks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.0] Silicon Docks is the Grand Canal Dock area, south of the River Liffey, where digital firms cluster.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.1] Microsoft has been in Ireland since 1985; Google has had its European headquarters in Dublin since 2003, at Barrow Street on Grand Canal Dock.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.2] Meta (Facebook), LinkedIn and Salesforce also have large Dublin offices.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.25.3] The Dublin Docklands Development Authority, set up in 1997, was created to plan and regenerate the docklands.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.b] Service firms are footloose, so they go where conditions suit them. Dublin offers several at once.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.0] Skilled graduates come from Trinity College Dublin, UCD, DCU, TU Dublin and Maynooth University.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.1] Firms recruit staff from across the EU for offices serving Europe, so language and skills are not limited to Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.2] Dublin Airport, with over 30 million passengers a year (2023), and fibre connectivity (high-speed internet cables) link the region to the world.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.3] A low tax rate and government incentives attract firms, because they cut costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.26.4] Agglomeration: firms gain from being close to each other, sharing skills and suppliers, so one firm attracts others.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.b] Clustering brings both gains and strains to the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.0] The cluster provides high-paid jobs in offices and related services.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.1] Demand for offices and housing has pushed rents up, because supply cannot keep pace.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.27.2] The concentration of firms and workers adds to congestion (crowded roads and transport) in Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.b] Data centres are buildings full of computers that store and process internet data. They are a newer footloose service, and they put heavy demand on the electricity supply.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.0] Data centres used about a fifth (about 21%) of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO), and most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.1] A cool climate reduces cooling costs, so running the machines is cheaper; fibre links connect them to global networks; firms cluster near each other; and tax is favourable.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.28.2] The problem is electricity demand: EirGrid (the national electricity grid operator) stated in 2022 that it would not connect new data centres in the Dublin region until about 2028.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.b] Shannon was an early planned zone for footloose industry. Its duty-free shop (1947) came before the Free Zone (1959).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.0] Brendan O'Regan opened the world's first duty-free airport shop at Shannon in 1947.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.1] The Shannon Free Airport Development Company (SFADCo) was set up in 1959, and the Shannon Free Zone that year offered firms tax relief and duty-free import of components, which cut their costs.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.2] Aircraft leasing (renting planes to airlines) grew from Shannon after Guinness Peat Aviation (GPA) was founded there in 1975 by Tony Ryan, who co-founded Ryanair in the 1980s.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.29.3] Ireland now manages about half of the world's leased commercial aircraft (2020s, approximately).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.b] Silicon Valley is the original region of footloose high-technology and service industry. It is the global comparison for Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.0] It covers the Santa Clara Valley and the southern San Francisco Bay Area; the name comes from the silicon used in microchips.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.1] Stanford University at Palo Alto encouraged spin-off firms (new firms started by its staff and graduates) and set up Stanford Research Park in 1951.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.2] Hewlett-Packard was founded in a Palo Alto garage in 1939; Intel was founded in Mountain View in 1968 and is now headquartered in Santa Clara.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.3] Apple is based in Cupertino and Google in Mountain View, which shows how the valley hosts the biggest firms.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.30.4] Problems: very high house prices and rents, traffic congestion, and homelessness alongside great wealth.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.31.b] Financial services are a highly footloose, high-value tertiary activity. They deal in money rather than goods.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.31.0] They include banking, insurance, fund administration, asset management (looking after investments for clients), aircraft leasing, payments and fintech (technology used to deliver financial services).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.32.b] The International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) is Ireland's main financial services case study. It was set up to bring in finance and to revive a run-down area.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.32.0] The IFSC was set up in 1987 by the Charles Haughey government on the derelict Custom House Docks, on the north bank of the Liffey.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.32.1] Approved firms paid a 10% corporation tax rate, so the IFSC was cheaper than elsewhere, later replaced by the 12.5% national rate.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.32.2] Firms were attracted by the low tax rate, government incentives, an educated English-speaking workforce, EU access, the time zone, and the chance to regenerate a declining dock area.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.33.b] The IFSC grew into a major international centre and changed the area around it.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.33.0] The IFSC became one of the world's largest centres for investment funds (pooled savings invested for clients); Irish-domiciled funds held over €3 trillion in assets (2020s, Central Bank of Ireland).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.33.1] Aircraft leasing is a second specialism. There is more on this in the block on the Shannon Free Zone and aircraft leasing.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.33.2] Effects: high-value jobs and the regeneration of the north docklands, served by the Luas Red Line from 2004, with new housing nearby.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.34.b] Finance brings risks as well as gains, but Brexit also created an opening for Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.34.0] The 2008 banking crisis damaged confidence in Irish finance, and the Central Bank of Ireland regulates the sector.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.34.1] After the 2016 Brexit vote, Bank of America and Barclays moved their main EU operations to Dublin, an opportunity for Ireland.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.35.b] London is the older and larger financial centre against which Dublin can be compared.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.35.0] The City of London (the Square Mile) was deregulated (its rules relaxed) by the 'Big Bang' of the London Stock Exchange in October 1986.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.35.1] Canary Wharf, built on derelict London docks from the late 1980s, became a second financial district.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.35.2] After Brexit, some banks moved EU operations from London to Dublin, Frankfurt and Paris.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.b] Tourism is a tertiary activity, and whether an area develops it depends on a mix of natural and human factors.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.0] Physical attractions: climate, coast, scenery, mountains and lakes.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.1] Cultural and historic attractions: heritage sites, language, music, festivals and sport.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.2] Access: airports, ports, roads and low-cost airlines.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.3] Accommodation and services, and marketing by government agencies.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.36.4] Cost and exchange rates, and safety and political stability.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.b] Tourism brings overseas visitors and supports jobs. State agencies develop it and market it abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.0] About 11 million overseas visits were made to the Republic of Ireland in 2019 (CSO).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.1] Tourism supported about a quarter of a million jobs in 2019 (industry estimate), which means it is a major employer.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.2] Britain, the USA and continental Europe (Germany, France) are the main source markets, so most visitors come from these.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.3] Fáilte Ireland, set up in 2003, develops tourism in the Republic; Tourism Ireland, an all-island marketing body created under the Good Friday Agreement (1998), markets the island abroad.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.37.4] The Wild Atlantic Way, launched in 2014, runs about 2,500 km from Inishowen to Kinsale; Ireland's Ancient East was launched in 2015.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.38.b] Tourism depends on people being free to travel, so it is exposed to sudden shocks.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.38.0] Overseas visits collapsed in 2020–2021 during COVID-19 travel restrictions, because people could not travel.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.38.1] Job losses, closed hotels and lost revenue hit remote areas that depend on visitors.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.39.b] Visitors arrive by air or sea, so access is a main factor in tourism. Exams ask you to name an airport and a ferry port.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.39.0] International airports: Dublin, Shannon, Cork, Ireland West Airport Knock (opened 1986) and Kerry Airport at Farranfore.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.39.1] Ferry ports: Dublin Port (to Holyhead), Rosslare Europort (to Fishguard and Pembroke, and since Brexit more direct routes to France) and Port of Cork at Ringaskiddy (to Roscoff, France).
[geo-economic-activities-s1.39.2] Ryanair, Europe's largest airline by passengers (2020s), is headquartered in Swords, Co. Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.40.b] More overseas visitors arrive on the east coast than anywhere else. The reasons all come back to Dublin.
[geo-economic-activities-s1.40.0] Dublin Airport and Dublin Port are the main entry points for overseas visitors.
