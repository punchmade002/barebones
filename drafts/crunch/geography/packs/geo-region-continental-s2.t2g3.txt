[geo-rivers-s1.0.b] Before looking at how rivers shape the land, you need the vocabulary for the parts of a river system. These words are used to label maps and diagrams.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.0] Source: where the river rises, meaning where it begins. The River Shannon, Ireland's longest river, rises at the Shannon Pot, Co. Cavan.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.1] Mouth: where the river ends by entering the sea, a lake or another river, so it is the opposite end from the source.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.2] Tributary: a smaller river that flows into a larger river, so it adds its water to the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.3] Confluence: the point where a tributary joins the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.4] Drainage basin (catchment): the whole area of land drained by a river and its tributaries, so all rain falling in it ends up in that river system.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.5] Watershed: the high ground that separates one drainage basin from the next, so water on either side flows to a different river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.6] Distributary: a channel that leaves the main river and flows away from it, found on deltas (fan-shaped landforms of deposited material at a river mouth).
[geo-rivers-s1.0.7] Estuary: the tidal lower course of a river, where fresh water meets salt water. The Shannon enters the Atlantic through the Shannon Estuary below Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.b] The long profile is the smooth, concave slope of a river from its source to its mouth. Because the slope changes along the way, the river is divided into three courses, and each course has its own landforms.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.0] The gradient (steepness of the river's slope) is steep near the source and gentle near the mouth, which is what makes the profile concave.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.1] Upper (youthful) course: steep, with V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs (ridges that overlap like the teeth of a zip), waterfalls, rapids and potholes.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.2] Middle (mature) course: meanders (looping bends) develop as the valley widens, because the gentler gradient lets the river swing from side to side.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.3] Lower (old) course: a wide floodplain (flat land beside the river), levees (natural banks built up beside the channel), meanders with oxbow lakes (cut-off loops), and a delta or estuary at the mouth.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.b] A river can only erode or carry material if it has energy. How much it has, and where it uses it, explains why the upper course looks so different from the lower course.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.0] A river's energy depends on its volume (discharge, the amount of water flowing past a point) and its gradient.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.1] Energy left after overcoming friction with the bed and banks is used for erosion (wearing away the land) and transport (carrying material, called the load).
[geo-rivers-s1.2.2] Volume increases downstream because tributaries keep joining the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.3] Vertical erosion (deepening the bed) dominates in the upper course, so valleys there are cut downwards.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.4] Lateral erosion (widening the valley sideways) dominates in the middle and lower courses, so the valley floor there becomes wide.
[geo-rivers-s1.2.5] Headward erosion lengthens a valley at its source, cutting back upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.b] Rivers wear away their bed and banks in four ways. Each works differently, and each is a named process you may be asked to explain.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.0] Hydraulic action: the force of moving water dislodges loose material and forces air into cracks in the bed and banks, widening them until rock breaks away.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.1] Abrasion (corrasion): the load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks like sandpaper. Pebbles swirled in eddies (small whirlpools) drill potholes into the bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.2] Attrition: particles of the load collide with each other and with the bed, so they become smaller and more rounded downstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.3.3] Solution (corrosion): slightly acidic water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone, carrying it away in solution (dissolved in the water).
[geo-rivers-s1.4.b] Once material has been eroded, the river carries it downstream. The method depends on the size of the particles and the speed of the flow.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.0] Traction: the largest boulders are rolled along the bed, mainly during floods when the flow is strongest.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.1] Saltation: pebbles and sand are too heavy to stay in the flow, so they bounce along the bed in a series of hops.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.2] Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried within the flow, giving the water its brown colour.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.3] Solution: dissolved minerals are carried invisibly in the water.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.4] Competence: the largest particle a river can carry. It rises sharply in flood because the faster, bigger flow can move larger particles.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.5] Capacity: the total load a river can carry. It also rises sharply in flood, so a flooded river carries far more material overall.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.b] A river drops its load when it no longer has the energy to carry it. Understanding why it drops it, and in what order, explains the shape of most depositional landforms.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.0] Deposition (dropping of the load) happens when velocity (speed of flow) falls: on entering a lake or the sea, on the inside of a bend, when floodwater recedes, or on a gentler gradient.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.1] Deposition also happens when the load becomes too great for the river's energy to carry, so the excess is dropped even if the flow has not slowed.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.2] Heavier material is deposited first and the finest last, so deposits are sorted by size. This contrasts with glacial till (material dropped by ice), which is unsorted and angular.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.b] A drainage pattern is the arrangement of a river and its tributaries, shaped by the underlying rock and relief (the height and shape of the land). Each pattern tells you something about the rock or land beneath it.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.0] Dendritic: tree-like, with tributaries joining at acute angles (sharp angles); forms on rock of uniform resistance, so the rivers have no reason to favour any direction.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.1] Trellised: tributaries meet the main rivers at right angles; forms where bands of hard and soft rock alternate. In the ridge-and-valley landscape of Munster, rivers follow the soft limestone valleys and tributaries flow off the sandstone ridges at right angles.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.2] Radial: rivers flow outward from a central high point like the spokes of a wheel, because the ground slopes away in every direction from the high point.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.3] Deranged: no clear pattern; forms on poorly drained glacial deposits (material left by ice), e.g. the drumlin belt of Co. Cavan, with many small lakes and marshes between the drumlins.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.4] Centripetal: rivers flow inward to a central lake or basin, e.g. Lough Neagh, into which rivers drain from all sides.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.5] On an Ordnance Survey map, dendritic streams branch like a tree at sharp angles, trellised streams run parallel with tributaries meeting them at right angles, radial streams spread out from a high point, and centripetal streams converge on a lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.b] In the upper course the river is steep and powerful, so it cuts downwards into its bed. This shapes a narrow valley with a V-shaped cross-section.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.0] 1. The steep gradient gives the river high energy, so it erodes vertically by hydraulic action and abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.1] 2. Weathering (breakdown of rock in place) and mass movement (downhill movement of loose material) loosen the valley sides and move material into the channel, widening the top of the valley.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.2] 3. The result is a narrow floor and steep sides in a V-shape, because the river cuts down faster than it widens the valley.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.3] 4. The river swings around areas of harder rock, leaving spurs (ridges of land) that interlock like the teeth of a zip.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.4] Interlocking spurs are fluvial (made by rivers) and are not to be confused with truncated spurs, which are glacial.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.5] On an Ordnance Survey map, the contours (lines joining points of equal height) form a V that points upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.6] Example: the upper course of the River Liffey in the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.7] Diagram: cross-section of a V-shaped valley; plan view of interlocking spurs with the river winding between them.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.b] A waterfall forms where a river crosses rock of different hardness. Two erosion processes, hydraulic action and abrasion, do the work in the undercutting and plunge-pool stages.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.0] 1. A waterfall forms where a band of resistant rock (rock that wears away slowly) lies over less resistant rock.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.1] 2. Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft rock below faster, undercutting the hard rock and leaving an overhang.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.2] 3. Falling water and its load scour a deep plunge pool at the base by hydraulic action and abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.3] 4. The unsupported overhang collapses, and the waterfall retreats upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.4] 5. Repeated retreat leaves a steep-sided gorge downstream of the falls, because the river has cut back through the rock the falls once occupied.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.5] Example: Niagara Falls, USA-Canada border, with a resistant cap of dolomite over weaker shale; the retreat of the falls has cut the Niagara Gorge below them.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.6] Irish waterfalls: Powerscourt Waterfall, River Dargle, Co. Wicklow, Ireland's highest waterfall at about 121 m; and Torc Waterfall, near Killarney, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rivers-s1.8.7] Diagram: cross-section showing hard rock cap, soft rock beneath, overhang, plunge pool and direction of retreat.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.b] Rapids and potholes are smaller features of the steep upper course, both made by erosion of the river bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.0] 1. Rapids form where alternating bands of hard and soft rock lie across the river bed.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.1] 2. Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft bands faster, leaving the hard bands standing as steps.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.2] 3. Fast, turbulent water tumbles over the steps, so the surface is broken and white, forming rapids.
[geo-rivers-s1.9.3] Potholes: hollows drilled into the bed by pebbles swirled in eddies, the grinding action of abrasion.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.b] A meander is a looping bend in a river. It is formed by both erosion and deposition, so it is classified under both.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.0] 1. Water flows fastest on the outside of a bend, where it erodes the bank by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a steep river cliff.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.1] 2. Water is slowest on the inside of the bend, where it deposits sand and gravel, forming a gently sloping point bar (slip-off slope).
[geo-rivers-s1.10.2] 3. Helicoidal (corkscrew-shaped) flow carries eroded material from the outer bank to the inner bank of the next bend.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.3] 4. Continued lateral erosion makes the bends more pronounced, so that only a narrow neck of land eventually separates them.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.4] Example: meanders on the River Shannon's floodplain, the Shannon Callows, between Athlone and Portumna.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.5] Diagram: plan view of a meander with the fastest-flow line, river cliff and point bar.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.b] If meanders keep growing, the loops can eventually be cut off from the river. This leaves a lake that later disappears.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.0] 1. During a flood the river cuts through the neck of a meander and takes the straighter, shorter course.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.1] 2. Deposition seals off the old bend, so the water left behind forms a crescent-shaped oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.2] 3. The oxbow lake slowly fills with silt and becomes a meander scar (a dry, curved hollow where the loop used to be).
[geo-rivers-s1.11.3] Example: oxbow lakes on the lower Mississippi, USA.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.4] Diagram: plan sequence showing the cut-off and the formation of the oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.b] Floodplains and levees are built by deposition during floods. They are among the main depositional landforms of the lower course.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.0] Lateral erosion by migrating meanders widens the valley floor, so there is a broad flat area for floodwater to spread over.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.1] When the river floods, it spreads across the valley, loses velocity and deposits a layer of fine silt called alluvium.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.2] Repeated floods build a wide, flat, fertile floodplain; on an Ordnance Survey map, widely spaced contours show it. Example: the Shannon Callows, a wide floodplain of seasonally flooded meadows.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.3] Levees: the coarsest material is dropped first, right beside the channel, because the water slows as soon as it leaves it. Each flood therefore raises the banks a little.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.4] Over time these build into natural embankments called levees, and the river bed itself may rise above the level of the floodplain.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.5] Example: levees along the lower Mississippi, USA, which have been artificially raised for flood control.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.6] Diagram: cross-section of a floodplain showing alluvium layers, levees and the raised channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.b] A delta is a landform of deposition at a river mouth. It builds up when the river drops its load faster than the sea or lake can remove it.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.0] A delta forms where a river carrying a heavy load enters a sea or lake and its velocity drops sharply.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.1] In salt water, fine clay particles clump together (flocculation) and sink faster.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.2] Deposits build up until they block the channel, forcing the river to split into smaller channels called distributaries.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.3] Conditions needed: a large load, and calm water with a small tidal range and weak currents, so that deposits are not removed.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.b] A delta is built up in layers, and deltas take different shapes depending on how the deposits spread. You should know the layers and the named types.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.0] Topset beds: flat, coarser deposits laid down on top of the delta, because the heavier material drops first.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.1] Foreset beds: steeply sloping deposits at the front of the delta, built out into the water so the delta grows forward.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.2] Bottomset beds: fine, flat layers laid down beyond the front of the delta, because the finest material is carried furthest.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.3] Arcuate (fan-shaped) delta, e.g. the Nile delta, Egypt.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.4] Bird's-foot delta: long fingers of deposits along distributaries, e.g. the Mississippi delta, USA.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.5] Estuarine delta: fills a drowned river mouth (a river mouth flooded by the sea).
[geo-rivers-s1.14.6] Lake delta, e.g. the delta between the Upper and Lower Lakes at Glendalough, Co. Wicklow, which divided what was once a single lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.7] Diagram: plan view of a delta with distributaries; cross-section showing topset, foreset and bottomset beds.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.b] Two more depositional features occur near the mouth, or where the load is large: the estuary and the braided channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.0] Estuary: in the tidal river mouth, fresh and salt water meet, and deposition builds mudflats and salt marsh.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.1] Example: the Shannon Estuary below Limerick, where the Shannon enters the Atlantic.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.2] Braided channel: the river splits into several channels around islands of deposited sand and gravel, called eyots or braid bars.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.3] Braided channels form where the load is large and discharge varies, so the river cannot carry all its sediment and drops it as islands in the channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.b] Every fluvial landform is formed by erosion, by deposition, or by both. Sorting them this way is a skill that exams test.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.0] Erosion: V-shaped valley, interlocking spurs, waterfall, gorge, potholes, rapids.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.1] Erosion and deposition: meander, oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.2] Deposition: floodplain, levee, delta, point bar, estuary mudflats.

CANDIDATES geo-population-migration-s1: 1. Changing population characteristics
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.b] Geographers describe a population with a small set of measures. Learn each definition exactly, because every later block uses them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.0] Crude birth rate: live births per 1,000 people per year. It is 'crude' because it counts the whole population, not just women of childbearing age.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.1] Crude death rate: deaths per 1,000 people per year.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.2] Natural change: birth rate minus death rate. A positive result is natural increase (more births than deaths); a negative result is natural decrease (more deaths than births).
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.3] Net migration: immigrants (people moving in) minus emigrants (people moving out). It is positive when more people arrive than leave.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.4] Total fertility rate: the average number of children per woman, which shows family size better than the crude birth rate.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.5] Replacement level: a total fertility rate of about 2.1 children per woman in developed countries, the level at which a population replaces itself without migration.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.6] Infant mortality rate: deaths of children under one year old per 1,000 live births.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.7] Life expectancy: the average number of years a newborn is expected to live.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.b] A population can only change in two ways: people are born or die, and people move in or out. The census, a regular count of everyone, shows which of the two drove the change.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.0] Population change equals natural change plus net migration. In any period between two censuses (an inter-censal period), the larger of the two components explains the growth or decline.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.1] The census is a count of every person on one night. In Ireland it is held every five years and run by the Central Statistics Office (CSO).
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.2] The Irish census records age, sex, place of residence, birthplace, nationality, religion, ethnicity, Irish language, housing, work and commuting.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.3] Census 2016 to Census 2022: the population grew by about 360,000 (about 7.6%). Natural increase contributed about 170,000 and net migration about 190,000, so net migration was the slightly larger component.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.b] The five-year rhythm of the Irish census has been broken a few times. Each break had a specific cause.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.0] The 1926 census was the first held by the Irish Free State (the Irish State of that time).
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.1] The 1976 census was cancelled; a special census was held in 1979 instead.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.2] The 2001 census was postponed to 2002 by foot-and-mouth disease (an animal disease outbreak).
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.3] The 2021 census was postponed to 2022 by COVID-19 and held on 3 April 2022.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.b] Governments collect census data because it tells them how many people there are, how old they are and where they live. That information shapes decisions on money and services.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.0] Planning schools, hospitals and housing: counts of children, patients and households show where services are needed.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.1] Pensions: the age structure (how many people are in each age group) sets the cost of pensions; the State pension age has been 66 since 2014.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.2] Transport and infrastructure: population size and location show where roads, rail and water services are needed, so money goes where demand is.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.3] Constituency boundaries: the population of each constituency (the area that elects TDs, members of the Dail) determines the number of TDs it elects.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.4] Funding and workforce planning: funding is allocated to regions by population, and population forecasts show future labour supply and the need for workers from abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.b] Two ideas describe where people live. They are different, so keep them apart: distribution is where people live. Density is the number of people per square kilometre of land.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.0] Distribution is the spread of people across an area, so a map can show crowded and empty places. Density turns that into a number you can compare.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.1] Density = population ÷ area in km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.2] Illustrative example (invented figures): a region of 400 km² with 120,000 people has a density of 120,000 ÷ 400 = 300 people per km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.b] Physical factors are features of the natural environment. They decide how easy it is to farm, build and live in a place.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.0] Relief (the shape and height of the land): lowlands attract more people than high mountains, which are costly to farm and build on.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.1] Climate: temperate climates (mild, without extremes) support dense settlement; places that are too hot, too cold or too dry have fewer people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.2] Soils and water supply: fertile soils and reliable water support farming and settlement.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.3] Natural resources: coalfields drew industrial populations in the 19th century, because mines and factories needed workers close by.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.b] Human factors are choices and developments made by people. They work alongside the physical factors to decide where populations cluster.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.0] Economic opportunity: jobs attract people, and regions with few jobs lose their young people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.1] Transport routes: roads, rail lines and navigable water (rivers deep enough for boats) attract settlement along them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.2] Cities and ports: cities and ports offer jobs and services, so they draw large populations.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.3] History: colonial ports and plantations (large farms for cash crops, set up by colonial powers) shaped where people settled.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.4] Government policy: policy can direct population into or out of particular areas.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.b] Low-density areas have few people per square kilometre. The reasons are mostly the opposite of the factors that attract people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.0] High, steep or cold land is difficult to farm, build on and reach.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.1] Deserts and dense forest offer little water, farmland or access.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.2] Poor soils and remoteness limit farming, and remote areas lack markets and services.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.3] Few jobs cause young people to leave, so the population falls and density drops.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.b] World population is very uneven. Most people crowd into a few regions, while large areas are almost empty.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.0] Dense: East Asia, South Asia, Europe, the north-eastern USA and the Nile valley, where farming, trade or industry support many people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.1] Sparse: the Sahara (a hot desert), the Amazon Basin (a vast rainforest), Siberia, northern Canada, Greenland and Antarctica.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.2] About 90% of the world's people live in the northern hemisphere.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.3] Most people live near coasts and rivers, where water and transport are easy to reach.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.b] Europe has the same unevenness on a smaller scale: a crowded core and a thinly settled edge.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.0] Dense core: the Benelux countries (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg), the Rhine-Ruhr (an industrial region of western Germany), south-east England, the Po valley (northern Italy) and the Paris region.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.1] Sparse periphery (the outer edge): northern Scandinavia, the Scottish Highlands and interior Spain.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.2] The Netherlands has over 500 people per km² (2020s), one of Europe's most densely populated countries.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.3] The EU average is about 110 people per km² (2020s), so the Netherlands is more than four times as dense.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.b] Ireland's people are not spread evenly. They gather around Dublin and the east coast, and thin out towards the west.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.0] The Greater Dublin Area (Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow) has about 2.1 million people, about 40% of the State's population (Census 2022).
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.1] Co. Dublin has about 1.46 million people at about 1,600 people per km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.2] The population is concentrated in the east and in the five cities, where jobs and services are found.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.3] Low density in the west and north-west, notably Mayo (about 25 people per km²), Leitrim and Roscommon, and in the uplands (high ground).
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.b] Today's eastern, urban pattern is not how Ireland always looked.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.0] Before the Famine (the Great Famine of 1845-49) the population was rural and weighted to the west.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.1] Since then the population has shifted to an eastern, urban pattern.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.2] Dublin's commuter counties of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow (counties whose people travel into Dublin to work) have grown rapidly.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.3] Every county's population grew between 2016 and 2022.
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.b] Density is population divided by area, so it rises when people are added to an area that does not grow.
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.0] The State's average density is about 73 people per km² (Census 2022).
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.1] Density is rising because the population has grown from natural increase (more births than deaths) and net immigration since the 1990s, while the land area is fixed.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.b] For most of history the world's population grew very slowly. Then growth took off, and it is only now easing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.0] World population grew slowly until about 1800, then rapidly, producing an exponential J-curve (a graph line that climbs ever more steeply, shaped like a J) driven by falling death rates. The growth rate is now slowing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.1] Milestones: about 1 billion around 1800, 2 billion in 1927, 3 billion in 1960 and 4 billion in 1974.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.2] Milestones continued: 5 billion in 1987, 6 billion in 1999, 7 billion in 2011 and 8 billion on 15 November 2022 (UN).
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.3] Global fertility is about 2.3 children per woman (early 2020s), down from about 5 in the 1950s.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.4] China and India each have over 1.4 billion people (2023); India overtook China as the most populous country in 2023 (UN estimate).
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.b] Growth is not even across the world. Some regions are growing fast while others have stopped growing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.0] Rapid growth: sub-Saharan Africa (the part of Africa south of the Sahara), where fertility is high.
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.1] Slow growth, stagnation (no growth) or decline: Europe and East Asia, where fertility is low.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.b] The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) shows how birth and death rates, and so population growth, change as a country develops. It has five stages, and stage 1 is where every society starts.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.0] Birth rate high, death rate high, and little growth. 'Stationary' means the population stays roughly the same size because births and deaths nearly cancel out.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.1] Example: no country is in stage 1 today; the best examples are pre-industrial societies (societies before factories and modern medicine).
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.b] In stage 2 the death rate drops quickly while births stay high, so the population expands fast. This stage is where the death rate falls most.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.0] Birth rate high; death rate falling rapidly, and the death rate falls most in this stage; growth is rapid because many more people are born than die.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.1] The death rate falls because of clean water and sanitation (safe disposal of sewage), vaccination, a better food supply and medical care.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.2] Example: Niger, because its death rate has fallen while its birth rate stays very high.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.b] In stage 3 the birth rate begins to fall too, so the gap between births and deaths narrows and growth slows.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.0] Birth rate falling; death rate low; growth slows because births are no longer far above deaths.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.1] Example: India, with a total fertility rate of about 2.0 (2019-21).
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.b] By stage 4 both rates are low. The population is large but steady, because births and deaths nearly balance.
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.0] Birth rate low, death rate low, and little growth.
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.1] Examples: Ireland and the USA. Ireland is in stage 4.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.b] Stage 5 goes beyond the original steady state: the birth rate drops below the death rate, so the population begins to shrink.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.0] Birth rate below death rate, so natural change is negative (natural decrease) and the population falls.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.1] Examples: Germany, Italy and Japan.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.b] Niger is the example of stage 2: very high fertility and a population dominated by children and teenagers.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.0] Niger is a West African, landlocked (no coastline) country, largely in the Sahel (the dry belt south of the Sahara) and Sahara.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.1] Population is about 27 million (2023), growing by over 3% a year (early 2020s).
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.2] Total fertility rate is about 6 children per woman (early 2020s), among the highest in the world.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.3] Median age (the age that splits the population into a younger half and an older half) is about 15, among the lowest in the world.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.4] Its population pyramid (a graph of the population by age and sex) has a very wide base, because so many children are born.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.5] Most people live in the south, where rainfall allows farming; the north is Sahara desert.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.b] Japan is the opposite of Niger. It has very few births and many older people, so its population is falling.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.0] Japan has the world's oldest population: about 29% of people are aged 65 or over (2023), the highest share in the world.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.1] Population peaked at about 128 million around 2008-2010, was about 124 million in 2024, and is falling.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.2] Total fertility rate is about 1.2 (2023), well below the replacement level of about 2.1.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.3] Few immigrants by European standards, so immigration does little to offset the fall.
[geo-population-migration-s1.21.4] Its population pyramid is top-heavy, with a narrow base (few children) and a wide top (many older people).
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.b] Moving from stage 2 to stage 3 means families choose to have fewer children. Several changes in society make smaller families sensible.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.0] Female education and paid work: women have later and fewer children.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.1] Contraception (methods of preventing pregnancy) is widely available, so couples can choose family size.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.2] Falling infant mortality removes the need for 'insurance' births (having extra children in case some die), as parents can expect their children to survive.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.3] Urbanisation (growth of towns and cities): children cost more and earn less in towns and cities, unlike on farms where they can work.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.4] Pensions replace children as old-age security, so parents no longer need many children to support them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.22.5] Later marriage, and weaker religious influence on family size.
[geo-population-migration-s1.23.b] A country with falling numbers and a rising share of older people faces economic strain. Governments have a few ways to respond.
[geo-population-migration-s1.23.0] Challenges: a shrinking workforce, labour shortages, rising pension and health costs, closing schools and rural depopulation (the loss of people from the countryside).
[geo-population-migration-s1.23.1] Responses: immigration, pro-natalist incentives (rewards that encourage couples to have more children) to raise birth rates, and later retirement.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.b] The DTM is a useful simplification, but it does not fit every country. Know why.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.0] The model is based on European experience, so it may not describe other regions.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.1] It ignores migration, although moving people can change a country's population sharply.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.2] Developing countries' death rates fell much faster than Europe's, because medicine was imported, so their growth has been faster.
[geo-population-migration-s1.24.3] Some countries may not complete the transition, and it has no fixed time scale.
[geo-population-migration-s1.25.b] A population pyramid is a graph that shows how a population is made up by age and sex. Planners and geographers read it to understand a country at a glance.
[geo-population-migration-s1.25.0] A population pyramid shows age cohorts (groups of people of similar age) in five-year bands, with males on the left and females on the right, as percentages or numbers.
[geo-population-migration-s1.25.1] Uses: planning services, forecasting future needs, comparing countries, and showing past events such as baby booms, wars and emigration.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.b] The overall shape of a pyramid tells you the stage a country has reached: how many children are born and how long people live.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.0] Wide base and narrow top: high fertility and short life expectancy, as in Niger.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.1] Straight sides: a stable population with low birth and death rates.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.2] Narrow base and wide top (top-heavy): an ageing population, as in Japan.
[geo-population-migration-s1.26.3] A developed country's pyramid is typically straight-sided or top-heavy, while a developing country's is wide-based.
[geo-population-migration-s1.27.b] Beyond the overall shape, bulges and notches in the sides record events in a country's past.
[geo-population-migration-s1.27.0] Bulges (a band wider than those around it) show baby booms or immigration of young adults.
[geo-population-migration-s1.27.1] Notches (a band narrower than those around it) show emigration, low birth years or war.
[geo-population-migration-s1.27.2] More women than men at the oldest ages, because women live longer.
[geo-population-migration-s1.28.b] Children and older people usually do not earn a wage, so they depend on the working-age population. The dependency ratio measures how heavy that load is.
[geo-population-migration-s1.28.0] Dependency ratio = (people aged 0-14 + people aged 65 and over) ÷ people aged 15-64 × 100.
[geo-population-migration-s1.28.1] Young dependency ratio = people aged 0-14 ÷ people aged 15-64 × 100. Old-age dependency ratio = people aged 65 and over ÷ people aged 15-64 × 100.
[geo-population-migration-s1.28.2] Illustrative example (invented figures): 30,000 aged 0-14, 10,000 aged 65 and over and 80,000 aged 15-64 give (30,000 + 10,000) ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 50.
[geo-population-migration-s1.29.b] The ratio is not the same everywhere inside a country. Migration within a country changes who lives in towns and who lives in the countryside.
[geo-population-migration-s1.29.0] Urban dependency ratios are usually lower than rural ones.
[geo-population-migration-s1.29.1] Young working adults migrate to cities for jobs and college, leaving older people, and sometimes children, in rural areas, which raises the share of dependants there.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.b] When the old-age dependency ratio (the number of people aged 65 and over for every 100 people aged 15-64) rises, there are more people to support and fewer workers to pay for it.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.0] Higher pension costs, because more people claim pensions for longer.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.1] More demand for health care, nursing homes and carers, because older people need more care.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.2] A smaller tax base (the pool of workers paying tax) from fewer workers.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.3] Pressure to raise the pension age, so that people work and pay tax for longer.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.4] Changing housing and transport needs, for example more homes suited to older people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.30.5] Planning responses in Ireland: home-care supports, pension reform, more geriatric services (health care for older people), encouraging immigration of workers, and age-friendly towns.
[geo-population-migration-s1.31.b] A country with many children has the opposite pressure: it must provide for the young now, but it gains workers later.
[geo-population-migration-s1.31.0] Demand for schools, maternity and child health care, and for future jobs and housing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.31.1] A future workforce, which is the basis of the demographic dividend (the economic boost a country can get when many young people grow up and start working). There is more on this in the overpopulation notes.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.b] Birth rates differ between countries because of economic and social conditions. These factors link income, culture and society.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.0] Income and economic development: birth rates fall as income rises.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.1] Status and education of women: educated women with status tend to have fewer children.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.2] Religion and culture, because beliefs and traditions shape ideas of a normal family size.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.3] Access to contraception, because it lets couples plan how many children to have.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.4] Government policy, for example rules or incentives that encourage or discourage births.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.5] Infant mortality: high infant mortality encourages larger families, because parents have more children to be sure some survive.
[geo-population-migration-s1.32.6] Urban or rural living: rural families tend to have more children.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.b] Death rates depend on how healthy and safe people's lives are. The main causes of death differ between rich and poor countries.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.0] Medical technology: vaccines and antibiotics (medicines that kill bacteria), which prevent or cure diseases that once killed many people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.1] Sanitation, diet, income and health services, because clean water, enough food and access to care all help people survive.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.2] Lifestyle diseases such as heart disease and cancer are the main causes of death in rich countries.
[geo-population-migration-s1.33.3] Conflict, famine and disease, such as malaria and HIV/AIDS, are the main causes of death in poor countries.
[geo-population-migration-s1.34.b] Fertility and mortality shape the pyramid directly: births fill the base and survival fills the top.
[geo-population-migration-s1.34.0] Falling fertility narrows the base of the pyramid, because fewer children are born.
[geo-population-migration-s1.34.1] Rising life expectancy widens the top of the pyramid, because more people survive into old age.
[geo-population-migration-s1.34.2] The result is an older population with a higher median age.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.b] Life expectancy is low where disease, poor water and poor nutrition kill people young. Each measure below tackles one of those causes.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.0] Clean water and sanitation, which stop water-borne disease.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.1] Vaccination, which prevents killer diseases.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.2] Mosquito nets and malaria treatment, because mosquitoes spread malaria.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.3] More doctors and clinics, so illness is treated sooner.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.4] Better nutrition, so people are stronger against disease.
[geo-population-migration-s1.35.5] Female education.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.b] Irish life expectancy is high because the causes of early death in poorer countries have been largely dealt with.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.0] A good diet and housing, which keep people healthy.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.1] Effective health services and vaccination, which treat and prevent illness.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.2] Clean water, so water-borne disease is rare.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.3] Fewer smokers, so fewer deaths from smoking-related disease.
[geo-population-migration-s1.36.4] Safer work, so fewer deaths from accidents.
[geo-population-migration-s1.37.b] All Irish census figures here are for the State (the 26-county area). Do not mix them with whole-island figures. For more than a century the population fell.
[geo-population-migration-s1.37.0] Census 1841: about 6.5 million, the starting point before the Famine.
[geo-population-migration-s1.37.1] Census 1851, after the Great Famine (1845-49): about 5.1 million, so the Famine's deaths and emigration cut the population sharply.
[geo-population-migration-s1.37.2] Census 1926: about 3.0 million, so the fall continued long after the Famine.
[geo-population-migration-s1.37.3] Census 1961: about 2.8 million, the lowest census population ever recorded.
[geo-population-migration-s1.37.4] The population fell at almost every census from 1841 to 1961; the only break was a very slight rise between Census 1946 and Census 1951.
[geo-population-migration-s1.38.b] From 1961 the trend reversed, and the population has grown with only one short pause.
[geo-population-migration-s1.38.0] Census 1971: about 3.0 million. Census 1981: about 3.4 million, so growth had clearly resumed.
[geo-population-migration-s1.38.1] Census 1986 and Census 1991: both about 3.5 million, a small fall between them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.38.2] Census 2002: about 3.9 million. Census 2006: about 4.2 million. Census 2011: about 4.6 million. Census 2016: about 4.76 million.
[geo-population-migration-s1.38.3] Census 2022: about 5.15 million, above 5 million for the first time since 1851.
[geo-population-migration-s1.39.b] Irish births stayed high for decades, then fell sharply. Even so, births still exceed deaths.
[geo-population-migration-s1.39.0] Fertility was high into the 1970s, with Catholic culture, large families and little access to contraception.
[geo-population-migration-s1.39.1] Births peaked at about 74,000 in 1980.
[geo-population-migration-s1.39.2] The total fertility rate was about 4 in the early 1970s, fell below replacement level around 1990, and was about 1.5 in 2023.
[geo-population-migration-s1.39.3] Births were about 55,000 in 2023 against about 35,000 deaths, giving natural increase of about 20,000 (55,000 minus 35,000).
[geo-population-migration-s1.40.b] Irish fertility fell because law, education, work and attitudes all changed. This is the core of the 30-mark question on how Ireland's population has changed since the 1950s.
[geo-population-migration-s1.40.0] Free secondary education, introduced in 1967 by Donogh O'Malley, and more women in third-level education.
[geo-population-migration-s1.40.1] The marriage bar, which forced women in the public service to resign on marriage, was removed in 1973.
[geo-population-migration-s1.40.2] Contraception was legalised by the Health (Family Planning) Act 1979, on prescription; access was widened in 1985 and the early 1990s.
