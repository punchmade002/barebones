TARGET geo-region-concept-s1: 1. Physical regions
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.b] Geographers divide the earth into regions so that places can be studied as groups instead of one by one. A region is an area of the earth's surface that shares one or more characteristics, called criteria, which make it different from the areas around it.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.0] Physical criteria: climate (the usual weather of a place), relief (the height and shape of the land) and rock type, such as limestone.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.1] Administrative criteria: the boundaries set by government, such as county lines.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.2] Cultural criteria: language and religion, which separate groups of people.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.3] Socio-economic criteria: wealth, employment and development (how advanced a place's economy and living standards are).
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.4] Urban criteria: a city and its hinterland (the surrounding area that depends on the city for services and jobs).
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.5] These five types of region are the five subsections of this chapter, and this subsection deals with physical regions.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.b] Regions can be big or small, and they can be defined by one criterion or by several. Their edges also differ: some are fixed by law, others fade gradually.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.0] Regions exist at different scales. A local region is a county or a city's hinterland, e.g. Co. Clare; a national region is the West of Ireland; a continental region is North West Europe; a global region is the Islamic world.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.1] A single index (one measurable criterion) defines a region by that one thing: rock type alone gives a karst region (a limestone landscape shaped by water dissolving the rock).
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.2] Multiple indices combine several criteria. For example, a peripheral region (one on the edge of a country's economic activity, far from its centre) can be defined by income, unemployment and out-migration (people leaving the area) together.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.3] Physical and cultural regions usually merge into their neighbours through transition zones (a gradual belt of change) rather than ending at a sharp line.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.4] Administrative regions have exact boundaries, fixed by law, so you can say precisely which side of the line a place lies on.
[geo-region-concept-s1.1.5] Regions also change over time as their criteria change.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.b] Climate is the usual pattern of weather in a place over many years. A climatic region is a large area with similar temperature, precipitation (rain, snow and other moisture falling from the sky) and seasonal pattern, usually with similar natural vegetation and soils.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.0] The world can be divided into climatic regions. This subsection studies the cool temperate oceanic type in detail.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.1] Cool temperate oceanic: Ireland and North West Europe, which is why Ireland is the case studied here.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.2] Warm temperate oceanic: the Mediterranean type, with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, so it differs from Ireland.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.3] Hot desert: e.g. the Sahara.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.b] The cool temperate oceanic climate is mild and wet, with no extremes of heat or cold. These are the features to describe, with the Irish figures (Met Éireann long-term averages).
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.0] Mild winters, because of the warm sea: mean January (coldest month) temperatures are about 4-7°C.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.1] Cool summers, so it is never hot: mean July (warmest month) temperatures are about 14-16°C.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.2] The annual range of temperature (the gap between the warmest and coldest month) is small, only about 10°C, and snow is rare on low ground.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.3] Rain falls in every month, so there is no dry season.
[geo-region-concept-s1.3.4] The weather is changeable, with prevailing (most frequent) south-westerly winds.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.b] Ireland's climate comes from several causes working together: where it sits on the globe, the ocean beside it and the winds that cross it. Each cause explains one feature of the climate.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.0] Latitude: Ireland lies between about 51.5°N and 55.5°N, in the mid-latitudes, where the sun's angle is moderate, so it is neither very hot nor very cold.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.1] North Atlantic Drift: a warm ocean current, the extension of the Gulf Stream, keeps winters mild and coasts free of ice.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.2] Maritime influence (the effect of being close to the sea): the sea heats and cools slowly, so the annual temperature range is small.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.3] Prevailing south-westerly winds blow off the Atlantic, carrying its mild, moist air over Ireland.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.4] Frequent Atlantic depressions (areas of low pressure that bring wind and rain) and their fronts (the boundaries between warm and cold air) bring rain. These weather systems are taught in the Weather and Climate chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s1.4.5] Relief rainfall: moist air is forced up the western mountains, cools and releases its rain on their western slopes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.b] The climate is not the same everywhere in Ireland. Because the wet south-westerly winds arrive from the Atlantic, the west gets the most rain and the east the least.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.0] The west is wetter than the east: about 1,000-1,250 mm of rain a year across much of the west, against about 750 mm in the driest parts of the east, around Dublin.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.1] Over 2,000 mm a year falls on many western mountains, because moist air is forced to rise over them.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.2] The east lies in a rain shadow (a drier area on the sheltered side of high ground), protected from the south-westerly winds by the western uplands.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.3] Wet days (days with measurable rain): about 150 a year on the east and south-east coasts, rising to about 225 a year in parts of the west.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.4] The south-east is the sunniest region, because it is sheltered from the wet south-westerlies.
[geo-region-concept-s1.5.5] The north is slightly cooler than the south, and coasts are milder in winter than the inland Midlands, because the sea warms them.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.b] North West Europe is the international setting for this climate, because the same conditions that affect Ireland affect its neighbours. Further east the climate changes step by step.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.0] The same climate covers Ireland, Britain, north-west France (Brittany), Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark and coastal Norway.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.1] All of these face the Atlantic, share the North Atlantic Drift and lie in the path of westerly depressions, which is why they have the same climate.
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.2] Moving east, away from the Atlantic, winters become colder, summers warmer, the annual range larger and rainfall lower, with a summer maximum (most rain falls in summer). This is the continental influence (the effect of being far from the sea).
[geo-region-concept-s1.6.3] The change is gradual, so the boundary of a climatic region is a transition zone, not a line.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.b] Climate shapes more than the weather. In a cool temperate oceanic region it also influences what grows naturally and how the land is farmed.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.0] Natural vegetation is deciduous forest (trees that lose their leaves in winter), mainly oak and ash.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.1] Soils are brown earth, the typical soil under deciduous forest.
[geo-region-concept-s1.7.2] Grass grows for most of the year, because winters are mild and rain falls in every month. This favours pastoral farming (rearing animals on grass) in Ireland, mainly cattle and dairying.
[geo-region-concept-s1.8.b] Geomorphology is the study of landforms (natural features of the land) and how they formed. A geomorphological region is an area with a distinctive landscape produced by the same rock type, geological structure (how rocks are arranged and folded) or landforming processes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.8.0] Three settings are studied here: the Burren (a karst region), Munster ridge and valley (South Munster) and the Northern European Plain.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.b] The Burren in north-west Co. Clare is a karst region on Carboniferous limestone, a rock that water slowly dissolves. Formation of its landforms is taught in the notes on Landforms associated with particular rock types; here each landform is only named.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.0] The name comes from the Irish Boireann, meaning rocky place.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.1] Limestone pavement (a bare, flat expanse of rock): blocks called clints, separated by fissures (cracks) called grikes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.2] Turloughs: seasonal lakes in hollows, which fill in winter and drain in summer.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.3] Caves: Aillwee Cave and Doolin Cave, which holds the Great Stalactite, about 7.3 m long.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.4] Mullaghmore: a hill of folded, terraced limestone, an example of how the rock forms stepped slopes.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.5] Very few surface rivers, because drainage is underground: water sinks through the cracks in the limestone.
[geo-region-concept-s1.9.6] Glaciation: granite erratics (boulders carried by ice and dropped far from their source) from Connemara rest on the limestone, showing that ice scraped off the soil and exposed the pavement.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.b] The limestone shapes life on the Burren as well as its landforms: it affects which plants grow, how farmers use the land and why visitors come.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.0] Plants from Arctic-alpine and Mediterranean environments grow side by side, for example the spring gentian and mountain avens.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.1] Winterage: cattle graze the upland limestone in winter, because the rock stores heat and the ground is dry.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.2] Poulnabrone, a Neolithic (New Stone Age) portal tomb (dolmen, a stone tomb of upright stones with a large capstone), stands on the limestone pavement.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.3] Burren National Park protects part of the area near Mullaghmore.
[geo-region-concept-s1.10.4] The Burren and Cliffs of Moher UNESCO Global Geopark (an area recognised internationally for its geological heritage): tourism is a major activity.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.b] South Munster is a region of Old Red Sandstone ridges alternating with Carboniferous limestone valleys. Its parallel pattern comes from folding of the rocks and then erosion.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.0] Armorican folding (a period of crustal folding) about 300 million years ago, on an east-west trend, produced anticlines (upfolds) and synclines (downfolds).
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.1] Differential erosion (different rocks wearing away at different rates) left the harder sandstone standing as ridges and wore the softer limestone down into valleys.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.2] Ridges (anticlines of sandstone): Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains, the Knockmealdown Mountains and the Caha Mountains.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.3] Valleys (synclines of limestone): the Blackwater valley and the Lee valley.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.4] Trellised drainage (a pattern of rivers following parallel valleys, with short links across them): rivers flow east along the valleys. The River Blackwater then turns sharply south at Cappoquin, Co. Waterford, and reaches the sea at Youghal. The Lee flows east to Cork Harbour.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.5] Drowned valleys at the coast, flooded by the sea, form rias (long, narrow inlets): Bantry Bay, Dingle Bay and the Kenmare River.
[geo-region-concept-s1.11.6] Human consequences: towns, routes and fertile farmland lie in the valleys, while the ridges carry rough grazing, forestry and bog.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.b] The Northern European Plain is a vast lowland, mostly low, flat or gently rolling, stretching from western France into Russia. Ice and wind shaped its surface, and its flat, fertile land supports many people.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.0] Countries crossed: France (north), Belgium, the Netherlands, northern Germany, Denmark and Poland, and into Russia.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.1] Glacial deposits (material left by ice sheets), including moraines (ridges of rock debris), outwash sands (sand washed out from melting ice) and lakes, shape the north of the plain.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.2] A belt of loess, a wind-blown silt, forms fertile soils along the southern edge.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.3] Loess soils are fertile, so they support crops such as wheat and sugar beet.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.4] The Rhine and Elbe flow north to the North Sea; the Oder and Vistula flow north to the Baltic, which shows the plain slopes gently north.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.5] Polders: land reclaimed from the sea in the Netherlands. The Afsluitdijk, completed in 1932, closed off the Zuiderzee.
[geo-region-concept-s1.12.6] Human consequences: fertile farmland, easy movement for transport because the land is flat, dense population and major cities, including Berlin, Warsaw, Hamburg and Amsterdam.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.b] Each of the three regions is defined by a different criterion, which shows how geomorphology can be used to define regions.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.0] The Burren is defined by its rock type, Carboniferous limestone, because that rock produces its karst landforms.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.1] Munster ridge and valley is defined by its geological structure, folded sandstone and limestone, because the folds gave alternating ridges and valleys.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.2] The Northern European Plain is defined by its relief and its surface deposits, glacial and loess, because it is flat and its surface is made of these materials.
[geo-region-concept-s1.13.3] The Wicklow Mountains and the Leinster batholith (a huge mass of granite) are granite uplands, also classed as geomorphological regions; their landforms are taught in the notes on Landforms associated with particular rock types.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s4: 4. Landforms associated with particular rock types
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.b] Karst is a landscape on limestone shaped mainly by solution (rock dissolving in water), with few surface streams and many underground features. The name comes from the Kras plateau of Slovenia. Limestone suits this because of its chemistry and the way water moves through it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.0] Limestone is calcium carbonate, which dissolves in weak acid. Carbonation: rainwater containing carbon dioxide forms weak carbonic acid, which reacts with the rock to form soluble calcium bicarbonate: CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 gives Ca(HCO3)2.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.1] Permeable rock lets water pass through it, mainly along cracks. Porous rock has tiny spaces between its grains that can hold water. Impermeable rock lets no water pass through.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.2] Limestone is permeable because water moves along its bedding planes (horizontal layers) and joints (vertical cracks), not through the solid rock itself.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.3] The ground water table is the upper surface of the zone where all cracks in the rock are saturated (completely full of water). Above it lies the zone of aeration (where cracks hold air). The table rises in wet weather and falls in dry weather.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.4] Percolation is the downward movement of water through soil and rock, which is how rainwater reaches the limestone's cracks.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.b] Limestone pavement is a bare, flat limestone surface. It exists because glacial ice scraped away the soil, leaving jointed rock open to the rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.0] Stage 1: ice removes the soil cover, exposing the jointed and bedded rock (rock with horizontal layers and vertical cracks) to rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.1] Stage 2: carbonation, the reaction of the rock with carbonic acid in rainwater, widens the joints into grikes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.2] Stage 3: the blocks left standing between the grikes are clints, because the rock there was not dissolved away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.3] Burren, Co. Clare: Ireland's best-known karst landscape, of Carboniferous limestone with extensive pavement of clints and grikes. Its name comes from the Irish 'Boireann', meaning rocky place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.4] Mullaghmore is a Burren hill with folded, terraced limestone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.b] A pavement is made of a few small features, and exam photographs ask you to name each one.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.0] Grikes: deep, narrow vertical cracks, because carbonation widens the rock along joints, where water collects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.1] Clints: flat-topped blocks of limestone between grikes, so together they look like paving.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.2] Karren (runnels): small grooves cut into clint surfaces because rainwater running across them slowly dissolves the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.3] Solution hollows (kamenitza or solution pans): small enclosed hollows on clint surfaces where rainwater collects and dissolves the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.4] Diagram: plan of clints and grikes; cross-section showing bedding planes and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.b] Once water can sink into limestone, the surface develops holes and hollows where streams vanish or the ground dissolves or collapses.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.0] Swallow hole (slugga): a surface stream flowing off impermeable rock reaches limestone and disappears underground down a hole widened by carbonation along a joint.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.1] Doline (sinkhole): a hollow formed by solution of the surface, or by collapse of a cave roof. A doline formed by cave collapse is still a doline.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.2] Uvala: several dolines merged into one larger hollow, as their edges dissolve or collapse into each other.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.3] Polje: a large, flat-floored depression that floods seasonally, for example when water cannot drain away fast enough. Poljes occur in Slovenia.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.4] Dry valley: a valley cut by a surface river when the ground was frozen (and so impermeable, letting water run over it) or the water table was higher. It now carries no stream.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.b] Water that sinks underground can also come back, and in Irish limestone the water table rising and falling makes lakes appear and vanish.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.0] Turlough: a seasonal lake in a hollow in limestone. It fills in winter when the water table rises and drains through the floor in summer.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.1] Turloughs are common in the Burren and east Co. Galway. Rahasane turlough, near Craughwell, Co. Galway, is one of the largest.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.2] River of resurgence: an underground river that re-emerges at the surface, usually where limestone meets impermeable rock below. It is the reverse of a swallow hole, which takes a surface stream underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.3] Diagram: cross-section from impermeable rock, through a swallow hole, underground to the resurgence.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.b] Below the surface, the same solution widens cracks in the limestone into hollow spaces that can be large enough to walk through.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.0] Caves and caverns: water dissolves passages along bedding planes and joints, and underground rivers enlarge them by erosion (wearing away rock). A cavern is a very large cave.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.1] Caves occur where limestone is dissolved, for example: Aillwee Cave, Co. Clare (Burren); Mitchelstown Cave, Co. Tipperary.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.2] Doolin Cave, Co. Clare, holds the Great Stalactite, about 7.3 m long, one of the longest free-hanging stalactites in the world.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.b] Cave water carries dissolved limestone, and when it drips it leaves some behind as solid calcite. Over time this builds the decorations seen in caves.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.0] Dripstone formation: water dripping from the roof carries dissolved calcium bicarbonate. When it loses carbon dioxide and evaporates, it deposits calcite (calcium carbonate).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.1] Stalactites hang from the roof and grow downward. Stalactite: C for ceiling.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.2] Stalagmites grow upward from the floor beneath, where the drips land, and are usually thicker and blunter. Stalagmite: G for ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.3] Pillars (columns) form where a stalactite and a stalagmite join, so the drip has built a solid link from roof to floor.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.4] Curtains are sheets of calcite formed where water trickles along a crack in the roof.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.5] Diagram: labelled cross-section of a cave showing stalactites, stalagmites, a pillar, a swallow hole and an underground river.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.7.b] A karst landscape develops through three stages.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.7.0] Youth: surface drainage begins to sink, so swallow holes form.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.7.1] Maturity: most drainage goes underground because the swallow holes now take it, with caves, dolines and uvalas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.7.2] Old age: cave roofs collapse because so much rock below has dissolved, the limestone is mostly removed, and isolated residual hills (left standing as the rest wears away) remain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.b] Ireland has three named karst settings in the syllabus. Besides the Burren, they show streams sinking into limestone and water travelling through it unseen.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.0] Marble Arch upland: the Marble Arch Caves lie on the slopes of Cuilcagh Mountain, Co. Fermanagh. They are part of the cross-border Marble Arch Caves Global Geopark with Co. Cavan.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.1] Streams flowing off the impermeable shale and sandstone of Cuilcagh sink into swallow holes where they reach the limestone, flow through the caves and re-emerge as the Cladagh River.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.2] The Marble Arch is a natural limestone arch over the Cladagh. The Shannon Pot, the source of the River Shannon, is on the slopes of Cuilcagh in Co. Cavan.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.3] Mask-Corrib lowland: water from Lough Mask flows underground through the limestone to Lough Corrib near Cong, Co. Mayo.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.4] The Cong Canal, built in the mid-1800s to link the two lakes, never held water because it leaked into the permeable limestone. It became known as the dry canal.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.9.b] Karst occurs wherever limestone is dissolved, and each international setting is known for a different feature.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.9.0] Slovenia: Postojna Cave and the Škocjan Caves, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Poljes occur here.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.9.1] South-west China: Guilin and the Li River have tower karst, steep-sided limestone hills. The South China Karst is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.9.2] Kentucky, USA: Mammoth Cave is the longest known cave system in the world.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.9.3] Jamaica: Cockpit Country has cockpit karst, steep conical hills separated by deep, star-shaped hollows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.b] Granite is hard, crystalline and resistant to erosion, and its landscapes reflect how it drains and weathers. Unlike limestone, it keeps water on the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.0] Granite is impermeable: water cannot enter it and runs off, so it has many surface streams and boggy ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.1] Granite is jointed (cracked). It weathers mainly by hydrolysis (a chemical reaction with water) of its feldspar, a mineral in granite, which forms clay.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.2] China clay (kaolin) is the clay that results. It is quarried at Lee Moor, on the south-west edge of Dartmoor.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.3] The Leinster batholith, the largest granite mass in Ireland and Britain, forms the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.4] Its rounded, dome-shaped uplands are worn down from rock that once covered the granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.5] Impermeable granite with high rainfall gives thin, acidic soils and blanket bog (a deep cover of wet peat), e.g. on the Sally Gap area of Co. Wicklow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.6] Ice cut deep valleys into the granite. There is more on this in Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms (Glaciation).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.b] Tors are the best-known granite landform. The main explanation is that they formed in two stages, with weathering underground first and stripping of the loose material afterwards.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.0] Tor: a block of granite standing on a hilltop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.1] Two-stage formation, put forward by D. L. Linton in 1955. Stage 1: under warm, wet conditions, deep chemical weathering (hydrolysis) attacked the granite along its joints, rotting it most where the joints were closest together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.2] Stage 2: the rotted material was later stripped away, e.g. by mass movement (downslope movement of material) under periglacial conditions (cold conditions beside ice), leaving solid, widely jointed blocks standing as tors.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.3] An alternative theory explains tors by freeze-thaw and solifluction alone. Solifluction is the slow flow of waterlogged soil down a slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.4] Clitter: blockfields of boulders on the slopes below tors, which is where the stripped blocks ended up.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.5] Dartmoor, Devon, England: a granite upland with tors such as Haytor and Hound Tor, clitter on its slopes and blanket bog.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.6] Diagram: cross-section showing the rotted zone along joints and the standing tor after stripping.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.b] Rock type changes across Ireland, and the landscape changes with it. Resistant rock stands up, weaker rock is worn low, and permeability decides whether water flows on the surface or underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.0] Ireland's saucer shape: a low central lowland of Carboniferous limestone is ringed by uplands of harder, more resistant rocks such as granite, quartzite and sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.1] Differential erosion (different rocks wearing away at different rates): resistant rock forms uplands, headlands and peaks, while weaker rock forms lowlands, valleys and bays.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.2] Quartzite peaks stand above the surrounding rock: Errigal, Co. Donegal; Croagh Patrick, Co. Mayo; the Great Sugar Loaf, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.3] Rock type and drainage: impermeable granite and shale give many surface streams and bog. Permeable limestone takes drainage underground, leaving few rivers and dry surfaces.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.4] Lough Corrib roughly divides the limestone lowland of east Co. Galway from the granite and metamorphic uplands of Connemara to the west.

CANDIDATES geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5: 5. Landforms from structures of deformation
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.b] Plate movement at boundaries (the lines where the Earth's crustal plates meet) puts the rock of the crust under stress, meaning a force that squeezes, stretches or drags it. Three kinds of stress occur, and each produces a different structure.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.0] Compression (pushing together) produces folds and reverse or thrust faults. It occurs where plates converge, meaning move towards each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.1] Tension (pulling apart) produces normal faults. It occurs where plates diverge, meaning move away from each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.2] Shearing (sliding past) produces tear, or strike-slip, faults. It occurs where plates slide past each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.3] Rock bends into folds when compressed slowly, especially when it is deep, hot and under pressure, because rock in those conditions can flex instead of snapping.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.4] Rock breaks, forming faults (fractures along which rock has moved), when stress is sudden or the rock is brittle, because brittle rock cannot bend without cracking.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.b] Folding is the first of the structures of deformation (changes to rock layers caused by stress). It starts with compression, and the folds that result have named parts you must be able to label.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.0] Compression is horizontal pressure that squeezes layers of rock into a shorter distance, so they buckle into folds. This is the short definition to give if asked.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.1] An anticline is an upfold: layers are pushed upwards into an arch. A helps you remember: A for apex.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.2] A syncline is a downfold: layers are pushed downwards into a trough (a U-shaped dip).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.3] The limbs are the sides of a fold; the axis is the line along its crest (the top of an anticline) or along the bottom of a trough.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.4] Diagram: label the anticline A and the syncline B.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.b] The more strongly rock is compressed, the more the fold is tilted and distorted. Five types are named, from the gentlest to the most extreme.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.0] Simple (symmetrical) fold: the two limbs dip at equal angles, so the fold looks the same on both sides.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.1] Asymmetrical fold: one limb is steeper than the other, because the compression is stronger from one side.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.2] Overfold: one limb is pushed over the other, because the compression is stronger still.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.3] Recumbent fold: the folded layers lie almost flat, because the fold has been pushed right over.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.4] Overthrust fold, or nappe: the fold breaks along a thrust fault (a low-angle fracture) and is pushed far forward over other rocks.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.5] Diagram: one cross-section sketch (a side-on slice through the rock) for each of the five types, in that order.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.b] Fold mountains are the large-scale landform made by folding. They form when two plates converge and the rock between them is crumpled upwards, so this is the answer to a question on how fold mountains are formed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.0] 1. Sediments (layers of mud, sand and other material laid down over time) are deposited in a sea lying between two converging plates.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.1] 2. The plates converge, and the sediments are compressed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.2] 3. The sediments are folded into anticlines and synclines.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.3] 4. Continued pressure produces overfolds, recumbent folds and nappes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.4] 5. The crust is thickened and uplifted into long, high ranges parallel to the plate edge.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.5] Diagram: before-and-after cross-sections of the sea floor and the resulting fold mountain.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.b] Fold mountains look different depending on their age, because weathering (the breakdown of rock at the surface) and erosion (the wearing away and carrying off of the broken material) lower them over time.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.0] Young fold mountains, such as the Alps, are high, steep and jagged because they have had little time to be worn down.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.1] Old fold mountains have been worn down by hundreds of millions of years of weathering and erosion into lower, rounded mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.2] Ireland's mountains are old fold mountains, formed by Caledonian folding (north-east to south-west trend: Donegal, Mayo, Wicklow) and Armorican folding (east to west trend: Munster).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.3] Other young fold ranges include the Himalayas, the Andes, the Rockies and the Atlas Mountains of north Africa.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.5.b] Different rocks wear away at different speeds, which is called differential erosion. In folded land this decides which rocks end up as high ground and which as low ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.5.0] The landform depends on which rocks are exposed by erosion, because hard rock resists wearing away and soft rock does not.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.5.1] In Munster, resistant sandstone survives on the anticlines as ridges.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.5.2] Weaker limestone survives in the synclines as valleys.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.6.b] Drainage means the pattern of rivers in an area. Folded rock shapes it because rivers take the easiest route.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.6.0] Rivers follow the weak rock along the synclines, because weak rock is easiest to cut into.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.6.1] Where a river breaks through a ridge, it cuts across it, giving trellised drainage (a pattern of main rivers running parallel with tributaries joining at right angles) and sharp right-angled bends.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.b] Armorican folding produced a landscape of east to west ridges and valleys in Munster, the southern province of Ireland. It shows differential erosion and folded drainage in one place.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.0] Ridges of Old Red Sandstone include Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains, the Knockmealdown Mountains and the Caha Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.1] Valleys of Carboniferous limestone include the Blackwater valley and the Lee valley.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.2] The River Blackwater flows east along a limestone valley, then turns sharply south at Cappoquin, Co. Waterford, to reach the sea at Youghal.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.3] The River Lee flows east to Cork Harbour, following its limestone valley, so it too runs along the folds.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.4] The coastal ends of the valleys in Co. Cork and Kerry were drowned by the sea to form rias (long inlets): Bantry Bay, Dingle Bay and the Kenmare River.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.8.b] Armorican folding was not equally strong everywhere. The Armorican front marks where it changes from strong to gentle.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.8.0] The Armorican front is the northern limit of strong Armorican folding, running east to west across Munster.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.8.1] South of the front, rocks were intensely folded and pushed, or thrust, northwards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.8.2] North of the front, folding is gentle, so the rocks there are less crumpled.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.b] The Alps are young fold mountains, high and jagged, formed by Alpine folding. Recumbent folds and nappes, two of the most strongly compressed fold types, are characteristic of them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.0] Alpine folding resulted from the collision of the African plate with the Eurasian plate.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.1] Recumbent folds and nappes are characteristic of the Alps, which fits the very strong compression there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.2] Mont Blanc is the highest peak of the Alps.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.10.b] The Appalachians show what old fold mountains look like. They link back to Ireland's own mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.10.0] The Appalachian Mountains of eastern North America are old fold mountains, so they are worn down rather than steep.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.10.1] They were folded in the same mountain-building episodes as Ireland's older mountains, when the two continents were joined.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.10.2] The Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians show long, parallel folded ridges and valleys, with trellised drainage.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.b] Doming is a different kind of upward bending. Instead of a long ridge of folds, the rock is pushed up into a single rounded shape.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.0] Doming pushes rock layers upwards from below into a circular or oval upfold, by compression or by rising magma (molten rock from underground).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.1] Erosion removes the top of the dome first, exposing older rocks in the centre, because the oldest layers lie at the bottom.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.2] Resistant layers form rings of escarpments (long steep slopes) facing inwards towards the centre.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.3] Drainage flows outwards from the centre in a radial pattern, like spokes of a wheel, because the ground slopes away from the middle.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.4] Example: the Lake District, England, with rivers and lakes radiating outwards from the centre.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.b] The Weald is the worked example of an eroded dome. It is a dome, not a basin, and its escarpments face inwards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.0] Rock layers in Kent, Surrey and Sussex were pushed up into a dome, an elongated anticline (an upfold stretched in one direction).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.1] Erosion removed the chalk from the top, exposing older sandstones and clays.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.2] The chalk now forms the North Downs and the South Downs, two escarpments that face inwards towards the Weald.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.3] Clay vales (low, wide valleys) lie inside the Downs, and sandstone forms the High Weald in the centre.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.13.b] Where rock is too brittle to bend, it breaks instead. A break along which the rock moves is a fault, and the way the rock moves is called displacement.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.13.0] A fault is a fracture in the crust along which rocks have moved.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.13.1] Displacement may be vertical (rocks move up or down), horizontal (rocks move sideways), or both.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.b] A normal fault is the fault produced when the crust is pulled apart.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.0] Tension pulls the crust apart.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.1] One block slides down the fault plane (the sloping surface of the fracture) relative to the other, so the crust is lengthened.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.2] Diagram: a block sliding down along a fault plane, marked as a normal fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.b] A reverse fault is the opposite of a normal fault. Compression squeezes the crust, so the movement along the fault is reversed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.0] Compression pushes one block up the fault plane over the other, so the crust is shortened.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.1] A thrust fault is a low-angle reverse fault that can push rocks a long way forward, linked to nappes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.2] Diagram: a block pushed up and over the other along a fault plane, marked as a reverse fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.b] In a tear fault the rock moves sideways, not up or down. Shearing, where blocks are dragged past each other, causes it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.0] Shearing makes blocks move horizontally past each other along a tear fault, so there is little up or down movement.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.1] A transform fault is a tear fault at a plate boundary, so it is where plates slide past each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.2] Examples of transform faults: the San Andreas Fault, California; the North and East Anatolian Faults, Turkey.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.b] The rock along a tear fault is crushed and weakened. Erosion works on it, and the sideways movement shifts anything that crosses the fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.0] The shattered rock of the fault zone is eroded into a straight valley, because crushed rock wears away easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.1] Ridges and rivers are offset sideways across the fault, because the blocks have moved horizontally.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.2] Example: the Great Glen Fault, Scotland, where Loch Ness lies in the straight valley eroded along it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.b] Ireland has its own tear faults, in Donegal. They show how a fault zone becomes a straight valley.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.0] The Donegal tear faults run north-east to south-west.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.1] Rivers and glaciers have eroded long, straight valleys along them, because the broken rock of the fault zone is easier to erode.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.2] The Gweebarra Fault and the Leannan Fault are two of them; the Gweebarra River valley follows the Gweebarra Fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.b] Faulting produces landforms when one block ends up higher than its neighbour. Exams ask for two of these landforms by name.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.0] A fault scarp is a steep slope or cliff where one block has been raised above the other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.1] A horst (block mountain) is a block raised between two parallel faults, or left standing as the land on either side sinks.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.2] A graben, or rift valley, is a block that sinks between two parallel normal faults, forming a long, flat-floored valley with steep walls, often holding lakes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.3] Diagram: a fault scarp as a steep cliff between two blocks at different heights.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.b] A horst and a graben form together, side by side, when tension stretches the crust. Both come from the same set of normal faults.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.0] 1. Tension stretches the crust, producing normal faults on either side of a block.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.1] 2. The central block sinks between the faults, forming a graben.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.2] 3. The blocks on either side stand higher, forming horsts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.3] Diagram: horst and graben shown together in one cross-section, with the normal faults labelled.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.b] Three real examples show a graben, with or without its neighbouring horsts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.0] The Rhine rift valley, a graben, lies between the Vosges, France, and the Black Forest, Germany, which are horsts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.1] The East African Rift Valley includes a graben in which Lake Tanganyika lies.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.2] The Central Lowlands of Scotland are a rift valley between the Highland Boundary Fault and the Southern Uplands Fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.22.b] Faults affect the landscape beyond the landforms they build directly.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.22.0] Faults help to guide the lines of coastlines and drainage.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.22.1] Earthquakes occur along active faults, meaning faults where the rock is still moving.
