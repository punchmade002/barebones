CANDIDATES geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5: 5. Landforms from structures of deformation
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.b] Plate movement at boundaries (the lines where the Earth's crustal plates meet) puts the rock of the crust under stress, meaning a force that squeezes, stretches or drags it. Three kinds of stress occur, and each produces a different structure.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.0] Compression (pushing together) produces folds and reverse or thrust faults. It occurs where plates converge, meaning move towards each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.1] Tension (pulling apart) produces normal faults. It occurs where plates diverge, meaning move away from each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.2] Shearing (sliding past) produces tear, or strike-slip, faults. It occurs where plates slide past each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.3] Rock bends into folds when compressed slowly, especially when it is deep, hot and under pressure, because rock in those conditions can flex instead of snapping.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.4] Rock breaks, forming faults (fractures along which rock has moved), when stress is sudden or the rock is brittle, because brittle rock cannot bend without cracking.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.b] Folding is the first of the structures of deformation (changes to rock layers caused by stress). It starts with compression, and the folds that result have named parts you must be able to label.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.0] Compression is horizontal pressure that squeezes layers of rock into a shorter distance, so they buckle into folds. This is the short definition to give if asked.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.1] An anticline is an upfold: layers are pushed upwards into an arch. A helps you remember: A for apex.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.2] A syncline is a downfold: layers are pushed downwards into a trough (a U-shaped dip).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.3] The limbs are the sides of a fold; the axis is the line along its crest (the top of an anticline) or along the bottom of a trough.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.4] Diagram: label the anticline A and the syncline B.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.b] The more strongly rock is compressed, the more the fold is tilted and distorted. Five types are named, from the gentlest to the most extreme.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.0] Simple (symmetrical) fold: the two limbs dip at equal angles, so the fold looks the same on both sides.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.1] Asymmetrical fold: one limb is steeper than the other, because the compression is stronger from one side.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.2] Overfold: one limb is pushed over the other, because the compression is stronger still.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.3] Recumbent fold: the folded layers lie almost flat, because the fold has been pushed right over.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.4] Overthrust fold, or nappe: the fold breaks along a thrust fault (a low-angle fracture) and is pushed far forward over other rocks.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.5] Diagram: one cross-section sketch (a side-on slice through the rock) for each of the five types, in that order.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.b] Fold mountains are the large-scale landform made by folding. They form when two plates converge and the rock between them is crumpled upwards, so this is the answer to a question on how fold mountains are formed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.0] 1. Sediments (layers of mud, sand and other material laid down over time) are deposited in a sea lying between two converging plates.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.1] 2. The plates converge, and the sediments are compressed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.2] 3. The sediments are folded into anticlines and synclines.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.3] 4. Continued pressure produces overfolds, recumbent folds and nappes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.4] 5. The crust is thickened and uplifted into long, high ranges parallel to the plate edge.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.5] Diagram: before-and-after cross-sections of the sea floor and the resulting fold mountain.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.b] Fold mountains look different depending on their age, because weathering (the breakdown of rock at the surface) and erosion (the wearing away and carrying off of the broken material) lower them over time.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.0] Young fold mountains, such as the Alps, are high, steep and jagged because they have had little time to be worn down.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.1] Old fold mountains have been worn down by hundreds of millions of years of weathering and erosion into lower, rounded mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.2] Ireland's mountains are old fold mountains, formed by Caledonian folding (north-east to south-west trend: Donegal, Mayo, Wicklow) and Armorican folding (east to west trend: Munster).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.3] Other young fold ranges include the Himalayas, the Andes, the Rockies and the Atlas Mountains of north Africa.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.5.b] Different rocks wear away at different speeds, which is called differential erosion. In folded land this decides which rocks end up as high ground and which as low ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.5.0] The landform depends on which rocks are exposed by erosion, because hard rock resists wearing away and soft rock does not.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.5.1] In Munster, resistant sandstone survives on the anticlines as ridges.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.5.2] Weaker limestone survives in the synclines as valleys.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.6.b] Drainage means the pattern of rivers in an area. Folded rock shapes it because rivers take the easiest route.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.6.0] Rivers follow the weak rock along the synclines, because weak rock is easiest to cut into.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.6.1] Where a river breaks through a ridge, it cuts across it, giving trellised drainage (a pattern of main rivers running parallel with tributaries joining at right angles) and sharp right-angled bends.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.b] Armorican folding produced a landscape of east to west ridges and valleys in Munster, the southern province of Ireland. It shows differential erosion and folded drainage in one place.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.0] Ridges of Old Red Sandstone include Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains, the Knockmealdown Mountains and the Caha Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.1] Valleys of Carboniferous limestone include the Blackwater valley and the Lee valley.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.2] The River Blackwater flows east along a limestone valley, then turns sharply south at Cappoquin, Co. Waterford, to reach the sea at Youghal.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.3] The River Lee flows east to Cork Harbour, following its limestone valley, so it too runs along the folds.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.4] The coastal ends of the valleys in Co. Cork and Kerry were drowned by the sea to form rias (long inlets): Bantry Bay, Dingle Bay and the Kenmare River.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.8.b] Armorican folding was not equally strong everywhere. The Armorican front marks where it changes from strong to gentle.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.8.0] The Armorican front is the northern limit of strong Armorican folding, running east to west across Munster.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.8.1] South of the front, rocks were intensely folded and pushed, or thrust, northwards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.8.2] North of the front, folding is gentle, so the rocks there are less crumpled.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.b] The Alps are young fold mountains, high and jagged, formed by Alpine folding. Recumbent folds and nappes, two of the most strongly compressed fold types, are characteristic of them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.0] Alpine folding resulted from the collision of the African plate with the Eurasian plate.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.1] Recumbent folds and nappes are characteristic of the Alps, which fits the very strong compression there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.2] Mont Blanc is the highest peak of the Alps.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.10.b] The Appalachians show what old fold mountains look like. They link back to Ireland's own mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.10.0] The Appalachian Mountains of eastern North America are old fold mountains, so they are worn down rather than steep.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.10.1] They were folded in the same mountain-building episodes as Ireland's older mountains, when the two continents were joined.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.10.2] The Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians show long, parallel folded ridges and valleys, with trellised drainage.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.b] Doming is a different kind of upward bending. Instead of a long ridge of folds, the rock is pushed up into a single rounded shape.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.0] Doming pushes rock layers upwards from below into a circular or oval upfold, by compression or by rising magma (molten rock from underground).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.1] Erosion removes the top of the dome first, exposing older rocks in the centre, because the oldest layers lie at the bottom.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.2] Resistant layers form rings of escarpments (long steep slopes) facing inwards towards the centre.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.3] Drainage flows outwards from the centre in a radial pattern, like spokes of a wheel, because the ground slopes away from the middle.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.4] Example: the Lake District, England, with rivers and lakes radiating outwards from the centre.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.b] The Weald is the worked example of an eroded dome. It is a dome, not a basin, and its escarpments face inwards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.0] Rock layers in Kent, Surrey and Sussex were pushed up into a dome, an elongated anticline (an upfold stretched in one direction).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.1] Erosion removed the chalk from the top, exposing older sandstones and clays.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.2] The chalk now forms the North Downs and the South Downs, two escarpments that face inwards towards the Weald.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.3] Clay vales (low, wide valleys) lie inside the Downs, and sandstone forms the High Weald in the centre.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.13.b] Where rock is too brittle to bend, it breaks instead. A break along which the rock moves is a fault, and the way the rock moves is called displacement.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.13.0] A fault is a fracture in the crust along which rocks have moved.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.13.1] Displacement may be vertical (rocks move up or down), horizontal (rocks move sideways), or both.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.b] A normal fault is the fault produced when the crust is pulled apart.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.0] Tension pulls the crust apart.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.1] One block slides down the fault plane (the sloping surface of the fracture) relative to the other, so the crust is lengthened.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.2] Diagram: a block sliding down along a fault plane, marked as a normal fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.b] A reverse fault is the opposite of a normal fault. Compression squeezes the crust, so the movement along the fault is reversed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.0] Compression pushes one block up the fault plane over the other, so the crust is shortened.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.1] A thrust fault is a low-angle reverse fault that can push rocks a long way forward, linked to nappes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.2] Diagram: a block pushed up and over the other along a fault plane, marked as a reverse fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.b] In a tear fault the rock moves sideways, not up or down. Shearing, where blocks are dragged past each other, causes it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.0] Shearing makes blocks move horizontally past each other along a tear fault, so there is little up or down movement.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.1] A transform fault is a tear fault at a plate boundary, so it is where plates slide past each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.2] Examples of transform faults: the San Andreas Fault, California; the North and East Anatolian Faults, Turkey.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.b] The rock along a tear fault is crushed and weakened. Erosion works on it, and the sideways movement shifts anything that crosses the fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.0] The shattered rock of the fault zone is eroded into a straight valley, because crushed rock wears away easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.1] Ridges and rivers are offset sideways across the fault, because the blocks have moved horizontally.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.2] Example: the Great Glen Fault, Scotland, where Loch Ness lies in the straight valley eroded along it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.b] Ireland has its own tear faults, in Donegal. They show how a fault zone becomes a straight valley.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.0] The Donegal tear faults run north-east to south-west.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.1] Rivers and glaciers have eroded long, straight valleys along them, because the broken rock of the fault zone is easier to erode.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.2] The Gweebarra Fault and the Leannan Fault are two of them; the Gweebarra River valley follows the Gweebarra Fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.b] Faulting produces landforms when one block ends up higher than its neighbour. Exams ask for two of these landforms by name.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.0] A fault scarp is a steep slope or cliff where one block has been raised above the other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.1] A horst (block mountain) is a block raised between two parallel faults, or left standing as the land on either side sinks.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.2] A graben, or rift valley, is a block that sinks between two parallel normal faults, forming a long, flat-floored valley with steep walls, often holding lakes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.3] Diagram: a fault scarp as a steep cliff between two blocks at different heights.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.b] A horst and a graben form together, side by side, when tension stretches the crust. Both come from the same set of normal faults.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.0] 1. Tension stretches the crust, producing normal faults on either side of a block.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.1] 2. The central block sinks between the faults, forming a graben.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.2] 3. The blocks on either side stand higher, forming horsts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.3] Diagram: horst and graben shown together in one cross-section, with the normal faults labelled.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.b] Three real examples show a graben, with or without its neighbouring horsts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.0] The Rhine rift valley, a graben, lies between the Vosges, France, and the Black Forest, Germany, which are horsts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.1] The East African Rift Valley includes a graben in which Lake Tanganyika lies.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.2] The Central Lowlands of Scotland are a rift valley between the Highland Boundary Fault and the Southern Uplands Fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.22.b] Faults affect the landscape beyond the landforms they build directly.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.22.0] Faults help to guide the lines of coastlines and drainage.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.22.1] Earthquakes occur along active faults, meaning faults where the rock is still moving.

CANDIDATES geo-population-migration-s1: 1. Changing population characteristics
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.b] Geographers describe a population with a small set of measures. Learn each definition exactly, because every later block uses them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.0] Crude birth rate: live births per 1,000 people per year. It is 'crude' because it counts the whole population, not just women of childbearing age.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.1] Crude death rate: deaths per 1,000 people per year.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.2] Natural change: birth rate minus death rate. A positive result is natural increase (more births than deaths); a negative result is natural decrease (more deaths than births).
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.3] Net migration: immigrants (people moving in) minus emigrants (people moving out). It is positive when more people arrive than leave.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.4] Total fertility rate: the average number of children per woman, which shows family size better than the crude birth rate.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.5] Replacement level: a total fertility rate of about 2.1 children per woman in developed countries, the level at which a population replaces itself without migration.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.6] Infant mortality rate: deaths of children under one year old per 1,000 live births.
[geo-population-migration-s1.0.7] Life expectancy: the average number of years a newborn is expected to live.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.b] A population can only change in two ways: people are born or die, and people move in or out. The census, a regular count of everyone, shows which of the two drove the change.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.0] Population change equals natural change plus net migration. In any period between two censuses (an inter-censal period), the larger of the two components explains the growth or decline.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.1] The census is a count of every person on one night. In Ireland it is held every five years and run by the Central Statistics Office (CSO).
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.2] The Irish census records age, sex, place of residence, birthplace, nationality, religion, ethnicity, Irish language, housing, work and commuting.
[geo-population-migration-s1.1.3] Census 2016 to Census 2022: the population grew by about 360,000 (about 7.6%). Natural increase contributed about 170,000 and net migration about 190,000, so net migration was the slightly larger component.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.b] The five-year rhythm of the Irish census has been broken a few times. Each break had a specific cause.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.0] The 1926 census was the first held by the Irish Free State (the Irish State of that time).
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.1] The 1976 census was cancelled; a special census was held in 1979 instead.
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.2] The 2001 census was postponed to 2002 by foot-and-mouth disease (an animal disease outbreak).
[geo-population-migration-s1.2.3] The 2021 census was postponed to 2022 by COVID-19 and held on 3 April 2022.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.b] Governments collect census data because it tells them how many people there are, how old they are and where they live. That information shapes decisions on money and services.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.0] Planning schools, hospitals and housing: counts of children, patients and households show where services are needed.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.1] Pensions: the age structure (how many people are in each age group) sets the cost of pensions; the State pension age has been 66 since 2014.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.2] Transport and infrastructure: population size and location show where roads, rail and water services are needed, so money goes where demand is.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.3] Constituency boundaries: the population of each constituency (the area that elects TDs, members of the Dail) determines the number of TDs it elects.
[geo-population-migration-s1.3.4] Funding and workforce planning: funding is allocated to regions by population, and population forecasts show future labour supply and the need for workers from abroad.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.b] Two ideas describe where people live. They are different, so keep them apart: distribution is where people live. Density is the number of people per square kilometre of land.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.0] Distribution is the spread of people across an area, so a map can show crowded and empty places. Density turns that into a number you can compare.
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.1] Density = population ÷ area in km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.4.2] Illustrative example (invented figures): a region of 400 km² with 120,000 people has a density of 120,000 ÷ 400 = 300 people per km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.b] Physical factors are features of the natural environment. They decide how easy it is to farm, build and live in a place.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.0] Relief (the shape and height of the land): lowlands attract more people than high mountains, which are costly to farm and build on.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.1] Climate: temperate climates (mild, without extremes) support dense settlement; places that are too hot, too cold or too dry have fewer people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.2] Soils and water supply: fertile soils and reliable water support farming and settlement.
[geo-population-migration-s1.5.3] Natural resources: coalfields drew industrial populations in the 19th century, because mines and factories needed workers close by.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.b] Human factors are choices and developments made by people. They work alongside the physical factors to decide where populations cluster.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.0] Economic opportunity: jobs attract people, and regions with few jobs lose their young people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.1] Transport routes: roads, rail lines and navigable water (rivers deep enough for boats) attract settlement along them.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.2] Cities and ports: cities and ports offer jobs and services, so they draw large populations.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.3] History: colonial ports and plantations (large farms for cash crops, set up by colonial powers) shaped where people settled.
[geo-population-migration-s1.6.4] Government policy: policy can direct population into or out of particular areas.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.b] Low-density areas have few people per square kilometre. The reasons are mostly the opposite of the factors that attract people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.0] High, steep or cold land is difficult to farm, build on and reach.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.1] Deserts and dense forest offer little water, farmland or access.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.2] Poor soils and remoteness limit farming, and remote areas lack markets and services.
[geo-population-migration-s1.7.3] Few jobs cause young people to leave, so the population falls and density drops.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.b] World population is very uneven. Most people crowd into a few regions, while large areas are almost empty.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.0] Dense: East Asia, South Asia, Europe, the north-eastern USA and the Nile valley, where farming, trade or industry support many people.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.1] Sparse: the Sahara (a hot desert), the Amazon Basin (a vast rainforest), Siberia, northern Canada, Greenland and Antarctica.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.2] About 90% of the world's people live in the northern hemisphere.
[geo-population-migration-s1.8.3] Most people live near coasts and rivers, where water and transport are easy to reach.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.b] Europe has the same unevenness on a smaller scale: a crowded core and a thinly settled edge.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.0] Dense core: the Benelux countries (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg), the Rhine-Ruhr (an industrial region of western Germany), south-east England, the Po valley (northern Italy) and the Paris region.
[geo-population-migration-s1.9.1] Sparse periphery (the outer edge): northern Scandinavia, the Scottish Highlands and interior Spain.

[geo-population-migration-s1.9.3] The EU average is about 110 people per km² (2020s), so the Netherlands is more than four times as dense.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.b] Ireland's people are not spread evenly. They gather around Dublin and the east coast, and thin out towards the west.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.0] The Greater Dublin Area (Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow) has about 2.1 million people, about 40% of the State's population (Census 2022).
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.1] Co. Dublin has about 1.46 million people at about 1,600 people per km².
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.2] The population is concentrated in the east and in the five cities, where jobs and services are found.
[geo-population-migration-s1.10.3] Low density in the west and north-west, notably Mayo (about 25 people per km²), Leitrim and Roscommon, and in the uplands (high ground).
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.b] Today's eastern, urban pattern is not how Ireland always looked.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.0] Before the Famine (the Great Famine of 1845-49) the population was rural and weighted to the west.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.1] Since then the population has shifted to an eastern, urban pattern.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.2] Dublin's commuter counties of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow (counties whose people travel into Dublin to work) have grown rapidly.
[geo-population-migration-s1.11.3] Every county's population grew between 2016 and 2022.
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.b] Density is population divided by area, so it rises when people are added to an area that does not grow.
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.0] The State's average density is about 73 people per km² (Census 2022).
[geo-population-migration-s1.12.1] Density is rising because the population has grown from natural increase (more births than deaths) and net immigration since the 1990s, while the land area is fixed.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.b] For most of history the world's population grew very slowly. Then growth took off, and it is only now easing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.0] World population grew slowly until about 1800, then rapidly, producing an exponential J-curve (a graph line that climbs ever more steeply, shaped like a J) driven by falling death rates. The growth rate is now slowing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.1] Milestones: about 1 billion around 1800, 2 billion in 1927, 3 billion in 1960 and 4 billion in 1974.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.2] Milestones continued: 5 billion in 1987, 6 billion in 1999, 7 billion in 2011 and 8 billion on 15 November 2022 (UN).
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.3] Global fertility is about 2.3 children per woman (early 2020s), down from about 5 in the 1950s.
[geo-population-migration-s1.13.4] China and India each have over 1.4 billion people (2023); India overtook China as the most populous country in 2023 (UN estimate).
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.b] Growth is not even across the world. Some regions are growing fast while others have stopped growing.
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.0] Rapid growth: sub-Saharan Africa (the part of Africa south of the Sahara), where fertility is high.
[geo-population-migration-s1.14.1] Slow growth, stagnation (no growth) or decline: Europe and East Asia, where fertility is low.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.b] The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) shows how birth and death rates, and so population growth, change as a country develops. It has five stages, and stage 1 is where every society starts.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.0] Birth rate high, death rate high, and little growth. 'Stationary' means the population stays roughly the same size because births and deaths nearly cancel out.
[geo-population-migration-s1.15.1] Example: no country is in stage 1 today; the best examples are pre-industrial societies (societies before factories and modern medicine).
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.b] In stage 2 the death rate drops quickly while births stay high, so the population expands fast. This stage is where the death rate falls most.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.0] Birth rate high; death rate falling rapidly, and the death rate falls most in this stage; growth is rapid because many more people are born than die.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.1] The death rate falls because of clean water and sanitation (safe disposal of sewage), vaccination, a better food supply and medical care.
[geo-population-migration-s1.16.2] Example: Niger, because its death rate has fallen while its birth rate stays very high.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.b] In stage 3 the birth rate begins to fall too, so the gap between births and deaths narrows and growth slows.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.0] Birth rate falling; death rate low; growth slows because births are no longer far above deaths.
[geo-population-migration-s1.17.1] Example: India, with a total fertility rate of about 2.0 (2019-21).
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.b] By stage 4 both rates are low. The population is large but steady, because births and deaths nearly balance.
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.0] Birth rate low, death rate low, and little growth.
[geo-population-migration-s1.18.1] Examples: Ireland and the USA. Ireland is in stage 4.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.b] Stage 5 goes beyond the original steady state: the birth rate drops below the death rate, so the population begins to shrink.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.0] Birth rate below death rate, so natural change is negative (natural decrease) and the population falls.
[geo-population-migration-s1.19.1] Examples: Germany, Italy and Japan.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.b] Niger is the example of stage 2: very high fertility and a population dominated by children and teenagers.
[geo-population-migration-s1.20.0] Niger is a West African, landlocked (no coastline) country, largely in the Sahel (the dry belt south of the Sahara) and Sahara.
