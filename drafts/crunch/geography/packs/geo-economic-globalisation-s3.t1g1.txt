TARGET geo-economic-globalisation-s3: 3. Patterns in world trade
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.b] Trade is the buying and selling of goods and services between countries. Exports leave a country and imports arrive in it, and data questions use these words constantly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.0] Visible trade is goods that can be seen and counted, such as cars, food and computer chips.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.1] Invisible trade is services, which cannot be held or counted in the same way, such as software, financial services, tourism and aircraft leasing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.2] Balance of trade: the value of exports minus the value of imports, which gives one number that shows whether a country sells more than it buys.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.3] A trade surplus means exports are greater than imports; a trade deficit means imports are greater than exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.b] The balance of trade is only part of the picture of a country's dealings with the world. The balance of payments is the wider record.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.0] Balance of payments: the record of all money flows between a country and the rest of the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.1] It includes trade in goods and services, plus investment flows (money put into businesses abroad), profits sent home, borrowing and transfers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.2] It is a wider measure than the balance of trade, which records only exports and imports, so the two are not the same thing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.b] A country that keeps buying more from abroad than it sells pays a price at home, while exports bring benefits. Each effect follows from where the money goes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.0] A persistent trade deficit (imports greater than exports year after year) means money flows out of the country to pay for the extra imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.1] The country may have to borrow or sell assets (things it owns, such as businesses or property) to pay for those imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.2] Home industries and jobs can be lost as cheaper imports replace domestic production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.3] The currency (the country's money) may weaken when a country persistently imports more than it exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.4] Exports bring jobs, tax revenue, foreign earnings and economic growth, because foreign buyers pay money into the country.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.b] A country that sells to many different countries is safer than one that sells mainly to one. This is the advantage of many export partners.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.0] A country with many export partners is less dependent on any one market.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.1] A recession (a fall in economic activity), tariff or crisis in one country, such as the US tariffs imposed in 2025, then does less damage to its overall trade, because the other markets keep buying.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.b] Governments can open their markets to imports or restrict them. Protectionism is the restricting approach, and international bodies try to set common rules.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.0] Protectionism restricts imports to protect home industries from foreign competition, so home producers face less pressure from cheaper foreign goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.1] Barriers to trade include tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on the quantity imported), subsidies (payments that help home producers compete with imports), and regulations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.2] Trade blocs (groups of countries that agree trade rules among themselves) remove barriers between their own members, so members trade with each other more easily than with outsiders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.3] GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade) was signed in 1947 to reduce tariffs between countries, so goods cost less to trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.4] The World Trade Organisation (WTO) was set up on 1 January 1995, with headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland; it has over 160 member countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.5] The WTO sets the rules of world trade and settles disputes between members, so countries have a common referee.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.6] Fair trade gives producers in developing countries a guaranteed minimum price and a premium (an extra payment), shown by the Fairtrade mark.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.b] World trade has grown much faster than world output since 1950, and most countries now depend on imports and exports. Several changes made this possible.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.0] Falling trade barriers, through GATT and the WTO, made it cheaper to trade across borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.1] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard-sized steel boxes) began with the first container ship voyage, the Ideal X of Malcolm McLean, USA, in 1956; containers made loading and shipping faster and cheaper.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.2] Shanghai, China, has been the world's busiest container port since 2010.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.3] Air freight and information and communication technology (ICT) speed up trade in high-value goods and services, for example by moving them quickly and sharing information instantly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.4] Multinational companies (MNCs, firms that operate in many countries) trade components between their own plants in different countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.5] The entry of China and Eastern Europe into world trade added large new producers and markets, so there were more goods to sell and more buyers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.b] The three triad areas and the rest of the world are linked by shipping routes and by products whose parts come from many countries. Because they are so linked, a disruption in one place spreads worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.0] One product links many countries: the iPhone and Intel chip supply chains (the series of countries and firms that make a product's parts and assemble it) each pass through several countries. There is more on this in One multinational company (MNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.1] The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, and the Panama Canal, opened in 1914, are chokepoints (narrow passages that much shipping must use) on the shipping routes linking the triad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.2] In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given blocked the Suez Canal for about six days, holding up hundreds of ships.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.3] From late 2023 attacks on shipping in the Red Sea forced many ships to sail around the Cape of Good Hope, Africa.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.4] The 2008-09 global financial crisis caused world trade to fall sharply in 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.5] COVID-19 (2020-21) disrupted supply chains, for example in the 2021 chip shortage.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.b] Most of the world's trade and production is concentrated in three areas. The trading triad is North America, Europe and the Pacific Rim, the three core economic areas of the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.0] The triad produces most of the world's manufactured goods, such as cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.1] It holds the headquarters of most multinational companies (MNCs), so company decisions are made there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.2] Most world trade takes place among the three areas, and most foreign direct investment (money that firms invest in businesses and factories in other countries) flows between them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.b] The USA is the triad's North American member and the dominant economy within it. Its trade is shaped by its size, its trade bloc and its use of tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.0] The USA is the world's largest economy and its largest importer, so its demand matters to exporters everywhere.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.1] It has had a trade deficit in goods every year since the mid-1970s, which means it imports more goods than it exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.2] NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement: USA, Canada, Mexico) took effect on 1 January 1994.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.3] USMCA (United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement) replaced NAFTA on 1 July 2020, so the three countries kept a trade bloc.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.4] Mexico overtook China in 2023 as the largest source of US imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.5] From 2018 the USA put tariffs (taxes on imports) on many Chinese goods in the US-China trade war.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.6] In 2025 the USA raised tariffs on imports from most countries, which raised the price of goods entering the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.b] Europe is one of the three triad areas, and it trades as a bloc. The EU is a union of countries that trade freely with one another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.0] The EU is a single market (members trade as if in one market) of 27 member states, since the UK left the EU.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.1] The single market began on 1 January 1993, removing barriers to trade between members, so goods move freely between them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.2] The EU is the largest trading bloc by value, with strong trade with the USA and China, for example in goods sold between the blocs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.3] The euro, the shared currency, was introduced in 1999; euro notes and coins followed in 2002.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.4] In July 2025 the EU and USA agreed a 15% US tariff on most EU goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.b] In the triad, the Pacific Rim means mainly East and South-East Asia, together with Australia. India is not part of it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.0] Members include, for example, Japan, China, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, the ASEAN countries and Australia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.1] ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) was founded in 1967 as a group of South-East Asian countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.2] APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) was founded in 1989.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.3] RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership) has 15 Asia-Pacific members, including China, Japan, South Korea, Australia and the ASEAN members.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.4] RCEP was signed in November 2020 and came into force from 2022; its members hold about 30% of the world's population and GDP (the total value of goods and services a country produces in a year).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.5] The Asian Tigers are South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, fast-growing Asian economies. China and Japan are not Tigers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.b] Japan is a Pacific Rim economy that grew after the Second World War by exporting manufactured goods. Its story shows how a country with few raw materials can grow by selling manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.0] After the Second World War Japan's growth was based on exporting manufactured goods, mainly cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.1] Japan has few raw materials, so it must import most of its oil, coal and iron ore.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.2] Japan was the world's second-largest economy from the late 1960s until China overtook it in 2010; Germany overtook Japan in 2023.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.3] Toyota, based in Toyota City, uses just-in-time production, which delivers parts as they are needed rather than storing them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.4] Other major Japanese companies are, for example, Honda, Sony and Nissan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.5] To avoid trade barriers, Japanese firms opened factories inside the EU and USA; Nissan's car plant at Sunderland, north-east England, opened in 1986, inside the European Economic Community (EEC, the EU's earlier name).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.6] Japan's growth has been slower since the 1990s, so it is no longer expanding as quickly as it did after the war.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.b] China moved from a closed economy to the world's largest exporter of goods, which is why it is called the workshop of the world. Government reforms opened it to trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.0] Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms, from 1978, opened China's closed economy to foreign trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.1] The first Special Economic Zones (SEZs, areas with special rules to attract foreign firms) were set up in 1980, including Shenzhen beside Hong Kong.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.2] Shenzhen is in mainland China and is not part of Hong Kong; it grew from a small town to over 17 million people (2020 census).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.3] China joined the WTO on 11 December 2001, which gave it a place under world trade rules.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.4] China has been the world's largest exporter of goods since 2009, and it overtook Japan as the second-largest economy in 2010.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.5] China runs a huge trade surplus, because it sells so much abroad; meaning its exports greatly exceed its imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.6] The Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, links China to Asia, Africa and Europe through ports, railways and roads.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.7] The trade war with the USA, begun in 2018, also affects China; the US side is in the North America notes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.b] South Korea is the standard example of a newly industrialised country (NIC, a country that has recently built a large manufacturing economy). It went from poverty to high income by selling manufactured goods abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.0] South Korea was war-damaged by the Korean War (1950-53) and was one of the world's poorest countries in the early 1960s.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.1] Under President Park Chung-hee (1961-79) it industrialised through exports and rose from poverty to high income.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.2] Its economy is dominated by chaebol, family-controlled business groups such as Samsung (founded 1938), Hyundai, LG and SK.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.3] Ulsan is home to Hyundai's shipyards, among the world's largest, and to a Hyundai car plant, as an example of an industrial city built on exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.4] South Korea is a world leader in shipbuilding, cars, electronics and memory chips, and Samsung makes smartphones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.5] South Korea is one of the Asian Tigers and joined the OECD (the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) in 1996.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.14.b] Not every region shares in the triad's trade. Some areas are left on the edges of world trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.14.0] Africa, much of Latin America and South Asia hold only a small share of world trade, mostly in raw materials. There is more on this in How the global economy is organised.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.b] An international division of labour means that countries and regions specialise in different stages of production and different types of work. The first pattern grew out of colonial rule.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.0] In the old, colonial pattern, colonies and developing countries exported raw materials and food, while Europe and later the USA manufactured goods and sold them back.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.1] Sudan's Gezira Scheme, from 1925, grew irrigated cotton for the British (Lancashire) textile mills.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.2] India exported raw cotton and tea to Britain under colonial rule, as an example of raw materials and food leaving a colony.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.3] Ghana (then the Gold Coast) exported cocoa; Cote d'Ivoire and Ghana together grow over half of the world's cocoa today, still mostly exported unprocessed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.b] From the 1960s-70s the pattern changed. Multinational companies began to split production, placing each stage where it suited them best.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.0] Multinational companies moved assembly and routine manufacturing to newly industrialised countries (NICs) and low-wage countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.1] The core (the rich, powerful economies) kept headquarters, research and development (R&D, work on new products and technology), design, finance and marketing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.2] The periphery (the poorer countries on the edge of the world economy) still supplies raw materials.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.3] As an example, Apple's packaging carried the line 'Designed by Apple in California. Assembled in China.'
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.4] Intel's R&D is at Hillsboro, Oregon; its fabs (chip factories) include Leixlip in Ireland; assembly and test take place in Malaysia and Vietnam. There is more on this in One multinational company (MNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.b] The pattern is not fixed. Services are now divided between countries, and countries move up or spread to new places.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.0] Services are now divided too: Bengaluru (Bangalore), India, is an IT services hub for firms such as Infosys, Wipro and Tata Consultancy Services, and IT and call centres are located in India.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.1] China is moving into high-value products instead of only low-cost assembly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.2] Low-cost assembly is spreading to Vietnam, Bangladesh and India, because firms look for cheaper places to assemble.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.b] Ireland is a very open economy: its exports are larger than its GDP. It has moved from supplying food to the UK to hosting high-value manufacturing and services by multinational companies (MNCs).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.0] Goods exports passed 200 billion euro in 2024 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office), a record.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.1] Most of this export value is produced by MNC plants, not by Irish-owned firms, so large exports reflect MNC production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.2] Main goods exports: pharmaceuticals and chemicals (the largest category), medical devices (a cluster at Galway, with firms such as Medtronic and Boston Scientific), computer chips and electronics, and food and drink (beef, dairy and whiskey).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.3] Main services exports: computer services (Ireland is one of the world's largest exporters of them), financial services, aircraft leasing and tourism.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.4] Irish-based lessors (companies that own aircraft and lease them to airlines) manage over half of the world's leased commercial aircraft, from Dublin and Shannon.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.5] Ireland has a large goods trade surplus, because it exports far more goods than it imports, the largest of it with the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.b] Where Irish goods go reflects who makes them. The main market is the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.0] The USA is the largest single market for Irish goods, taking about a third of them, which makes it Ireland's biggest buyer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.1] Other main markets, such as EU partners Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands, then the UK and China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.2] US pharmaceutical and technology MNCs export from their Irish plants back to the USA and worldwide, which is why the USA dominates.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.3] In early 2025 Irish exports to the USA rose sharply as US buyers stockpiled medicines (bought extra in advance) before new tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.b] Ireland's trade with the UK changed after the UK left the EU. Brexit is the usual reason to give.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.0] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition period (when the old trade rules still applied) ended on 31 December 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.1] Brexit brought customs checks (inspections of goods at the border) on trade with the UK and took the UK out of the EU single market.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.2] A weaker pound makes Irish goods dearer for UK buyers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.3] Irish firms have diversified into other markets, so they depend less on the UK.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.b] Ireland's export success depends on a narrow base, which brings risk.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.0] Ireland depends on a few US MNCs and on the US market, so a problem for those firms or that market hits Irish exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.1] US tariffs from 2025 expose Irish exports to the USA to new barriers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.b] Data-response questions give charts and tables of trade and ask you to calculate. The method is the same every time.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.0] Read values from the chart or table and state their units before calculating.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.1] Balance of trade for one country: subtract imports from exports; a negative result is a trade deficit.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.2] Difference between two countries: subtract the smaller value from the larger, so the answer is positive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.3] Total exports: add the values for every partner, so none is missed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.4] Use a bar chart to compare values between countries and a line graph to show change over time, because each suits that kind of data.

CANDIDATES geo-graph-skills-s1: Figure interpretation
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.b] Every figure question starts the same way: read the labels and units before reading any value. Most lost marks come from a wrong unit, year or column, because a correct reading of the wrong one still scores nothing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.0] Check the title, both axis labels (the words along the horizontal and vertical lines), the key or legend (the box explaining colours or symbols), the units (%, thousands, millions, €m, hectares, mm, °C, per 1,000), the years shown and the source line. Each one changes what a number means.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.1] Read a bar or line value by running a ruler from the top of the bar or point across to the vertical axis, estimating between gridlines, and quoting the value with its unit.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.2] In a table, find the row and the column and read the cell where they meet. Check the totals row before using a figure, so you know whether a number is a part or the whole.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.3] Compare two years by reading neighbouring columns. To spot the one that did not change, read across the whole row, because the unchanged value is the one that stays the same between years.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.4] For highest and lowest, scan every value, not just the first and last, because the extreme can sit in the middle. For a ranking, list the values from largest to smallest.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.5] To count how many items are above an average line, count the bars or points whose tops lie above it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.6] For 'twice as much', double the smaller value and look for the value that matches it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.7] An infographic (a single page combining headline numbers, icons, small charts and county maps) has several panels. Read each panel's own label and unit: headline figures are often totals, while panels may show percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.b] A description states what the figure shows. Using exact trend words and quoting numbers turns a vague answer into a complete one.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.0] Trend vocabulary: increase, rise or growth; decrease, fall or decline; steady, constant or no change; fluctuate (go up and down); peak (highest point); trough (lowest point); gradual or rapid (sharp); levelling off (the change slows and flattens).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.1] The overall trend is the long-term direction. A short-term change, such as a single year's dip, is described separately so it does not hide the main pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.2] To describe a trend, state the overall direction, the start and end values with their years and units, and the period of fastest change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.3] Name any peak, trough or reversal (a change of direction), and quantify the change as the difference, the percentage change, or both.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.4] Point out anomalies: values that break the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.b] Once a trend is described, the next marks come from comparing it with another, saying why it happened and judging whether the figure can be trusted.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.0] To compare two series (two lines or two sets of bars), say which is higher, when the lines cross, whether the gap widens or narrows, and the difference in a given year. For example, net migration = immigration minus emigration.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.1] To explain a trend, link the pattern to one cause from the relevant chapter, in one clause. Explaining means saying why the figure looks as it does, not just what it shows.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.2] To evaluate a figure, check whether an axis that does not start at zero or has uneven intervals exaggerates change, because a small rise then looks large.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.3] Also check whether unevenly spaced dates distort the trend, whether the sample (the group measured) is small, and whether the source is reliable and recent.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.4] Percentages can hide small totals: a large percentage rise may be a small change in numbers, because a small starting number is easy to double.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.b] These worked values come from the invented (illustrative) population of six towns, in thousands: Town A 18, Town B 32, Town C 25, Town D 41, Town E 12, Town F 29. Each statistic has a short method that you can repeat on any data set.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.0] Total (sum): add every value. 18 + 32 + 25 + 41 + 12 + 29 = 157 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.1] Difference: subtract the smaller value from the larger and say which is larger. Town B (32) exceeds Town C (25) by 7 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.2] Mean (average) = total of the values ÷ number of values: 157 ÷ 6 = 26.2 thousand, to one decimal place (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.3] Median (the middle value): put the values in order (12, 18, 25, 29, 32, 41); with an even number of values, average the middle two: (25 + 29) ÷ 2 = 27 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.4] Mode: the most frequent value. The six towns have no mode because no value repeats.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.5] Range = highest value minus lowest value: 41 − 12 = 29 thousand (illustrative), which shows how spread out the values are.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.b] Percentages let you compare parts of different-sized wholes, but the method matters: dividing by the wrong number, or mixing up a percentage with a percentage point, gives a wrong answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.0] Percentage of a total = part ÷ total × 100. Illustrative: Town D's share of the six towns is 41 ÷ 157 × 100 = 26.1%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.1] Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100. A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.2] Always divide by the old (earlier) value, because the change is measured against where you started. Illustrative Country P, broadband households: 20% in 2000 and 90% in 2025, so the percentage change is (90 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 350%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.3] Percentage points: when both values are already percentages, the change is a difference in percentage points. A rise from 20% to 30% is a 10 percentage-point increase, but a 50% increase in the percentage change, (30 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.4] When a question asks by what percentage a percentage changed, the subtraction is the usual reading: give the answer in percentage points and add the percentage change if unsure. Use the percentage-change formula when the values are numbers rather than percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.5] Missing value X: parts of a whole add to 100% (pie chart or percentage table) or to a stated total (stacked bar, a bar split into parts, or a total row), so X = total minus the sum of the known parts.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.6] Illustrative Country Q: if all segments except 'other' total 85%, then other = 100 − 85 = 15%, because the parts of a pie chart must reach 100%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.7] To find a number from a percentage: number = percentage ÷ 100 × total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.b] Raw numbers are hard to compare between places of different size, so geographers use rates, densities and ratios. How you present the answer matters too.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.0] Rate per 1,000 = number ÷ population × 1,000, which lets places of different size be compared. A birth rate is births ÷ population × 1,000.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.1] Density = number ÷ area, given as people per km².
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.2] Ratio: compare two values as a ratio, e.g. 2 : 1. Illustrative: Town D (41) is more than three times the size of Town E (12), a ratio of about 3.4 : 1.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.3] 'Twice as much' means two times the other value; 'half' means divided by two.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.4] Round to the precision the data uses, or to one decimal place where needed, and keep the unit and the multiplier (thousands, millions) in every answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.5] In a calculation question, show the working: a correct answer with working is safe, and the working can earn marks if the answer slips.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.b] When you are asked to draw a graph from a small table of data, marks are given for each of these rules, so each one is worth doing even when the shape of the graph is easy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.0] Use graph paper, a sharp pencil and a ruler; draw the graph large enough to fill most of the space.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.1] The title says what the data is, where and when, taken from the wording of the question.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.2] The horizontal (x) axis shows categories or time; the vertical (y) axis shows values. Label both axes with units.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.3] The scale starts at zero, is evenly divided, and its interval is chosen so the largest value fits (e.g. steps of 5 or 10 units).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.4] On a line graph, time intervals are spaced in proportion to the years. If the data is for 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2015, the last gap is half the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.5] Plot every value accurately. Bars have equal widths and equal gaps; points are joined with ruled straight lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.6] Use a key or legend (a box saying what each colour or shading means) whenever more than one colour or shading is used, and shade or colour each category distinctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.b] Illustrative data: population of six towns, in thousands, from the totals block above. Following the rules in order shows how each mark is earned.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.0] Graph choice: a bar chart, because the values are one measure for separate towns.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.1] Title: 'Population of six towns (thousands), illustrative'.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.2] x-axis: 'Town', with Towns A to F in order.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.3] y-axis: 'Population (thousands)', from 0 to 45 in steps of 5, so the largest value (41) fits.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.4] Six bars of equal width with equal gaps, each drawn to its value: A 18, B 32, C 25, D 41, E 12, F 29.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.5] A single data series (one set of values) needs no key; check each bar top against the scale before finishing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.b] The question 'draw a suitable graph' is answered by naming the graph type, then giving one sentence of justification. The type depends on what the data is. Each example below is illustrative.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.0] Values for separate categories, e.g. the populations of six towns: bar chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.1] Change over time, e.g. the percentage of an urban population from 1980 to 2020: line graph (a bar chart is usually also accepted).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.2] Parts of a whole, or percentage shares adding to 100%, e.g. car sales by fuel type in one year: pie chart or divided bar.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.3] Two related variables for the same places, e.g. income and birth rate for twenty countries: scatter graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.4] Three components that add to 100%, e.g. sand, silt and clay at a soil site: triangular graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.5] Age and sex structure of a population: population pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.6] Monthly temperature and rainfall for one place: climate graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.7] Values for areas on a map, e.g. population density by region: choropleth map (a map with areas shaded by value).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.b] Bar charts show amounts for separate categories. They come in several forms, depending on whether you are comparing categories, groups within a category, or parts of a total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.0] Simple bar chart: separate categories, one bar per category, equal widths and equal gaps; vertical or horizontal.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.1] Multiple (clustered or comparative) bar chart: two or more bars side by side per category, e.g. males and females, or two years; a key is needed to tell the bars apart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.2] Compound (stacked or divided) bar chart: each bar is split into components, and the top of the whole bar is the total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.3] To read a component in a compound bar, take the top of its segment minus the bottom of its segment.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.4] Percentage compound bar: every bar reaches 100%, so the segments show shares rather than totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.5] Illustrative Region R, tree planting: total 2,000 hectares, of which conifers 1,350 ha. Broadleaf = 2,000 − 1,350 = 650 ha, a share of 650 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 32.5%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.b] A histogram looks like a bar chart but is used for continuous data, while a line graph is the standard way to show one variable changing over time.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.0] Histogram: like a bar chart but for continuous data grouped in classes (e.g. age groups or rainfall amounts), drawn with no gaps between bars because one class runs straight into the next.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.1] Line graph (trend graph): one variable over time; plot the points at the correct years and join them with ruled lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.2] Multiple line graph: two or more series on the same axes, with a key so each line can be identified.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.3] The gradient (steepness) of a line shows the rate of change: a steeper section means faster change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.4] Illustrative Country P, households with broadband: 2000 20%, 2005 38%, 2010 61%, 2015 74%, 2020 86%, 2025 90%. The rise is continuous; the fastest change is 2005 to 2010 (+23 percentage points), and the rise slows to +4 points from 2020 to 2025 as the line levels off.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.5] Illustrative Country P: the change from 2000 to 2025 is +70 percentage points, which is not the same as the 350% percentage change above, because one is a subtraction and the other is measured against the old value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.b] A pie chart shows how a whole is split into parts. Because a full circle is 360° and the parts add to 100%, each 1% takes up 3.6° of the circle.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.0] Pie chart: parts of a whole. Degrees = percentage × 3.6, because 360° ÷ 100 = 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.1] For raw numbers rather than percentages: degrees = value ÷ total × 360. Do not multiply raw numbers by 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.2] Drawing steps: draw a circle with a compass, draw a radius (a line from the centre to the edge) to 12 o'clock, then measure each angle clockwise with a protractor, largest segment first.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.3] Shade, key or label each segment with its percentage, and check that the angles add to 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.4] Illustrative Country Q, electricity sources (gas 40%, wind 25%, solar 10%, coal 5%, hydro 5%, other 15%): angles 144°, 90°, 36°, 18°, 18° and 54°, totalling 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.5] Two pie charts compare the shares in two years, not the totals, unless the circles are drawn in proportion to the totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.6] Divided (percentage) bar: a single bar split into shares that add to 100%, an alternative to a pie chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.b] A pictogram uses icons to stand for quantities. A population pyramid is a special graph showing how many people of each age and sex a country has.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.0] Pictogram: each icon stands for a fixed quantity stated in the key, so check the key first; a part-icon is a fraction of that quantity.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.1] Population pyramid: a back-to-back horizontal bar chart, which is a histogram on its side.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.2] Age groups, usually in 5-year bands, sit on the central vertical axis: 0 to 4 at the bottom and the oldest group at the top.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.3] Males are drawn on the left and females on the right. The horizontal axis shows the percentage (or number) of the population, not age, scaled outward from zero on both sides.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.4] Reading a pyramid: a wide base means a high birth rate; a narrow top means low life expectancy; a narrowing base means a falling birth rate; bulges and indentations suggest baby booms, migration, war or famine.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.5] Describe the dependent groups (the young and the old, who rely on others) and the working-age group when reading a pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.6] Drawing one band (illustrative, Country Z): males aged 20 to 24 are 3.5% and females 3.4%, so at the 20 to 24 level draw a bar 3.5 units long left of the centre axis and one 3.4 units long to the right.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.7] The shapes of pyramids and the stages of demographic transition (how a country's birth and death rates change over time) are taught fully elsewhere. There is more on this in Changing population characteristics.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.b] A climate graph combines two kinds of data for one place on a single set of axes: rainfall as bars and temperature as a line, across 12 months.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.0] Months J to D run along the bottom axis. Rainfall is shown as bars against a left-hand axis in mm.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.1] Mean (average) monthly temperature is shown as a line, plotted at the middle of each month, against a right-hand axis in °C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.2] From the graph, read the hottest and coldest months, the wettest and driest months, and the seasonal pattern (rain all year, or a wet and dry season).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.3] Calculations from the graph: annual temperature range = maximum mean monthly temperature minus minimum; mean annual temperature = sum of the 12 monthly means ÷ 12; total annual rainfall = sum of the 12 bars.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.4] Illustrative Station M, mean monthly temperature (°C) J to D: 5, 5, 7, 9, 12, 14, 16, 16, 14, 11, 8, 6. Rainfall (mm) J to D: 110, 80, 85, 60, 65, 70, 70, 85, 90, 115, 110, 120.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.5] Illustrative Station M results: warmest in July and August (16°C); coldest in January and February (5°C); annual range 16 − 5 = 11°C; mean annual temperature 123 ÷ 12 = 10.3°C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.6] Illustrative Station M: total rainfall 1,060 mm; wettest month December (120 mm); driest month April (60 mm). Rain falls in every month with a winter maximum, and the mild winters and cool summers resemble a cool temperate oceanic pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.7] Identifying a climate type from a climate graph is taught elsewhere. There is more on this in Physical regions and Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.b] A scatter graph tests whether two measurements for the same set of places are related, for example whether land value changes with distance from a city centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.0] Scatter graph: two variables for the same set of places. The independent variable (the one that may cause the change) goes on the x-axis; each place is plotted as a point, and the points are not joined.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.1] Draw a line of best fit by eye through the middle of the points, so it shows the general direction.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.2] Positive correlation: both variables rise together. Negative correlation: one rises as the other falls. No correlation: no clear pattern. Strength is described as strong or weak.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.3] Outliers, or anomalies, are points that lie away from the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.4] Correlation does not prove cause: two things can move together without one causing the other.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.5] Illustrative distance from a city centre and land value at eight sites: 1 km €900 per m²; 2 km €700; 3 km €620; 4 km €450; 5 km €400; 6 km €250; 7 km €260; 8 km €150.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.6] Illustrative: the result is a strong negative correlation, with the 7 km site a slight anomaly because it is higher than the 6 km site. A one-clause explanation: accessibility falls with distance from the centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.b] A triangular graph is used when a total is split into exactly three parts. Each point is placed using all three values at once.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.0] Triangular graph: shows three components that always total 100%, e.g. the percentage employed in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.1] Each side is a 0 to 100% scale. Read each component along the gridlines parallel to the side where its 0 is, following the direction in which its numbers run.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.2] Check that the three readings add to 100, which confirms you have read the scales correctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.3] To plot a point: find the first value on its scale, follow its gridline in, and mark where it meets the second value's gridline; check the point with the third value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.4] A point clustered near a corner is dominated by the component that corner represents.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.5] Illustrative employment structure: Country X is primary 10%, secondary 25%, tertiary 65%; it sits near the tertiary corner, typical of a developed economy. Country Y is primary 60%, secondary 15%, tertiary 25%; it sits near the primary corner, typical of a less developed economy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.b] Statistical maps put values onto a map so you can see how they vary from place to place. The choropleth is the main one; four other types each show a different kind of data.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.0] Choropleth map: areas (counties, regions or countries) shaded by value class; darker shading means a higher value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.1] Classes must not overlap and must cover every value, e.g. 0 to 49, 50 to 99, 100 to 149, 150 and over. A key is essential.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.2] Choropleth maps suit densities and percentages. Their weakness is that they imply the value is even across each area and hide variation inside it, and the boundaries look like sudden changes.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.3] Illustrative regional population densities (people per km²): North 35, East 160, South 85, West 40, Midlands 60. North and West are the lightest shade and East the darkest; the empty 100 to 149 class still appears in the key.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.4] Dot map: each dot stands for a fixed number, showing the distribution of a value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.5] Isoline map: lines join places of equal value, e.g. isobars, isotherms, isohyets or contours.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.6] Flow-line map: arrow width is proportional to the amount moving, e.g. migration, traffic or trade.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.7] Proportional symbols: circles or bars sized in proportion to the value, placed on the map.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.b] Sometimes a table is a better way to present data than a graph, and tables, graphs and maps all feed into the Geographical Investigation.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.0] Use a table rather than a graph when many exact values must be given. A table cannot show a trend at a glance.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.1] Graphs, maps and tables are expected in the presentation of results in the Geographical Investigation report. There is more on this in The Geographical Investigation (20%).

CANDIDATES geo-economic-eu-s1: 1. EU trading patterns
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.b] A trading bloc is a group of countries that remove trade barriers between themselves and act together in trade with the rest of the world. The EU is the biggest example, and its two founding trade tools are a customs union and the single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.0] The EU accounted for about 13% of world goods trade in 2024, second to China in goods. Counting goods and services together, the EU is the world's largest trader, with China and the USA as the other two of the three largest.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.1] Customs union: completed on 1 July 1968 (as the EEC, the European Economic Community, the EU's earlier name), it removes tariffs (taxes on imports) between members and applies a common external tariff to imports from outside the EU, so every member charges the same rate.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.b] The single market, in force from 1 January 1993 and set up by the Single European Act of 1986, goes beyond the customs union. A customs union only deals with tariffs, whereas the single market also tackles every other obstacle to trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.0] It guarantees the four freedoms: free movement of goods, services, capital (money and investment) and people.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.1] It removes non-tariff barriers (obstacles to trade other than taxes): border checks, different national product standards and national preference in public contracts (governments favouring their own firms).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.2] Common standards, such as the CE mark (a label showing a product meets EU rules), replace those barriers, so one product can be sold in every member state.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.3] The single market extends beyond the 27 members: the European Economic Area (EEA) brings in Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein, while Switzerland, which is in neither the EU nor the EEA, trades under bilateral agreements (separate deals made with the EU).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.b] Trade questions use a small set of terms, and examiners expect you to define them precisely.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.0] Imports are goods or services bought from abroad; exports are goods or services sold abroad.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.1] Visible trade is trade in goods, things you can see and touch; invisible trade is trade in services, such as finance, tourism and transport.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.2] Balance of trade: the value of exports minus the value of imports. A positive balance is a surplus (the country sells more than it buys); a negative balance is a deficit (it buys more than it sells).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.3] A tariff is a tax on imports; a quota is a limit on the quantity of imports allowed. Both make imports harder to sell.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.4] Intra-EU (internal) trade is trade between member states; extra-EU (external) trade is trade with countries outside the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.b] Most member states trade mainly with each other, because the single market makes it the easiest and cheapest option. The share, though, varies a great deal from country to country.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.0] In 2024 intra-EU goods exports were about €4.0 trillion, against about €2.6 trillion of extra-EU goods exports. So about three-fifths (about 60%) of members' goods exports stayed within the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.1] Germany is by far the largest intra-EU trader, with intra-EU goods exports of about €845 billion in 2024; Cyprus is the smallest, at about €1 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.2] The core of the EU, the Blue Banana (a curved band of dense industry and population) from south-east England through the Benelux countries and the Rhine valley to northern Italy, together with the Paris Basin, dominates internal trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.3] Peripheral members (those on the edge of the EU, far from the core), such as Cyprus and Greece, trade much less within the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.4] Eastern members depend most on intra-EU trade. Intra-EU trade was about 81% of Czechia's goods trade in 2024, compared with about 30% for Cyprus, because of their proximity to Germany and their integration into its supply chains (the linked steps in making a product, spread across countries).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.b] The goods that cross borders inside the EU are mostly high-value manufactures. The pattern of trade takes three forms: swapping similar goods, linked production chains, and swapping different goods.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.0] Manufactured goods dominate: machinery, vehicles and parts, and chemicals and pharmaceuticals. Food and farm produce come next, and services such as finance, tourism and transport add to the total.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.1] Intra-industry trade: members swap similar goods, such as cars for cars, and components cross borders several times in supply chains before the finished product is sold.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.2] Since the 2004 enlargement the car industry has linked Germany with Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary and Poland, where assembly plants supply the German-centred industry. Volkswagen took over Škoda (Czechia) in stages from 1991 and has fully owned it since 2000.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.3] Slovakia makes more cars per head of population than any other country.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.4] Complementary trade (members swapping goods the other lacks): greenhouse fruit and vegetables from Almería in southern Spain are trucked north to northern Europe, while northern manufactured goods flow south and east.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.b] Some trade figures look larger than the country's own economy would suggest, because goods pass through its ports on the way to somewhere else.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.0] Rotterdam in the Netherlands is Europe's largest port and Antwerp in Belgium is the second largest; both sit at the mouth of the Rhine-Meuse-Scheldt delta, where the Rhine reaches the sea.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.1] Goods entering and leaving the single market through these ports are counted as trade with the Netherlands and Belgium, which gives both countries very large trade totals.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.2] The Rotterdam effect: goods recorded as trade with the Netherlands may be going on to other member states, so the figure overstates what the Netherlands itself buys or sells.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.b] Trade inside the EU is high because several things lower its costs and widen its market. Exams ask you to explain these factors, so give each with its reason.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.0] Removal of tariffs and border controls: the customs union, the single market and Schengen border-free travel for most members cut costs and delays.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.1] The euro: 21 countries used it from 1 January 2026, with Bulgaria the newest. Trade among them has no exchange costs or exchange-rate risk, and prices are easy to compare.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.2] The euro is not the currency of the whole EU, which has 27 members. The euro's history is in The complexity of regions (i).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.3] Proximity and transport: short distances, dense motorway and rail networks, the Rhine (navigable to Basel in Switzerland), the Channel Tunnel (opened 1994) and the Øresund Bridge between Copenhagen and Malmö (opened 2000).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.4] EU-funded Trans-European Transport Networks (TEN-T) also add links, so goods move quickly and cheaply between member states.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.5] Common policies: the CAP (Common Agricultural Policy, the EU's farm policy) and common food standards make farm and food trade easy within the EU. There is more on the CAP in How the EU influences the Irish economy.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.b] The size of the market and changes in membership also shape internal trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.0] A large, wealthy market: about 450 million people in 2024, with high incomes and broadly similar tastes, so a product made for one country sells easily in others.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.1] Enlargement widened the single market: ten countries joined in 2004, Bulgaria and Romania in 2007 and Croatia in 2013. New members opened new markets and lower-cost production sites in the east.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.2] Brexit moved the UK from internal to external partner. The UK left on 31 January 2020; the Trade and Cooperation Agreement has applied since 1 January 2021, with zero tariffs and zero quotas on qualifying goods (those meeting rules of origin).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.3] Trade with the UK nonetheless became more costly because of customs declarations, checks and rules-of-origin paperwork.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.b] In trade with the outside world the EU does not act as 27 separate countries. Its common trade policy gives it a single negotiating position.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.0] The European Commission (the EU's executive body) negotiates trade for all 27 members under the Common Commercial Policy, so the EU speaks with one voice in the World Trade Organization (WTO, the body that sets global trade rules).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.1] All members apply the same common external tariff, so the EU bargains as one bloc rather than as 27 separate countries, which gives it more weight.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.b] Outside the EU, a handful of big economies take most of the EU's goods and supply most of its imports. The EU sells more to them than it buys overall.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.0] Export partners for extra-EU goods: the USA is the largest at about 21%, then the UK at about 13%, China at about 8%, Switzerland at about 7-8% and Turkey at about 4%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.1] Import partners for extra-EU goods: China is the largest at about a fifth (about 20-21%), then the USA at about 13-14%, the UK at about 7%, Switzerland at about 6% and Turkey at about 4%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.2] Overall, extra-EU goods exports were about €2.58 trillion and imports about €2.43 trillion, a goods surplus (exports exceed imports) of about €150 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.3] The EU had a surplus with the USA and a large deficit with China, meaning it bought far more from China than it sold there. It also had a surplus with the UK.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.b] The EU sells high-value manufactured goods and buys energy, raw materials and cheaper manufactured goods, because it has little energy of its own.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.0] Exports: machinery and vehicles were about €1.0 trillion (about 39% of extra-EU exports); chemicals and related products, including pharmaceuticals, were about €560 billion (about 22%). Food, drink and services are also exported.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.1] Imports: energy products, mainly oil and gas, were about 15% of EU imports by value in 2024, down from about 23% in 2022.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.2] Imports also include raw materials, consumer electronics and other manufactured goods from China and Asia, and tropical foods such as coffee, cocoa and bananas, which cannot be grown in the EU's climate.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.b] Political events change trade patterns quickly, as the change in energy imports shows.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.0] Russia was a major gas supplier before its full-scale invasion of Ukraine began on 24 February 2022.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.1] Russian gas fell from about 45% of EU gas imports in 2021 to about 19% in 2024, through sanctions (penalties that restrict trade with a country) and the REPowerEU plan (the EU's plan to cut energy dependence on Russia, launched May 2022).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.2] The USA supplied almost half of EU liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so ships can carry it) imports in 2024, replacing much of the lost Russian gas.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.3] The EU plans to end Russian gas imports by the end of 2027.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.b] The EU widens its external trade by signing agreements with other countries. It also gives poorer countries easier access to its market, though critics say its past policies hurt them.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.0] Trade agreements widen external trade: CETA with Canada has been provisionally applied (in use before final approval) since September 2017; the EU-Japan Economic Partnership Agreement has been in force since February 2019; the EU-South Korea agreement has applied since 2011.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.1] EU-Mercosur negotiations with Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay concluded in December 2024. Irish farm organisations strongly opposed the deal because of competition from South American beef.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.2] The Lomé Convention (1975, with African, Caribbean and Pacific (ACP) countries) and the Cotonou Agreement (2000) gave preferential access (easier, lower-tariff entry to the EU market) to poorer countries.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.3] Everything But Arms (2001) gives the least developed countries duty-free, quota-free access for all goods except arms.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.4] Critics argue that tariffs on processed goods (tariff escalation, where the tariff rises as a product is processed further) and past subsidised farm exports (sold cheaply with EU government support) harmed producers in the developing world.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.b] The EU also uses tariffs to protect its industries, and it is on the receiving end of other countries' tariffs. In each case it acts as a single bloc.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.0] The EU-USA agreement of July 2025 capped the US tariff on most EU goods at 15%, including pharmaceuticals; Irish pharmaceutical exports to the USA are among the goods it covers. Steel and aluminium remained at 50%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.1] EU definitive duties (lasting tariffs, not temporary ones) on Chinese-made electric cars, from October 2024, reached up to about 35%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.2] In both disputes the EU acted as one bloc, so one set of duties or one deal applies to all 27 members.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.b] Several factors explain why the EU trades with the outside world as it does. Exams ask you to explain them after describing the patterns.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.0] The size and wealth of the single market gives the EU bargaining power in trade negotiations, because other countries want access to its consumers.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.1] High-tech, high-value industry makes manufactured goods the main exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.2] A lack of energy and some raw materials makes the EU dependent on imports, especially of fuels.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.3] Historical and colonial links, including former colonies in Africa, the Caribbean and the Pacific and the Commonwealth ties of the former UK member, shaped the preferential agreements.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.4] Globalisation (the growing worldwide linking of economies): low-cost manufacturing in China and Asia draws in imports.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.5] The trading triad is the three main trading regions, the USA, the EU and the Pacific Rim (the countries around the Pacific Ocean). There is more on the trading triad in Patterns in world trade, in The Global Economy.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.6] Politics redirects trade: sanctions on Russia, US tariff disputes and Brexit each changed the direction of trade flows.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.b] Exam data questions give export and import shares for each partner. Use the invented values below only to practise the method.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.0] Identify the largest partner: the biggest slice or the highest value.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.1] Calculate a missing share: subtract the known shares from 100. Illustrative: exports to Country A 25% and Country B 15% leave 100 minus 25 minus 15 = 60% for all other partners.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.2] Compare exports and imports with one partner. If the value sent to it is higher than the value received, the EU has a surplus with that partner; if it is lower, a deficit.
