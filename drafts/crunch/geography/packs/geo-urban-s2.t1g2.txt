TARGET geo-urban-s2: 2. Rural settlement
[geo-urban-s2.0.b] Rural settlement is the farmhouses, hamlets (very small groups of houses) and villages in the countryside. Its pattern is the way the buildings are spread over the land, and geographers sort patterns into three types.
[geo-urban-s2.0.0] Dispersed: individual farmhouses scattered across the land, each on its own farm.
[geo-urban-s2.0.1] Clustered (also called nucleated): buildings grouped together in one place, usually for a shared reason such as a crossroads or a water supply.
[geo-urban-s2.0.2] Linear (also called ribbon): houses strung along a line such as a road, a coast road, a valley or a ridge.
[geo-urban-s2.0.3] Clustered and nucleated mean the same pattern, and linear is the same as ribbon when it extends out of a town along approach roads, so there are three patterns, not four.
[geo-urban-s2.0.4] In Census 2016 (the national count of the population), about 63% of Ireland's population lived in urban areas and about 37% in rural areas.
[geo-urban-s2.1.b] Dispersed settlement is the dominant rural pattern in Ireland. It exists for farming, historical and physical reasons.
[geo-urban-s2.1.0] Irish farms are family-owned, and each family lives on its own holding (its own farm), so the houses end up spread out across the countryside.
[geo-urban-s2.1.1] The Land Commission was set up in 1881 under the Land Law (Ireland) Act 1881. After the Land Acts, consolidation of holdings (joining scattered plots into one compact farm) moved houses out of clusters onto individual farms.
[geo-urban-s2.1.2] The Wyndham Land Act of 1903 helped tenants to buy their holdings, which strengthened the pattern of families owning and living on their own land.
[geo-urban-s2.1.3] Fertile, well-drained lowland with plentiful water lets houses be built almost anywhere, because no site is much better than another.
[geo-urban-s2.1.4] Pastoral farming (rearing animals, especially cattle and sheep) keeps the farmer close to the animals, so the house sits on the farm.
[geo-urban-s2.2.b] Buildings are grouped together where a shared reason made the site worth using. Each reason below explains why people chose to build close together instead of apart.
[geo-urban-s2.2.0] Route focus: a crossroads where roads meet brings travellers and trade, so buildings gather there.
[geo-urban-s2.2.1] A bridging point or ford (a shallow place where a river can be crossed) draws settlement because routes must pass through it.
[geo-urban-s2.2.2] A church, a castle or estate gates act as a focus, and people settle beside them for work, worship or protection.
[geo-urban-s2.2.3] Defence: a hill or other defensible site was chosen so the settlement could be defended.
[geo-urban-s2.2.4] Water supply at a spring or well gives a wet-point site, where people settle because they need water.
[geo-urban-s2.2.5] A dry-point site is a patch of dry ground above flood land, chosen so houses stay dry.
[geo-urban-s2.2.6] A harbour is the reason for a fishing village, because the boats and the catch are landed there.
[geo-urban-s2.2.7] Services such as a shop, pub, school and Garda station (police station) gather in the cluster, which then attracts further settlement.
[geo-urban-s2.2.8] Estate village: Enniskerry, Co. Wicklow, lies at the gates of the Powerscourt estate (a large landowner's property), and Adare, Co. Limerick, grew by the Dunraven estate, with thatched cottages.
[geo-urban-s2.3.b] Before the Famine many farmers in the west lived in clachans instead of on separate farms. The way land was held in common explains why.
[geo-urban-s2.3.0] A clachan is a cluster of farmhouses of related families, with no church, shop or other services. It is not a village, because a village has services.
[geo-urban-s2.3.1] Rundale was a landholding system in which families worked land in common, in scattered strips of infield (the better land near the houses) and outfield (poorer land further out). It explains the clachan; it is not a settlement pattern.
[geo-urban-s2.3.2] Clachans and rundale were common in the west before the Famine.
[geo-urban-s2.3.3] The Great Famine (1845-49), when the potato crop failed and many people died or emigrated, hit the clachan districts of the west hardest.
[geo-urban-s2.3.4] Clachans broke up as the population fell and holdings were consolidated. The Congested Districts Board, set up in 1891 for the poor western districts, consolidated holdings, built roads and piers, and rehoused families on separate farms.
[geo-urban-s2.3.5] The Land Commission also moved houses onto individual farms, which is how dispersed settlement replaced the clachans.
[geo-urban-s2.3.6] A few clachans survive in the west.
[geo-urban-s2.4.b] Linear settlement is the third pattern. Houses line up along a feature that offers a route or dry, firm ground.
[geo-urban-s2.4.0] Houses are strung along a line: a road, a coast road, a valley floor, a river or a raised ridge such as an esker (a long ridge of gravel left by a glacier) or the edge of a drumlin (an oval hill of glacial deposit).
[geo-urban-s2.4.1] Linear settlement avoids marshy or steep land, because it is hard and costly to build on.
[geo-urban-s2.5.b] Some land on a map has no settlement at all, and exams ask you to explain why. There is more on reading this from OS maps in Map interpretation.
[geo-urban-s2.5.0] Uplands are steep, exposed and have poor soils, so they are hard to build on and farm.
[geo-urban-s2.5.1] Bogs are wet, poorly drained land, so houses cannot be built safely on them.
[geo-urban-s2.5.2] Flood plains (flat land beside a river that floods) are unsuitable for houses because of the risk of flooding.
[geo-urban-s2.5.3] Lakes take up land that could hold houses.
[geo-urban-s2.5.4] Forestry plantations are large areas of planted trees with no houses.
[geo-urban-s2.6.b] Two European examples show how planning and history shape rural settlement very differently from the Irish pattern.
[geo-urban-s2.6.0] Noordoostpolder, Netherlands, a polder (land drained from water) drained in 1942, is a planned rural landscape with a central town, Emmeloord, ringed by ten planned villages. There is more on this in Urban settlement: site, hierarchy, hinterland and central place theory.
[geo-urban-s2.6.1] In southern Italy, the Mezzogiorno's rural population lived in hilltop villages. The 1950 land reform scattered new farmhouses on reclaimed lowlands, so settlement moved from clustered to dispersed. There is more on this in A Peripheral European Region: the Mezzogiorno.
[geo-urban-s2.7.b] Ribbon development is houses built one plot deep along the roads leading out of towns and villages, and along rural roads generally. Several pressures made roadsides the favoured building sites.
[geo-urban-s2.7.0] Sites are cheaper than in towns, so buyers get more for their money.
[geo-urban-s2.7.1] Road frontage gives access to services such as electricity, water mains and phone or broadband lines, which already run along the road.
[geo-urban-s2.7.2] Farmers sold roadside sites, because these were the easiest plots to sell.
[geo-urban-s2.7.3] People wanted rural living near a town, with the countryside around them and work or schools close by.
[geo-urban-s2.7.4] Car ownership made commuting (travelling daily to work) and rural living practical.
[geo-urban-s2.7.5] Planning control was historically weak, so few limits stopped houses going up along roads.
[geo-urban-s2.7.6] Tax incentives and the property boom of the Celtic Tiger (the period of rapid economic growth in Ireland) encouraged building.
[geo-urban-s2.8.b] Ribbon housing suits the people who live in it, but it causes problems for roads, services, the environment and towns.
[geo-urban-s2.8.0] Traffic hazards: many entrances onto fast roads, because every house needs its own access and drivers have to slow or turn.
[geo-urban-s2.8.1] It is costly to service. School buses, post, waste collection, water and sewers must all reach scattered houses, which are far apart.
[geo-urban-s2.8.2] Septic tanks (private tanks that treat a house's own sewage) can pollute groundwater and private wells.
[geo-urban-s2.8.3] Loss of scenic landscape, because the roadsides fill with houses.
[geo-urban-s2.8.4] Car dependence: residents need a car for almost every journey, as shops and services are far away.
[geo-urban-s2.8.5] Town centres decline as people live outside them and spend less in them.
[geo-urban-s2.8.6] The boundary between town and country becomes blurred, because houses line the roads where the town should end.
[geo-urban-s2.9.b] Rural people often see ribbon housing differently, because it helps keep their communities going.
[geo-urban-s2.9.0] Ribbon development keeps rural populations, schools, GAA clubs and shops alive, because they need people living nearby to survive.
[geo-urban-s2.9.1] It lets families live near relatives and farms.
[geo-urban-s2.9.2] Housing is cheaper than in towns.
[geo-urban-s2.10.b] Planning is how the State controls where building takes place. It works through the local authority (the county or city council), with plans made at county and local level.
[geo-urban-s2.10.0] Planning permission is granted by the local authority.
[geo-urban-s2.10.1] Decisions can be appealed to An Bord Pleanála, the national planning appeals board, set up in 1977. It was renamed An Coimisiún Pleanála in 2025.
[geo-urban-s2.10.2] The Planning and Development Act 2000 requires each planning authority to make a County Development Plan every six years.
[geo-urban-s2.10.3] The County Development Plan sets zoning (deciding which land may be used for housing, industry or farming), rural housing policy, design rules and protected areas.
[geo-urban-s2.10.4] Local Area Plans cover towns and villages.
[geo-urban-s2.11.b] National policy sets the aims that county plans must follow. Rural housing is treated differently depending on how much pressure an area is under.
[geo-urban-s2.11.0] The National Spatial Strategy (2002) and the National Planning Framework (2018, also called Project Ireland 2040) set rural housing aims. There is more on both in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s2.11.1] Sustainable Rural Housing Guidelines for Planning Authorities, 2005, tell councils how to treat different types of rural area.
[geo-urban-s2.11.2] In areas under strong urban influence (close to a city or large town), rural houses are limited to people with a local economic or social need, to prevent commuter housing spreading.
[geo-urban-s2.11.3] In structurally weak areas (areas losing people and jobs), rural housing is encouraged to keep the population.
[geo-urban-s2.12.b] County Development Plans turn national aims into rules for each county. These are the typical rural policies.
[geo-urban-s2.12.0] Local-needs criteria restrict who may build a house in some rural areas, for example to those with a local economic or social need.
[geo-urban-s2.12.1] New entrances onto national and regional roads are restricted, because many entrances onto fast roads are a traffic hazard.
[geo-urban-s2.12.2] New housing is directed into villages and serviced sites (sites with water and sewers), which avoids scattered one-off houses.
[geo-urban-s2.12.3] Design guidelines cover siting, materials and hedgerows.
[geo-urban-s2.12.4] Minimum site sizes apply to septic tanks, so that there is enough land to treat the waste safely.
[geo-urban-s2.12.5] Scenic areas and views are protected from development.
[geo-urban-s2.12.6] Land is zoned around towns, so that building is guided to where it is planned.
[geo-urban-s2.13.b] Septic tanks serve houses with no connection to a public sewer, so poorly maintained ones can pollute the water that rural households drink.
[geo-urban-s2.13.0] Water Services (Amendment) Act 2012: domestic septic tanks must be registered, and they are inspected under an EPA (Environmental Protection Agency) plan.
[geo-urban-s2.13.1] Ireland has about half a million domestic wastewater treatment systems, mostly septic tanks (EPA estimate).
[geo-urban-s2.14.b] These programmes respond to rural depopulation (people leaving rural areas) and the decline of services such as post offices, Garda stations and pubs. They aim to sustain rural communities.
[geo-urban-s2.14.0] LEADER, an EU rural development programme, from 1991.
[geo-urban-s2.14.1] CLÁR, a State programme for rural areas suffering depopulation, from 2001.
[geo-urban-s2.14.2] The Town and Village Renewal Scheme, from 2016.
[geo-urban-s2.14.3] Our Rural Future, the Government's rural development policy for 2021-2025, published in 2021, supports remote working hubs (shared workplaces where people can work remotely from home areas).
[geo-urban-s2.15.b] To judge how well rural planning works, weigh the aims against each other and against what has happened.
[geo-urban-s2.15.0] The central tension is keeping rural communities alive while preventing sprawl (unplanned spread of housing).
[geo-urban-s2.15.1] Local-needs conditions have been criticised as unfair. In 2013 the EU Court of Justice ruled against a Flemish (Belgian) rule restricting who could buy land in some communes, which raised doubts about Irish local-needs conditions.
[geo-urban-s2.15.2] Planning has reduced ribbon development but has not stopped it.
[geo-urban-s2.15.3] Villages need sewage treatment before they can take more housing, which limits the policy of directing housing into villages.

CANDIDATES geo-region-ireland-s2: 2. A Peripheral Irish Region: the West of Ireland
[geo-region-ireland-s2.0.b] This case study uses the West Region, made up of Counties Galway (including Galway city), Mayo and Roscommon. It is a peripheral region, meaning it lies far from the country's economic core around Dublin and from European markets.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.0.0] Galway and Mayo lie on the Atlantic coast, so they face the ocean that shapes the region's weather, while Roscommon is inland.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.0.1] Galway city is the region's only city, so it dominates the region's services and jobs, and Castlebar is the largest town in Mayo.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.0.2] The three counties together form the State's West regional unit for statistics, so this is the standard definition of the region to use throughout.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.b] A region is peripheral when several disadvantages add up and keep it behind the core. In the West they reinforce each other: distance and a harsh physical environment lead to a weak economy, and that drives people away.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.0] Remote location: long distances to Dublin and to European markets raise transport costs for goods.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.1] Difficult physical conditions: high rainfall, poor soils and rugged relief (the shape and height of the land) limit farming and building.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.2] The population is small, scattered and ageing, after a long history of out-migration (people leaving the region) since the Great Famine of the late 1840s.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.3] The economy depends on primary activities (farming, forestry, fishing) and the public sector, with lower incomes than the core.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.1.4] Infrastructure (roads, rail, broadband) is weaker than in the core, with Galway city as the main exception, because it is a growth centre (a place planned and chosen to attract investment and population).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.b] A sketch map of the region is set as a question. Build it in layers: the outline first, then water, then towns, then relief (upland and lowland).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.0] Outline Galway, Mayo and Roscommon, with the Atlantic coast along the western edge of Galway and Mayo.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.1] Draw the River Corrib flowing from Lough Corrib through Galway city to Galway Bay.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.2] Draw the River Moy reaching Killala Bay at Ballina, and the River Shannon along Roscommon's eastern edge.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.3] Mark the urban centres, such as Galway city, Castlebar, Westport and Ballina.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.4] Mark one upland: the Twelve Bens in Connemara, or Mweelrea in west Mayo.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.5] Shade the limestone lowland (low, flat to rolling land on limestone rock) of east Galway and Roscommon.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.2.6] Mark the Aran Islands offshore from Galway, for example as a small group of islands in the Atlantic.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.b] The West faces the Atlantic Ocean, which controls its weather. Moist, mild air arrives from the south-west and meets the western mountains, so the region is wet, windy, mild and cloudy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.0] The climate is cool temperate oceanic (mild and wet all year, with no extremes), strongly influenced by the Atlantic Ocean.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.1] Prevailing south-westerly winds (the winds that blow most often) bring frequent depressions (low-pressure weather systems that bring rain and wind) and gales from the Atlantic.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.2] Relief rainfall: moist Atlantic air is forced up over the western mountains, where it cools and drops its rain.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.3] Annual rainfall is about 1,000 to 1,250 mm on lowlands and over 2,000 mm on some mountains, against about 750 to 1,000 mm in the east.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.4] Winters are mild: mean January temperatures are about 4 to 7 °C, warmed by the North Atlantic Drift (a warm ocean current), so frost and snow are rare at the coast.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.5] Summers are cool: mean July temperatures are about 14 to 16 °C, so the annual temperature range (the gap between winter and summer) is small.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.3.6] The west is cloudier and less sunny than the east, because the moist Atlantic air arrives there first.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.b] The wet, windy climate shapes what the land can be used for. It suits grass but works against almost everything else.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.0] Grass grows well, but waterlogged land and wet weather make tillage (growing crops) difficult and hay hard to save, because it needs dry spells to dry.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.1] Exposure to Atlantic gales limits the land that can be farmed and the crops that can be grown.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.2] Gales and storms damage forests and power lines, because both stand exposed to the wind.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.4.3] For example, Storm Éowyn, January 2025, caused widespread damage and power cuts across the West.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.b] Relief (the shape and height of the land) divides the West in two: rugged, hard-rock uplands in the west and low limestone land in the east. Between them lies an ice-scoured lowland, and the coast is cut up by the sea.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.0] Rugged uplands in the west: the Twelve Bens and Maumturks in Connemara, and Mweelrea, Nephin and Croagh Patrick in Co. Mayo.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.1] Mweelrea, 814 m, is the highest peak in Connacht (the province containing the West), so it is the tallest of these; Nephin is 806 m and Croagh Patrick is 764 m.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.2] These are hard rocks, which resist erosion and so form the high ground: quartzite forms the Twelve Bens and Croagh Patrick; granite and mica schist form the uplands of Connemara.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.3] The ice-scoured Connemara lowland has bare rock and many small lakes, because glaciers scraped away the soil and left hollows.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.4] A low, flat to rolling limestone lowland lies in east Galway and Roscommon; Lough Corrib roughly divides it from the Connemara uplands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.5] Clew Bay's drowned drumlins are glacial hills (low hills of glacial deposit) that the sea now covers in part.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.6] The Atlantic coast is heavily indented (cut by many inlets): Killary Harbour, a fjord (a long narrow inlet carved by a glacier) on the Galway–Mayo border, Clew Bay, and Achill Island.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.5.7] Achill Island is the largest island off the Irish coast, so it is the biggest of Ireland's offshore islands; the Aran Islands are Carboniferous limestone, a continuation of the Burren limestone.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.b] Soils follow relief and rainfall. Wet, steep, acidic ground gives poor soils in the west, and only the limestone lowland in the east has good ones. This is why most of the region suits only grazing.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.0] Blanket bog (peat, a thick wet layer of partly rotted plants) covers much of west Mayo, around Bellacorick, and Connemara, where high rainfall keeps the ground waterlogged.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.1] Leached, acidic podzols (soils with nutrients washed out of the top layer) with an iron pan (a hard, iron-rich layer) lie on the uplands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.2] Heavy, poorly drained gley soils (sticky, waterlogged clay soils) lie on the drumlins and clay lowlands.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.3] Better-drained, more fertile brown earths lie on the limestone of east Galway and Roscommon.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.6.4] Thin, rocky soils cover the limestone pavement (bare, flat limestone rock) of the Aran Islands, where islanders made soil from layers of sand and seaweed on bare rock.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.b] Drainage means how water leaves the land through rivers, lakes and underground. In the West, limestone sends some water underground, while poor natural drainage elsewhere leaves land liable to flood.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.0] The River Corrib flows only a few kilometres from Lough Corrib through Galway city to Galway Bay.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.1] Lough Corrib is the largest lake in the Republic of Ireland, so it is a major feature of the drainage.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.2] Lough Mask's water flows underground to Lough Corrib near Cong, through the Cong 'dry canal', because limestone lets water drain through it.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.3] Turloughs, seasonal lakes on limestone, such as Rahasane turlough near Craughwell, Co. Galway, flood in winter and drain away underground.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.4] The River Moy reaches Killala Bay at Ballina, so Ballina lies on the river near its mouth.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.5] The River Shannon and Lough Ree form much of Roscommon's eastern boundary; the River Suck flows through Ballinasloe to the Shannon.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.6] Poor natural drainage leaves low land beside the Shannon liable to flooding.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.7.7] The Moy arterial drainage scheme (deepening and straightening a river), run by the Office of Public Works under the Arterial Drainage Act 1945, was built to reduce flooding on the Moy.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.b] Physical factors give the West a short effective grazing season (the part of the year when grass can actually be grazed) and restrict farming to grazing on most of the land.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.0] High rainfall, waterlogged and leached soils, steep uplands and exposure give a short effective grazing season.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.1] Tillage is restricted to the better-drained limestone soils of east Galway and Roscommon, because crops need drained soil.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.2] Brown earths on the limestone of east Galway and Roscommon are the region's most fertile farmland, so this is where the best farming is.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.8.3] Blanket bog and gley soils restrict most of the rest of the region to grazing, because they are too wet and poorly drained for crops.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.b] Human and economic factors add to the physical limits. Farms are small, incomes are low and the region is far from the buyers of its produce.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.0] Farms are small and often fragmented (split into several separate plots), a legacy of the pre-Famine rundale system (shared land held in scattered strips) and of population pressure on the land.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.1] The Congested Districts Board, set up in 1891, improved conditions in the poor western districts through land consolidation (joining scattered plots), piers and cottage industries.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.2] The Land Commission later consolidated holdings, which reduced fragmentation further.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.3] Part-time farming, with off-farm jobs supplementing low farm incomes, because farm income alone is not enough.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.4] Income depends heavily on subsidies and EU payments, which come from the EU's Common Agricultural Policy (CAP).
[geo-region-ireland-s2.9.5] Distance from markets and ports adds to the cost of moving livestock and produce.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.b] Because most of the land suits only grass, the West's farming is based on animals, mainly cattle and sheep.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.0] Cattle rearing is the main enterprise: suckler herds (cows that rear their own calves) rear calves, and young store cattle (young animals not yet ready for slaughter) are sold through local marts (livestock markets) to farmers in the east to be fattened.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.1] Sheep graze the hill commonage (shared hill land) of Connemara and the Mayo mountains.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.2] There is little tillage or dairying compared with the south and east, because the wet, poorly drained land is poorly suited to both.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.3] The October Fair at Ballinasloe, Co. Galway, is one of Europe's oldest horse fairs, an example of the West's animal-based farming.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.10.4] The Connemara pony is Ireland's native pony breed, another example of the region's livestock.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.b] Low farm incomes and the pressure of subsidy-driven sheep farming brought two kinds of problem: people leaving the land and damage to the hills. Schemes then tried to put these right.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.0] Low incomes, ageing farmers and few young successors lead to land abandonment, because no one is left to work the farm.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.1] CAP headage payments (payments per animal kept) from the 1980s raised sheep numbers on the hill commonage of Connemara and the Mayo mountains.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.2] Overgrazing stripped vegetation from the hills and eroded the peat.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.3] The Rural Environment Protection Scheme (REPS), from 1994, was a response to the damage.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.4] Commonage Framework Plans, from the early 2000s, set out how shared hill land is to be managed.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.5] Area-based payments paid farmers on the area of land they farm, not per animal, so there was no reward for keeping more sheep.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.11.6] Diversification (adding new income sources) into agri-tourism and forestry.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.b] Forestry is a primary activity (using natural resources) that suits land too poor for farming. Trees can be grown on wet, acidic ground where grazing pays badly.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.0] Conifer plantations, mainly Sitka spruce (native to western North America), cover marginal land (poor land where farming barely pays) and blanket bog where farming pays poorly.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.1] Coillte, founded in 1988, is the State forestry company, so it is the main planter and manager of State forests.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.2] Planting grants and premiums (payments to landowners) encourage forestry on farmland and bog.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.3] Benefits: income from timber, jobs, and carbon storage in the trees.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.12.4] Forest cover in Ireland is about 11% (2020s), which is low, so there is room for more planting.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.b] Forestry on this scale has costs for water, landscape and the stability of the ground.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.0] Acidification of streams from conifer plantations, which harms the water for fish.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.1] Monoculture (one species grown over a large area) and landscape impact of large blocks of single-species forest.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.2] Peat slide (a mass of wet peat slipping downhill) at Derrybrien, Slieve Aughty Mountains, Co. Galway, on 16 October 2003: about 450,000 m³ of peat slid during wind-farm construction on forested bog.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.13.3] The EU Court of Justice fined Ireland in 2019 over Derrybrien, so the slide had legal consequences.
[geo-region-ireland-s2.14.b] The Atlantic coast and the inland lakes and rivers give the West fishing, fish farming (aquaculture), seaweed harvesting and angling (fishing with rod and line). The sheltered bays are used for farming the sea.
