[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.b] The Greater Dublin Area (GDA) is the core region of Ireland's east coast, facing Britain and Europe across the Irish Sea. Knowing exactly which counties it includes is the starting point for every question on it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.0] The GDA is Dublin City and County Dublin, plus Counties Meath, Kildare and Wicklow. Louth, including Drogheda, is not part of it, so do not count it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.0.1] Dublin City, Fingal, South Dublin and Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown are the four local authorities (the councils that run local services) of Dublin; the single Co. Dublin county council was split into the last three in 1994.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.b] A core region is one that concentrates people, wealth and services, in contrast to peripheral regions (remote areas with fewer of these). The GDA fits this description in several ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.0] Its large, dense and growing population is the largest in the State, which gives firms customers and workers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.1] It holds most of the State's wealth, jobs, multinational investment, government and services, which means firms and workers want to be there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.2] It has the best infrastructure in the State, including the main port, airport and motorway network, so goods and people move easily.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.3] It attracts in-migrants (people moving in) from the rest of Ireland and from abroad.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.1.4] Cumulative causation (growth that feeds more growth): growth attracts firms and workers, which attracts further investment and widens the gap with peripheral regions such as the West.

[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.b] The GDA has a cool temperate oceanic climate (mild and wet because of the nearby ocean), with prevailing south-westerly winds bringing moist Atlantic air. Because of where the mountains sit, the east is drier than the west.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.0] Rain shadow (the dry area on the sheltered side of high ground): the Wicklow Mountains and the uplands further west force moist air to drop its rain on the western slopes, so the east is the driest part of Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.1] Rainfall in the east is about 750–1,000 mm a year, so it is much drier than the west; Dublin Airport records about 750 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.2] By contrast, the west receives 1,000–1,250 mm a year and some mountains exceed 2,000 mm.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.3] The south-east is the sunniest part of Ireland, which adds to the east's value for crops.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.4] Mean January temperatures in Ireland are about 4–7 °C and mean July temperatures about 14–16 °C, so winters are mild and summers cool.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.5] Being farther from the Atlantic than the west gives a slightly greater temperature range: warmer summers, colder winters and more frost.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.3.6] Heavier rain and snow fall on the Wicklow uplands than on the lowlands, because rising air cools and drops its moisture.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.b] Climate decides what the land can be used for. The dry east and the wet uplands are used in different ways.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.0] Effect on farming: the dry, relatively sunny lowlands suit tillage (growing crops such as cereals and vegetables) and horticulture (growing vegetables, fruit and flowers).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.4.1] Effect on land use: heavy upland rain supports conifer forestry and reservoirs, because the rain feeds fast-growing trees and fills water stores.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.b] Relief means the height and shape of the land. The GDA has a wide lowland to the north and west and a mountain block to the south, and each is used differently.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.0] A low-lying, gently undulating lowland covers most of Meath, Kildare and north Co. Dublin, part of the Central Lowlands.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.1] The Central Lowlands are underlain by Carboniferous limestone (a sedimentary rock), covered by glacial deposits.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.2] The Wicklow and Dublin Mountains lie to the south, with a core of the Leinster granite batholith (a huge mass of granite that cooled underground), about 400 million years old (Caledonian, the mountain-building episode that made it).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.3] Lugnaquilla, 925 m, in Co. Wicklow, is the highest peak in Leinster, so it is a good example of the region's upland relief.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.4] Kippure, about 757 m, stands on the Dublin–Wicklow border, so the mountains are close enough to the city to be used for recreation.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.5] The Great Sugar Loaf, Co. Wicklow, is a quartzite peak (quartzite is a very hard rock) that stands out from the granite.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.5.6] Effect: the lowland is easy to farm, build on and cross with roads and rail; the uplands suit forestry, water supply and recreation.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.b] Ice sheets and the sea have both shaped the region. These named landforms are the examples to use.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.0] The U-shaped valleys (steep-sided, flat-floored valleys) of Glendalough and Glenmalure were carved by glacial erosion, which is why their sides are steep and their floors flat.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.1] Esker Riada is a long ridge of sand and gravel deposited by glacial meltwater, which is why it is made of loose, rounded material.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.2] Howth Head is joined to the mainland by the Sutton tombolo (a bar of sand or shingle linking an island or headland to the land).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.6.3] North Bull Island lies in Dublin Bay, so it is an example of a coastal feature there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.b] Soil follows the rock and drainage beneath it. The lowlands have deep, fertile soils, while the granite uplands and bogs have poor ones, so soil sets how land is used.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.0] Deep, fertile brown earths (a well-developed soil rich in nutrients) on limestone boulder clay (stony clay left by ice sheets) cover Meath, Kildare and north Co. Dublin, among the best farmland in Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.1] Brown earths are well drained, which suits both tillage and grassland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.2] The Curragh, Co. Kildare, has free-draining sand and gravel from glacial outwash (material washed out from the ice by meltwater), with well-drained grassland used for horse training.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.3] The Bog of Allen, Co. Kildare, is a raised bog (a dome of peat built up from dead plants) of deep peat, which is unsuitable for farming unless drained.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.4] Blanket bog (peat covering the ground like a blanket) on granite at the Sally Gap, Co. Wicklow, is waterlogged and acidic, so it is of little use for farming.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.5] Acidic, leached podzols (grey soils with nutrients washed out of the top layer) cover the granite uplands of Wicklow, poor for farming but suitable for conifers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.7.6] Effect on land use: fertile lowland soils support farming; thin, acidic upland soils and bog are used for forestry, recreation and water catchment (land that collects rain for a river or reservoir).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.b] Drainage covers the rivers, canals and reservoirs of the region. In the GDA they matter because a large city needs a reliable water supply.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.0] The River Liffey rises in the Wicklow Mountains, flows through Kildare and enters the sea at Dublin Bay, so it links upland to city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.1] The Poulaphouca dam on the Liffey created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, used for hydro-electricity and for Dublin's drinking water.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.2] The Vartry Reservoir at Roundwood, Co. Wicklow, supplies Dublin with water, so the uplands serve the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.3] The River Boyne flows through Trim and Navan, Co. Meath, and reaches the sea near Drogheda, outside the GDA, so its mouth is not in the region.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.4] The smaller Dublin rivers, the Dodder and the Tolka, flow through the city to Dublin Bay, so they drain urban areas.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.5] The Royal and Grand Canals link Dublin to the River Shannon, so they connect the east coast to the interior.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.6] Uisce Éireann (Irish Water) has proposed a pipeline to bring water from the River Shannon to the Dublin region, because demand on local supplies is growing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.8.7] Paved surfaces in the city let rain run off quickly into rivers, raising urban flood risk.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.b] Farming does well in the GDA because physical and human advantages come together. Each factor below can be developed as a separate point in an answer.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.0] Climate: the dry, relatively sunny east suits tillage (growing crops) and horticulture (growing vegetables and similar crops).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.1] Relief: flat lowland allows large fields and mechanised farming, which cuts the cost of each crop.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.2] Soils: deep, fertile brown earths in Meath and Kildare give high-quality land for crops and grass.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.3] Market: Dublin, the largest urban area in the State, lies on the farms' doorstep, so produce has a short journey to many buyers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.4] Transport: Dublin Port and Dublin Airport export farm produce and livestock, so farmers can sell abroad.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.9.5] Policy and technology: EU and Irish agricultural policy and modern machinery support large, productive farms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.b] Farming here is large-scale and commercial, but each county specialises in something different, shaped by its soil, relief and nearness to Dublin.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.0] Large, commercial and highly productive farms dominate the lowlands, because the flat, fertile land suits them.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.1] Meath, for example: beef cattle fattening (raising cattle to slaughter weight) and tillage on large farms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.2] Kildare: bloodstock (breeding of racehorses) and racehorse training, centred on the Curragh racecourse and the Irish National Stud at Tully, Kildare town.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.3] Fingal, for example: horticulture, with field vegetables and glasshouse crops centred on Rush and Lusk.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.10.4] Wicklow: sheep grazing on the upland pasture of the Wicklow Mountains, because the steep, poor land suits sheep better than crops.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.11.b] The pressure of a huge city next door is the main problem for farmers in the GDA, along with market and policy pressures.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.11.0] Urban sprawl (spread of housing and industry outward from the city) takes farmland for housing and industry.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.11.1] High land prices, especially near Dublin, push farmers to sell land to developers.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.11.2] Part-time farming is common near the city, where farm owners depend on jobs off the farm, because farm income alone is not enough.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.11.3] Horticulture needs seasonal labour, which is costly and sometimes in short supply.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.11.4] Growers face competition from imported vegetables, which can undercut their prices.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.11.5] Changes to the rules of the EU Common Agricultural Policy (CAP, the EU's farm support scheme) alter farm incomes and force farmers to adapt.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.12.b] Forestry uses the land that is too poor to farm. In the GDA that means the Wicklow uplands, though forests cover only a small share of Ireland.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.12.0] Wicklow is among Ireland's most forested counties and is known as the Garden of Ireland, because so much of its upland is wooded.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.12.1] Conifer plantations, mainly Sitka spruce (native to western North America), grow on poor upland soils unsuited to farming.

[geo-region-ireland-s1.12.3] Wicklow Mountains National Park, set up in 1991, protects the upland landscape and provides recreation, so it serves both conservation and visitors.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.12.4] Forest recreation areas near Dublin are used by city residents for walking and cycling, because they are close to the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.12.5] Ireland's forest cover is about 11% (2020s), one of the lowest in the EU, so forestry covers a small share of the land.

[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.1] Long commutes from Meath, Kildare and Wicklow into the city add to congestion.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.2] Low-density sprawl makes households car-dependent, because homes are too spread out for frequent public transport.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.3] Responses include investment in public transport, such as DART, Luas and planned MetroLink.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.4] Cycle lanes encourage travel by bicycle, so fewer people need to drive.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.30.5] The National Transport Authority (NTA) sets out the Greater Dublin Area Transport Strategy 2022–2042.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.b] Besides tourism and transport, the GDA holds the State's main financial, technology and public services, concentrated in the capital.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.0] The International Financial Services Centre (IFSC), a district for banking and finance firms, in Custom House Docks, was set up in 1987.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.1] Google's European headquarters, on Barrow Street in Grand Canal Dock, is part of 'Silicon Docks'.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.2] The Dublin Docklands Development Authority, set up in 1997, regenerated the docklands for offices and tech firms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.31.3] Government, health and education services are concentrated in the capital, because Dublin is the seat of national government.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.32.b] Census 2022 shows how large the GDA's population is and how it is shared between the counties. The State total was about 5.1 million.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.32.0] Census 2022 counted about 2.1 million people in the GDA, about 40% of the State's population of about 5.1 million.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.32.1] Co. Dublin had about 1.45 million people; Kildare about 250,000; Meath about 220,000; Wicklow about 156,000.

CANDIDATES geo-region-ireland-s1: 1. A Core Irish Region
[geo-region-ireland-s1.32.2] Dublin city and suburbs had about 1.26 million people; the Dublin City Council area about 590,000.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.32.3] Co. Dublin has a density of about 1,600 people per km², against a State average of about 73.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.33.b] Population is not spread evenly across the region. Each high-density or low-density area has its own reason, and exam questions ask for those reasons.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.33.0] The highest densities are in Dublin city and suburbs, then in commuter towns along motorways and rail lines.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.33.1] The lowest densities are on the Wicklow uplands, because steep relief and poor soils limit settlement and farming.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.33.2] Jobs concentrated in Dublin draw population to the city and its commuter belt.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.33.3] Lowland relief and fertile soils in Meath and Kildare make settlement and building easier than on the uplands.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.33.4] Transport routes along motorways and rail lines make commuter towns accessible to Dublin jobs.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.34.b] Population dynamics means how a population changes over time. The GDA is growing steadily, and the growth has a pattern.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.34.0] Growth comes mainly from natural increase (births minus deaths): the population is young, with more births than deaths.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.34.1] In-migration comes from the rest of Ireland and from abroad, because people move for jobs.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.34.2] The age structure is young and has low dependency (few children and old people compared with working adults) compared with the West.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.34.3] The population is increasingly multicultural, because immigrants have settled in the region.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.34.4] Growth is spreading outward into the commuter counties of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow, because people move out to find cheaper housing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.b] Dublin's growth began over a thousand years ago and was shaped by successive rulers. Georgian Dublin added the planned streets that still define the centre.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.0] Dublin was founded as a Viking settlement in 841, which makes it a very old city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.1] Norman rule expanded the town in medieval times.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.2] The Wide Streets Commissioners, set up in 1757, widened and laid out Georgian streets; squares such as Merrion Square were developed by private estates.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.35.3] Georgian Dublin grew into a planned capital with wide streets and squares.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.b] A question on how one urban area developed asks for factors. For Dublin, these are site, route focus, port, capital-city functions, industry and planning.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.0] Site: Dublin grew at the lowest bridging point of the Liffey (the lowest place the river could be crossed by a bridge), sheltered by Dublin Bay.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.1] Route focus: roads and routeways radiate from the city, linking it to the rest of the region, so people and goods pass through it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.2] Port: the harbour on the Liffey gave the settlement a trading role, so the city grew by trade.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.3] Capital-city functions: national government, administration and services concentrate in the city, which creates jobs.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.4] Industry: brewing and food processing grew near the port, because ships brought in materials and carried out products.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.36.5] Planning: the Wide Streets Commissioners, the Wright Report and the Docklands Development Authority shaped the city's growth.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.b] In the 20th century the city spread outward, while its inner areas were rebuilt. Planning and regeneration (renewing run-down areas) drove both changes.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.0] Suburban expansion spread the city outward during the 20th century, because more people needed housing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.1] The Wright Report, 1967, proposed new towns at Tallaght, Clondalkin and Lucan.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.2] Ballymun high-rise flats, built in the 1960s, were demolished during regeneration from the 2000s.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.3] Temple Bar was regenerated in the 1990s as a cultural quarter, so a run-down inner-city area gained new uses.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.4] The Docklands and IFSC renewed the inner city, drawing offices and tech firms.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.37.5] Commuter towns have expanded: Navan and Ashbourne (Meath); Naas, Newbridge and Maynooth (Kildare); Bray and Greystones (Wicklow).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.b] Rapid growth has left Dublin with problems that affect both the city and the commuter towns around it.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.0] Urban sprawl spreads low-density housing into farmland and commuter towns.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.1] A housing shortage pushes up rents and house prices, because demand is greater than supply.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.2] Homelessness is a serious problem in the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.3] Commuting traffic causes congestion on the city's roads, because so many workers travel in at the same time.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.38.4] Growing demand puts pressure on the water supply.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.b] In the West, rural areas are losing people. In the counties around Dublin the opposite is true: the countryside is under pressure from the city.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.0] Rural areas of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow are under urban pressure, not decline.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.1] Commuter villages grow as city workers move out to live in the countryside, for example in Meath and Kildare.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.2] One-off houses (single houses built in the countryside) and urban-generated housing (housing built for people who work in the city) are built in rural areas.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.3] Rising land prices lead to the loss of farmland to housing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.4] Growing populations need new schools and services in rural towns and villages, so rural areas are building rather than closing.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.39.5] Contrast: the West faces rural decline and depopulation.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.b] English is the everyday language of the GDA, but Irish and many other languages are also spoken there.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.0] English is the dominant language of the GDA, so most daily life is in English.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.1] Nationally, about 1.9 million people (about 40%) said they could speak Irish in Census 2022; about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.2] Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools) are growing in number in the region, so more children are learning through Irish.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.3] Ráth Chairn, Co. Meath, is a Gaeltacht (an area where Irish is the community language) founded in 1935 by families resettled from Connemara by the Land Commission.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.40.4] Immigration has made the region multilingual; Polish is the most common foreign language spoken at home in Ireland (Census 2022).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.b] The GDA is mostly Roman Catholic, as is Ireland as a whole, but Dublin is less Catholic than the national figure.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.0] Nationally, about 69% of people are Roman Catholic and about 14% have no religion (Census 2022).
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.1] Dublin has a lower Catholic share and a higher no-religion share than the national figure, so it is more secular than the State as a whole.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.2] The Church of Ireland has a historic presence, shown by Christ Church and St Patrick's cathedrals in Dublin.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.3] Immigration has added Muslim, Orthodox and other communities; the Islamic Cultural Centre of Ireland is in Clonskeagh.
[geo-region-ireland-s1.41.4] Secularisation (the decline in the role of religion in society) has weakened the role of organised religion in the city.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s4: 4. Socio-economic regions
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.b] Geographers group places into regions by how wealthy and developed they are, not just by landscape. A socio-economic region is defined by its level of wealth and development, judged by several indicators at once, because no single figure tells the whole story.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.0] The indicators include income or GDP per head (the value of what an area produces, divided by its population), unemployment, the mix of primary, secondary and tertiary jobs, migration, age structure, education and services.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.1] Primary jobs take materials from the land and sea (farming, fishing, forestry, mining); secondary jobs make goods in factories; tertiary jobs provide services. The mix of these three is a clue to how developed a region is.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.2] The core–periphery idea: wealth, people and investment (money put into businesses and infrastructure) concentrate in a core, while the periphery (the outer, distant area) loses people and capital to it.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.3] Cumulative causation means each advantage in the core attracts more, so the gap tends to widen unless governments or the EU intervene.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.b] A core region has a strong, growing economy, high incomes, and a concentration of population, investment and decision-making. Everything else about it follows from that economic strength.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.0] It has high GDP per head and low unemployment, because jobs are plentiful.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.1] It has a large tertiary (services) sector, including finance, company headquarters and high-tech industry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.2] Most foreign investment goes to the core, because firms from abroad want its markets, workers and transport links.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.3] Net in-migration (more people arriving than leaving) of young, educated workers gives it a young population, because they move to where the jobs are.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.4] It has dense, well-developed infrastructure (the transport networks that move people and goods): motorways, airports, ports and rail lines.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.5] It has universities and research institutions, which supply skilled workers, and usually the capital city with the seat of government.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.6] Causes: accessibility, a large market and labour force, an early start, government and capital functions, and agglomeration, where firms attract more firms to the same place because they gain from being near suppliers, customers and workers.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.7] Problems of success: congestion, high house prices, urban sprawl (the city spreading outwards over farmland) and pressure on services.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.b] Three examples show what a core looks like at national and European scale. Each is a place where population, government and business cluster.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.0] Greater Dublin Area: Dublin, Kildare, Meath and Wicklow. County Dublin had about 1.45 million people in Census 2022, when the State's population was about 5.1 million.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.1] Dublin, the capital, has the seat of government, Ireland's main port (Dublin Port) and busiest airport (Dublin Airport). Its docklands host the European headquarters of many technology firms, e.g. Google. There is more on this in the Irish Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.2] Paris Basin, France: Île-de-France, the region around Paris at the basin's core, has about 12 million people. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.3] The Blue Banana: the curved belt of Europe's main core, running from south-east England and London through the Benelux countries (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg) and the Rhine valley to northern Italy (Milan).
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.b] A peripheral region is distant, physically or economically, from the core, with a weaker economy and fewer opportunities. Its problems tend to feed each other, so people leave and the region falls further behind.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.0] Remote location and poor access to markets and services, so it is costly to sell goods or reach shops, hospitals and schools.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.1] Difficult physical conditions: mountains, high rainfall, poor soils and a short growing season, which limit what farmers can produce.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.2] Dependence on primary activity (taking materials from land and sea): small farms, fishing and forestry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.3] Low incomes and higher unemployment, so people look elsewhere for work, or underemployment (people working fewer hours or in lower-skilled jobs than they want).
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.4] Net out-migration of young adults, so the people left behind are older and rural populations fall.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.5] Fewer services, and small, scattered settlements, which means services costly to provide.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.b] Because the periphery tends to fall further behind, governments and the EU act to slow the loss of people and jobs.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.0] EU regional and cohesion funds (money meant to narrow the gap between richer and poorer regions) and state agencies channel investment into the periphery.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.1] Better roads and broadband, and decentralisation of government offices (moving them out of the capital), help peripheral areas keep people and jobs.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.2] Tourism builds on the scenery and culture of peripheral areas, so it brings jobs without needing a nearby core.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.3] There is more on each example in the case-study chapters.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.b] These four examples are far from their country's or Europe's core, in the west, south and far north.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.0] West of Ireland: the Northern and Western regional assembly area. Co. Leitrim has the smallest population of any Irish county.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.1] The Mezzogiorno, southern Italy. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.2] Northern Norway: Arctic, remote and reliant on fishing.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.3] North-west Scotland: the Highlands and Islands.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.b] A less-developed region is defined by how far its development falls below an average, and the EU uses a precise cut-off to decide which regions get the most help.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.0] A less-developed region is one whose level of development is well below the national or EU average.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.1] In EU regional policy, a less-developed region has GDP per head below 75% of the EU average, and receives the most EU aid.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.2] Most less-developed regions in Europe are also peripheral: the Mezzogiorno, and much of eastern Europe such as Romania and Bulgaria.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.3] Peripheral is not the same as less-developed, because a peripheral region can lie in a rich country, such as the West of Ireland or northern Norway.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.4] Ireland was a single Objective 1 region (the category receiving the highest level of EU aid) until 1999. From 2000 to 2006 only the BMW (Border, Midland and Western) region kept Objective 1 status.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.5] At a global scale, less-developed regions are developing countries. India is the continental example. There is more on India in its own chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.6] The Mezzogiorno's development history began with the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno (Fund for the South) in 1950.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.b] A region of industrial decline grew on heavy, traditional industry and has since lost much of it. The loss then spreads through the local economy.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.0] Its traditional industries include coal mining, iron and steel, shipbuilding and textiles.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.1] Causes: coal seams exhausted or too costly to work; cheaper imports and competition from newly industrialised countries (countries that built up their factory industry more recently, often with lower costs).
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.2] Further causes: coal replaced by oil and gas; out-of-date plant (old machinery and factories); and automation (machines doing work people once did), which needs fewer workers.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.3] Effects: high unemployment, especially among older male workers; derelict factories, mines and spoil heaps (waste rock dumped from mining); and polluted land.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.4] Further effects: out-migration, falling services, and a poor image that deters new investment.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.5] Responses: retraining; clearing and cleaning derelict land; attracting new light industry and services; universities and technology parks; reuse of industrial heritage for tourism and culture; EU funds.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.b] Most examples are old coal and steel regions of north-west Europe. The Irish examples are port and shipbuilding areas.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.0] Sambre–Meuse valley, Wallonia, Belgium: coal mining and steel along the Sambre and Meuse, e.g. Charleroi on the Sambre and Liège on the Meuse.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.1] Ruhr, Germany (North Rhine-Westphalia): Europe's largest coal and steel region, so its decline was large, with cities such as Essen, Dortmund and Duisburg.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.2] Prosper-Haniel in Bottrop, the last hard-coal mine in Germany, closed in 2018. The Zollverein coal mine complex in Essen became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001 and is now a cultural centre, an example of reusing industrial heritage.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.3] South Wales coalfield, including the Rhondda valleys: coal mining collapsed in the late 20th century.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.4] Nord–Pas-de-Calais, France, is a further European example.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.5] Belfast, for example: the Harland and Wolff shipyard, which built the Titanic (launched 1911), declined greatly in the late 20th century.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.6] Dublin docklands: port jobs fell with containerisation (goods shipped in standard metal containers, which need far fewer dock workers). The International Financial Services Centre (IFSC), established in 1987 on derelict dockland, regenerated the area.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.7] There is more on Irish economic evidence in the Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Economic Activities chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.b] The clearest difference between the two region types is the kind of work people do. Compare them sector by sector: primary, secondary and tertiary.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.0] Primary, for example: large, commercial, efficient farms on good land in the core; small, fragmented farms on poor land in the periphery, with more fishing and forestry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.1] Secondary: concentrations of manufacturing and multinational investment (factories owned by firms based in other countries) in the core; few, small and often grant-aided (helped by state money) factories in the periphery.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.2] Tertiary: finance, headquarters, government, universities, and major ports and airports in the core; tourism and public services in the periphery.
