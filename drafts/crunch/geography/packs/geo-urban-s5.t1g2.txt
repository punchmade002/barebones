TARGET geo-urban-s5: 5. Urban problems in developed-world cities
[geo-urban-s5.0.b] Congestion occurs when the volume of traffic on a road network exceeds the space and capacity available, so vehicles move slowly or stop. Most causes come from how cities grew and how people travel in them.
[geo-urban-s5.0.0] Rising car ownership puts more vehicles onto streets that were laid out for far fewer, so the same road space has to carry more traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.0.1] Low-density suburbs (spread-out housing with few homes per hectare) and commuter towns (towns whose residents travel to work elsewhere) are poorly served by public transport, so most journeys are made by car.
[geo-urban-s5.0.2] Radial roads (roads that run out from the centre like spokes) converge on a historic centre with narrow streets and few river crossings, funnelling traffic into one place.
[geo-urban-s5.0.3] Journeys to work and school peak at the same times of day, so the network is filled all at once.
[geo-urban-s5.0.4] Delivery vehicles and heavy goods vehicles (HGVs, large lorries) share the same streets as commuters and shoppers.
[geo-urban-s5.0.5] Jobs and shopping have dispersed (spread out) to suburbs that can be reached only by car, so even short trips add to road traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.0.6] Historically weak investment in rail has left cities without a fast alternative to the car.
[geo-urban-s5.1.b] Congestion costs a city time, money and health. Several of its effects feed back into more congestion.
[geo-urban-s5.1.0] Workers lose time and productivity, and businesses face higher costs because deliveries and staff are delayed.
[geo-urban-s5.1.1] Commuters suffer stress and a reduced quality of life.
[geo-urban-s5.1.2] Stop-start traffic causes air and noise pollution, with carbon emissions from idling engines (engines left running while the vehicle is stationary).
[geo-urban-s5.1.3] Crowded routes lead to road accidents.
[geo-urban-s5.1.4] Buses are slowed by the same traffic, which makes public transport less attractive and so adds to car use.
[geo-urban-s5.1.5] Businesses and tourism are harmed by poor access to the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.1.6] There is less space for pedestrians and cyclists, because road space is given over to traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.2.b] Dublin is the Irish city the exams use for traffic congestion. Its congestion comes from the way it spread out and the late arrival of alternatives to the car.
[geo-urban-s5.2.0] Sprawl (the spread of low-density development at the city edge) and car dependence bring large numbers of commuters into the city every day by car.
[geo-urban-s5.2.1] Radial routes converge on the centre, and there are few crossings of the River Liffey, so traffic is squeezed onto a handful of bridges.
[geo-urban-s5.2.2] The light rail network (electric trams) arrived late: the Luas opened in 2004, after the city's roads had already become congested.
[geo-urban-s5.2.3] Port traffic formerly ran through the city centre until the Port Tunnel opened in 2006.
[geo-urban-s5.3.b] Cities tackle congestion in two ways: by giving people a better alternative to the car and by managing where and how vehicles travel. These measures are mostly drawn from Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.3.0] Public transport: DART (the electric commuter rail along the coast, opened 1984), Luas (opened 2004), commuter rail and BusConnects (a redesign of the bus network), plus the planned MetroLink, which has no date or cost yet.
[geo-urban-s5.3.1] Luas Cross City linked the Red and Green lines through the city centre in December 2017, so passengers could travel between the two lines.
[geo-urban-s5.3.2] Bus lanes and quality bus corridors give buses priority over cars, so buses are not slowed by general traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.3.3] Park and ride: car parks at the edge of the city where drivers can switch to public transport, so fewer cars enter the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.3.4] Cycle lanes and Dublin Bikes, the public bicycle scheme launched in 2009.
[geo-urban-s5.3.5] Integrated ticketing: the Leap card, introduced in 2011, allows one card to be used across different transport services.
[geo-urban-s5.3.6] Orbital roads and bypasses: the M50 orbital motorway, built in stages from 1990, takes traffic around the city instead of through it.
[geo-urban-s5.3.7] The Port Tunnel, opened in 2006, took port traffic out of the city centre; a 2007 restriction banned most five-axle heavy goods vehicles from the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.4.b] The second group of measures aims to cut the number of car journeys, by making driving dearer or by making journeys unnecessary.
[geo-urban-s5.4.0] Pedestrianisation: the busiest shopping streets are closed to traffic.
[geo-urban-s5.4.1] Parking charges and limits discourage commuters from driving into the centre.
[geo-urban-s5.4.2] Congestion charging: the London Congestion Charge, introduced in February 2003, is a daily charge to drive into central London on weekdays, and traffic in the zone fell in its early years.
[geo-urban-s5.4.3] Car sharing, remote working (working from home) and staggered working hours (different start times) spread traffic over time.
[geo-urban-s5.4.4] Land-use planning that puts homes close to jobs and transport reduces the need to travel by car.
[geo-urban-s5.5.b] Urban decay is the physical, economic and social decline of part of a city, usually the inner city (the older area close to the centre). It is decline inside the built-up area, not growth at the edge.
[geo-urban-s5.5.0] Old industries and port jobs close, removing the economic base of the inner city.
[geo-urban-s5.5.1] Better-off residents move to the suburbs, leaving a smaller and poorer population behind.
[geo-urban-s5.5.2] Buildings are left derelict (empty and falling into disrepair) and are poorly maintained, particularly social housing (homes provided by the local authority).
[geo-urban-s5.5.3] Reduced rates income (the local tax paid by businesses and property) means the local authority has less money to maintain buildings and services.
[geo-urban-s5.5.4] Dereliction attracts crime and vandalism, which deter investment and residents.
[geo-urban-s5.5.5] Road schemes and planning blight: the threat of future road building leaves property uncertain, so owners stop investing in repairs.
[geo-urban-s5.6.b] Once decay begins, its effects reinforce each other, so a run-down area is hard to recover without outside help.
[geo-urban-s5.6.0] Unemployment and poverty among residents of the affected area, because local jobs have gone.
[geo-urban-s5.6.1] Derelict sites stand empty for long periods, because owners and investors see little reason to rebuild.
[geo-urban-s5.6.2] Population decline as families leave, so fewer people remain to support local shops and services.
[geo-urban-s5.6.3] Drug and crime problems, which grow where there are empty buildings and few jobs.
[geo-urban-s5.6.4] Poor services, because fewer people and less money remain to support them.
[geo-urban-s5.6.5] Stigma: the reputation of the area discourages investment and visitors.
[geo-urban-s5.6.6] A cycle of decline, in which each problem makes the others worse.
[geo-urban-s5.7.b] Dublin's inner city shows how the loss of port jobs and the clearing of old housing left an area empty and run down.
[geo-urban-s5.7.0] Dublin's docks lost most of their jobs with containerisation (the shift to shipping goods in standard metal containers handled by machinery, needing far fewer workers) from the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-urban-s5.7.1] Inner-city tenements (old, overcrowded rented housing) were cleared, and their residents moved to the suburbs.
[geo-urban-s5.7.2] Dereliction of inner-city streets and buildings followed through the 1970s and 1980s. There is more on this in Urban planning and renewal, which covers the renewal of these areas.
[geo-urban-s5.8.b] Limerick is an Irish example of decay concentrated in social-housing estates rather than in the old centre.
[geo-urban-s5.8.0] Disadvantage (poverty and lack of opportunity) was concentrated in social-housing estates: Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston.
[geo-urban-s5.8.1] After an arson attack on two children in Moyross in 2006, the Government commissioned John Fitzgerald's report, published in 2007.
[geo-urban-s5.8.2] The report led to the Limerick Regeneration programme for Moyross, Southill, St Mary's Park and Ballinacurra Weston. There is more on its results in Urban planning and renewal.
[geo-urban-s5.9.b] Detroit, Michigan, USA, is known as 'Motor City', the home of Ford, General Motors and Chrysler. Its decline shows decay on a city-wide scale.
[geo-urban-s5.9.0] Population peaked at about 1.85 million at the 1950 US census and fell to about 640,000 at the 2020 US census.
[geo-urban-s5.9.1] Middle-class, largely white families moved to the suburbs from the 1950s, helped by freeways (high-speed urban motorways), which left the city with fewer taxpayers.
[geo-urban-s5.9.2] Car-industry jobs moved out of the city or were automated (done by machines), so the city lost its main employer base.
[geo-urban-s5.9.3] Tens of thousands of houses were abandoned, because families had left and there was no demand for them.
[geo-urban-s5.9.4] Detroit filed for bankruptcy in July 2013, the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history, and exited it in December 2014.
[geo-urban-s5.9.5] Downtown and Midtown have since seen new investment, although many areas remain derelict.
[geo-urban-s5.10.b] Urban sprawl is the uncontrolled outward spread of low-density housing and other development over rural land at the edge of a city. It is growth at the edge, unlike urban decay, which is decline inside the city.
[geo-urban-s5.10.0] Population growth, high prices in the inner city, and demand for houses with gardens push development outwards.
[geo-urban-s5.10.1] Car ownership and motorways make distant sites accessible, so people can live far from where they work.
[geo-urban-s5.10.2] Planning that zoned greenfield land (land never built on before) for development, and developers' preference for cheaper greenfield sites, drive building at the edge.
[geo-urban-s5.10.3] Tax incentives during the Celtic Tiger boom (the period of rapid Irish economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s) encouraged building.
[geo-urban-s5.11.b] Sprawl spreads people thinly over a large area, which is costly for the people who live there and for the city they left behind.
[geo-urban-s5.11.0] Long commutes and congestion, and car dependence, because distances are too long to walk or cycle and public transport is poor.
[geo-urban-s5.11.1] Loss of farmland and habitats, because rural land is built on. There is more on this in Urban land use and planning.
[geo-urban-s5.11.2] Costly services, such as roads, water and schools, spread thinly over a large area, so each household costs more to serve.
[geo-urban-s5.11.3] Dormitory towns, whose residents sleep there and work elsewhere, leaving little local activity.
[geo-urban-s5.11.4] Decline of the city centre as people and spending move out, and higher carbon emissions from longer car journeys.
[geo-urban-s5.12.b] Dublin is the Irish example of sprawl, and its sprawl left a visible legacy when the property market collapsed.
[geo-urban-s5.12.0] Dublin's sprawl has extended into the commuter counties beyond the city boundary.
[geo-urban-s5.12.1] A Government survey in 2010 found almost 3,000 unfinished housing estates, known as ghost estates, after the 2008 property crash (the sudden collapse in house prices and building).
[geo-urban-s5.13.b] Solutions either stop the city spreading outwards or make better use of land already built on.
[geo-urban-s5.13.0] Green belts: a protected ring of open land around the city to stop its spread.
[geo-urban-s5.13.1] Compact growth and brownfield development (reusing land that was built on before), set out in the National Planning Framework, to build on land within the city.
[geo-urban-s5.13.2] Higher densities of housing near public transport, so that fewer people need cars.
[geo-urban-s5.13.3] New towns planned as self-contained settlements with their own services, so residents do not have to travel far.
[geo-urban-s5.13.4] Regeneration of inner-city sites that are vacant or derelict, so growth is directed inwards.
[geo-urban-s5.14.b] A community is a group of neighbours who know each other and share local life. In some urban areas the way housing was planned or built stops this from forming.
[geo-urban-s5.14.0] New estates are built quickly without schools, shops, community centres or public transport, so residents have nowhere to meet.
[geo-urban-s5.14.1] High-rise flats have no shared space where neighbours can meet.
[geo-urban-s5.14.2] Long commutes leave little time for local life.
[geo-urban-s5.14.3] Transient renting populations (tenants who move often) do not put down roots.
[geo-urban-s5.14.4] Old inner-city communities were demolished when tenements were cleared, breaking up established networks of family and neighbours.
[geo-urban-s5.14.5] Social segregation separates people by income and class into different areas.
[geo-urban-s5.15.b] When a place has no shared life, the people in it feel the lack, and the area itself suffers.
[geo-urban-s5.15.0] Isolation and loneliness among residents who do not know their neighbours.
[geo-urban-s5.15.1] Anti-social behaviour, which is harder to deal with where neighbours do not know each other.
[geo-urban-s5.15.2] Little sense of belonging or local organisation, so there are few residents' groups or clubs to take action on local problems.
[geo-urban-s5.16.b] Examples come from Dublin and from Paris. The responses aim to build the facilities and involvement that were missing.
[geo-urban-s5.16.0] Ballymun's tower blocks in Dublin: seven 15-storey towers built 1966 to 1969, demolished 2004 to 2015.
[geo-urban-s5.16.1] Commuter estates and the ghost estates of Dublin's commuter counties.
[geo-urban-s5.16.2] Paris banlieues (the suburbs on the edge of Paris): large grands ensembles (estates built in the 1950s to 1970s) such as Sarcelles, with high unemployment and the riots that began in Clichy-sous-Bois in October 2005.
[geo-urban-s5.16.3] Responses: community facilities built alongside housing, with phasing conditions (planning rules) so that schools and shops come before the homes are occupied.
[geo-urban-s5.16.4] Responses: regeneration with resident participation, mixed tenure (a mix of owned, rented and social homes in one area), and sports clubs and family resource centres.
[geo-urban-s5.17.b] Urban heritage includes historic buildings, streetscapes (the look of a street as a whole), archaeological sites and monuments, together with their setting. It is valuable in three ways.
[geo-urban-s5.17.0] Identity: heritage gives a city and its residents a sense of their history and place.
[geo-urban-s5.17.1] Tourism: historic buildings and monuments attract visitors and the income they bring.
[geo-urban-s5.17.2] Economic value: restored heritage buildings can support shops, hotels and cultural venues.
[geo-urban-s5.18.b] Heritage is easily lost because the pressures of a growing city work against old buildings.
[geo-urban-s5.18.0] Demolition for offices and roads, as when historic buildings are cleared for new development.
[geo-urban-s5.18.1] Neglect and dereliction of old buildings, so that without repair they decay and may have to be demolished.
[geo-urban-s5.18.2] Inappropriate new buildings, with heights that are out of scale with the historic streetscape.
[geo-urban-s5.18.3] Traffic vibration and pollution, which damage old buildings and monuments.
[geo-urban-s5.18.4] Tourist pressure, which can wear down historic sites and crowd out local residents.
[geo-urban-s5.19.b] Dublin has both losses and successes. The losses show the threats above, and the reuse examples show heritage put to work.
[geo-urban-s5.19.0] Lower Fitzwilliam Street: the ESB (Ireland's electricity company) demolished a row of 16 Georgian houses in 1965 to build its head office. That office block has itself since been demolished and replaced.
[geo-urban-s5.19.1] Wood Quay: one of the largest Viking-age sites in Europe. Despite protests and court cases in the late 1970s, Dublin Corporation (the city council) built its civic offices on the site.
[geo-urban-s5.19.2] 14 to 17 Moore Street, where the 1916 leaders spent their last hours before surrender, was declared a national monument in 2007.
[geo-urban-s5.19.3] Positive reuse: Temple Bar was regenerated in the 1990s, and Docklands warehouses have been reused for new purposes.
[geo-urban-s5.20.b] Heritage is protected by law, by voluntary groups and by international listing.
[geo-urban-s5.20.0] The Planning and Development Act 2000 created the Record of Protected Structures (a list of buildings the law protects) and Architectural Conservation Areas (whole areas protected for their character).
[geo-urban-s5.20.1] National monuments are protected by the State.
[geo-urban-s5.20.2] An Taisce (the National Trust for Ireland), founded in 1948, campaigns for the protection of heritage.
[geo-urban-s5.20.3] UNESCO World Heritage listing (international recognition of sites of outstanding value): Ireland's two World Heritage Sites are Brú na Bóinne, listed in 1993, and Skellig Michael, listed in 1996. Neither is urban.
[geo-urban-s5.20.4] International urban examples: the Old and New Towns of Edinburgh, listed by UNESCO in 1995, and the Historic Centre of Rome, listed in 1980.
[geo-urban-s5.21.b] Heritage attracts visitors in smaller cities as well as in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.21.0] Kilkenny's Medieval Mile attracts heritage tourism to a smaller Irish city. There is more on its medieval history in Pre-historic and historic settlement.
[geo-urban-s5.22.b] Environmental quality is how clean, quiet and pleasant an urban area is. Air quality is the first aspect, and Dublin's ban on smoky coal is the Irish example of a response to air pollution.
[geo-urban-s5.22.0] Sources of urban air pollution: smoke from coal fires, and traffic emissions of nitrogen dioxide and particulates (tiny solid particles in the air).
[geo-urban-s5.22.1] Dublin's severe winter smog (smoke and fog mixed) in the 1980s led to the ban on smoky coal in Dublin from 1 September 1990.
[geo-urban-s5.22.2] Research after the ban found that black smoke levels and winter death rates fell.
[geo-urban-s5.22.3] The ban on smoky coal was extended nationwide in October 2022.
[geo-urban-s5.23.b] Cities produce large amounts of sewage and polluted runoff, which must be treated before it reaches rivers and the sea.
[geo-urban-s5.23.0] The Ringsend wastewater treatment plant treats most of Dublin's sewage and discharges into Dublin Bay.
[geo-urban-s5.23.1] The Ringsend plant is being upgraded, so that it can keep treating the sewage of a growing city.
[geo-urban-s5.23.2] Storm overflows: heavy rain can send mixed sewage and surface water into urban rivers and the bay, because the drains cannot carry the extra volume.
[geo-urban-s5.23.3] Urban rivers carry pollution from paved surfaces, roads and drains.
[geo-urban-s5.24.b] Waste that is not recycled or treated becomes litter or goes to landfill, which harms the look and quality of an urban area. Ireland has used charges, recycling and energy recovery to cut it.
[geo-urban-s5.24.0] Litter and landfill: waste that is not recycled or treated goes to landfill (buried in the ground).
[geo-urban-s5.24.1] Plastic bag levy: introduced in March 2002 at 15 cent, after which plastic bag use fell sharply, showing a charge can change behaviour.
[geo-urban-s5.24.2] Recycling schemes reduce the amount of waste sent to landfill, because materials are reused instead of buried.
[geo-urban-s5.24.3] Poolbeg (Dublin Waste-to-Energy) incinerator, operating since 2017, burns residual waste (what is left after recycling) to generate energy.
[geo-urban-s5.25.b] Not all pollution is chemical. Noise and an ugly environment also lower the quality of life in a city.
[geo-urban-s5.25.0] Noise from traffic, construction and night-time activity affects residents, for example by disturbing sleep.
[geo-urban-s5.25.1] Visual pollution: graffiti, poor signage and unsightly derelict buildings reduce the quality of the streetscape.
[geo-urban-s5.25.2] Derelict sites: the Derelict Sites levy is a charge on owners of derelict property to encourage its reuse.
[geo-urban-s5.26.b] Green space gives wildlife a home and gives residents somewhere to relax. Dublin has several examples.
[geo-urban-s5.26.0] Parks provide habitats and recreation: Phoenix Park, about 700 hectares, established 1662.
[geo-urban-s5.26.1] Canals provide green corridors (strips of greenery that wildlife can move along) through the city: the Royal Canal and the Grand Canal.
[geo-urban-s5.26.2] North Bull Island, in Dublin Bay, is a green space beside the city.
[geo-urban-s5.26.3] Loss of green space to infill: small gaps between buildings are built on, reducing open space for wildlife and residents.
[geo-urban-s5.27.b] Building over land changes how water and heat behave in a city, which causes two problems.
[geo-urban-s5.27.0] Urban flooding: paved surfaces stop rain soaking into the ground, so runoff (water flowing over the surface) rises quickly into rivers and drains.
[geo-urban-s5.27.1] Hurricane Charley, in August 1986, caused severe flooding of the River Dodder in Dublin.
[geo-urban-s5.27.2] Urban heat island: built-up areas are warmer than the surrounding countryside because buildings and paving absorb and hold heat.

CANDIDATES geo-population-migration-s6: 6. Rural-to-urban migration in developed and developing regions
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.b] Rural-to-urban migration means people leaving the countryside (the donor region, which gives up people) for cities (the receiver region, which takes them in). It is one of the biggest forces reshaping where the world's people live.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.0] Urbanisation is the growing share of people living in towns, while urban growth is the rise in the number of people living in towns. The two are different measures, so keep them apart.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.1] Rural-to-urban migration is the main driver of urbanisation in developing countries (poorer countries with fast-growing populations). Natural increase in the cities, meaning births outnumbering deaths, is the other driver.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.2] Over half the world's people have lived in urban areas since about 2007 (UN), so the world passed the point where towns held more people than the countryside.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.3] About 68% of the world's people are expected to be urban by 2050 (UN, 2018 projection), which shows the trend is still rising.
[geo-population-migration-s6.0.4] The fastest urban growth is now in Asia and Africa, where megacities (cities with very large populations) are forming.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.b] Push factors are the conditions that make people want to leave the countryside. Most come down to too little work, income or service in the place they live.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.0] Lack of jobs and low farm incomes leave rural people with too little money to live on, so they look for work elsewhere.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.1] Mechanisation of farming (using machines instead of workers) reduces the labour needed on the land, so fewer people are employed in farming.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.2] Small, subdivided holdings (farms split into ever smaller pieces) cannot support a family, so family members move away.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.3] Poor services, including schools, hospitals, broadband and transport, make rural life harder and push people toward places that have them.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.4] Drought, famine and land degradation (the land losing its ability to grow crops) push people out in the developing world.
[geo-population-migration-s6.1.5] Isolation and lack of social life push people out, because rural life offers little to do and few people to meet.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.b] Pull factors are the attractions that draw people to cities. They are largely the opposite of the push factors, which is why movement from the countryside is so steady.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.0] Jobs in industry and services (work such as shops, offices and banks) give people a way to earn a living.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.1] Higher wages in the city make the move worth the cost.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.2] Third-level education (university and college) is found in cities, so young people go there to study.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.3] Better health care and services are available in cities, where there are more hospitals and facilities.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.4] Entertainment and the 'bright lights' of the city attract young people looking for a more exciting social life.
[geo-population-migration-s6.2.5] Existing family networks create chain migration, which means one person moves and relatives and neighbours follow because someone is already there to help them settle.
[geo-population-migration-s6.3.b] Not all movement is toward cities. In developed countries some people now move the other way.
[geo-population-migration-s6.3.0] Counter-urbanisation is people moving out of cities to towns and the countryside, and it is found in developed countries (richer countries).
[geo-population-migration-s6.3.1] Remote working since COVID-19 lets people live outside cities while working in them, because they no longer need to be near the office every day.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.b] Ireland was a rural country until the later 20th century, and its population then concentrated in cities. This is the main developed-world case study.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.0] Census 1971 was the first census in which more people lived in towns than in rural areas, so the urban share crossed half in that year.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.1] Census 2016: about 63% of the population was urban.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.2] Flight from the land happened because farming mechanised and small farms became unviable (could no longer earn enough to survive), so people moved to cities.
[geo-population-migration-s6.4.3] Industrial and service jobs concentrated in Dublin and the cities, which pulled the people who left farming toward them.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.b] Growth is concentrated in Dublin, with the commuter counties (counties near Dublin where people live and travel to work) and the regional cities also growing. Be careful with which Dublin figure you use.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.0] Census 2022: the State had about 5.15 million people.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.1] Co. Dublin had about 1.46 million; Dublin city and suburbs had about 1.26 million; the Greater Dublin Area (Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow) had about 2.1 million, about 40% of the State's population.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.2] Dublin is the primate city, a city much larger than any other in its country, so it dominates Irish life.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.3] Commuter towns, where many residents travel daily to work in Dublin, include Navan, Drogheda, Naas and Newbridge.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.4] Regional cities (city and suburbs): Cork about 220,000; Limerick about 100,000; Galway city about 85,000; Waterford about 60,000.
[geo-population-migration-s6.5.5] Dublin's earlier planned growth created the new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown under the Myles Wright plan of 1967.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.b] Receiving large numbers of migrants brings Dublin both benefits and strains. The positives come from having many people in one place, and the negatives from fitting them all in.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.0] A large labour pool (many workers available to hire) attracts multinationals and service firms.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.1] Economies of scale support transport, universities and hospitals, because a large population makes these services cheaper per person to provide.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.2] A rich cultural life develops where so many people live.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.3] Housing shortages, high rents and prices, and homelessness occur because homes cannot be built as fast as people arrive.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.4] Urban sprawl (the city spreading outward over farmland) reaches into Meath, Kildare and Wicklow.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.5] Long commutes and traffic congestion result when workers live far out and travel in.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.6] Pressure on water supply and other services builds as the population grows.
[geo-population-migration-s6.6.7] High-rise and infill development pressure (building taller, and building on gaps within the city) grows because land is scarce.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.b] Rural decline in Ireland is now local, in remote parishes and townlands (the smallest rural land divisions), rather than county-wide. The figures below show how uneven population is across the country.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.0] Census 2022: the population of every county grew between 2016 and 2022, so decline is within counties rather than whole counties.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.1] Leitrim had about 155,000 people in 1841 and about 35,000 in Census 2022, the least populous county.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.2] Mayo had about 138,000 people and about 25 people per km² in Census 2022; Co. Dublin had about 1,600 per km², so the contrast in density is very large.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.3] An ageing population and higher dependency ratios (more people too old or too young to work for each worker) follow because young adults leave.
[geo-population-migration-s6.7.4] Fewer young adults and lower birth rates follow, since the people most likely to have children are the ones who move away.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.b] When young people leave, the services and community life that depended on them go too. Migration is not all loss for the donor region, though.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.0] Closure of schools, post offices, Garda stations, shops and pubs, and derelict houses, happen because too few customers and users remain to keep them open.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.1] Loss of community and sporting clubs, with GAA teams amalgamating (joining with neighbouring clubs to field a team), and farms with no successor, follow as young people leave.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.2] Cultural loss, including the decline of the Gaeltacht (the Irish-speaking regions), occurs as Irish speakers move away.
[geo-population-migration-s6.8.3] Positive effects: less pressure on land, remittances (money sent home by migrants) and returning migrants, and some farm consolidation (neighbouring farms joined into larger, more viable ones).
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.b] The State has tried to slow decline in the regions and to spread growth more evenly, with plans and agencies set up between 1980 and 2018.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.0] The National Spatial Strategy (2002) set out gateways and hubs (larger and smaller centres chosen to attract growth) to spread growth.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.1] The decentralisation programme, announced in 2003, moves public service jobs out of Dublin.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.2] The Western Development Commission has been a statutory body (set up by law) since 1999.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.3] CLÁR, a programme of investment in depopulated rural areas, was launched in 2001.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.4] Údarás na Gaeltachta, founded in 1980, creates jobs in the Gaeltacht.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.5] The National Planning Framework (Project Ireland 2040), published in February 2018, plans for about 1 million more people by 2040, with about half of growth in the five cities (Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford) and their suburbs, and with the four cities other than Dublin growing faster than Dublin.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.6] The Town and Village Renewal Scheme and rural broadband with remote working hubs support rural areas, since remote work lets people stay in the countryside.
[geo-population-migration-s6.9.7] Limitation: rural areas and regional cities still depend on the jobs and services that drew people to Dublin, so the pull of Dublin remains.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.b] Two European countries show the same donor and receiver pattern: a poorer region loses people to richer cities, and one capital dominates its country.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.0] Italy: after the Second World War, people moved from the Mezzogiorno (southern Italy) to the industrial cities of the north, Turin and Milan, and abroad to Germany, Switzerland and Belgium.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.1] The Mezzogiorno has about 20 million people (2020s), about one-third of Italy's population.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.2] Depopulating southern villages responded with €1 house schemes (selling empty houses very cheaply to attract new residents), such as Sambuca di Sicilia.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.3] France: Paris dominates the country; Jean-François Gravier's Paris et le désert français (1947) described the regions outside Paris as a French desert.
[geo-population-migration-s6.10.4] Île-de-France (the region around Paris) has about 12 million people (2020s), about 18% of France's population.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.b] Migration from poor rural states to the big cities fills slums and sends remittances back to the villages.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.0] Migrants come from poorer rural states, Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, to Mumbai, Delhi and Kolkata.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.1] Push factors: small holdings, debt, landlessness (owning no land to farm) and drought.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.2] Pull factors: jobs in construction, factories and the informal sector (work without contracts or regular pay, such as street trading).
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.3] Census 2011: about 31% of Indians were urban, up from about 17% in 1951; about 833 million people (about 69%) were rural.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.4] Census 2011 urban agglomerations (a city with its built-up surroundings): Greater Mumbai about 18.4 million; Delhi about 16.3 million; Kolkata about 14.1 million.
[geo-population-migration-s6.11.5] Bihar is India's densest state, at about 1,100 per km², which adds to the pressure to leave.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.b] The cities cannot house everyone who arrives, so slums grow. Villages left behind lose workers but gain money sent home.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.0] About 65 million people lived in slums (crowded areas of poor housing).
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.1] Dharavi, Mumbai, is one of Asia's largest slums, about 2 km² near the city centre, with hundreds of thousands of residents and many small industries such as recycling, leather, pottery and garments. Kolkata's slums are called bustees.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.2] Receiver impacts: pressure on water, sanitation and transport; informal work; and a large supply of cheap labour.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.3] Donor impacts: male out-migration leaves women, children and the old to farm, and working-age labour is lost.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.4] Remittances (money sent home) fund houses, education and farm inputs in donor villages.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.5] India is the world's largest recipient of remittances, mostly from international migrants, so the figure does not measure rural-to-urban flows directly.
[geo-population-migration-s6.12.6] In the March 2020 COVID-19 lockdown, millions of migrant workers walked or travelled home from the cities.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.b] Brazil's migration runs from the drought-prone North-East to the south-east, and Brazil is now highly urbanised.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.0] Migrants from the sertão, the drought-prone semi-arid interior of the North-East (nordestinos), moved to São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro; some also helped build Brasília, the capital from 1960.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.1] Census 2022: Brazil had about 203 million people, about 87% of them urban (2020s).
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.2] São Paulo city had about 11.5 million people (Census 2022), and over 20 million in its metropolitan region; it is Brazil's largest city and industrial centre.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.3] Favelas (shanty towns) grow on hillsides and at the urban edge; Rocinha in Rio de Janeiro is one of Brazil's largest.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.4] Informal work is common in the favelas, and some favelas have crime and gang violence.
[geo-population-migration-s6.13.5] The North-East, the donor region, loses young people.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.b] In China the government's household registration rules shape who can settle in a city, so migrants often work in cities without the rights of residents.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.0] The hukou (household registration) system, introduced in 1958, ties public services to a person's registered home, so a migrant is not fully entitled to services in the city.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.1] Nearly 300 million rural migrant workers (2023), many of them in coastal cities such as Shenzhen, Guangzhou and Shanghai.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.2] Shenzhen grew from a small town to a city of over 17 million after becoming China's first Special Economic Zone (an area with special rules to attract business) in 1980.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.3] China's urban share passed 50% in 2011.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.4] Rural villages are left with 'left-behind' children and elderly people, because working-age adults have gone to the cities.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.5] Migrants lack full access to urban schools, health care and housing, because their services are tied to their registered home.
[geo-population-migration-s6.14.6] Hukou reform is gradual.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.b] The scale, speed and capacity of cities differ between the two kinds of region, so the impacts differ.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.0] In developed regions migration is slower, and cities can largely absorb it with planned housing and services.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.1] In developed regions the donor problem is ageing rural decline.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.2] In developing regions migration is fast and very large; cities cannot provide housing, jobs or services, so slums and the informal sector grow.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.3] In developing regions donor villages depend on remittances and lose working-age men.
[geo-population-migration-s6.15.4] Urban growth in developing cities comes from both migration and high natural increase among young migrants, because young adults have many children.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s3: 3. Environmental pollution
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.b] Pollution is a problem caused by people, and it can be small or enormous in scale. Knowing how to define it, classify it and place it on a scale lets you tackle any pollution question.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.0] Pollution is the release by human activity of substances or energy (heat, noise, light) into the environment in amounts that harm living things, health or property. Harm is what makes a release pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.1] Types of pollution: air, water (fresh and marine), land and soil, noise and visual. For example, oil in the sea is water pollution and a motorway's roar is noise pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.2] Scales: local (one river or town), national (a country's water quality) and global (the whole atmosphere or ocean). The scale tells you how many people and places are affected.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.0.3] Transboundary pollution (pollution that crosses national borders) means the place it harms may be far from its source. Acid rain, rain made acidic by gases from burning fuels, is the European example, taught in the note Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.b] Pollution can be traced back to its source in two ways, depending on whether it leaves from one spot or from many. This matters because a single outlet is easier to find and control than thousands of small ones.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.0] Point sources come from one identifiable place, such as a factory pipe or a sewage outfall (the pipe where sewage enters a river or the sea).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.1] Diffuse sources come from many places at once, such as runoff (rainwater washing substances off the land) from many farms, or traffic.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.1.2] Economic activities that pollute include agriculture, manufacturing, energy generation, transport, mining, tourism, households and urban waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.b] Global warming is the main global-scale example of pollution. It starts with a natural process that human activity has strengthened.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.0] The natural greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases (gases in the atmosphere that trap heat) let incoming short-wave solar radiation through but absorb outgoing long-wave radiation (heat given off by the earth), which keeps the earth habitable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.2.1] The enhanced greenhouse effect: human activity adds greenhouse gases to the atmosphere, which trap more heat and so warm the planet further.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.b] Each greenhouse gas has its own human sources, and many of them are economic activities. Knowing which activity gives which gas lets you explain the causes of warming.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.0] CO2 (carbon dioxide) comes from burning fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas), deforestation (clearing forests, which removes trees that absorb CO2) and cement production.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.1] Methane comes from cattle and sheep, rice paddies, landfill (rubbish buried in the ground) and gas leaks.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.2] Nitrous oxide comes from nitrogen fertilisers and slurry (liquid animal manure).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.3.3] CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons, man-made gases) and other fluorinated gases are also greenhouse gases.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.b] Scientists measure warming in several independent ways, and they all point the same way. Each measurement below is a piece of evidence you can quote.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.0] Atmospheric CO2 was about 280 ppm (parts per million) before the Industrial Revolution. It passed 400 ppm in the mid-2010s and was over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s. These readings are measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, which has recorded them since 1958.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.1] Global average surface temperature has risen by about 1.1 to 1.2 °C since the late 1800s (IPCC, the UN's climate science panel).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.2] 2024 was the warmest year on record and the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels (Copernicus, the EU's Earth observation programme).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.3] Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.4] Global sea level rose by about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.4.5] Glaciers (large masses of ice on land) are retreating worldwide, which is a visible sign that temperatures are rising.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.b] A warmer atmosphere changes oceans, ice, weather and ecosystems, and these changes harm people and wildlife. The effects are worst for places that are low-lying or already vulnerable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.0] Sea-level rise, from thermal expansion of seawater (water expands as it warms) and melting land ice, threatens low-lying countries and deltas: Bangladesh (the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta), the Maldives, Tuvalu and Kiribati.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.1] More intense heatwaves, droughts, floods and storms. The Sahel (the dry belt south of the Sahara) has suffered droughts.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.2] Glacier retreat threatens water supplies, because many places depend on meltwater from glaciers.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.3] Coral bleaching (corals losing their colour and dying when seas get too warm): the Great Barrier Reef, Australia, has suffered repeated mass bleaching since the late 1990s.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.5.4] Species shifts and extinctions, crop failures in vulnerable regions, and climate refugees (people forced to leave their homes because of climate change).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.b] Ireland is not exempt from global warming. Its temperature has risen, and the effects are felt on land, at the coast and at sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.0] Ireland's average temperature has risen by about 0.9 °C since 1900 (EPA and Met Éireann, the national weather service).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.1] Warmer temperatures bring wetter winters and more flooding, a risk of summer drought, stronger storms and coastal erosion (the sea wearing away the coast).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.2] Effects also include changes to farming and to marine species, and a threat to low-lying coastal towns.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.6.3] Storm Éowyn, in January 2025, caused record damage and power cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.b] Ireland's emissions come mainly from a few sectors, and farming dominates. This is why so many Irish climate measures target agriculture.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.0] Agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, the largest share, mainly methane and nitrous oxide from cattle, slurry and fertiliser.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.1] Transport, mainly fuel burned by vehicles, is the next largest source, at about a fifth of emissions (EPA, early 2020s), followed by energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.7.2] Ireland's emissions per person are high for an EU country, which is largely because of its large farming sector.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.b] Warming is a global problem, so governments have tried to answer it through international agreements. Each one built on the one before.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.0] The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) was agreed at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in 1992.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.1] The Kyoto Protocol, agreed in 1997 and in force from 2005, set legally binding cuts for developed countries only. The USA never ratified it (it signed but did not formally approve it), so it was not a full party.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.2] The Paris Agreement, 2015, aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C. Each country sets its own national plan (nationally determined contribution).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.8.3] Countries meet every year at COP meetings (conferences of the countries that signed the UNFCCC). COP30 was held at Belém, Brazil, in November 2025.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.b] The EU and Ireland have turned the global agreements into laws, targets and charges. Together they put a price on emissions and set deadlines for cutting them.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.0] The EU Emissions Trading System began in 2005. Power stations and heavy industry must hold an allowance (a permit) for each tonne of CO2 they emit, which gives them a cost reason to cut emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.1] The European Green Deal (2019) aims for a climate-neutral EU by 2050 (no net emissions). Its Fit for 55 package aims to cut net emissions by at least 55% by 2030, compared with 1990.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.2] Ireland's Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 requires a 51% cut in emissions by 2030, compared with 2018, and net zero by 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.3] Sectoral ceilings (limits on each sector's emissions, set in 2022) require agriculture to cut its emissions by 25% by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.4] An Irish carbon tax (a charge on fuels based on their CO2) was introduced in 2010 and is rising in stages towards €100 a tonne by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.9.5] The Climate Action Plan aims to retrofit (upgrade with insulation and heating) 500,000 homes to a B2 energy rating by 2030, and includes renewables, electric vehicles and measures for agriculture.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.b] Governments cannot cut emissions alone, because homes, transport and food are all household choices. Everyday changes add up.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.0] Improve energy efficiency and insulate homes, so less fuel is burned for heating.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.1] Use public transport, cycling and walking in place of car journeys, so fewer fossil fuels are burned.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.2] Reduce food waste, because rotting food in landfill gives off methane.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.10.3] Switch to renewable heating, because it does not burn fossil fuels such as oil and gas, so it gives off less CO2.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.b] Agricultural water pollution is the Irish national case study of water pollution. Nutrients meant to grow crops end up in rivers and lakes, where they set off a chain of events called eutrophication.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.0] Cause: nitrogen and phosphorus from slurry, manure and chemical fertilisers wash off fields into rivers and lakes, especially after heavy rain or when spread on wet or frozen ground. Silage effluent (liquid from stored grass) and sewage from towns and septic tanks (private sewage tanks) add more.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.1] Eutrophication (water becoming overloaded with nutrients): the nutrients feed rapid algal growth, forming algal blooms (dense layers of algae).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.2] When the algae die, bacteria decomposing them use up the dissolved oxygen, so fish and other life suffocate in fish kills.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.11.3] The water turns green and smells. Some blue-green algae are toxic.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.b] Polluted water harms wildlife first, and then the people and businesses that rely on clean water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.0] Loss of fish and other wildlife, such as in a fish kill, and damage to angling and tourism, because few people visit or fish in green, smelly water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.1] Drinking water needs costly treatment, and nitrates (nitrogen compounds from fertiliser) contaminate groundwater (water held underground).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.12.2] Coastal waters, including estuaries (where rivers meet the sea), are affected by algal mats, because rivers carry the nutrients downstream.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.b] The EPA, which monitors water quality, shows that pollution is a national problem and not only a local one. Farming is the main pressure.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.0] An EPA assessment for 2016 to 2021 found that only about half of Ireland's rivers, lakes and estuaries were in good or high ecological status.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.1] Agriculture is the most common pressure on water quality, followed by urban wastewater (sewage from towns), because fertiliser and slurry wash into streams.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.13.2] Milk quotas (limits on how much milk farms could produce) were abolished on 1 April 2015. The dairy herd then grew strongly, especially in the south and east, and raised nitrogen levels there.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.b] Lough Neagh is a real example of eutrophication, and the 2023 bloom shows the processes above in action. Treat it as a single event, and do not claim it has been solved.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.0] Lough Neagh, Northern Ireland, is the largest lake on the island of Ireland. In summer 2023 a huge toxic blue-green algal bloom covered much of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.1] The bloom was blamed on nutrient pollution from farm runoff, sewage and septic tanks, together with warm weather and invasive zebra mussels that clear the water, letting more light reach the algae.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.14.2] Lough Neagh supplies a large share of Northern Ireland's drinking water, so a toxic bloom threatens a key water supply.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.b] Reducing the pollution means keeping nutrients on the land and out of water. Most of the rules limit when, where and how much farmers spread.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.0] The EU Nitrates Directive (1991) and Ireland's Nitrates Action Programme set closed periods each winter when slurry and fertiliser may not be spread, because rain would wash them straight into rivers.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.1] The Nitrates Action Programme also limits nitrogen per hectare, requires slurry storage, and requires buffer strips (strips of vegetation) beside watercourses, which trap nutrients before they reach the water.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.2] The EU Water Framework Directive (2000) aims for good status for all waters, which gives countries a target to work towards.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.15.3] Other measures: upgrading urban wastewater treatment, EPA monitoring of water quality, and farm advisory programmes.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.16.b] Oil in the sea comes from accidents and from deliberate or careless releases. It is a global pollution problem because oil is moved and drilled for all over the world.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.16.0] Tanker accidents, drilling blow-outs (uncontrolled releases of oil from a well), illegal tank washing at sea, and pipeline leaks release oil into the sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.b] The Exxon Valdez is a tanker accident that caused huge damage to a wild coast and led to a change in law. Remember it is in Alaska, not the Gulf of Mexico.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.0] The tanker ran aground on Bligh Reef, Prince William Sound, Alaska, on 24 March 1989.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.1] About 11 million US gallons (about 37,000 tonnes) of crude oil were spilled, and about 2,000 km of coastline was oiled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.2] Hundreds of thousands of seabirds and thousands of sea otters were killed, and the herring fishery collapsed.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.17.3] The response was the US Oil Pollution Act 1990, which required double-hulled tankers (tankers with two layers of steel, so a hit is less likely to leak) in US waters.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.b] The Prestige shows that a tanker accident can damage the coasts of several countries and the fishing that depends on them.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.0] The single-hulled tanker Prestige broke in two and sank off Galicia, north-west Spain, in November 2002. It spilled tens of thousands of tonnes of heavy fuel oil.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.1] Oil reached the Costa da Morte and the coasts of Spain, Portugal and France. Galician shellfish and fishing industries were closed for months.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.18.2] The disaster sped up the EU's phase-out of single-hulled tankers, because a single hull is more likely to leak after damage.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.b] Deepwater Horizon is an example of oil pollution from drilling, and not from a tanker. It is in the Gulf of Mexico.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.0] The Deepwater Horizon rig (an offshore drilling platform), operated by BP, exploded on 20 April 2010 in the Gulf of Mexico. Eleven workers were killed.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.19.1] About 4.9 million barrels of oil were spilled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.b] Whatever the cause, oil spills have similar effects, and the same responses are used in each. Know the effects first, then the clean-up and the prevention.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.0] Effects: oiled seabirds and mammals, smothered shorelines, fishing bans and lost tourism, because beaches and catches are unusable.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.1] Clean-up: booms (floating barriers) to contain the oil, skimmers (machines that scoop oil off the surface) to remove it, and dispersants (chemicals) to break it up.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.20.2] Prevention: double-hulled tankers, and routing ships away from coasts, so that any accident happens far from the shore.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.b] Plastic is cheap and lasts a very long time, which is why it is a problem once it is thrown away. It pollutes at local and global scale.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.0] Causes: single-use packaging that is thrown away after one use, litter, illegal dumping and landfill (burying rubbish in the ground).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.1] Plastics take hundreds of years to break down, and they fragment into microplastics (tiny pieces of plastic).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.2] Effects: litter in towns and countryside, and wildlife entangled in or eating plastic, which can injure or kill animals.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.3] Ocean garbage patches: the Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a large area of the North Pacific where floating plastic collects in an ocean gyre (a large circling ocean current).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.21.4] Microplastics enter food chains, as animals eat them. Landfill produces leachate (polluted liquid that drains from rubbish) and methane.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.b] Ireland has tried to cut plastic and waste by putting a price on it, by rewarding returns, and by ranking how waste should be handled.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.0] The plastic bag levy (a charge on each bag) was introduced in March 2002 at 15 cent a bag, raised to 22 cent in 2007. Plastic bag use fell by over 90% almost at once, and the money funds environmental projects.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.1] The Deposit Return Scheme (Re-turn) began on 1 February 2024 for plastic drinks bottles and cans.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.2] The waste hierarchy ranks reduce, reuse, recycle, recover and dispose. The EPA licenses waste facilities.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.22.3] The Dublin Waste-to-Energy incinerator at Poolbeg (a plant that burns waste to make energy) has operated since 2017.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.b] Ozone depletion is a different problem from global warming, with a different cause and a different treaty. It is also a success story.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.0] CFCs, from aerosols, fridges and air conditioning, destroy stratospheric ozone (the ozone in the upper atmosphere), which absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.1] British Antarctic Survey scientists reported the Antarctic ozone hole (a thin area in the ozone layer over Antarctica) in 1985.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.2] More ultraviolet radiation causes skin cancer and eye cataracts, and damages plankton and crops.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.3] The Montreal Protocol, agreed in 1987, phased out CFCs. Every UN member state has ratified it, and it is often cited as the most successful global environmental agreement.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.23.4] The Antarctic ozone layer is expected to recover to 1980 levels around the 2060s (UN assessment, 2023).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.b] Pollution also occurs closer to home, from traffic, old mines and large structures. Each of these is a local example.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.0] Air pollution in towns: traffic produces nitrogen dioxide and fine particles. Home burning of solid fuel adds smoke, which is why smoke-free zones exist in some towns (see the note Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources for smoke-free zones).
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.1] Mining pollution: acid mine drainage (acidic water flowing out of old mines) from old copper mines at Avoca, Co. Wicklow, discolours and pollutes the Avoca River.
[geo-economic-environment-s3.24.2] Noise and visual pollution (unwanted sound and ugly views) from airports, motorways, quarries, wind farms and pylons.
