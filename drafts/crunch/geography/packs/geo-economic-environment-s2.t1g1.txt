MODE: cost and links
TARGET geo-economic-environment-s2: 2. Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.b] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas formed from ancient living things) supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy (the energy taken straight from nature before conversion) in the 2020s. Their production is concentrated in a few places, so most countries must import them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.0] The Middle East (e.g. Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq) holds about half the world's proven oil reserves (oil known to be recoverable). Venezuela has the largest proven reserves of any single country.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.1] The USA has been the world's largest oil producer since the late 2010s, through shale oil and gas (trapped in rock and released by fracking, which fractures the rock with pressurised liquid).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.2] Russia is one of the largest gas exporters. Qatar is a leading exporter of liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so that ships can carry it).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.3] China produces and consumes about half the world's coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.4] Fossil fuels are traded over long distances by tanker, pipeline and LNG ship, so importers depend on exporters for their energy security (a steady, affordable supply of energy).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.b] Energy use is very uneven across the world. It follows wealth and industry, and the pattern is shifting towards Asia.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.0] Developed countries use far more energy per person than developing countries. Demand is now growing fastest in Asia, notably China and India, because their economies and populations are growing.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.1] The USA, Canada and Australia use many times more energy per person than India or most of sub-Saharan Africa.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.2] China has been the world's largest CO2 (carbon dioxide) emitter in total since the mid-2000s, but the USA emits far more CO2 per person.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.b] Burning a fossil fuel releases the carbon and impurities locked inside it. Each pollutant causes a different problem, so keep them apart.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.0] Carbon dioxide (CO2) is the main greenhouse gas released, and it drives global warming.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.1] Sulphur dioxide (SO2), mainly from coal and heavy oil (thick, impure oil), is a cause of acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.2] Nitrogen oxides (NOx), mainly from vehicles and power stations, are also a cause of acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.3] Smoke, fine particles (soot, tiny solid specks that can be breathed deep into the lungs) and carbon monoxide are released as well.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.b] The greenhouse effect is the natural trapping of heat by gases in the atmosphere. Burning fossil fuels adds extra gas to it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.0] CO2 traps heat in the atmosphere, so extra CO2 from burning fossil fuels enhances the greenhouse effect and causes global warming. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.b] Acid rain is a transboundary problem (one that crosses national borders): the countries that cause it are often not the ones that suffer.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.0] Normal rain is slightly acidic, at about pH 5.6, because CO2 dissolves in it naturally. Acid rain is rain with a pH below 5.6, not below pH 7.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.1] SO2 and NOx dissolve in water vapour to form sulphuric and nitric acids. These fall as wet deposition in rain, or as dry deposition of acidic particles and gases.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.2] Sources include coal-fired power stations, heavy industry and vehicles, because all of them burn fuels that contain sulphur or release nitrogen oxides.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.3] Emissions from Britain and Germany were carried by the prevailing south-westerly winds (the usual wind direction) to Norway and Sweden, which is why Scandinavia suffered from other countries' pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.4.4] The Black Triangle, the border region of former East Germany, Poland and Czechia, had lignite-burning power stations (lignite is a low-grade, smoky coal) that caused severe forest death in the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.b] Acid rain harms living things, buildings and people. The damage can appear far from the source, because the wind carries the pollution across borders.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.0] Forest damage: needle and leaf loss, and nutrients leached (washed out) from the soil. German forests, such as the Black Forest, were reported dying in the 1980s (Waldsterben, meaning forest death).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.1] Lake acidification: thousands of Scandinavian lakes were acidified and lost their fish. Aluminium released from the soil by the acid kills fish.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.2] Limestone and marble buildings and statues dissolve, through the same process as the chemical weathering of limestone (carbonation, where weak acid reacts with the rock).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.5.3] Corrosion of metal, for example, and respiratory (breathing) problems in people.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.b] Because acid rain crosses borders, responses had to be international as well as national. They work either by cutting emissions or by repairing the damage.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.0] The 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution, signed at UNECE (the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe) in Geneva, was an international agreement on air pollution that crosses borders.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.1] EU directives (laws binding on member states) limit emissions from large power stations.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.2] Flue-gas desulphurisation (scrubbers, which remove SO2 from the smoke before it leaves the chimney) is used at power stations. Moneypoint, Co. Clare, opened in 1985 and had scrubbers fitted in the late 2000s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.3] Low-sulphur fuels cut SO2 at source. Catalytic converters (exhaust devices that reduce NOx) became compulsory on new petrol cars in the EU in 1993.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.4] Sweden and Norway have limed acidified lakes (added lime to neutralise the acid) since the 1970s and 1980s.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.5] Countries switched from coal to gas and renewables, which release little or no SO2.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.6.6] Outcome: European SO2 emissions have fallen by well over three-quarters since 1990, and many lakes are recovering.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.b] Smog is smoke mixed with fog. It builds up when smoke from home fires cannot escape upwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.0] Burning coal and turf in homes releases smoke and fine particles, because the fuel burns incompletely in an open fire.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.1] In calm, cold winter weather an anticyclone (a high-pressure system with settled air) creates a temperature inversion, where a layer of warm air sits above cold air near the ground.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.2] The inversion traps the smoke near the ground, because the warm layer stops it rising and blowing away. This forms smog.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.3] Smog causes bronchitis, asthma, heart and lung disease, and extra deaths, because the smoke and fine particles are breathed in.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.b] Smog led governments to ban smoky fuels in city areas. London's disaster in 1952 was the earlier precedent, and Dublin's ban is the Irish case study.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.0] London's Great Smog, in December 1952, caused about 4,000 deaths at the time. Later estimates are far higher.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.1] The Great Smog led to the UK Clean Air Act 1956, which created smokeless zones (areas where smoky fuel may not be burned).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.2] Dublin suffered severe winter smogs in the early 1980s from household coal burning, which led to the ban.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.3] The sale of smoky (bituminous) coal was banned in Dublin from 1 September 1990. Black smoke levels fell by about 70%.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.4] The ban covered smoky coal only, so smokeless fuel and turf could still be burned in Dublin at first.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.5] A study published in The Lancet in 2002 estimated that the ban was linked to about 350 fewer deaths a year in Dublin from respiratory and heart disease.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.8.6] The ban was extended to Cork in 1995, and in stages to other cities and large towns as low-smoke zones. National rules from 2022 further restrict the sale of smoky solid fuels and wet wood.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.b] Beyond the air pollutants, getting and using fossil fuels harms land, water and rivers.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.0] Oil spills during transport, for example from tankers, pollute the sea and coasts. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.1] Landscape damage comes from coal mining and peat cutting, which scar and strip the land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.2] Thermal pollution of rivers is caused by the cooling water of power stations, which is returned to the river warmer than it was taken.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.b] Wind turns turbine blades that drive a generator. Turbines are built onshore on land and offshore at sea.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.0] Ireland: wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s, from onshore farms such as Galway Wind Park, Connemara (2017), and from Arklow Bank Wind Park, off Co. Wicklow (2004), Ireland's first offshore wind farm.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.1] Denmark: wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.2] Advantage: strong, frequent Atlantic winds give Ireland a good wind resource, and wind is the main renewable source in Irish electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.3] Disadvantage: output varies with the wind, and local residents object on grounds of noise, shadow flicker (flickering shadows cast by moving blades) and visual impact.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.4] Disadvantage: Derrybrien, Co. Galway, suffered a peat slide of about 450,000 m³ on 16 October 2003 during construction of a wind farm on bog. The EU Court of Justice fined Ireland in 2019 for the lack of a proper environmental impact assessment (a study of the likely harm before building).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.b] Hydroelectric power uses falling water to turn turbines, through three main methods.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.0] Dams and reservoirs release stored water through turbines at a controlled rate.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.1] Run-of-river schemes use the river's natural flow, so they need little or no stored water.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.2] Pumped storage pumps water uphill at times of low demand and releases it at peak times, so it acts like a store of energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.3] Ireland: Ardnacrusha on the River Shannon (Shannon Scheme, opened 1929, built by Siemens) and Turlough Hill, Co. Wicklow (1974), the country's pumped-storage station. Poulaphouca on the Liffey, Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid on the Lee, and Cathaleen's Fall at Ballyshannon on the Erne also generate power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.4] Advantage: output can be raised quickly at peak times (when demand is highest), as at Turlough Hill.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.5] Disadvantage: Ireland has little hydro potential left for large new schemes.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.6] Norway: hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of its electricity, for example.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.7] Three Gorges Dam, Yangtze River, China: the world's largest power station by installed capacity. It was completed in 2006, fully operational in 2012, and over one million people were relocated. It gives flood control downstream but traps sediment (silt that would otherwise travel downriver).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.b] Photovoltaic panels convert sunlight into electricity, and solar thermal panels use sunlight to heat water.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.0] Ireland: solar power has grown rapidly since the early 2020s, through large solar farms on farmland and panels on rooftops. SEAI (Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland) grants help homes to install panels.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.12.1] Disadvantage: Ireland's cloud cover and short winter days limit output, because panels need strong daylight.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.b] Waves and tides drive turbines or barrages (dams built across an estuary). The three technologies are different, so do not mix them up.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.0] Wave: the Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is a wave test site.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.1] Tidal barrage: La Rance, Brittany, France, a barrage across the Rance estuary, opened in 1966. It was one of the first and largest tidal power stations.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.2] Tidal stream: SeaGen in Strangford Lough, Co. Down, was installed in 2008 as the world's first commercial-scale tidal-stream turbine, using the fast tidal flow through the narrows. It has since been removed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.3] Advantage: tides are predictable, because they follow the moon's cycle. Disadvantages: high cost and marine impacts (harm to sea life and habitats).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.b] Geothermal energy uses heat from the Earth's interior, to heat water or to generate steam.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.0] Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (a plate boundary where the crust is thin) over a hotspot (a rising plume of hot rock). The Hellisheiði power station generates electricity from geothermal water, and about 9 in 10 Icelandic homes are heated by it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.1] Ireland uses low-temperature heat pumps only, which draw modest heat from the ground.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.2] Advantage: output does not depend on the weather. Disadvantage: high-temperature sources are found only where the crust is hot, as in Iceland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.b] Biomass is organic material (from plants or animal waste) burned or converted to energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.0] Sources: wood chip, energy crops such as willow and miscanthus, and biogas from slurry and food waste produced by anaerobic digestion (rotting without oxygen).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.1] Biofuels (fuels made from plants) are blended into petrol and diesel, by law in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.2] Edenderry, Co. Offaly, was converted from peat to biomass, an example of a power station switching fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.3] Advantage: carbon-neutral in principle, because the crops absorb as much CO2 as they release when burned. Disadvantage: energy crops compete with food production for land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.b] Nuclear power uses fission, the splitting of uranium atoms, to release heat that makes steam to drive turbines. Nuclear is not renewable.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.0] France: nuclear power supplies about two-thirds of its electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.1] Advantages: low carbon emissions, so little CO2, plus reliable and large output.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.2] Disadvantages, such as radioactive waste, accident risk, very high cost, and the cost of decommissioning (safely shutting down and dismantling) old reactors.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.3] Chernobyl, Ukraine (then USSR), 26 April 1986: a reactor exploded, and radioactive fallout spread across Europe, including Ireland. A 30 km exclusion zone remains.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.4] Fukushima Daiichi, Japan, 11 March 2011: an earthquake and tsunami knocked out cooling and caused meltdowns.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.b] Ireland has never had a nuclear power station. A combination of law, public opinion and practical limits explains why.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.0] The Electricity Regulation Act 1999 bans the use of nuclear fission to generate electricity in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.1] The ESB (Electricity Supply Board) planned a nuclear power station at Carnsore Point, Co. Wexford, in the 1970s. It was abandoned after large public protests and was never built.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.2] Other reasons: Ireland's small electricity grid, the high cost, the problem of waste disposal, and fears raised by Chernobyl.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.17.3] Sellafield, Cumbria, England, a nuclear reprocessing site on the Irish Sea coast, has discharges that Irish governments have long objected to, which adds to Irish unease about nuclear power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.b] Renewable energy comes from sources that are naturally replaced. These advantages apply to wind, hydro, solar, wave, tidal and geothermal power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.0] Renewables are inexhaustible, so supply is secure: the sun, wind, water and tides will not run out.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.1] They produce little or no CO2, SO2 or NOx, so they cause less global warming and acid rain.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.2] They reduce the fossil-fuel import bill and improve energy security.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.3] They create jobs, often in rural areas.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.4] Once a plant is built, the fuel is free.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.5] They help meet EU and national targets and avoid penalties for missing them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.b] Renewables also have costs and limits, and these explain why fossil fuels are still widely used.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.0] Renewables are intermittent (not constant), because wind and sun vary, so backup or storage is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.1] The initial cost of building a plant is high.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.2] Turbines and dams have visual impact, and cause noise and local objections.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.3] Remote sites need grid connections (cables linking them to the electricity network).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.4] Some habitats are damaged: birds are killed by turbines, and dams flood valleys. Peat slides can also happen at wind farms on bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.b] Ireland imports a large share of its energy, so renewable energy matters for both its environment and its economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.0] Ireland imported about three-quarters of its energy in the early 2020s, at a cost of billions of euro a year. Renewables reduce this import bill and the exposure to price shocks (sudden rises in world fuel prices).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.1] Ireland must meet its 2030 targets for renewable electricity and emissions. The Climate Action Plan aims for 80% renewable electricity by 2030, and missing EU targets exposes Ireland to the cost of buying compliance.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.2] Energy security improves through interconnectors (cables that link electricity grids): the East-West Interconnector to Wales (2012) and the Celtic Interconnector to France (due in the late 2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.3] Renewables provide jobs and income in rural areas, because wind farms, solar farms and dams are built there.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.4] Green power attracts multinational companies that want renewable electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.5] Cleaner air and water follow from burning less fossil fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.b] Map questions ask you to choose and justify a wind-farm site on an Ordnance Survey map, and to explain how conflict may arise. Match each feature of the site to a need of the wind farm.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.0] A wind farm needs a high, exposed upland or coastal site with strong winds, because output depends on wind speed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.1] It should be away from houses, to limit noise and shadow flicker.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.2] It needs an access road close by for construction, and a connection close to the electricity grid, by transmission line (the high-voltage cable that carries power).
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.3] It should avoid protected areas and deep peat, because peat can slide when disturbed. It is best placed on land of low farming value.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.21.4] Conflict arises when a site lies near homes or in a protected or scenic area, so the site must balance wind strength against these constraints.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-environment-s1: 1. Renewable and non-renewable resources in the economy
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.b] A resource is anything in the environment that people use to meet their needs. Resources are classed by whether nature can replace them, and this decides whether a country can keep using them for ever.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.0] Resources include energy, minerals, water, soil, forests, fish, land and scenery, so the word covers far more than fuels.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.1] Non-renewable (stock, finite) resources formed over millions of years, so they are used up far faster than they form and will eventually run out. Examples are coal, oil, natural gas, peat, metal ores and uranium.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.2] Renewable (flow) resources are replaced naturally, so they will not run out however much is used. Examples are wind, solar, hydro (running water), wave, tidal and geothermal heat (heat from inside the Earth).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.3] Some resources are renewable only if managed sustainably (used no faster than they are replaced): forests, fish stocks, soils, groundwater and biomass (plant material used as fuel). Overuse turns them into exhausted resources, as happens with fish stocks. There is more on this in Sustainable economic development.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.b] Three kinds of fuel are non-renewable. Fossil fuels and peat formed from once-living material, and uranium is a finite mineral.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas and peat) formed from the remains of plants or marine organisms buried and compressed over millions of years. Burning them releases the carbon they stored.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.1] Peat is non-renewable because a bog (wet ground where dead plants build up as peat) grows only about 1 mm a year, far slower than it is cut.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.2] Nuclear energy from uranium is an alternative to fossil fuels with low carbon emissions, but it is not renewable because uranium is finite.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.b] Energy is the resource that the rest of the economy runs on. This is why a country's energy supply matters so much to its wealth and security.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.0] Primary energy is energy in its raw form, such as oil, gas or wind. Electricity is a secondary form made from primary sources, for example by burning gas or by turning turbines with wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.1] Renewables give a far larger share of electricity than of total energy, because much of total energy is oil used for transport and heating, which renewables supply little of.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.2] Energy powers industry, transport, farming and homes, so resources are central to the economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.3] Energy security means a secure and affordable supply that is not disrupted by price shocks (sudden large rises in price) or supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.4] A country that imports its energy pays a large import bill (the money spent buying energy from abroad) and is exposed to price shocks and supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.b] Ireland has few fossil fuels of its own, so it depends heavily on imported energy. The mix of fuels it uses shows where that dependence lies.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.0] Import dependence (the share of energy bought from abroad): Ireland imported close to 90% of its energy in the years before Corrib gas came ashore in 2015.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.1] The share fell after Corrib began and has risen again as Corrib's output declines. In the early 2020s Ireland still imported about three-quarters of its energy (SEAI, the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.2] Early 2020s primary energy mix (SEAI): oil just under half and the largest single source; natural gas about a third; renewables a little over a tenth; coal and peat small and falling.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.3] Adding oil, gas, coal and peat shows that fossil fuels still supply most of Ireland's primary energy, while renewables are growing.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.b] Oil is Ireland's largest single source of primary energy, and Ireland produces none of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.0] All of Ireland's oil is imported, which adds to the import bill and leaves the country exposed to price shocks.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.1] Oil is used mainly for transport and home heating.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.2] Whitegate, Co. Cork, is Ireland's only oil refinery (the plant that turns crude oil into fuels).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.b] Natural gas is Ireland's second-largest primary energy source and its largest source of electricity. Home gas fields supplied it for decades but are now running down.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.0] Kinsale Head gas field, off Co. Cork, was discovered in 1971 and began production in 1978 as Ireland's first commercial gas field. Production ended in 2020 when the field was depleted (used up), so it is no longer a working field.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.1] Corrib gas field, off north-west Co. Mayo, was discovered in 1996 and first flowed in December 2015. Its gas is processed at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.2] For a time after 2015 Corrib supplied over half of Ireland's gas. Its output is now declining, which is why import dependence has risen again.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.3] Most of Ireland's gas now comes from Britain through interconnector pipelines (cross-border links) from Moffat, Scotland.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.4] Gas is the largest single source of Irish electricity, because gas-fired power stations generate more of it than any other fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.b] Ireland's own coal fields are small and long closed. Moneypoint, the big coal-burning station, ran on imported coal and has now stopped burning coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.0] Ireland's native coalfields are small and long closed. They are Arigna, Co. Roscommon, where mining ended in 1990 and which is now the Arigna Mining Experience, and Castlecomer, Co. Kilkenny.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.1] Moneypoint, Co. Clare, on the Shannon Estuary, is an ESB (Electricity Supply Board) coal-fired station opened in 1985. At about 900 MW (megawatts, a measure of power output) it is Ireland's largest power station.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.2] Moneypoint burned imported coal, brought in by large ships to its deep-water jetty (a landing pier where water is deep enough for big ships).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.3] Moneypoint stopped burning coal in 2025.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.b] Peat, cut from the bogs of the Midlands, was Ireland's own fuel and powered stations for decades. It has now ended, and the cutaway bogs (bogs from which the peat has been removed) are being given new uses.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.0] Bord na Móna, the State peat company, was founded in 1946.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.1] Peat-fired stations at Lanesborough (Lough Ree) and Shannonbridge (West Offaly) closed in December 2020. Bord na Móna ended all peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.2] Edenderry, Co. Offaly, switched from peat to biomass, a renewable fuel of wood and energy crops.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.3] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo: the peat station closed in 2003 and was replaced by wind farms on the cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.4] Cutaway bogs are reused. Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly, has turned a cutaway bog into parkland, wetlands and walking and cycling trails.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.b] Hydroelectric power uses falling water to turn turbines (machines that spin to drive generators). The Shannon Scheme at Ardnacrusha was an early large scheme, and little potential is left for more.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.0] Ardnacrusha, on the River Shannon near Limerick, was built by the German firm Siemens and opened in 1929 as the Shannon Scheme. The ESB was set up in 1927 to run it, and for some years it supplied most of the country's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.1] Other stations: Poulaphouca on the River Liffey, Co. Wicklow; Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid on the River Lee, Co. Cork; and Cathaleen's Fall at Ballyshannon on the River Erne, Co. Donegal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.2] Turlough Hill, Co. Wicklow, opened in 1974 as an ESB pumped-storage station. Water is pumped uphill when demand is low and released through turbines at peak times, so it stores energy for when it is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.3] Little potential is left for large new hydro schemes, because the best sites were already used.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.b] Wind is Ireland's main renewable source. The country's position on the Atlantic edge of Europe gives it an advantage.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.0] Wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s. Renewables, mostly wind, supplied about two-fifths.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.1] Strong, frequent Atlantic winds make the western coasts and uplands the best sites for wind farms.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.2] Bellacorick wind farm, Co. Mayo (1992), was Ireland's first commercial wind farm. Oweninny wind farm was built on the same cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.3] Galway Wind Park, Connemara, completed in 2017, is one of Ireland's largest onshore (on land) wind farms.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.4] Arklow Bank Wind Park, off Co. Wicklow, opened in 2004 as Ireland's first offshore (at sea) wind farm, with seven turbines. Offshore wind is planned to grow strongly, with at least 5 GW targeted by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.b] Ireland's other renewable sources are smaller than wind. Some are growing quickly and some are still at an early stage.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.0] Solar power has grown rapidly since the early 2020s, through large solar farms on farmland and panels on rooftops. SEAI grants support home installations.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.1] The Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is Ireland's wave-energy test site, where wave machines are trialled.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.2] Geothermal energy is limited to low-temperature ground-source heat pumps (devices that take heat from the ground to warm buildings).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.3] Tidal energy harnesses the rise and fall of the sea, and it is at an early stage in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.4] Biomass (wood and energy crops) and biofuels (fuels made from plant material) are the remaining renewable sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.b] Governments set targets to push renewables up and cut emissions. Ireland missed its 2020 target and has set tougher ones for 2030 and 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.0] Ireland's 2020 EU target was 16% of gross final energy consumption (all the energy used by final users such as homes, industry and transport) from renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.1] Ireland reached only about 13.5% and missed the target. It was one of the furthest from its target of any EU member state.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.2] Climate Action Plan: 80% of electricity from renewables by 2030, and at least 5 GW (gigawatts) of offshore wind by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.3] Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021: cut greenhouse gas emissions by 51% by 2030, compared with 2018, and reach climate neutrality (net zero, meaning emissions no higher than what is removed) by 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.b] Ireland's renewable share fell short of its 2020 target for six linked reasons. Several come from how Irish people live and travel, and some from delays in building.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.0] Heavy dependence on cars and road freight for transport, which runs on oil, so renewables supply little of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.1] Oil used for home heating in a dispersed rural settlement pattern (homes spread out, so gas networks and shared heating are hard to provide).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.2] Slow planning and grid connection for wind farms, which delayed new capacity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.3] No large hydro potential left for new schemes, because the best sites were already used.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.4] A late start on offshore wind, which left Ireland with less renewable capacity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.5] Strong economic and population growth raising energy demand, which made the renewable share harder to raise.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.b] Interconnectors are cables or pipelines that link Ireland's energy network to other countries. They help security of supply because power and gas can flow in when Ireland needs them.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.0] The East-West Interconnector, opened in 2012, links Co. Dublin to north Wales by electricity cable.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.1] The Celtic Interconnector, from Co. Cork to Brittany, France, is under construction and due to link Ireland to the EU mainland grid in the late 2020s.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.2] Gas also arrives by interconnector pipelines from Scotland, which is how most of Ireland's gas now reaches it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.b] Knowing which sectors use most energy shows where demand is highest and where savings or renewables matter most.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.0] Transport is the largest energy-using sector in Ireland (SEAI).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.1] Heat for homes and businesses comes next, then electricity generation for industry and services.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.2] Data centres (buildings full of computer servers) used about a fifth of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office). Most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.b] Europe depends heavily on imported energy, and its main oil and gas comes from the North Sea and from suppliers outside the EU.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.0] The EU imports close to three-fifths of its energy (early 2020s, Eurostat, the EU's statistics office).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.1] The North Sea, shared by the UK and Norway, is Europe's main oil and gas province (an area rich in oil and gas).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.2] Norway is not an EU member but is one of the EU's main gas suppliers, by pipeline.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.b] Relying on one supplier for a vital fuel is risky. Europe learned this when Russia, its biggest gas supplier, invaded Ukraine.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.0] Before 2022 Russia supplied about two-fifths of the EU's imported gas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.1] Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022. Gas and electricity prices soared across Europe.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.2] REPowerEU plan, May 2022: cut dependence on Russian fossil fuels by saving energy, importing more liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so ships can carry it, much of it from the USA) and speeding up renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.3] Russia's share of EU gas imports has since fallen sharply.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.17.b] National energy mixes differ widely across Europe. France relies on nuclear power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.17.0] Nuclear power (electricity made from splitting uranium atoms) supplies about two-thirds of France's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.b] Germany is changing its energy mix by moving to renewables and ending nuclear power, though it still burns coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.0] The Energiewende (energy transition) is Germany's long-term switch to renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.1] After the Fukushima disaster in Japan in March 2011 (a nuclear accident), Germany decided to phase out nuclear power. Its last three reactors closed on 15 April 2023.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.2] Germany still burns lignite (brown coal) from huge open-cast mines (mines dug from the surface) in the Rhineland. A 2020 law sets 2038 at the latest as the end of coal-fired power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.19.b] Poland shows a country whose power still rests on coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.19.0] Coal generates well over half of Poland's electricity (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.b] Denmark shows a country that has built its electricity supply around wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.0] Wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity (2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.1] Denmark was an early leader in wind power and is home to the turbine maker Vestas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.b] Two European countries that are not EU members get nearly all their electricity from renewable sources, thanks to their landscapes.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.0] Hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of Norway's electricity, from its mountains, high rainfall and glacial lakes, which give plenty of falling water.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.1] Geothermal and hydro supply almost all of Iceland's electricity, and about 9 in 10 Icelandic homes are heated geothermally.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.b] The EU sets renewable targets for all its members. It met its 2020 target and has raised the goal for 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.0] The EU target of 20% renewable energy by 2020 was achieved, at about 22%.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.1] The revised Renewable Energy Directive (2023, an EU law binding on member states) requires at least 42.5% renewable energy by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.2] Renewables supplied about a quarter of EU gross final energy consumption in 2023, so the EU still has far to go to reach 42.5%.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.b] Despite the growth of renewables, fossil fuels still supply most of the EU's energy. Final energy (the energy used by consumers) is spread across several sectors.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.0] Fossil fuels (oil, gas and coal) still supply most of the EU's energy. Oil is the largest single source, mainly for transport.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.1] Transport, households and industry each take roughly a quarter to a third of final energy. Services take a smaller share.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.b] Energy production and consumption are not spread evenly across Europe. Which role a country plays depends on its industry and its natural resources.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.0] Core industrial countries, such as Germany and France, consume most of the energy, because their large industry and populations need so much.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.1] Resource-rich countries, such as Norway, export energy, because they produce more than they need.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.2] Peripheral countries, such as Ireland, depend on imports, because they have few fuels of their own.

CANDIDATES geo-mapwork-s1: Map interpretation
[geo-mapwork-s1.0.b] Exam map questions are set on an Ordnance Survey (OS) map, the official survey map of Ireland. To answer them you need to know what the map shows and how its scale (the link between a distance on paper and the real distance on the ground) works. Worked examples use an invented town called Rivertown and are labelled illustrative.
[geo-mapwork-s1.0.0] The exam map is a 1:50 000 Ordnance Survey (Discovery Series) extract, meaning a cut-out piece of the national map, and questions say 'examine the map and legend'. The legend is the key that explains the symbols.
[geo-mapwork-s1.0.1] Every symbol, colour and road-class answer comes from the legend, so read it before answering instead of guessing from memory.
[geo-mapwork-s1.0.2] A ratio (representative fraction) of 1:50 000 means 1 unit on the map equals 50 000 of the same units on the ground, for example 1 cm on the map stands for 50 000 cm on the ground.
[geo-mapwork-s1.0.3] A linear scale (scale bar, a ruler-like line printed on the map) and a statement scale ('2 cm to 1 km', written in words) express the same scale in different forms.
[geo-mapwork-s1.0.4] On 1:50 000, 1 cm = 0.5 km and 2 cm = 1 km, so a grid square (one square of the printed grid) of 2 cm × 2 cm is 1 km × 1 km = 1 km².
[geo-mapwork-s1.0.5] A large-scale map (e.g. 1:1 000) shows a small area in great detail; a small-scale map (e.g. 1:1 000 000) shows a large area with little detail. The bigger the ratio number, the smaller the scale.
[geo-mapwork-s1.0.6] 1:50 000 is a medium scale, suited to a town and its surroundings because it shows buildings, roads and relief clearly while still covering a useful area.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.b] A grid reference is a short code that pins a place on the map. Learn the two types, because a four-figure reference names a whole square and a six-figure reference names a point inside it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.0] The Irish Grid divides Ireland into 25 sub-zones, each 100 km × 100 km, lettered A to Z with I omitted. The sub-zone letter is printed on the map and starts every grid reference, so that the code points to one place only.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.1] Eastings are the vertical grid lines, numbered along the top and bottom, and the numbers increase eastward. Read them first, because the grid reference runs across before it runs up.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.2] Northings are the horizontal grid lines, numbered up the sides, and the numbers increase northward. Read them second, so that the reference reads across then up.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.3] Memory aid for the order: along the corridor, then up the stairs.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.4] A four-figure reference identifies a whole 1 km square by the easting and northing of its bottom-left (south-west) corner. Use it for areas such as a lake, a forest or a slope, which cover a whole square rather than a single point.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.5] A six-figure reference identifies a point to the nearest 100 m. Mentally divide the square into tenths, then read the tenths east of the easting line and the tenths north of the northing line.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.6] Six-figure references locate point features such as a building symbol, a road junction, a spot height (a dot marking an exact height) or the point where a road leaves the map. Give the reference for the centre of the symbol.
[geo-mapwork-s1.1.7] Illustrative: the square containing the ringfort (an early Christian farmstead) in Rivertown is the four-figure reference R 25 43, easting 25 first and then northing 43, with R as the sub-zone letter.
[geo-mapwork-s1.2.b] Questions use grid references in two directions: you are given a reference and asked what is there, or told a feature and asked to locate it. Position can also be given in words, relative to other things.
[geo-mapwork-s1.2.0] To name a feature at a grid reference, find the sub-zone letter, then the easting, then the northing, and name what the map or legend labels at that point exactly.
[geo-mapwork-s1.2.1] A common Ordinary task is naming the feature at three or four grid references, such as an antiquity (a historic monument), a Garda station, a post office or a tourist feature.
[geo-mapwork-s1.2.2] Illustrative: the bridge over the River Dallow is at six-figure reference R 268 441, so the easting 268 is read before the northing 441; the feature at R 280 436 is a motte (a Norman earth mound).
[geo-mapwork-s1.2.3] Absolute location is a precise position given by a grid reference or by latitude and longitude (degrees measured from fixed lines on the globe), so it can be checked exactly.
[geo-mapwork-s1.2.4] Relative location describes a position against other features or directions, so it is approximate, e.g. 'north-west of the town, beside the river'.
[geo-mapwork-s1.2.5] Exams ask which area of the map (north-west, south-east and so on) a named feature lies in, for example the north-east.
[geo-mapwork-s1.3.b] Latitude and longitude give a global position in degrees from two fixed reference lines, which makes them useful for any place on Earth, not only places on one OS map.
[geo-mapwork-s1.3.0] Latitude is the angular distance north or south of the Equator (0°, the line round the middle of the Earth), up to 90°N and 90°S at the poles. Lines of latitude (parallels) run east–west, parallel to each other.
[geo-mapwork-s1.3.1] Longitude is the angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian (0°), which passes through Greenwich, London, up to 180°. Lines of longitude (meridians) run north–south and meet at the poles.
[geo-mapwork-s1.3.2] Each degree is divided into 60 minutes (′). Write latitude first with N or S, then longitude with E or W, e.g. 53°20′N, 6°15′W.
[geo-mapwork-s1.3.3] Key lines: the Equator (0°); the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn at about 23.5°N and 23.5°S; the Arctic and Antarctic Circles at about 66.5°N and 66.5°S; the International Date Line roughly follows 180°.
[geo-mapwork-s1.3.4] One degree of latitude is about 111 km everywhere, because parallels are evenly spaced. One degree of longitude is about 111 km at the Equator and shrinks to zero at the poles, because the meridians meet there.
[geo-mapwork-s1.4.b] Longitude is linked to time, and it also lets you describe where Ireland sits on the globe. Each coordinate system has its own best use.
[geo-mapwork-s1.4.0] The earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so 15° of longitude equals 1 hour of time. Places to the east are ahead.
[geo-mapwork-s1.4.1] Illustrative: a place 30° east of Greenwich is 2 hours ahead, since 30 ÷ 15 = 2.
[geo-mapwork-s1.4.2] Ireland's position is fixed by coordinates: it lies between about 51.5°N and 55.5°N and about 5.5°W and 10.5°W, so it is in the northern hemisphere (north of the Equator) and the western hemisphere (west of Greenwich).
[geo-mapwork-s1.4.3] Example: Dublin lies at about 53°20′N, 6°15′W, which puts it north of the Equator and west of Greenwich.
[geo-mapwork-s1.4.4] Grid references are used on OS maps because they are quicker and more precise at local scale; latitude and longitude are used for global location.
[geo-mapwork-s1.5.b] Distance questions ask for a length in km between two points. A straight-line distance is measured directly; a curved distance has to follow the bends of a road, railway, river or walk.
[geo-mapwork-s1.5.0] Straight-line distance: measure with a ruler in cm and multiply by 0.5 to get km, or lay a paper edge between the two points and read it against the scale bar. Give the answer in km to one decimal place.
[geo-mapwork-s1.5.1] From two grid references: find the difference in eastings (ΔE) and the difference in northings (ΔN) in km, then distance = √(ΔE² + ΔN²). This is Pythagoras' theorem applied to the grid.
[geo-mapwork-s1.5.2] Curved distance (a road, railway, river or walk): mark the start on the straight edge of a strip of paper, pivot the strip at each bend and mark as you go, then straighten it and measure against the scale bar. A string laid along the route also works.
[geo-mapwork-s1.5.3] Start and stop exactly at the points named, such as where the road enters the map, a junction or a roundabout, so that the length is not too long or too short.
[geo-mapwork-s1.5.4] Illustrative: the bridge at R 268 441 to the summit pillar at R 240 405 measures 9.1 cm, or 4.6 km (rounded); by grid, ΔE = 2.8 km and ΔN = 3.6 km, so √(2.8² + 3.6²) = √20.8 ≈ 4.6 km.
[geo-mapwork-s1.5.5] Illustrative: the R990 from the town to the crossroads village measures 9.0 cm along its bends, or 4.5 km.
[geo-mapwork-s1.6.b] Area is found by counting grid squares, because each 1 km square on a 1:50 000 map is 1 km². The method depends on whether the shape is a neat rectangle or irregular.
[geo-mapwork-s1.6.0] Rectangular areas (the whole map, or the part north of a named northing or west of a named easting): count kilometres east–west × kilometres north–south; each 1 km square is 1 km².
[geo-mapwork-s1.6.1] Irregular features (a lake, a bay, land above a contour): count the full squares; count squares more than half covered as full; ignore squares less than half covered, or estimate part-squares as fractions.
[geo-mapwork-s1.6.2] Give the answer in km² and state that it is approximate, since counting part-squares is an estimate.
[geo-mapwork-s1.6.3] Land on one side of a road or railway: count the squares on that side and estimate partly covered squares; exclude sea where the question asks for land.
[geo-mapwork-s1.6.4] Illustrative: the whole extract is 14 km × 8 km = 112 km²; the land north of northing 44 is 14 km × 4 km = 56 km².
[geo-mapwork-s1.6.5] Illustrative: an invented lake covering 3 full squares and 4 squares more than half covered is about 7 km².
[geo-mapwork-s1.7.b] Direction questions ask which way you would travel between two places, or which way a feature runs. The map has a fixed north, so every answer is read against it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.7.0] North is the top of the OS map (grid north). The eight compass points are N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W and NW; sixteen points add NNE, ENE, ESE, SSE, SSW, WSW, WNW and NNW.
[geo-mapwork-s1.7.1] Method: draw a line from the start point to the destination, imagine a compass centred on the start, and read the direction of travel.
[geo-mapwork-s1.7.2] 'From A to B' means the direction of travel, read at A. Quick check: is B above or below A (north or south), and to the left or right (west or east)?
[geo-mapwork-s1.7.3] Direction along a route (a walk, a road, a river's flow) is the overall direction from its start to its end. A river flows from its source on high ground to its mouth on low ground, which spot heights and contours show.
[geo-mapwork-s1.7.4] Bearings (optional for Higher): the angle measured clockwise from north, from 0° to 360°; E = 090°, S = 180°, W = 270°.
[geo-mapwork-s1.7.5] Illustrative: from the bridge at R 268 441 to the summit pillar at R 240 405, the pillar is 2.8 km west and 3.6 km south, so the direction of travel is south-west; from the pillar to the bridge it is north-east. The River Dallow flows towards the north-east.
[geo-mapwork-s1.8.b] The OS map shows height with several devices. Contours are the main one, and you must be able to read a height from each.
[geo-mapwork-s1.8.0] Contours are lines joining places of equal height above sea level. On the Discovery Series the vertical interval (the height difference between neighbouring contours) is 10 m, and some contours are numbered with their height.
[geo-mapwork-s1.8.1] Spot heights are dots with the height in metres, which is useful for exact heights; triangulation pillars are survey points with their height; layer colouring (colour bands for ranges of height) is shown in the legend.
[geo-mapwork-s1.8.2] Height of a point: read the spot height or pillar beside it, or the nearest numbered contour, and count contours up or down at 10 m each.
[geo-mapwork-s1.8.3] The highest point on the map is the highest spot height or pillar; check the upland areas, because high ground is where the top height will be.
[geo-mapwork-s1.9.b] The spacing of contours shows how steep the ground is, and the pattern of spacing gives the shape of the slope. This is how you recognise a slope type on the map.
[geo-mapwork-s1.9.0] Contours close together mean a steep slope, because the ground rises 10 m over a short distance; contours far apart mean a gentle slope; a wide area with no contours is flat ground, such as a floodplain or coastal lowland.
[geo-mapwork-s1.9.1] Even slope: contours are evenly spaced, so the gradient stays constant, as on a straight ramp.
[geo-mapwork-s1.9.2] Concave slope: contours close together near the top and widely spaced near the bottom, so the slope is steep above and gentle below, and the whole slope can be seen from the top.
[geo-mapwork-s1.9.3] Convex slope: contours widely spaced near the top and close together near the bottom, so the slope is gentle above and steep below, and the foot cannot be seen from the top.
[geo-mapwork-s1.9.4] Stepped slope, for example a terraced hillside: alternating bands of close and wide contour spacing, which gives steep sections and flat sections in turn.
[geo-mapwork-s1.10.b] Different relief features make distinct contour shapes. Learn the shape of each and you can name the feature from the contours alone.
[geo-mapwork-s1.10.0] Valley: contours form V or U shapes pointing upstream (towards higher ground, where the river rises), with a river along the floor.
[geo-mapwork-s1.10.1] Spur: a ridge of land sticking out from a hill, so water runs off it either side, with contours forming V shapes pointing downhill, towards lower ground.
[geo-mapwork-s1.10.2] Plateau: a high, flat or gently sloping top with steep edges; contours crowd round the edge and are few on the top.
[geo-mapwork-s1.10.3] Ridge: a long, narrow stretch of high ground, shown by elongated contours along its crest (the top line).
[geo-mapwork-s1.10.4] Col or saddle: a low pass between two summits, shown as a dip between two high points, so it is a natural route across a range.
[geo-mapwork-s1.10.5] Escarpment: a steep slope on one side and a gentle slope on the other, so contours are close on one side and wide on the other.
[geo-mapwork-s1.10.6] Knoll or isolated hill: a closed ring of contours, because the ground falls away on every side.
[geo-mapwork-s1.11.b] Gradient measures steepness as a number, aspect says which way a slope faces, and intervisibility asks whether two points can see each other. All three use the heights and distances on the map.
[geo-mapwork-s1.11.0] Gradient is the steepness of a slope: vertical interval (VI, the difference in height) ÷ horizontal equivalent (HE, the ground distance), both in the same units, written as 1 in x or 1:x. A larger x means a gentler slope.
[geo-mapwork-s1.11.1] Method: find the height of each point and subtract to get the VI; measure the distance on the map and convert it to metres for the HE; divide HE by VI. The result is an average gradient between the two points.
[geo-mapwork-s1.11.2] Aspect is the direction a slope faces, which is the direction you look when facing downhill, not the uphill direction: a slope falling towards the south is south-facing, with a southerly aspect.
[geo-mapwork-s1.11.3] In Ireland, south-facing slopes receive more sunshine and are warmer.
[geo-mapwork-s1.11.4] Intervisibility: whether one point can be seen from another. A convex slope or intervening high ground blocks the view, so check with a cross-section (a side-on drawing of the ground between them).
[geo-mapwork-s1.11.5] Illustrative: the summit at 512 m to the bridge at 52 m gives VI = 460 m; HE = 4.6 km = 4 600 m; gradient = 4 600 ÷ 460 = 1 in 10.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.b] A cross-section is a side-on view of the ground along a line between two points, built from the contours the line crosses. It shows the shape of the relief and lets you test slopes and views.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.0] 1. Join the two points with a straight line on the map.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.1] 2. Lay a straight paper edge along the line and mark where every contour crosses it, with its height, plus rivers, roads and other features.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.2] 3. Draw a horizontal axis the same length as the line (horizontal scale = map scale) and a vertical axis with a chosen height scale.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.3] 4. Plot each height above its mark and join the points with a smooth curve.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.4] 5. Label the rivers, roads, the summit, the slope types and the ends (X and Y, with grid references); give a title and both scales.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.5] Vertical exaggeration: the vertical scale is stretched so that relief shows, since otherwise the hills would look almost flat. It equals the horizontal scale distance ÷ the vertical scale distance for the same map length, and is stated in the title or key.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.6] Illustrative: a cross-section north from the summit to the river uses a horizontal scale of 1:50 000 (1 cm = 500 m) and a vertical scale of 1 cm = 50 m (1:5 000); vertical exaggeration = 500 m ÷ 50 m = ×10.
[geo-mapwork-s1.12.7] Illustrative: the slope from the summit is concave, with contours crowded near the top and widely spaced near the river.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.b] In some questions the profile is already drawn for you. Your job is to match each labelled point on it to the map, then read facts off the map at that spot.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.0] A printed profile runs along a named easting or northing between points X and Y, with features labelled A to E; match each labelled point to the map by its distance along the line and its height.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.1] A river crossing is a dip in the profile, because the land falls to the river, with a blue line on the map at that spot.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.2] A road is identified by its number on the map, for example the N99 in the illustrative example below.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.3] The height at a point is read from the vertical axis or from the map, so cross-check one against the other.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.4] The slope type between two points (concave, convex, even, steep, gentle, flat) is read from the profile curve and the contours.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.5] Land use or forestry type is read from the map symbol at that point.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.6] A named walk, cycle route or tourist attraction crossing the line, and the townland (the smallest Irish land division) or place name at a point, are read from the map labels.
[geo-mapwork-s1.13.7] Illustrative: along northing 42 from X (R 200 420) to Y (R 300 420), point A is a dip where the River Dallow crosses, B is on the N99, C is on coniferous plantation, and the slope between D and E is concave.
[geo-mapwork-s1.14.b] Symbols are drawn or named by copying them from the legend, in proportion and in the legend's colour where colour is used. The legend is provided in the exam, so the skill is knowing its groups and reading it quickly.
[geo-mapwork-s1.14.0] Legend groups: roads; railways and stations; water features; height; vegetation; antiquities; tourist and leisure information; public services; boundaries.
[geo-mapwork-s1.14.1] Road letters: M = motorway; N = national road (national primary and national secondary routes); R = regional road. N stands for National and R for Regional, not 'route' or 'rural'.
[geo-mapwork-s1.14.2] Vegetation: coniferous plantation (pointed, conifer-shaped tree symbols), deciduous woodland (rounded tree symbols) and mixed woodland (both); use the legend's terms.
[geo-mapwork-s1.14.3] Symbols exams have asked students to draw or name: viewpoint, picnic site, parking, boating activities, triangulation pillar, ringfort, county boundary, wind farm, post office, youth or independent holiday hostel, golf course, camping site, electricity transmission line, waymarked walk and cycle route. Learn them from the legend.
[geo-mapwork-s1.15.b] Antiquities are historic monuments, and the map labels each one. Some maps also mark a cultural area, so you should know how to spot it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.15.0] Antiquities are grouped in the legend; name one exactly as the map labels it, e.g. 'Castle (in ruins)', 'Ringfort', 'Standing Stone', 'Megalithic Tomb', 'Church (in ruins)', 'Holy Well' or 'Motte'.
[geo-mapwork-s1.15.1] Cultural areas: some maps mark a Gaeltacht (Irish-speaking area) boundary, which the legend identifies.
[geo-mapwork-s1.15.2] Irish-language place names on the map are further evidence of a cultural area.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.b] A sketch is a ruled, labelled drawing of the features asked for, placed accurately on a frame. It is never a tracing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.0] Higher asks for a sketch to half scale (20 marks): half the length and half the breadth of the extract. Ordinary asks for a sketch map of the area shown (30 marks); a frame of similar shape, about half size, is safest.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.1] Measure the extract, or count squares (each 2 cm square becomes 1 cm), and draw a frame with four ruled sides.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.2] Draw light pencil grid lines on the frame at the same eastings and northings (every 1 cm at half scale, or every second line) and label the edge numbers. These let you place features accurately.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.3] Draw a coastline as a firm line, so that it stands out from the grid lines; draw a river as a line between the named grid references, or along its entire course; draw broad rivers and lakes as outlined shapes.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.4] Draw roads and railways as lines along their entire route where asked; draw areas (land above a contour, plantation, built-up area, golf course) as shaded closed shapes; draw point features (post office, triangulation pillar, viewpoint, camping site) as a symbol or dot.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.5] 'Land above 300 m': follow the 300 m contour accurately round every part of the area asked for. 'Entire area' means all of it; 'at' or 'in the south-west of the map' means only that part.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.6] Label every feature on the sketch or in a key; an unlabelled feature earns nothing. Add a title, a north arrow and the words 'half scale'.
[geo-mapwork-s1.16.7] Never trace the map. Draw only the features asked for, in the correct part of the frame, since a feature placed in the wrong part loses marks.
[geo-mapwork-s1.17.b] Illustrative: the extract is 28 cm × 16 cm, so the frame is 14 cm × 8 cm, with grid lines every 1 cm. Here is how each requested feature of the invented Rivertown map is drawn and labelled.
[geo-mapwork-s1.17.0] River Dallow, entire course: a line from its entry at R 200 402 in the south-west to its exit at R 340 470 in the north-east, following its meanders across the floodplain east of the town; label it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.17.1] N99, entire route: a road line from the south-west to the north-east through the town; label it N99.
[geo-mapwork-s1.17.2] Railway: a line with its station at R 272 445; label it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.17.3] Built-up area of Rivertown: a shaded closed shape around the town centre and the housing estates on the northern edge, with the industrial estate on the N99 at the north-eastern edge; label it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.17.4] Area above 300 m at Slievenagh: a shaded closed shape following the 300 m contour round the summit triangulation pillar at R 240 405; label it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.b] A drainage pattern is the shape that a river and its tributaries (the smaller streams that join it) make on the map. Each pattern has a recognisable look, and the shape hints at the rock or land beneath.
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.0] Dendritic: tree-like, with tributaries joining the main river at acute angles, on rock of uniform resistance (rock that wears away at the same rate everywhere).
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.1] Trellised: tributaries join the main river at right angles, on alternating bands of hard and soft rock, so the streams follow the soft bands.
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.2] Radial: streams flow outward in all directions from a central hill or mountain, because the water runs downhill every way, as the small streams do around the summit of Slievenagh.
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.3] Centripetal: streams flow inward to a central lake or lowland, because that is the lowest ground.
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.4] Deranged: no clear pattern, with many small lakes and marshes, on glacial deposits (material left by ice).
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.5] Real examples, one clause each: trellised in the Munster ridge and valley; deranged in the Co. Cavan drumlin belt; centripetal at Lough Neagh.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.b] Fluvial landforms are those made by rivers. On the map each one has a clue, such as a loop, a flat area with no contours, or contours crowded across the river.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.0] V-shaped valley: the upland valley pattern given under landforms read from contours, with sharp contour Vs pointing upstream; the river runs along the floor.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.1] Meander: a river swinging in loops, such as those on a flat floodplain. Oxbow lake: a crescent-shaped lake beside a meandering river.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.2] Floodplain: wide and flat, with no contours, along a lowland river; often marshy.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.3] Levees: embankments (raised banks) along the channel, where the map shows them.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.4] Braiding: the river splits, so it looks like a plait, into several channels around islands.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.5] Confluence: the point where a tributary joins the main river, which you can see as a Y-shaped junction of blue lines.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.6] Delta: deposits where a river enters a lake or the sea.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.7] Waterfall: shown by a symbol or name, with contours crowded across the river.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.b] Glacial landforms were shaped by ice. They show up on the map as hollows, narrow ridges and groups of small hills, each with a distinctive contour shape.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.0] Corrie: an armchair-shaped hollow (the shape ice carves, with a steep back wall), with contours close on three sides and open downhill.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.1] Tarn: a small, round lake in a corrie, often high up.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.2] Arête: a narrow ridge with very close contours on both sides, because the ground falls steeply away either side.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.3] U-shaped valley: a broad, flat floor with steep sides, and contours close together on the valley sides.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.4] Ribbon lake: a long, narrow lake on the floor of a U-shaped valley.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.5] Drumlins: many small oval closed contour rings, often with small lakes between them.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.6] Esker: a long, narrow, winding ridge on lowland, so it stands out from the flat ground around it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.b] Coastal landforms are found where land meets sea. Karst landforms belong to limestone country, where water drains underground. Both are recognised by their shapes, symbols and names.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.0] Beach: for example sand or shingle (small rounded stones) shading along the shore.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.1] Cliff: a cliff symbol, or contours crowded together at the coast, because the land drops steeply to the sea.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.2] Headland: land projecting into the sea, often high.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.3] Bay: a curved inlet of the sea, so the coastline bends inland.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.4] Sand spit (a narrow strip of sand built out from the coast), tombolo (a ridge joining an island to the mainland) and lagoon (a shallow pool cut off from the sea): identified by their shapes and names on the map.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.5] Sand dunes: shown by dune shading or a name.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.6] Swallow hole: a stream ends abruptly where it disappears underground; limestone areas have few surface streams.
[geo-mapwork-s1.22.b] A lake's origin can usually be read from its shape and setting on the map, so questions ask you to give the type at a grid reference.
[geo-mapwork-s1.22.0] Lake type at a grid reference: glacial (tarn or ribbon lake), fluvial (oxbow), coastal (lagoon) or artificial (reservoir, shown by a straight dam edge, a dam symbol or a 'Reservoir' label).
[geo-mapwork-s1.23.b] Some Higher questions link the map to mass movement (the downhill movement of rock and soil), and ask you to find extremes of slope. Steepness comes from contour spacing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.23.0] A very steep area is shown by crowded contours or cliffs; locate it with a six-figure grid reference.
[geo-mapwork-s1.23.1] A very gentle area is shown by widely spaced contours; locate it with a six-figure grid reference.
[geo-mapwork-s1.23.2] There is more on mass movement on steep ground in the rocks and weathering chapter.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.b] Settlement pattern means how buildings are arranged on the land. Three patterns appear on OS maps, and each has a look and a reason.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.0] Nucleated: buildings clustered at a crossroads, bridge, church or village, such as the crossroads village on the invented Rivertown map.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.1] Linear (ribbon): buildings strung along a road, sometimes a coast road or a valley floor, such as the houses along the R990 north of Rivertown.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.2] Dispersed: individual farmhouses scattered across farmland, each on its own land, so there is no cluster.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.3] Nucleated settlement forms around a route focus (where routes meet) or a service; linear settlement forms where a road gives access to services; dispersed settlement reflects farming and land consolidation (joining small plots into larger farms).
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.4] Ribbon development on the approach roads to a town: linear housing spreads out along the main roads, because of cheaper rural sites, road access and services along the road.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.b] Houses are not spread evenly. Map evidence shows where people avoided building and where they chose to cluster, and each reason must be backed by that evidence.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.0] Absence from high land above about 200–300 m, which is cold, wet, exposed and has thin soils; give a four-figure grid reference and the map evidence.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.1] Absence on steep slopes, shown by crowded contours, because building and farming are difficult there.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.2] Absence on poorly drained land, shown by a marsh or bog symbol, and on floodplains liable to flooding, because houses there would be damp or flooded.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.3] Absence where forestry plantations cover the land, since the land is used for trees, and on lakes, because they cannot be built on.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.4] High density of settlement: give a four-figure grid reference for lowland, gently sloping, well-drained land near roads, a town or the coast.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.5] Explain each factor that favours high density: flat or gently sloping land, good drainage, road access, and nearness to a town or the coast.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.b] A variations question asks you to compare how settlement is spread in two parts of the map. Use the same set of factors for both parts so that the comparison is fair.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.0] A variations question (Higher, 30 marks) usually compares two contrasting parts of the map, e.g. north and south of a named northing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.1] For each part, compare relief and height, drainage, the soils implied by the land use, communications (roads and rail), and the pull of a town or the coast.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.2] State each factor with a grid reference and explain it for both areas.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.3] Illustrative: settlement is nucleated at the crossroads village, linear along the R990 north of the town and dispersed in the north-west of the lowland; there is no settlement on Slievenagh above 300 m (R 23 40: high, steep, exposed, forestry) or on the floodplain east of the town (flood risk).
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.b] Historic settlement questions ask you to name monuments on the map and say what period they belong to. The prehistoric period runs from the Stone Age to the Iron Age.
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.0] Stone Age to Iron Age sites, such as megalithic tombs (stone-built burial monuments: court, portal or dolmen, passage and wedge tombs), standing stones, stone circles and cists (stone-lined graves).
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.1] Also prehistoric: fulachta fia (Bronze Age cooking sites), promontory forts (forts on land jutting out into water) and crannógs (lake dwellings).
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.2] Who lived there: early farming communities. Why the site was chosen: defence (promontory forts) and a lake (crannógs). What it shows: farming economy, and burial and ritual sites for religion.
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.3] Illustrative: a megalithic tomb at R 225 412 and a standing stone at R 231 418.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.b] The early Christian (early medieval) period left many farmsteads and church sites. Several different map names point to the same kind of site.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.0] Early Christian (early medieval) sites include ringforts, also named rath, lios, dún, caher or cashel on the map; souterrains (underground passages); ecclesiastical (church-related) sites with churches and graveyards; holy wells; round towers; and high crosses.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.1] Ringforts were the farmsteads of the period, enclosed by an earth bank (stone in a cashel or caher).
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.2] Ringforts belong to the early Christian period, not to the prehistoric or Norman periods.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.3] What it shows: a farming society, and the spread of Christianity, seen in churches, round towers and high crosses.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.4] Illustrative: a ringfort at R 255 430 and a church in ruins with its graveyard at R 262 446.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.b] The Normans were medieval invaders who built castles to hold land. Their sites are usually placed to guard something, such as a river crossing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.0] Norman (medieval) sites include mottes and motte-and-bailey sites (earth mounds, with a walled yard in the second type), castles, town walls, abbeys and friaries, and towns at bridging points.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.1] Mottes are Norman sites; fulachta fia are Bronze Age, and ringforts are early Christian, so check the label before dating a site.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.2] Why the site was chosen: defence, and control of a river crossing, so that the Normans could hold the land around it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.3] What it shows: Norman military control, and the religious life of the abbeys and friaries.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.4] Illustrative: a motte at R 280 436 and a castle in ruins at R 269 439, beside the bridge.
[geo-mapwork-s1.30.b] The later periods leave planned towns, large estates and industrial and transport remains. Each tells you about how the country was organised and how it developed.
[geo-mapwork-s1.30.0] Tower houses are fortified stone houses, built for protection; planned towns have a regular layout with a square or wide main street; demesnes are large houses with estate woodland.
