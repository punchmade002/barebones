TARGET geo-mapwork-s1 (part 2 of 2): Map interpretation
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.2] Radial: streams flow outward in all directions from a central hill or mountain, because the water runs downhill every way, as the small streams do around the summit of Slievenagh.
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.3] Centripetal: streams flow inward to a central lake or lowland, because that is the lowest ground.
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.4] Deranged: no clear pattern, with many small lakes and marshes, on glacial deposits (material left by ice).
[geo-mapwork-s1.18.5] Real examples, one clause each: trellised in the Munster ridge and valley; deranged in the Co. Cavan drumlin belt; centripetal at Lough Neagh.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.b] Fluvial landforms are those made by rivers. On the map each one has a clue, such as a loop, a flat area with no contours, or contours crowded across the river.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.0] V-shaped valley: the upland valley pattern given under landforms read from contours, with sharp contour Vs pointing upstream; the river runs along the floor.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.1] Meander: a river swinging in loops, such as those on a flat floodplain. Oxbow lake: a crescent-shaped lake beside a meandering river.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.2] Floodplain: wide and flat, with no contours, along a lowland river; often marshy.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.3] Levees: embankments (raised banks) along the channel, where the map shows them.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.4] Braiding: the river splits, so it looks like a plait, into several channels around islands.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.5] Confluence: the point where a tributary joins the main river, which you can see as a Y-shaped junction of blue lines.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.6] Delta: deposits where a river enters a lake or the sea.
[geo-mapwork-s1.19.7] Waterfall: shown by a symbol or name, with contours crowded across the river.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.b] Glacial landforms were shaped by ice. They show up on the map as hollows, narrow ridges and groups of small hills, each with a distinctive contour shape.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.0] Corrie: an armchair-shaped hollow (the shape ice carves, with a steep back wall), with contours close on three sides and open downhill.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.1] Tarn: a small, round lake in a corrie, often high up.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.2] Arête: a narrow ridge with very close contours on both sides, because the ground falls steeply away either side.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.3] U-shaped valley: a broad, flat floor with steep sides, and contours close together on the valley sides.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.4] Ribbon lake: a long, narrow lake on the floor of a U-shaped valley.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.5] Drumlins: many small oval closed contour rings, often with small lakes between them.
[geo-mapwork-s1.20.6] Esker: a long, narrow, winding ridge on lowland, so it stands out from the flat ground around it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.b] Coastal landforms are found where land meets sea. Karst landforms belong to limestone country, where water drains underground. Both are recognised by their shapes, symbols and names.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.0] Beach: for example sand or shingle (small rounded stones) shading along the shore.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.1] Cliff: a cliff symbol, or contours crowded together at the coast, because the land drops steeply to the sea.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.2] Headland: land projecting into the sea, often high.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.3] Bay: a curved inlet of the sea, so the coastline bends inland.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.4] Sand spit (a narrow strip of sand built out from the coast), tombolo (a ridge joining an island to the mainland) and lagoon (a shallow pool cut off from the sea): identified by their shapes and names on the map.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.5] Sand dunes: shown by dune shading or a name.
[geo-mapwork-s1.21.6] Swallow hole: a stream ends abruptly where it disappears underground; limestone areas have few surface streams.
[geo-mapwork-s1.22.b] A lake's origin can usually be read from its shape and setting on the map, so questions ask you to give the type at a grid reference.
[geo-mapwork-s1.22.0] Lake type at a grid reference: glacial (tarn or ribbon lake), fluvial (oxbow), coastal (lagoon) or artificial (reservoir, shown by a straight dam edge, a dam symbol or a 'Reservoir' label).
[geo-mapwork-s1.23.b] Some Higher questions link the map to mass movement (the downhill movement of rock and soil), and ask you to find extremes of slope. Steepness comes from contour spacing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.23.0] A very steep area is shown by crowded contours or cliffs; locate it with a six-figure grid reference.
[geo-mapwork-s1.23.1] A very gentle area is shown by widely spaced contours; locate it with a six-figure grid reference.
[geo-mapwork-s1.23.2] There is more on mass movement on steep ground in the rocks and weathering chapter.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.b] Settlement pattern means how buildings are arranged on the land. Three patterns appear on OS maps, and each has a look and a reason.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.0] Nucleated: buildings clustered at a crossroads, bridge, church or village, such as the crossroads village on the invented Rivertown map.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.1] Linear (ribbon): buildings strung along a road, sometimes a coast road or a valley floor, such as the houses along the R990 north of Rivertown.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.2] Dispersed: individual farmhouses scattered across farmland, each on its own land, so there is no cluster.
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.3] Nucleated settlement forms around a route focus (where routes meet) or a service; linear settlement forms where a road gives access to services; dispersed settlement reflects farming and land consolidation (joining small plots into larger farms).
[geo-mapwork-s1.24.4] Ribbon development on the approach roads to a town: linear housing spreads out along the main roads, because of cheaper rural sites, road access and services along the road.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.b] Houses are not spread evenly. Map evidence shows where people avoided building and where they chose to cluster, and each reason must be backed by that evidence.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.0] Absence from high land above about 200–300 m, which is cold, wet, exposed and has thin soils; give a four-figure grid reference and the map evidence.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.1] Absence on steep slopes, shown by crowded contours, because building and farming are difficult there.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.2] Absence on poorly drained land, shown by a marsh or bog symbol, and on floodplains liable to flooding, because houses there would be damp or flooded.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.3] Absence where forestry plantations cover the land, since the land is used for trees, and on lakes, because they cannot be built on.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.4] High density of settlement: give a four-figure grid reference for lowland, gently sloping, well-drained land near roads, a town or the coast.
[geo-mapwork-s1.25.5] Explain each factor that favours high density: flat or gently sloping land, good drainage, road access, and nearness to a town or the coast.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.b] A variations question asks you to compare how settlement is spread in two parts of the map. Use the same set of factors for both parts so that the comparison is fair.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.0] A variations question (Higher, 30 marks) usually compares two contrasting parts of the map, e.g. north and south of a named northing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.1] For each part, compare relief and height, drainage, the soils implied by the land use, communications (roads and rail), and the pull of a town or the coast.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.2] State each factor with a grid reference and explain it for both areas.
[geo-mapwork-s1.26.3] Illustrative: settlement is nucleated at the crossroads village, linear along the R990 north of the town and dispersed in the north-west of the lowland; there is no settlement on Slievenagh above 300 m (R 23 40: high, steep, exposed, forestry) or on the floodplain east of the town (flood risk).
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.b] Historic settlement questions ask you to name monuments on the map and say what period they belong to. The prehistoric period runs from the Stone Age to the Iron Age.
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.0] Stone Age to Iron Age sites, such as megalithic tombs (stone-built burial monuments: court, portal or dolmen, passage and wedge tombs), standing stones, stone circles and cists (stone-lined graves).
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.1] Also prehistoric: fulachta fia (Bronze Age cooking sites), promontory forts (forts on land jutting out into water) and crannógs (lake dwellings).
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.2] Who lived there: early farming communities. Why the site was chosen: defence (promontory forts) and a lake (crannógs). What it shows: farming economy, and burial and ritual sites for religion.
[geo-mapwork-s1.27.3] Illustrative: a megalithic tomb at R 225 412 and a standing stone at R 231 418.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.b] The early Christian (early medieval) period left many farmsteads and church sites. Several different map names point to the same kind of site.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.0] Early Christian (early medieval) sites include ringforts, also named rath, lios, dún, caher or cashel on the map; souterrains (underground passages); ecclesiastical (church-related) sites with churches and graveyards; holy wells; round towers; and high crosses.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.1] Ringforts were the farmsteads of the period, enclosed by an earth bank (stone in a cashel or caher).
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.2] Ringforts belong to the early Christian period, not to the prehistoric or Norman periods.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.3] What it shows: a farming society, and the spread of Christianity, seen in churches, round towers and high crosses.
[geo-mapwork-s1.28.4] Illustrative: a ringfort at R 255 430 and a church in ruins with its graveyard at R 262 446.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.b] The Normans were medieval invaders who built castles to hold land. Their sites are usually placed to guard something, such as a river crossing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.0] Norman (medieval) sites include mottes and motte-and-bailey sites (earth mounds, with a walled yard in the second type), castles, town walls, abbeys and friaries, and towns at bridging points.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.1] Mottes are Norman sites; fulachta fia are Bronze Age, and ringforts are early Christian, so check the label before dating a site.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.2] Why the site was chosen: defence, and control of a river crossing, so that the Normans could hold the land around it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.3] What it shows: Norman military control, and the religious life of the abbeys and friaries.
[geo-mapwork-s1.29.4] Illustrative: a motte at R 280 436 and a castle in ruins at R 269 439, beside the bridge.
[geo-mapwork-s1.30.b] The later periods leave planned towns, large estates and industrial and transport remains. Each tells you about how the country was organised and how it developed.
[geo-mapwork-s1.30.0] Tower houses are fortified stone houses, built for protection; planned towns have a regular layout with a square or wide main street; demesnes are large houses with estate woodland.
[geo-mapwork-s1.30.1] 19th and 20th century sites include canals, railways and stations, mills, coastguard stations, later housing and industrial estates.
[geo-mapwork-s1.30.2] What it shows: the growth of planned towns and estates, and of transport and industry.
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.b] Many Irish place names are built from Irish words that describe the land or what stood there. Knowing the elements helps you read the history and site of a place from its name.
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.0] Settlement and building elements: baile/bally (townland or settlement), cill/kil (church), ráth/rath and lios/lis (ringfort), dún/dun (fort), cathair/caher (stone fort), áth/ath (ford), droichead (bridge).
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.1] Land elements: cnoc/knock (hill), sliabh/slieve (mountain), ard (height), drom/drum (ridge), gleann/glen (valley), mullach/mullagh (summit).
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.2] Water, vegetation and other elements: loch/lough (lake), inis/inch (island), cluain/clon (meadow), doire (oak wood), carraig/carrick (rock).
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.3] Size elements: beag/beg (small), mór/more (big).
[geo-mapwork-s1.31.4] Townlands are the smallest land division in Ireland; townland names are printed on OS maps, and exams ask which townland a point lies in.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.b] A 30-mark question asks for three reasons, each named, evidenced from the map with a grid reference and explained. Choose the three for which the map gives the clearest evidence.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.0] Bridging point: the town grew where a river could be crossed, and bridges and roads converge on it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.1] Route focus: several N and R roads and a railway meet, so the town became a market and service centre.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.2] Defence: a castle, town walls, a hill or a river bend guards the site.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.3] River or coastal site: water supply, transport, power for mills, and a harbour or pier for trade and fishing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.4] Flat or gently sloping lowland: easy to build on, with widely spaced contours.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.5] Fertile surrounding farmland: dispersed farms and no marsh, so the town serves as a market town for its hinterland (the area around it that it serves).
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.6] Ecclesiastical origin: an abbey or early church site. Tourism: a coast, beaches, lakes or uplands attract visitors to the town.
[geo-mapwork-s1.32.7] Illustrative: the bridging point is the bridge at R 268 441, with roads converging on it; the route focus is the N99, the R990 and the railway station at R 272 445; defence is the castle at R 269 439 and the motte at R 280 436, guarding the crossing.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.b] A function is a job a town does for its people and surroundings. Name each function, give the symbol or label and its grid reference, and explain it.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.0] Residential: built-up area and housing estates, because they show where people live.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.1] Market and retail: a route focus and a central square, because people gather where routes meet to buy and sell.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.2] Services: hospital, Garda station, post office, schools and courthouse, where labelled.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.3] Transport: railway station, bus routes and motorway junction, such as the Rivertown station at R 272 445.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.4] Industrial: industrial estate or factory labels, because they show where goods are made.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.5] Tourist and recreational: tourist information office, golf course, hostels, camping and caravan sites, marinas, viewpoints and walks.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.6] Port or fishing: pier, harbour and slipway, because boats land and load there.
[geo-mapwork-s1.33.7] Ecclesiastical: churches and abbeys. Defensive in the past: castle.
[geo-mapwork-s1.34.b] A town's layout records its history, with the oldest parts near the crossing point and newer parts at the edge.
[geo-mapwork-s1.34.0] Old core, the first part to be built: narrow streets, a square, and a castle or abbey near the bridge.
[geo-mapwork-s1.34.1] Newer edge, where there is more space: housing estates, industrial estates, a bypass or motorway with junctions, and ribbon development.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.b] Choose a site with a six-figure grid reference and give three reasons, each supported by map evidence.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.0] Flat land: widely spaced contours, because it is cheaper and easier to build on.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.1] Access: an N road, a motorway junction, a railway or a port, so that goods and workers can reach the site.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.2] Nearness to a town for labour, services and markets; space to expand; a site off the floodplain and away from protected or scenic areas.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.3] Wind farms need exposed high ground away from houses; hotels need to be near tourist attractions.
[geo-mapwork-s1.35.4] State a disadvantage when asked, e.g. visual impact, traffic, loss of farmland or conflict with residents.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.b] Economic activities are the ways people earn a living, grouped as primary, secondary and tertiary. The map shows each through symbols and labels, and tourism can be judged from what the map offers visitors.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.0] Primary activity (using natural resources): forestry plantations, farming (shown by dispersed farms and field land), quarries and fishing piers.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.1] Secondary activity (making goods): industrial estates and factories, such as the industrial estate on the N99 at the north-eastern edge of Rivertown.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.2] Tertiary activity (providing services): services, tourism and transport, such as shops, hotels and railways.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.3] Each activity is evidenced by a map symbol or label and its grid reference.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.4] Tourism potential, accessibility and services, so that visitors can get there and stay: road access, parking, accommodation and tourist information.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.5] Tourism potential, attractions that draw visitors, such as antiquities, viewpoints, walks, cycle routes, beaches, lakes and golf courses.
[geo-mapwork-s1.36.6] Each factor is evidenced with grid references and explained.
[geo-mapwork-s1.37.b] Statistical maps show numbers on a map. The OS map links to them through its contours and its key.
[geo-mapwork-s1.37.0] Contours are isolines (lines joining places of equal value), so they work like the lines on a weather map, the same kind of line used on isoline statistical maps, where the value is a statistic instead of height.
[geo-mapwork-s1.37.1] Choropleth, dot, isoline, proportional-symbol and flow-line maps are taught in Graph skills; there is more on them there.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.b] Map questions fall into short questions, matching questions, sketch maps and long map-evidence questions. Short questions are worth Higher 2 marks each; Ordinary 3–5 marks, often tick-box.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.0] Short questions: a six-figure reference for a named building, road junction or exit point; the antiquity, public service or feature at a grid reference; the height of a spot height, pillar or highest point; the length of a road, motorway or railway; the straight-line distance between two points.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.1] Short questions also ask for the area of the whole map, of a lake or bay, of land above a contour, or of land east, west, north or south of a grid line or railway.
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.2] More short questions ask for the direction of travel; the part of the map a feature lies in; the type of forestry; what N or R stands for; a legend symbol; a cultural area (Gaeltacht).
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.3] Matching questions give features (such as a meander, a drumlin or a headland) to grid references, and ask for two drainage patterns, the type of lake or the aspect of a slope at a grid reference; elevation profile questions and true or false profile statements (Higher).
[geo-mapwork-s1.38.4] Sketch maps: Higher 20 marks at half scale; Ordinary 30 marks; typically four or five features.

CANDIDATES geo-graph-skills-s1: Figure interpretation
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.b] Every figure question starts the same way: read the labels and units before reading any value. Most lost marks come from a wrong unit, year or column, because a correct reading of the wrong one still scores nothing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.0] Check the title, both axis labels (the words along the horizontal and vertical lines), the key or legend (the box explaining colours or symbols), the units (%, thousands, millions, €m, hectares, mm, °C, per 1,000), the years shown and the source line. Each one changes what a number means.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.1] Read a bar or line value by running a ruler from the top of the bar or point across to the vertical axis, estimating between gridlines, and quoting the value with its unit.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.2] In a table, find the row and the column and read the cell where they meet. Check the totals row before using a figure, so you know whether a number is a part or the whole.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.3] Compare two years by reading neighbouring columns. To spot the one that did not change, read across the whole row, because the unchanged value is the one that stays the same between years.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.4] For highest and lowest, scan every value, not just the first and last, because the extreme can sit in the middle. For a ranking, list the values from largest to smallest.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.5] To count how many items are above an average line, count the bars or points whose tops lie above it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.6] For 'twice as much', double the smaller value and look for the value that matches it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.7] An infographic (a single page combining headline numbers, icons, small charts and county maps) has several panels. Read each panel's own label and unit: headline figures are often totals, while panels may show percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.b] A description states what the figure shows. Using exact trend words and quoting numbers turns a vague answer into a complete one.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.0] Trend vocabulary: increase, rise or growth; decrease, fall or decline; steady, constant or no change; fluctuate (go up and down); peak (highest point); trough (lowest point); gradual or rapid (sharp); levelling off (the change slows and flattens).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.1] The overall trend is the long-term direction. A short-term change, such as a single year's dip, is described separately so it does not hide the main pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.2] To describe a trend, state the overall direction, the start and end values with their years and units, and the period of fastest change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.3] Name any peak, trough or reversal (a change of direction), and quantify the change as the difference, the percentage change, or both.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.4] Point out anomalies: values that break the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.b] Once a trend is described, the next marks come from comparing it with another, saying why it happened and judging whether the figure can be trusted.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.0] To compare two series (two lines or two sets of bars), say which is higher, when the lines cross, whether the gap widens or narrows, and the difference in a given year. For example, net migration = immigration minus emigration.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.1] To explain a trend, link the pattern to one cause from the relevant chapter, in one clause. Explaining means saying why the figure looks as it does, not just what it shows.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.2] To evaluate a figure, check whether an axis that does not start at zero or has uneven intervals exaggerates change, because a small rise then looks large.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.3] Also check whether unevenly spaced dates distort the trend, whether the sample (the group measured) is small, and whether the source is reliable and recent.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.4] Percentages can hide small totals: a large percentage rise may be a small change in numbers, because a small starting number is easy to double.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.b] These worked values come from the invented (illustrative) population of six towns, in thousands: Town A 18, Town B 32, Town C 25, Town D 41, Town E 12, Town F 29. Each statistic has a short method that you can repeat on any data set.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.0] Total (sum): add every value. 18 + 32 + 25 + 41 + 12 + 29 = 157 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.1] Difference: subtract the smaller value from the larger and say which is larger. Town B (32) exceeds Town C (25) by 7 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.2] Mean (average) = total of the values ÷ number of values: 157 ÷ 6 = 26.2 thousand, to one decimal place (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.3] Median (the middle value): put the values in order (12, 18, 25, 29, 32, 41); with an even number of values, average the middle two: (25 + 29) ÷ 2 = 27 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.4] Mode: the most frequent value. The six towns have no mode because no value repeats.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.5] Range = highest value minus lowest value: 41 − 12 = 29 thousand (illustrative), which shows how spread out the values are.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.b] Percentages let you compare parts of different-sized wholes, but the method matters: dividing by the wrong number, or mixing up a percentage with a percentage point, gives a wrong answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.0] Percentage of a total = part ÷ total × 100. Illustrative: Town D's share of the six towns is 41 ÷ 157 × 100 = 26.1%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.1] Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100. A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.2] Always divide by the old (earlier) value, because the change is measured against where you started. Illustrative Country P, broadband households: 20% in 2000 and 90% in 2025, so the percentage change is (90 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 350%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.3] Percentage points: when both values are already percentages, the change is a difference in percentage points. A rise from 20% to 30% is a 10 percentage-point increase, but a 50% increase in the percentage change, (30 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.4] When a question asks by what percentage a percentage changed, the subtraction is the usual reading: give the answer in percentage points and add the percentage change if unsure. Use the percentage-change formula when the values are numbers rather than percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.5] Missing value X: parts of a whole add to 100% (pie chart or percentage table) or to a stated total (stacked bar, a bar split into parts, or a total row), so X = total minus the sum of the known parts.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.6] Illustrative Country Q: if all segments except 'other' total 85%, then other = 100 − 85 = 15%, because the parts of a pie chart must reach 100%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.7] To find a number from a percentage: number = percentage ÷ 100 × total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.b] Raw numbers are hard to compare between places of different size, so geographers use rates, densities and ratios. How you present the answer matters too.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.0] Rate per 1,000 = number ÷ population × 1,000, which lets places of different size be compared. A birth rate is births ÷ population × 1,000.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.1] Density = number ÷ area, given as people per km².
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.2] Ratio: compare two values as a ratio, e.g. 2 : 1. Illustrative: Town D (41) is more than three times the size of Town E (12), a ratio of about 3.4 : 1.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.3] 'Twice as much' means two times the other value; 'half' means divided by two.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.4] Round to the precision the data uses, or to one decimal place where needed, and keep the unit and the multiplier (thousands, millions) in every answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.5] In a calculation question, show the working: a correct answer with working is safe, and the working can earn marks if the answer slips.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.b] When you are asked to draw a graph from a small table of data, marks are given for each of these rules, so each one is worth doing even when the shape of the graph is easy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.0] Use graph paper, a sharp pencil and a ruler; draw the graph large enough to fill most of the space.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.1] The title says what the data is, where and when, taken from the wording of the question.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.2] The horizontal (x) axis shows categories or time; the vertical (y) axis shows values. Label both axes with units.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.3] The scale starts at zero, is evenly divided, and its interval is chosen so the largest value fits (e.g. steps of 5 or 10 units).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.4] On a line graph, time intervals are spaced in proportion to the years. If the data is for 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2015, the last gap is half the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.5] Plot every value accurately. Bars have equal widths and equal gaps; points are joined with ruled straight lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.6] Use a key or legend (a box saying what each colour or shading means) whenever more than one colour or shading is used, and shade or colour each category distinctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.b] Illustrative data: population of six towns, in thousands, from the totals block above. Following the rules in order shows how each mark is earned.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.0] Graph choice: a bar chart, because the values are one measure for separate towns.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.1] Title: 'Population of six towns (thousands), illustrative'.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.2] x-axis: 'Town', with Towns A to F in order.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.3] y-axis: 'Population (thousands)', from 0 to 45 in steps of 5, so the largest value (41) fits.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.4] Six bars of equal width with equal gaps, each drawn to its value: A 18, B 32, C 25, D 41, E 12, F 29.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.5] A single data series (one set of values) needs no key; check each bar top against the scale before finishing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.b] The question 'draw a suitable graph' is answered by naming the graph type, then giving one sentence of justification. The type depends on what the data is. Each example below is illustrative.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.0] Values for separate categories, e.g. the populations of six towns: bar chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.1] Change over time, e.g. the percentage of an urban population from 1980 to 2020: line graph (a bar chart is usually also accepted).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.2] Parts of a whole, or percentage shares adding to 100%, e.g. car sales by fuel type in one year: pie chart or divided bar.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.3] Two related variables for the same places, e.g. income and birth rate for twenty countries: scatter graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.4] Three components that add to 100%, e.g. sand, silt and clay at a soil site: triangular graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.5] Age and sex structure of a population: population pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.6] Monthly temperature and rainfall for one place: climate graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.7] Values for areas on a map, e.g. population density by region: choropleth map (a map with areas shaded by value).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.b] Bar charts show amounts for separate categories. They come in several forms, depending on whether you are comparing categories, groups within a category, or parts of a total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.0] Simple bar chart: separate categories, one bar per category, equal widths and equal gaps; vertical or horizontal.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.1] Multiple (clustered or comparative) bar chart: two or more bars side by side per category, e.g. males and females, or two years; a key is needed to tell the bars apart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.2] Compound (stacked or divided) bar chart: each bar is split into components, and the top of the whole bar is the total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.3] To read a component in a compound bar, take the top of its segment minus the bottom of its segment.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.4] Percentage compound bar: every bar reaches 100%, so the segments show shares rather than totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.5] Illustrative Region R, tree planting: total 2,000 hectares, of which conifers 1,350 ha. Broadleaf = 2,000 − 1,350 = 650 ha, a share of 650 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 32.5%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.b] A histogram looks like a bar chart but is used for continuous data, while a line graph is the standard way to show one variable changing over time.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.0] Histogram: like a bar chart but for continuous data grouped in classes (e.g. age groups or rainfall amounts), drawn with no gaps between bars because one class runs straight into the next.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.1] Line graph (trend graph): one variable over time; plot the points at the correct years and join them with ruled lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.2] Multiple line graph: two or more series on the same axes, with a key so each line can be identified.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.3] The gradient (steepness) of a line shows the rate of change: a steeper section means faster change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.4] Illustrative Country P, households with broadband: 2000 20%, 2005 38%, 2010 61%, 2015 74%, 2020 86%, 2025 90%. The rise is continuous; the fastest change is 2005 to 2010 (+23 percentage points), and the rise slows to +4 points from 2020 to 2025 as the line levels off.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.5] Illustrative Country P: the change from 2000 to 2025 is +70 percentage points, which is not the same as the 350% percentage change above, because one is a subtraction and the other is measured against the old value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.b] A pie chart shows how a whole is split into parts. Because a full circle is 360° and the parts add to 100%, each 1% takes up 3.6° of the circle.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.0] Pie chart: parts of a whole. Degrees = percentage × 3.6, because 360° ÷ 100 = 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.1] For raw numbers rather than percentages: degrees = value ÷ total × 360. Do not multiply raw numbers by 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.2] Drawing steps: draw a circle with a compass, draw a radius (a line from the centre to the edge) to 12 o'clock, then measure each angle clockwise with a protractor, largest segment first.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.3] Shade, key or label each segment with its percentage, and check that the angles add to 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.4] Illustrative Country Q, electricity sources (gas 40%, wind 25%, solar 10%, coal 5%, hydro 5%, other 15%): angles 144°, 90°, 36°, 18°, 18° and 54°, totalling 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.5] Two pie charts compare the shares in two years, not the totals, unless the circles are drawn in proportion to the totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.6] Divided (percentage) bar: a single bar split into shares that add to 100%, an alternative to a pie chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.b] A pictogram uses icons to stand for quantities. A population pyramid is a special graph showing how many people of each age and sex a country has.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.0] Pictogram: each icon stands for a fixed quantity stated in the key, so check the key first; a part-icon is a fraction of that quantity.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.1] Population pyramid: a back-to-back horizontal bar chart, which is a histogram on its side.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.2] Age groups, usually in 5-year bands, sit on the central vertical axis: 0 to 4 at the bottom and the oldest group at the top.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.3] Males are drawn on the left and females on the right. The horizontal axis shows the percentage (or number) of the population, not age, scaled outward from zero on both sides.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.4] Reading a pyramid: a wide base means a high birth rate; a narrow top means low life expectancy; a narrowing base means a falling birth rate; bulges and indentations suggest baby booms, migration, war or famine.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.5] Describe the dependent groups (the young and the old, who rely on others) and the working-age group when reading a pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.6] Drawing one band (illustrative, Country Z): males aged 20 to 24 are 3.5% and females 3.4%, so at the 20 to 24 level draw a bar 3.5 units long left of the centre axis and one 3.4 units long to the right.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.7] The shapes of pyramids and the stages of demographic transition (how a country's birth and death rates change over time) are taught fully elsewhere. There is more on this in Changing population characteristics.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.b] A climate graph combines two kinds of data for one place on a single set of axes: rainfall as bars and temperature as a line, across 12 months.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.0] Months J to D run along the bottom axis. Rainfall is shown as bars against a left-hand axis in mm.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.1] Mean (average) monthly temperature is shown as a line, plotted at the middle of each month, against a right-hand axis in °C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.2] From the graph, read the hottest and coldest months, the wettest and driest months, and the seasonal pattern (rain all year, or a wet and dry season).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.3] Calculations from the graph: annual temperature range = maximum mean monthly temperature minus minimum; mean annual temperature = sum of the 12 monthly means ÷ 12; total annual rainfall = sum of the 12 bars.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.4] Illustrative Station M, mean monthly temperature (°C) J to D: 5, 5, 7, 9, 12, 14, 16, 16, 14, 11, 8, 6. Rainfall (mm) J to D: 110, 80, 85, 60, 65, 70, 70, 85, 90, 115, 110, 120.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.5] Illustrative Station M results: warmest in July and August (16°C); coldest in January and February (5°C); annual range 16 − 5 = 11°C; mean annual temperature 123 ÷ 12 = 10.3°C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.6] Illustrative Station M: total rainfall 1,060 mm; wettest month December (120 mm); driest month April (60 mm). Rain falls in every month with a winter maximum, and the mild winters and cool summers resemble a cool temperate oceanic pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.7] Identifying a climate type from a climate graph is taught elsewhere. There is more on this in Physical regions and Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.b] A scatter graph tests whether two measurements for the same set of places are related, for example whether land value changes with distance from a city centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.0] Scatter graph: two variables for the same set of places. The independent variable (the one that may cause the change) goes on the x-axis; each place is plotted as a point, and the points are not joined.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.1] Draw a line of best fit by eye through the middle of the points, so it shows the general direction.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.2] Positive correlation: both variables rise together. Negative correlation: one rises as the other falls. No correlation: no clear pattern. Strength is described as strong or weak.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.3] Outliers, or anomalies, are points that lie away from the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.4] Correlation does not prove cause: two things can move together without one causing the other.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.5] Illustrative distance from a city centre and land value at eight sites: 1 km €900 per m²; 2 km €700; 3 km €620; 4 km €450; 5 km €400; 6 km €250; 7 km €260; 8 km €150.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.6] Illustrative: the result is a strong negative correlation, with the 7 km site a slight anomaly because it is higher than the 6 km site. A one-clause explanation: accessibility falls with distance from the centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.b] A triangular graph is used when a total is split into exactly three parts. Each point is placed using all three values at once.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.0] Triangular graph: shows three components that always total 100%, e.g. the percentage employed in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.1] Each side is a 0 to 100% scale. Read each component along the gridlines parallel to the side where its 0 is, following the direction in which its numbers run.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.2] Check that the three readings add to 100, which confirms you have read the scales correctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.3] To plot a point: find the first value on its scale, follow its gridline in, and mark where it meets the second value's gridline; check the point with the third value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.4] A point clustered near a corner is dominated by the component that corner represents.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.5] Illustrative employment structure: Country X is primary 10%, secondary 25%, tertiary 65%; it sits near the tertiary corner, typical of a developed economy. Country Y is primary 60%, secondary 15%, tertiary 25%; it sits near the primary corner, typical of a less developed economy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.b] Statistical maps put values onto a map so you can see how they vary from place to place. The choropleth is the main one; four other types each show a different kind of data.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.0] Choropleth map: areas (counties, regions or countries) shaded by value class; darker shading means a higher value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.1] Classes must not overlap and must cover every value, e.g. 0 to 49, 50 to 99, 100 to 149, 150 and over. A key is essential.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.2] Choropleth maps suit densities and percentages. Their weakness is that they imply the value is even across each area and hide variation inside it, and the boundaries look like sudden changes.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.3] Illustrative regional population densities (people per km²): North 35, East 160, South 85, West 40, Midlands 60. North and West are the lightest shade and East the darkest; the empty 100 to 149 class still appears in the key.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.4] Dot map: each dot stands for a fixed number, showing the distribution of a value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.5] Isoline map: lines join places of equal value, e.g. isobars, isotherms, isohyets or contours.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.6] Flow-line map: arrow width is proportional to the amount moving, e.g. migration, traffic or trade.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.7] Proportional symbols: circles or bars sized in proportion to the value, placed on the map.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.b] Sometimes a table is a better way to present data than a graph, and tables, graphs and maps all feed into the Geographical Investigation.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.0] Use a table rather than a graph when many exact values must be given. A table cannot show a trend at a glance.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.1] Graphs, maps and tables are expected in the presentation of results in the Geographical Investigation report. There is more on this in The Geographical Investigation (20%).
