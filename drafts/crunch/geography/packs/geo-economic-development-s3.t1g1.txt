[geo-economic-development-s3.0.b] Colonialism is the control of one territory and its people by a foreign power. The colonising country rules the colony (the territory it controls), settles it and/or exploits its resources for its own benefit, and that purpose shaped the colony's economy.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.0] Imperialism is the wider policy of building an empire, that is, a group of territories ruled from one centre. Colonialism is how that policy works on the ground in each territory.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.1] The main European colonial powers were Britain, France, Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands, Belgium and Germany.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.2] The Americas were colonised first, then India and Asia, and then Africa, so the periods of colonial rule differ from region to region.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.3] Africa was divided among European powers in the Scramble for Africa after the Berlin Conference (1884–85), which set the rules for European claims. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia in Africa were still independent of European rule.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.4] Colonies supplied cheap raw materials (unprocessed goods) such as cotton, sugar, rubber and minerals to the colonial power, and bought its manufactured goods. This made the colony a supplier and a market rather than a manufacturer.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.5] Local industries that competed with these imports were destroyed, so the colony lost its own manufacturing.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.6] Plantations (large farms growing one crop for sale) and mines used forced or slave labour, which kept the cost of raw materials low.
[geo-economic-development-s3.0.7] Food crops were replaced by cash crops (crops grown for sale and export, not for local food), a pattern called monoculture, meaning growing mainly one crop.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.b] Colonial powers built transport and institutions to serve their own needs, and independent states inherited the results. The legacy (what was left behind) was economic, political and social.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.0] Railways and roads ran from the interior to the ports so that raw materials could be exported. They were not built to link regions with each other, so movement between regions stayed poor.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.1] Ports and capitals grew into the main cities, so development was concentrated on the coast.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.2] Positive legacies are debated: railways, ports, some irrigation, legal and administrative systems, and a world language. Present both sides in an answer.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.3] Most of this infrastructure was built to serve the colonial power rather than the colony's own people, which is why its benefits are debated.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.4] Artificial borders drawn in Europe split some ethnic groups and grouped rival ones together, which created tension inside the new states.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.5] The colonial power's policy of divide and rule (keeping a population weak by setting its groups against one another) set groups against one another.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.6] European languages and religions spread, education reached only a small elite, racial hierarchies (rankings of people by race) were enforced, and land was owned by a few.
[geo-economic-development-s3.1.7] Weak institutions (governments, courts and civil services) were handed over at independence, and conflict and coups (seizures of power by the army) followed in many new states.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.b] After independence, governments tried a sequence of strategies to make their economies work in a world market dominated by rich countries. Each country below follows some of these stages.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.0] Import substitution industrialisation: new home industries were protected from foreign competition by tariffs (taxes on imported goods), so local firms could make what had been imported.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.1] Neo-colonialism (new colonialism without direct rule): former colonies often stayed dependent on exporting a few raw materials to rich countries at low and unstable prices, and on foreign loans and companies.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.2] Debt crises in the 1980s and 1990s, when countries could not repay what they owed, led to IMF (International Monetary Fund) and World Bank structural adjustment programmes. These cut government spending, privatised state firms (sold them to private owners), opened markets to trade, and devalued the currency.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.3] Liberalisation: opening the economy to trade and foreign investment, and privatising state firms.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.4] Export diversification: moving beyond a few raw materials into manufactured and service exports, so the country is less exposed to one price.
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.5] Joining the World Trade Organisation (WTO, the body that sets rules for trade between countries) and regional trading blocs (groups of countries that trade on easier terms).
[geo-economic-development-s3.2.6] Growth of services and of remittances (money sent home by emigrants).
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.b] British control of India grew in two steps: first a trading company, then the Crown. Learn the dates, because the answer should name them.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.0] The East India Company (a British trading company) was chartered on 31 December 1600 and first traded through trading posts.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.1] The Battle of Plassey in 1757, won by Robert Clive, began Company rule in Bengal, so a trading firm became a ruler.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.2] The Rebellion of 1857 led to the Government of India Act 1858, which transferred rule from the Company to the British Crown. This began direct British rule, known as the Raj.
[geo-economic-development-s3.3.3] India gained independence, so British rule ended, and was partitioned (divided into separate states) on 15 August 1947.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.b] Under British rule India moved from being a major manufacturer to a supplier of raw materials. This is deindustrialisation, the loss of an economy's industry.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.0] India's large handloom textile industry, including Bengal's fine cottons and Dhaka (now in Bangladesh) muslin, was destroyed by cheap machine-made cloth from the cotton mills of Lancashire in north-west England.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.1] Raw cotton was exported from India to Britain to feed those mills, so India sent out the raw material and bought back the cloth.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.2] Economic historian Angus Maddison estimated India's share of world GDP (the value of all goods and services produced) at about a quarter around 1700 and about 4% by 1950. These are estimates, not exact figures.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.3] Cash crops replaced food crops: cotton, jute, indigo (blue dye), tea from plantations in Assam from the 1830s–40s, and opium grown in India and exported to China.
[geo-economic-development-s3.4.4] Jute was processed in mills along the Hooghly near Calcutta, so the crop was processed near the port.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.b] British land taxes and the flow of money to Britain left Indian farmers poor and the country vulnerable to famine.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.0] The Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793, Lord Cornwallis) fixed land revenue (the tax paid on land) and collected it through zamindars, landlords who passed the tax burden on to peasants.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.1] Heavy land taxes left the peasantry in debt and poverty, because the tax burden was passed on to them.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.2] Famines (severe food shortages causing mass death) occurred under British rule: the Great Famine of 1876–78 and the Bengal famine of 1943.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.3] The Bengal famine killed about 3 million people (estimates range from about 2 to 3 million). Grain was still exported while people starved.
[geo-economic-development-s3.5.4] The drain of wealth: revenue, profits and salaries were sent from India to Britain instead of being spent in India. Dadabhai Naoroji set out this argument in 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' (1901).
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.b] India's railways show how colonial infrastructure served the colonial power first.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.0] The first passenger train ran from Bombay to Thane on 16 April 1853.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.1] The railway network was built mainly to carry raw materials to the ports of Bombay and Calcutta and to move troops, so it served exports and control rather than Indian travellers.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.2] Indians came to use the railways heavily, so they brought some benefit, although they were not built for Indian passengers' needs.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.3] Ports and colonial cities grew, including Bombay (now Mumbai) and Calcutta (now Kolkata), because trade passed through them.
[geo-economic-development-s3.6.4] Calcutta was the capital of British India, until 1911, when the capital moved to Delhi.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.b] British rule left India with lasting institutions, a language and a painful division, but also with a movement that ended it.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.0] English is the language of government and business. It has since become an asset for India's IT industry. There is more on this in India in A Continental / Subcontinental Region.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.1] India inherited a civil service, courts and a parliamentary system from British rule, so it did not start without institutions.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.2] At independence, literacy was very low: about 18% in the 1951 census. Life expectancy was around 32 years.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.3] Partition on 15 August 1947, the division of British India into separate states, displaced more than 10 million people, and hundreds of thousands were killed in the violence.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.4] The Kashmir region has been disputed with Pakistan since 1947, so Partition left a lasting political conflict.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.5] Mahatma Gandhi led a non-violent independence movement. Its methods included the boycott of British cloth, the promotion of swadeshi (buying Indian goods) and the spinning of khadi (hand-spun cloth), which struck at the cloth trade colonialism had built.
[geo-economic-development-s3.7.6] The Salt March of 1930 was a major act of resistance against British control, which is why it is a key example of resistance.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.b] After independence India's first adjustment was to let the state plan and own heavy industry, behind trade barriers.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.0] The First Five-Year Plan (1951), under Jawaharlal Nehru, began a planned economy, in which the government sets targets for the economy in advance.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.1] Import substitution (making at home what had been imported) behind high tariffs protected home industry from foreign competition.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.2] The state built heavy industry, including steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.3] The 'Licence Raj' meant that firms needed government licences to set up or expand, which restricted competition and investment.
[geo-economic-development-s3.8.4] Growth was slow, which is why it earned the nickname the 'Hindu rate of growth'.
[geo-economic-development-s3.9.b] Food was a second adjustment, because the colonial economy had left India short of food.

[geo-economic-development-s3.9.2] As a result India became self-sufficient in food, meaning it could feed itself without relying on imports.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s1: 1. Measuring economic development
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.b] Economic development is one part of development, and growth alone does not show whether people are better off. Geographers need both ideas, and the gap between them is the reason broader measures such as the HDI exist.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.0] Economic development means growth in a country's output (the goods and services it produces) and income, with rising living standards for its people.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.1] Development is wider than income: it also includes health, education, political freedom, equality and quality of life. A country can grow richer without improving these, which is why income alone is not enough.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.2] Developed (high-income) economies have high output and incomes per head, meaning per person; developing economies are low- and middle-income.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.3] The World Bank classes countries as low, lower-middle, upper-middle or high income by GNI per head (gross national income divided by population). The thresholds change each year, so do not quote any.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.4] Newly industrialised countries (NICs) sit between developed and developing, because their economies have recently shifted towards manufacturing. South Korea, Singapore and Taiwan are examples; with Hong Kong they are called the Asian Tigers.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.b] These totals all measure the value of a country's output, but they differ in whether they count output by location or by ownership. Telling them apart is the key skill for the Ireland case below.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.0] Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of all goods and services produced within a country's borders in one year, whoever owns the firms. It is a measure of location.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.1] Gross national product (GNP) is GDP plus income earned abroad by the country's residents and firms, minus income sent out of the country to foreign owners as profits, interest and dividends (payments to shareholders).
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.2] GNP therefore measures the output owned by the nation's residents, while GDP measures the output produced on its soil. This is the difference between ownership and location.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.3] Gross national income (GNI) is the term the World Bank and UN now use, and it is essentially the same as GNP. Data tables may use either term, so treat them as the same measure.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.4] Totals are converted to US dollars for comparison. Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts for price differences between countries, so a dollar buys more in a low-cost country and PPP makes the comparison fairer.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.5] Per capita (per head) figures divide the total by the population, and are needed to compare countries of different size. A large total GNP, as in India, can hide a low GNP per head because it is shared among so many people.
[geo-economic-development-s1.2.b] Growth is the percentage change in output each year, and it shows whether an economy is expanding or shrinking. Governments aim for it because of what it brings.
[geo-economic-development-s1.2.0] The GDP growth rate is the percentage change in output per year. Two quarters (six months) of falling output is the usual definition of recession, when the economy is shrinking.
[geo-economic-development-s1.2.1] Economic growth brings more jobs, higher incomes, more tax revenue (money the government collects) to fund services and infrastructure (roads, water, power and other basic networks), and less emigration because people find work at home.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.b] Output per head is the most common single measure of development. It is popular because it is easy to use and to compare.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.0] It is simple and gives one comparable figure, so countries can be ranked at a glance.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.1] It is available for almost every country, collected in a standard way, so like is compared with like.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.2] It allows change over time to be tracked, so a country's progress or decline can be seen.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.3] It is closely linked to a government's ability to fund health, education and other services, because higher output means more tax revenue.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.b] A single average cannot show how well people really live. Each weakness below is a way the figure can mislead.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.0] It is an average, so it hides inequality between rich and poor, and between regions.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.1] It ignores the informal economy (work done for cash or unrecorded) and subsistence farming (growing food for the family, not for sale), which are large in developing countries. It also ignores unpaid work such as caring, housework and volunteering.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.2] It counts harmful spending, such as pollution clean-up, disaster repair and crime, as if it added to well-being, because spending raises output whatever its cause.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.3] It ignores environmental damage and the depletion (using up) of resources.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.4] Exchange-rate swings (changes in the value of one currency against another) distort dollar comparisons unless PPP is used.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.5] It says nothing directly about health, education, freedom or happiness, which is why a broader measure was needed.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.b] Ireland is the clearest case where GDP and GNP give very different pictures of the economy. Foreign-owned firms produce a great deal in Ireland, but much of the profit leaves.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.0] Multinationals (firms operating in many countries) own much of Irish output and send their profits abroad, so Irish GNP has long been well below GDP. GNP is lower because profits flow out to foreign owners.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.1] Irish GDP growth for 2015 was about 26%, first published in July 2016. The US economist Paul Krugman called it "leprechaun economics" because it was not believable as real growth.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.2] The jump came mainly from multinationals relocating intellectual property (patents and other intangible assets, meaning things of value you cannot touch) and aircraft-leasing assets to Ireland, not from Irish people becoming richer.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.3] From 2017 the Central Statistics Office (CSO) published modified gross national income (GNI*), which removes the profits of redomiciled firms (firms that moved their legal base to Ireland), depreciation (loss of value through wear and age) on foreign-owned intellectual property and leased aircraft.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.4] In the early 2020s Ireland's GNI* was only a little over half of its GDP, which shows how large the distortion is.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.5] Ireland has the second-highest GDP per head in the EU, after Luxembourg (Eurostat, early 2020s), so GDP overstates Irish prosperity. Use GNI* as the better guide to Irish living standards.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.b] The HDI combines health, education and income into one value. It was designed to measure people's well-being, not just the economy.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.0] The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) first published the HDI in its Human Development Report in 1990.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.1] Mahbub ul Haq, a Pakistani economist, created the HDI working with Amartya Sen, an Indian economist who won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1998.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.2] A long and healthy life is measured by life expectancy at birth (how many years a newborn can expect to live).
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.3] Knowledge is measured by mean (average) years of schooling for adults aged 25 and over, and expected years of schooling for children starting school.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.4] A decent standard of living is measured by GNI per head at PPP dollars. The income indicator is GNI per head, not GDP.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.5] Each dimension is turned into an index from 0 to 1, and the three are combined into one value between 0 and 1. The closer the value is to 1, the higher the development.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.6] The four categories are very high (0.800 and above), high (0.700 to 0.799), medium (0.550 to 0.699) and low (below 0.550).
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.7] Older textbooks use adult literacy and gross enrolment (the share of children enrolled in school) for education; these were replaced by mean and expected years of schooling in 2010.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.b] HDI is highest in the richer regions and lowest in parts of Africa. One named example of each category is enough for an exam answer.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.0] Very high HDI: Europe (especially Scandinavia and Switzerland), North America, Australia and Japan. Norway ranked 1st (0.957) in the Human Development Report 2020, using 2019 data. Ireland and Switzerland were joint 2nd (0.955) in the same report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.1] Switzerland ranked 1st (0.967) in the Human Development Report 2023/24, using 2022 data. Ireland was in the top ten in that report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.2] High HDI: Brazil, at about 0.76 in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.3] Medium HDI: India, 134th of 193 countries (0.644) in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.4] Low HDI: mostly sub-Saharan Africa (the countries south of the Sahara desert), including Niger, Chad, South Sudan, Somalia and the Central African Republic.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.5] Niger was last of 189 countries (0.394) in the 2020 report, and Somalia was last of 193 (0.380) in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.b] The HDI was built to fix the weaknesses of income per head, so its strengths mostly come from being broader and people-centred.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.0] It is broader than income alone, combining health, education and income.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.1] It focuses on people and outcomes rather than output.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.2] It shows countries whose income is high but whose human development lags, such as oil-rich states, and countries that achieve good health and education on modest incomes.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.3] It can be tracked over time, showing progress or decline.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.4] It helps governments and aid agencies target spending, for example on schooling or health, because it shows which area is weakest.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.b] Like any single number, the HDI leaves things out and hides differences. The UNDP has tried to answer some of these criticisms.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.0] As a national average it hides inequality between rich and poor, men and women, regions and ethnic groups.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.1] It leaves out political freedom, human rights, gender equality, environmental sustainability, safety and happiness.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.2] Data are poor or out of date in some developing countries, and the figures are published about two years after the data year.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.3] The three dimensions are weighted equally, which is a judgement: nobody can prove they matter equally.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.4] Very high-income countries cluster close to 1, so small differences between them mean little.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.5] The UNDP introduced three newer measures in 2010 to answer some of these criticisms: the inequality-adjusted HDI, the Gender Inequality Index and the Multidimensional Poverty Index, the last developed with Oxford University's Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.b] GNP and HDI measure different things, so a country can rank very differently on each. Using both, with other indicators, gives the fullest picture.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.0] GNP measures output and income; HDI measures human outcomes in health, education and living standards.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.1] Equatorial Guinea has far higher GNI per head than its HDI rank suggests, because of oil wealth; the gap between its income rank and its HDI rank is large.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.2] Oil-rich Gulf states generally rank higher on income than on HDI.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.3] Cuba ranks higher on HDI than on income, because of strong public health care and education.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.4] GNI per head is one of the four HDI indicators, so the two measures overlap; the HDI adds life expectancy and schooling to it.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.b] No single number captures development, so geographers use a set of social and economic indicators. Data-response questions give these in tables.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.0] Social indicators: life expectancy, infant and under-five mortality (deaths of babies and young children), literacy rate, years of schooling, doctors per 1,000 people, access to safe water and sanitation, and daily calorie intake.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.1] Economic indicators: share of workers in the primary sector (farming and other raw-material work, high in developing economies), unemployment rate, energy use per head, and internet and phone access.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.2] Several indicators used together give a more reliable picture than any one measure, because each one can mislead on its own.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.b] Life expectancy at birth varies by decades around the world. The reasons are mostly health, living conditions and conflict.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.0] Access to health care: doctors, hospitals and clinics are scarce in many poorer countries, so illness goes untreated.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.1] Vaccination and clean water and sanitation prevent disease; where they are lacking, life expectancy is lower.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.2] Infectious disease: malaria caused about 600,000 deaths in 2022, over nine-tenths of them in Africa (WHO World Malaria Report 2023).
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.3] HIV/AIDS lowered life expectancy in southern Africa in the 1990s and 2000s, in Botswana, Zimbabwe and South Africa; it has since recovered with antiretroviral drugs (medicines that control the virus).
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.4] Lifestyle: diet, smoking, alcohol and obesity shorten life in some rich countries.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.5] War and conflict, and income and poverty, reduce life expectancy.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.6] Life expectancy at birth is about 84 years in Japan and about 82 to 83 years in Ireland, among the highest in the EU. It is below 60 in several sub-Saharan African countries, including Chad, Nigeria and Lesotho (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.b] How long children stay in school depends on what the state provides and on what families can afford. Each reason below keeps children out of the classroom.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.0] Government spending on schools and teachers largely determines how many children are educated.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.1] Poverty forces children to work or stay at home, because families need the income or help.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.2] Distance to schools is a barrier in rural areas.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.3] Fees, uniforms and books are costs some families cannot pay.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.4] Cultural attitudes to girls' education and early marriage keep girls out of school.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.5] Conflict closes schools.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.b] Education is both a result of development and a cause of it. A skilled workforce brings benefits that feed back into the economy.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.0] An educated workforce attracts foreign investment (money from overseas firms) in high-tech and service industries, which need skilled workers.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.1] Higher productivity (output per worker) and wages raise tax revenue, which funds further services.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.2] An educated workforce produces more innovation and more new firms.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.3] Educated women tend to have healthier, smaller families.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.4] Ireland has one of the highest shares of 25 to 34-year-olds with third-level education in the EU (early 2020s, Eurostat). Free secondary education was introduced in 1967.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.5] Universities and technological universities supply graduates to multinationals in pharmaceuticals, medical devices and information technology.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.b] The unemployment rate is the share of people who want work but cannot find it. A high rate signals a weak economy and causes problems of its own.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.0] Effects of high unemployment: lost income and poverty, emigration of young people, lower tax revenue and higher welfare costs (payments to people without work), and social problems.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.1] Ways to reduce unemployment: attracting investment, retraining and education, support for small firms, infrastructure spending and promoting tourism, each of which creates jobs or prepares people for them.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.2] Ireland's unemployment rose to about 15% in 2012 after the 2008 crash and fell below 5% by 2022 (CSO).
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.b] Data-response questions ask for a chart drawn from a table and a calculated difference. The values below are illustrative only, not real figures.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.0] A bar chart needs a title, labelled axes with units, a suitable scale, accurate bars and a key.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.1] A pie chart needs the same title and key, with each slice's share of the whole shown clearly.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.2] To calculate a difference, subtract the lower value from the higher and state the unit: for example, an illustrative country with a life expectancy of 80 years and another with 60 years differ by 20 years.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.3] There is more on the detailed graph method in the Tables, Graphs and Data Skills chapter.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-development-s2: 2. Spatial variations in development
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.b] Development (how wealthy and well-off people are, in income, health and education) is not spread evenly. It varies between continents, countries, regions within a country and districts within cities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.0] The Brandt Line, from the Brandt Report North–South: A Programme for Survival (1980), separates the rich North from the poorer South. It dates from 1980, so it is not a current UN classification.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.1] The Brandt Commission that wrote the report was chaired by the former West German Chancellor Willy Brandt. The line runs roughly along 30°N, dipping south so that Australia and New Zealand fall in the North.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.2] The North (North America, Europe, Russia, Japan, Australia and New Zealand) is richer than the South (Latin America, Africa and most of Asia).
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.3] The line is a simplification because some countries in the South are now rich: Singapore, South Korea and the Gulf states.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.4] The terms First World and Third World are outdated. The critique of these labels belongs to the Global Interdependence option.
[geo-economic-development-s2.0.5] Global inequality is extreme: the richest tenth of the world's people own about 76% of global wealth, and the poorest half own about 2% (World Inequality Report 2022). The justice angle is in the Global issues and a justice perspective note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.b] The Human Development Index (HDI) combines income, health and education into one score per country. Mapping it shows the same North–South pattern as the Brandt Line, with important exceptions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.0] Very high HDI is found in places such as Europe, North America, Australia, Japan and the Gulf.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.1] High and medium HDI cover most of Latin America, East and South-East Asia and North Africa.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.2] Low HDI is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (Africa south of the Sahara), with Afghanistan, Yemen and Haiti outside Africa. Examples in Africa include Niger, Chad, South Sudan, Somalia and the Central African Republic. Haiti is the poorest country in the Americas.
[geo-economic-development-s2.1.3] The named rankings and categories are in the Measuring economic development note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.b] Even inside one rich trading bloc, development differs sharply. GDP per head (the value of a country's output divided by its population) shows a richer core and a poorer edge.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.0] Luxembourg has the highest GDP per head in the EU, Ireland the second-highest, and Bulgaria the lowest (Eurostat, early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.1] Luxembourg's GDP per head is inflated by cross-border commuters, who add to its output but live elsewhere, so it overstates living standards there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.2] The EU's core is a richer north-west belt, the Blue Banana, and its periphery (the edge, far from the main centres) lies in the east and south. There is more on core and periphery in the Socio-economic regions note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.3] Within member states, Italy's Mezzogiorno (the poorer south of Italy) is about 22% of Italy's GDP, with GDP per head a little over half that of the Centre-North in recent years.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.4] Romania and Bulgaria contain many of the EU's less developed regions, which fits Bulgaria's lowest GDP per head.
[geo-economic-development-s2.2.5] An EU less developed region has GDP per head under 75% of the EU average. Such regions receive most of the EU's cohesion funding (money paid to narrow the gap between richer and poorer regions).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.b] Ireland is rich overall, but incomes differ by region. Dublin pulls ahead because of the jobs and functions concentrated there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.0] The Dublin region has the highest disposable income per person (income left after tax, available to spend) in the State. The Border and Midland regions are consistently among the lowest (CSO county income figures, 2010s to 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.1] County Dublin has about 1.45 million of the State's about 5.1 million people (Census 2022), so it holds about 28% of the population.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.2] Dublin's advantages are its capital and port functions, multinational and financial-services investment, universities and the airport. Together these provide most of the high-value jobs, which is why incomes are highest there.
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.3] The West and North-West are peripheral, with remoteness, weaker infrastructure, dependence on farming and small firms, and out-migration (people leaving to find work elsewhere).
[geo-economic-development-s2.3.4] Peripheral location does not by itself mean poverty: the West is peripheral within a very rich country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.b] The State has tried to narrow the gap between Dublin and the rest. Policy combines regional bodies, planning targets and spending on transport and broadband.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.0] The BMW region (Border, Midland and Western) held Objective 1 status, the EU's top category for regions needing extra funding, for 2000 to 2006.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.1] Three regional assemblies (regional bodies of local councillors) have operated since 2015, so regional planning has its own bodies: Northern and Western; Southern; and Eastern and Midland.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.2] Project Ireland 2040, launched in February 2018, combines the National Planning Framework (the planning strategy) and the National Development Plan (the investment plan).
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.3] The National Planning Framework aims for the Northern and Western and Southern regions together to grow as much as the Eastern and Midland region, so that growth is balanced.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.4] It also aims for half of future population growth to go to the five cities: Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.5] Metrolink is a planned metro line from Swords, via Dublin Airport, to Dublin city centre. The National Broadband Plan (contract signed 2019) aims to bring high-speed broadband to rural homes.
[geo-economic-development-s2.4.6] Transport investment can reduce regional income differences: better access attracts firms to regional towns, cuts costs and commuting times, and widens the job market. It also reduces congestion, shortens journeys and lowers emissions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.b] National averages hide local differences. Rich countries contain poor districts, and large countries contain regions at very different levels.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.0] Even rich places contain pockets of deprivation, and poor regions exist within richer countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.1] India: Census 2011 literacy was about 94% in Kerala and about 62% in Bihar. Fertility (children per woman) was about 1.8 in Kerala and about 3.0 in Bihar (NFHS-5, 2019 to 2021).
[geo-economic-development-s2.5.2] Brazil: the poor North-East contrasts with the rich South-East, an example of a poor region inside one country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.b] Physical factors affect development by limiting or supporting farming, health and transport. They influence development but do not determine it.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.0] The Sahel, the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara (Mali, Niger, Chad and Burkina Faso), has suffered repeated droughts since the 1970s, which limit farming and cause famine, because crops fail without reliable rain.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.1] In Ethiopia, outside the Sahel, drought worsened by civil war led to the 1984 to 1985 famine, followed by Live Aid (the 1985 charity appeal) in 1985.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.2] Extreme heat and tropical storms add difficulty, while temperate climates (mild, without extremes) suit productive farming.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.3] Climate does not decide development: Singapore is tropical, yet it is one of the world's richest countries. Botswana shows that a poor start can be overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.4] Malaria and other tropical diseases reduce life expectancy, the productivity of workers and children's schooling, because ill people cannot work or attend school. The malaria figure is in the Measuring economic development note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.6.5] Mountains, deserts and poor soils limit farming and make transport costly, while fertile lowlands and river plains support dense populations and farming surpluses.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.b] Where a country lies, what lies under it and what hazards strike it all shape its wealth. These can help or hold back development.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.0] Landlocked countries (with no coast) depend on neighbours' roads and ports, which raises trade costs. Landlocked developing countries include Chad, Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, Malawi, Uganda, Afghanistan and Bolivia.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.1] Coastal countries with good harbours trade more easily, because ships can reach them directly. Peripheral location adds to transport costs for Ireland and for the EU's outlying regions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.2] Natural resources can fund development: Botswana's diamonds were discovered at Orapa in 1967 and are mined by Debswana, a joint venture of the government and De Beers.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.3] Norway's oil has been extracted from the North Sea since the early 1970s, and its sovereign wealth fund (a state-owned investment fund), the Government Pension Fund Global, is one of the world's largest.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.4] Resources can also bring the resource curse of corruption, conflict and dependence on one export. Nigeria's oil from the Niger Delta dominates its exports, and oil spills and conflict have affected the Delta.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.5] The DR Congo produces about 70% of the world's cobalt (early 2020s), yet it is one of the poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.6] Haiti's earthquake of 12 January 2010, magnitude 7.0, struck near Port-au-Prince and killed over 100,000 people (estimates vary).
[geo-economic-development-s2.7.7] Floods, cyclones and droughts destroy infrastructure and set poor countries back for years, while rich countries recover faster.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.b] Social factors concern the people themselves: how many there are, how educated and healthy they are, and how women are treated. Each affects how productive a country can become.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.0] Rapid population growth spreads spending on schools and health thinly and creates more young dependants (children supported by workers). Niger has the world's highest fertility rate, about 6 to 7 children per woman (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.1] A falling birth rate can give a demographic dividend, a larger share of working-age people to drive growth.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.2] An educated workforce raises productivity and attracts investment. Low literacy, especially among women, holds development back, as the Kerala and Bihar contrast shows.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.3] Disease, malnutrition and HIV/AIDS reduce the workforce and life expectancy, and health spending raises productivity.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.4] Where girls are educated and women work outside the home, families are smaller, children are healthier and household incomes are higher.
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.5] Brain drain: doctors, nurses and graduates leave poor countries, which loses skills. Remittances (money sent home by migrants) help offset this; India is the world's largest recipient of remittances (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.8.6] Water, sanitation, electricity, roads and internet are both a cause and a result of development: richer places can afford them, and having them makes growth easier.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.b] Cultural factors are the beliefs, customs and social divisions of a society. They can widen or narrow people's opportunities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.0] Attitudes to women's roles and girls' education matter because they shape schooling: early marriage reduces schooling and raises fertility.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.1] Social structures and inequality limit opportunity for some groups: caste in India, land concentration in Latin America (a few owners holding most land) and ethnic divisions.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.2] Many languages within borders drawn in the colonial period can make national unity and education harder. A world language such as English can help, as in India's IT sector.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.3] The colonial cultural legacy, which still shapes society, includes European languages, education aimed at a small elite and a culture of exporting raw materials.
[geo-economic-development-s2.9.4] Attitudes to enterprise, saving and education differ between societies: South Korea places a high value on education. Traditional farming practices and land tenure (how land is owned and held) can slow change.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.b] Governments set the conditions for growth: peace, honest institutions and sensible policy attract investment, while conflict and mismanagement drive it away.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.0] Political stability and good governance attract investment and allow long-term planning. Botswana became independent in 1966 and has held regular elections as a multi-party democracy since independence.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.1] War destroys infrastructure, displaces people and scares off investors, so growth stops. Syria's civil war from 2011 displaced millions. South Sudan became independent in July 2011 and entered civil war in December 2013; There is more on Sudan in the Case study of a developing economy note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.2] Corruption (misuse of office for private gain) diverts public money and aid. Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index ranks Denmark at or near the top and Somalia and South Sudan at or near the bottom (2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.3] Colonial history left borders, export economies and weak institutions; the detail is in the Case study of a developing economy note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.4] Government economic policy matters: education, infrastructure, openness to trade and investment, and industrial policy (state support for chosen industries), as in South Korea, China and Ireland, contrast with mismanagement, as in Zimbabwe and Venezuela.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.5] Debt repayments take money from schools and hospitals, and rich countries' farm subsidies and tariffs (taxes on imports) disadvantage poor exporters. The detail is in the Global issues and a justice perspective note.
[geo-economic-development-s2.10.6] EU membership and funds helped Ireland, Spain and Portugal, all major recipients of EU regional and cohesion funds, to converge with richer states (catch up with them).
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.b] Levels of development are not permanent. Countries can move up or down, and theory suggests why gaps can persist or close.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.0] Development is not fixed: countries can rise, as Ireland, South Korea, China and Botswana have, or fall, as Zimbabwe, Venezuela and Syria have.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.1] A falling HDI is usually caused by war, economic collapse or disease, because each destroys incomes, health or schooling.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.2] Cumulative causation: core regions keep growing because they attract capital and skilled migrants, so success feeds further success.
[geo-economic-development-s2.11.3] Spread effects: growth can sometimes spread from the core to the periphery. Full core–periphery theory is in The Global Economy: Multinationals and Globalisation chapter; models of development are in the Global Interdependence option.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.b] South Korea went from war-torn poverty to a high-income industrial economy. Its growth came from planning, industry and education rather than resources.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.0] The Korean War (1950 to 1953) destroyed so much that it left the South among the world's poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.1] From the 1960s, under Park Chung-hee, the government planned export-led industrialisation (growth by making goods to sell abroad): textiles first, then steel, shipbuilding, cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.2] Chaebol, large family-controlled conglomerates (groups of many firms) such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG, drove the industrial growth.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.3] The government invested heavily in education. South Korea joined the OECD (a club of rich countries) in 1996.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.4] The 1997 Asian financial crisis hit South Korea hard, but it recovered. UNCTAD (the UN trade and development body) reclassified it as a developed country in 2021, and it is now a very high HDI country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.12.5] Its growth came from industry and education, not from natural resources.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.b] China's rise began with deliberate reform. It has lifted huge numbers out of poverty, but the gains are uneven.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.0] Economic reforms began in 1978 under Deng Xiaoping, known as opening up (allowing trade and foreign investment).
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.1] Special Economic Zones (areas with special rules to attract foreign business) were set up from 1980, the first at Shenzhen, beside Hong Kong.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.2] China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, which gave its exporters easier access to world markets.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.3] The World Bank estimates that close to 800 million Chinese were lifted out of extreme poverty since 1978 (World Bank, 2022).
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.4] China has been the world's second-largest economy since 2010, overtaking Japan, but it is upper-middle income, not a developed country.
[geo-economic-development-s2.13.5] Coastal provinces are much richer than the western interior, and hundreds of millions of rural people migrated to coastal cities.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.b] Botswana began as one of the world's poorest countries and put its diamond revenue into roads, schools and clinics. It shows that a poor start can be overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.0] Botswana became independent in 1966 as one of the world's poorest countries.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.1] Diamond revenue funded roads, schools and clinics, so the money was turned into development, and stable democratic government and comparatively low corruption helped.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.2] Botswana is now an upper-middle-income country, so its start as one of the poorest was overcome.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.3] HIV/AIDS sharply reduced life expectancy in the 1990s and 2000s; it recovered with free antiretroviral treatment.
[geo-economic-development-s2.14.4] Dependence on diamonds is a weakness of the economy, because the country relies on one resource.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.b] Ireland moved from stagnation and emigration in the 1950s to one of the richest EU states. Policy change, foreign investment and EU membership drove it, with a serious setback in 2008.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.0] Heavy emigration in the 1950s, a sign of stagnation: net emigration was about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.1] T. K. Whitaker's Economic Development (1958) led to the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958), which offered free trade, grants and tax breaks for foreign firms.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.2] Free secondary education came in 1967, and Ireland joined the EEC (the forerunner of the EU) on 1 January 1973.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.3] The Celtic Tiger boom ran from the mid-1990s to 2007. Free secondary education, free trade and foreign investment helped drive it.
[geo-economic-development-s2.15.4] A property crash and banking crisis from 2008 led to an EU–IMF bailout of €85 billion, agreed in November 2010; Ireland exited the programme in December 2013.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.b] Development can also go into reverse. These three countries show economic mismanagement, dependence on one export and war pulling living standards down.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.0] Zimbabwe: land seizures from 2000 cut farm output, so the economy suffered; hyperinflation (prices rising out of control) peaked in 2008, and the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned in 2009.
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.1] Venezuela: the economy depended on oil. After oil prices collapsed in 2014, the economy shrank by well over half, and more than 7 million people emigrated (2024).
[geo-economic-development-s2.16.2] Syria: its HDI fell because of the civil war that began in 2011, reversing decades of progress.
