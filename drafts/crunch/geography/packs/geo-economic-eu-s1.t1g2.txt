TARGET geo-economic-eu-s1: 1. EU trading patterns
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.b] A trading bloc is a group of countries that remove trade barriers between themselves and act together in trade with the rest of the world. The EU is the biggest example, and its two founding trade tools are a customs union and the single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.0] The EU accounted for about 13% of world goods trade in 2024, second to China in goods. Counting goods and services together, the EU is the world's largest trader, with China and the USA as the other two of the three largest.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.0.1] Customs union: completed on 1 July 1968 (as the EEC, the European Economic Community, the EU's earlier name), it removes tariffs (taxes on imports) between members and applies a common external tariff to imports from outside the EU, so every member charges the same rate.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.b] The single market, in force from 1 January 1993 and set up by the Single European Act of 1986, goes beyond the customs union. A customs union only deals with tariffs, whereas the single market also tackles every other obstacle to trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.0] It guarantees the four freedoms: free movement of goods, services, capital (money and investment) and people.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.1] It removes non-tariff barriers (obstacles to trade other than taxes): border checks, different national product standards and national preference in public contracts (governments favouring their own firms).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.2] Common standards, such as the CE mark (a label showing a product meets EU rules), replace those barriers, so one product can be sold in every member state.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.1.3] The single market extends beyond the 27 members: the European Economic Area (EEA) brings in Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein, while Switzerland, which is in neither the EU nor the EEA, trades under bilateral agreements (separate deals made with the EU).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.b] Trade questions use a small set of terms, and examiners expect you to define them precisely.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.0] Imports are goods or services bought from abroad; exports are goods or services sold abroad.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.1] Visible trade is trade in goods, things you can see and touch; invisible trade is trade in services, such as finance, tourism and transport.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.2] Balance of trade: the value of exports minus the value of imports. A positive balance is a surplus (the country sells more than it buys); a negative balance is a deficit (it buys more than it sells).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.3] A tariff is a tax on imports; a quota is a limit on the quantity of imports allowed. Both make imports harder to sell.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.2.4] Intra-EU (internal) trade is trade between member states; extra-EU (external) trade is trade with countries outside the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.b] Most member states trade mainly with each other, because the single market makes it the easiest and cheapest option. The share, though, varies a great deal from country to country.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.0] In 2024 intra-EU goods exports were about €4.0 trillion, against about €2.6 trillion of extra-EU goods exports. So about three-fifths (about 60%) of members' goods exports stayed within the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.1] Germany is by far the largest intra-EU trader, with intra-EU goods exports of about €845 billion in 2024; Cyprus is the smallest, at about €1 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.2] The core of the EU, the Blue Banana (a curved band of dense industry and population) from south-east England through the Benelux countries and the Rhine valley to northern Italy, together with the Paris Basin, dominates internal trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.3] Peripheral members (those on the edge of the EU, far from the core), such as Cyprus and Greece, trade much less within the EU.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.3.4] Eastern members depend most on intra-EU trade. Intra-EU trade was about 81% of Czechia's goods trade in 2024, compared with about 30% for Cyprus, because of their proximity to Germany and their integration into its supply chains (the linked steps in making a product, spread across countries).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.b] The goods that cross borders inside the EU are mostly high-value manufactures. The pattern of trade takes three forms: swapping similar goods, linked production chains, and swapping different goods.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.0] Manufactured goods dominate: machinery, vehicles and parts, and chemicals and pharmaceuticals. Food and farm produce come next, and services such as finance, tourism and transport add to the total.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.1] Intra-industry trade: members swap similar goods, such as cars for cars, and components cross borders several times in supply chains before the finished product is sold.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.2] Since the 2004 enlargement the car industry has linked Germany with Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary and Poland, where assembly plants supply the German-centred industry. Volkswagen took over Škoda (Czechia) in stages from 1991 and has fully owned it since 2000.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.3] Slovakia makes more cars per head of population than any other country.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.4.4] Complementary trade (members swapping goods the other lacks): greenhouse fruit and vegetables from Almería in southern Spain are trucked north to northern Europe, while northern manufactured goods flow south and east.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.b] Some trade figures look larger than the country's own economy would suggest, because goods pass through its ports on the way to somewhere else.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.0] Rotterdam in the Netherlands is Europe's largest port and Antwerp in Belgium is the second largest; both sit at the mouth of the Rhine-Meuse-Scheldt delta, where the Rhine reaches the sea.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.1] Goods entering and leaving the single market through these ports are counted as trade with the Netherlands and Belgium, which gives both countries very large trade totals.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.5.2] The Rotterdam effect: goods recorded as trade with the Netherlands may be going on to other member states, so the figure overstates what the Netherlands itself buys or sells.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.b] Trade inside the EU is high because several things lower its costs and widen its market. Exams ask you to explain these factors, so give each with its reason.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.0] Removal of tariffs and border controls: the customs union, the single market and Schengen border-free travel for most members cut costs and delays.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.1] The euro: 21 countries used it from 1 January 2026, with Bulgaria the newest. Trade among them has no exchange costs or exchange-rate risk, and prices are easy to compare.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.2] The euro is not the currency of the whole EU, which has 27 members. The euro's history is in The complexity of regions (i).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.3] Proximity and transport: short distances, dense motorway and rail networks, the Rhine (navigable to Basel in Switzerland), the Channel Tunnel (opened 1994) and the Øresund Bridge between Copenhagen and Malmö (opened 2000).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.4] EU-funded Trans-European Transport Networks (TEN-T) also add links, so goods move quickly and cheaply between member states.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.6.5] Common policies: the CAP (Common Agricultural Policy, the EU's farm policy) and common food standards make farm and food trade easy within the EU. There is more on the CAP in How the EU influences the Irish economy.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.b] The size of the market and changes in membership also shape internal trade.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.0] A large, wealthy market: about 450 million people in 2024, with high incomes and broadly similar tastes, so a product made for one country sells easily in others.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.1] Enlargement widened the single market: ten countries joined in 2004, Bulgaria and Romania in 2007 and Croatia in 2013. New members opened new markets and lower-cost production sites in the east.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.2] Brexit moved the UK from internal to external partner. The UK left on 31 January 2020; the Trade and Cooperation Agreement has applied since 1 January 2021, with zero tariffs and zero quotas on qualifying goods (those meeting rules of origin).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.7.3] Trade with the UK nonetheless became more costly because of customs declarations, checks and rules-of-origin paperwork.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.b] In trade with the outside world the EU does not act as 27 separate countries. Its common trade policy gives it a single negotiating position.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.0] The European Commission (the EU's executive body) negotiates trade for all 27 members under the Common Commercial Policy, so the EU speaks with one voice in the World Trade Organization (WTO, the body that sets global trade rules).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.8.1] All members apply the same common external tariff, so the EU bargains as one bloc rather than as 27 separate countries, which gives it more weight.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.b] Outside the EU, a handful of big economies take most of the EU's goods and supply most of its imports. The EU sells more to them than it buys overall.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.0] Export partners for extra-EU goods: the USA is the largest at about 21%, then the UK at about 13%, China at about 8%, Switzerland at about 7-8% and Turkey at about 4%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.1] Import partners for extra-EU goods: China is the largest at about a fifth (about 20-21%), then the USA at about 13-14%, the UK at about 7%, Switzerland at about 6% and Turkey at about 4%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.2] Overall, extra-EU goods exports were about €2.58 trillion and imports about €2.43 trillion, a goods surplus (exports exceed imports) of about €150 billion.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.9.3] The EU had a surplus with the USA and a large deficit with China, meaning it bought far more from China than it sold there. It also had a surplus with the UK.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.b] The EU sells high-value manufactured goods and buys energy, raw materials and cheaper manufactured goods, because it has little energy of its own.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.0] Exports: machinery and vehicles were about €1.0 trillion (about 39% of extra-EU exports); chemicals and related products, including pharmaceuticals, were about €560 billion (about 22%). Food, drink and services are also exported.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.1] Imports: energy products, mainly oil and gas, were about 15% of EU imports by value in 2024, down from about 23% in 2022.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.10.2] Imports also include raw materials, consumer electronics and other manufactured goods from China and Asia, and tropical foods such as coffee, cocoa and bananas, which cannot be grown in the EU's climate.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.b] Political events change trade patterns quickly, as the change in energy imports shows.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.0] Russia was a major gas supplier before its full-scale invasion of Ukraine began on 24 February 2022.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.1] Russian gas fell from about 45% of EU gas imports in 2021 to about 19% in 2024, through sanctions (penalties that restrict trade with a country) and the REPowerEU plan (the EU's plan to cut energy dependence on Russia, launched May 2022).
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.2] The USA supplied almost half of EU liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so ships can carry it) imports in 2024, replacing much of the lost Russian gas.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.11.3] The EU plans to end Russian gas imports by the end of 2027.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.b] The EU widens its external trade by signing agreements with other countries. It also gives poorer countries easier access to its market, though critics say its past policies hurt them.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.0] Trade agreements widen external trade: CETA with Canada has been provisionally applied (in use before final approval) since September 2017; the EU-Japan Economic Partnership Agreement has been in force since February 2019; the EU-South Korea agreement has applied since 2011.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.1] EU-Mercosur negotiations with Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay concluded in December 2024. Irish farm organisations strongly opposed the deal because of competition from South American beef.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.2] The Lomé Convention (1975, with African, Caribbean and Pacific (ACP) countries) and the Cotonou Agreement (2000) gave preferential access (easier, lower-tariff entry to the EU market) to poorer countries.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.3] Everything But Arms (2001) gives the least developed countries duty-free, quota-free access for all goods except arms.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.12.4] Critics argue that tariffs on processed goods (tariff escalation, where the tariff rises as a product is processed further) and past subsidised farm exports (sold cheaply with EU government support) harmed producers in the developing world.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.b] The EU also uses tariffs to protect its industries, and it is on the receiving end of other countries' tariffs. In each case it acts as a single bloc.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.0] The EU-USA agreement of July 2025 capped the US tariff on most EU goods at 15%, including pharmaceuticals; Irish pharmaceutical exports to the USA are among the goods it covers. Steel and aluminium remained at 50%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.1] EU definitive duties (lasting tariffs, not temporary ones) on Chinese-made electric cars, from October 2024, reached up to about 35%.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.13.2] In both disputes the EU acted as one bloc, so one set of duties or one deal applies to all 27 members.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.b] Several factors explain why the EU trades with the outside world as it does. Exams ask you to explain them after describing the patterns.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.0] The size and wealth of the single market gives the EU bargaining power in trade negotiations, because other countries want access to its consumers.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.1] High-tech, high-value industry makes manufactured goods the main exports.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.2] A lack of energy and some raw materials makes the EU dependent on imports, especially of fuels.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.3] Historical and colonial links, including former colonies in Africa, the Caribbean and the Pacific and the Commonwealth ties of the former UK member, shaped the preferential agreements.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.4] Globalisation (the growing worldwide linking of economies): low-cost manufacturing in China and Asia draws in imports.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.5] The trading triad is the three main trading regions, the USA, the EU and the Pacific Rim (the countries around the Pacific Ocean). There is more on the trading triad in Patterns in world trade, in The Global Economy.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.14.6] Politics redirects trade: sanctions on Russia, US tariff disputes and Brexit each changed the direction of trade flows.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.b] Exam data questions give export and import shares for each partner. Use the invented values below only to practise the method.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.0] Identify the largest partner: the biggest slice or the highest value.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.1] Calculate a missing share: subtract the known shares from 100. Illustrative: exports to Country A 25% and Country B 15% leave 100 minus 25 minus 15 = 60% for all other partners.
[geo-economic-eu-s1.15.2] Compare exports and imports with one partner. If the value sent to it is higher than the value received, the EU has a surplus with that partner; if it is lower, a deficit.

CANDIDATES geo-region-european-s4: 4. The complexity of regions (ii)
[geo-region-european-s4.0.b] The Gaeltacht is the area where Irish is the community language, the language people use in everyday life. Its extent has shrunk as speakers have declined, and the decline began in the 1800s.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.0] Census 1851 (a count of the whole population) was the first census to count Irish speakers. It found about 1.5 million Irish speakers, about 23% of the population, and Irish-speaking areas then covered much of the west and south.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.1] The Great Famine (1845–49) and emigration (people leaving the country) hit the poor, western, Irish-speaking areas hardest, so the Irish-speaking population fell fastest where Irish was strongest.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.2] National schools, set up in 1831, taught through English, so children learned to read and write in a language that was not their home language.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.3] English became the language of law, trade, politics, the Church and emigration. Parents saw English as the route to work, so many chose not to pass Irish on to their children.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.4] Census 1891 shows the result: about 680,000 Irish speakers, about 14% of the population, down from about 23% in 1851.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.b] From the 1890s people tried to reverse the decline. The new state then gave the Gaeltacht its first official boundaries, and later redrew them.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.0] The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge), a revival movement, founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill, promoted the language, to stop its decline.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.1] The new state's language policy made Irish compulsory in schools and required for some public jobs, so that the state itself supported the language.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.2] Coimisiún na Gaeltachta (the commission set up to define the Gaeltacht) reported in 1926 and defined two types of Gaeltacht. Fíor-Ghaeltacht ('true' Gaeltacht): districts where 80% or more spoke Irish.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.3] Breac-Ghaeltacht ('partly' Gaeltacht): districts with 25–79% Irish speakers. The 1926 definitions together covered a large area across many western and southern counties.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.4] The Gaeltacht Areas Order 1956 (a government order, not a new law) shrank the official Gaeltacht to areas where Irish was still the community language, because Irish had weakened in the Breac-Ghaeltacht districts.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.5] The Department of the Gaeltacht (Roinn na Gaeltachta) was set up in 1956. Gaeltarra Éireann, the Gaeltacht industry body, followed in 1957. Later small extensions were made by order.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.b] Today's Gaeltacht lies in seven counties, and one of them, Meath, is an unusual case because its Gaeltacht was planned by the state.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.0] Gaeltacht areas lie in seven counties: Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford and Meath.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.1] The Meath Gaeltacht was built by planned colonies (settlements created by the state, not areas where Irish had survived): Ráth Chairn, settled in 1935 by families moved from Connemara, and Baile Ghib, settled in 1937.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.2] Galway (Conamara) has the largest Gaeltacht population; Donegal is second.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.3] Census 2022: the Gaeltacht population was about 106,000, and about 20,000 Gaeltacht residents speak Irish daily outside the education system, so only a minority use it as an everyday language.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.4] Nationally (Census 2022): about 1.87 million people (about 40%) said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.b] The state and communities have built institutions to protect Irish, from development agencies to broadcasting and official recognition.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.0] Údarás na Gaeltachta, the Gaeltacht development authority, was set up in 1980 to replace Gaeltarra Éireann. It creates jobs so people can stay in the Gaeltacht. The Gaoth Dobhair (Gweedore) industrial estate in Co. Donegal is an example of Gaeltacht industry.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.1] Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972. TG4 (originally Teilifís na Gaeilge) launched on 31 October 1996. Both give Irish a presence in the media.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.2] The Official Languages Act 2003 (amended 2021) gives Irish official status, which means public bodies must provide services in it.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.3] Irish became an official EU language in 2007 and gained full working-language status on 1 January 2022.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.4] The 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language, a long-term state plan, covers 2010 to 2030.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.5] Gaeltacht summer colleges give young people intensive immersion in Irish, so they use the language all day.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.b] The 2012 Act changed how the Gaeltacht is defined. Instead of a fixed map, boundaries now depend on how much Irish is actually spoken.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.0] The Gaeltacht Act 2012 defined the Gaeltacht by language use rather than by a fixed map, so boundaries now depend on language use.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.1] Gaeltacht Language Planning Areas: 26, so the Gaeltacht is now a set of planning units, each needing a language plan (a local plan to strengthen the use of Irish).
[geo-region-european-s4.4.2] Gaeltacht Service Towns, and Irish Language Networks outside the Gaeltacht, extend Irish use beyond the core areas.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.b] Even with these supports, the use of Irish as a daily language in the Gaeltacht has kept falling. A 2007 study set out how much daily use is needed to keep it alive.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.0] The Comprehensive Linguistic Study of the Use of Irish in the Gaeltacht (2007) found that Irish is viable as a community language only where at least 67% of people speak it daily.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.1] Its 2015 update warned that Irish could cease to be the community language in most Gaeltacht districts within about ten years.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.2] In-migration of English speakers (people moving in from outside) weakens Irish, because English becomes the language of daily life in the area.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.3] Suburban growth, such as Galway city's spread west, brings the Gaeltacht edge into contact with English-speaking suburbs.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.4] Tourism, emigration of young people and English-language media add further pressure on Irish as the language of the community.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.b] Responses target the pressures above, by keeping Irish speakers in the Gaeltacht and giving them reasons and ways to use the language.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.0] Housing with language conditions, so that Irish speakers can afford to stay in Gaeltacht areas.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.1] Jobs through Údarás na Gaeltachta and Gaeltacht industry, so that people do not have to leave to find work.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.2] Irish-medium schooling (teaching through Irish) and preschools, so children learn the language early.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.3] Language plans in each Language Planning Area.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.4] Family supports for Irish-speaking households, and Irish-language media such as TG4 and Raidió na Gaeltachta.
[geo-region-european-s4.7.b] Belgium is a brief comparison from outside Ireland: a language boundary fixed in law. There is more on its present-day relations in the notes on the next part of this topic.
[geo-region-european-s4.7.0] The language border between Dutch- and French-speaking areas was fixed by laws of 1962 and 1963, so the boundary between language regions became official.
[geo-region-european-s4.7.1] The first state reform came in 1970. Belgium became a federal state in 1993 (power shared between central and regional governments), with Flanders, Wallonia and Brussels-Capital as its regions. Brussels is officially bilingual.
[geo-region-european-s4.8.b] Dublin's growth has spread from the city into the surrounding counties, creating a city region (a city and the area that depends on it).
[geo-region-european-s4.8.0] Suburban expansion (new housing on the edge of the city) from the 1950s and 1960s led to the Advisory Regional Plan for Dublin (Myles Wright, 1967), which proposed new towns west of the city: Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown.

[geo-region-european-s4.8.3] The Greater Dublin Area (Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow) had about 2.1 million people in Census 2022, about 40% of the State's population.
[geo-region-european-s4.8.4] Commuter towns (towns whose residents travel to work in the city) in the surrounding counties, such as Navan, Drogheda, Naas and Newbridge.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.b] As Dublin grew, the areas that govern it were redrawn several times, from adding townships to splitting the county council.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.0] Rathmines and Pembroke townships were added to Dublin city in 1930, extending the city boundary.

[geo-region-european-s4.10.b] A growing city region brings problems, and the state has responded with planning, transport, regeneration and new forms of governance.
[geo-region-european-s4.10.0] Problems: urban sprawl (a city spreading outwards over farmland), long commutes, congestion, housing costs, one-off housing (single houses built in the countryside) and dormitory growth (towns that people sleep in but work elsewhere).

[geo-region-european-s4.10.5] Limerick elected Ireland's first directly elected mayor in June 2024, after a plebiscite (a public vote on one question) in 2019.

[geo-region-european-s4.12.b] The European Union began as a way to make war between European states impossible, by tying their economies together after the Second World War.
[geo-region-european-s4.12.0] Reasons: peace after two world wars; rebuilding after the war; coal and steel placed under shared control, so no state could secretly build up weapons; economic growth; the Cold War (the rivalry between the communist East and the capitalist West).
[geo-region-european-s4.12.1] Schuman Declaration, 9 May 1950 (now Europe Day), proposed pooling coal and steel production.
[geo-region-european-s4.12.2] The Treaty of Paris (1951) put the shared control of coal and steel into practice, because it set up the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC).
[geo-region-european-s4.12.3] The Treaty of Rome (1957, in force 1958) set up the European Economic Community (EEC) and Euratom (the European Atomic Energy Community).
[geo-region-european-s4.12.4] The six founders ('the Six') were Belgium, France, West Germany, Italy, Luxembourg and the Netherlands.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.b] Deepening means the members drew closer together, with more shared rules and institutions. Only the dates are given here; the issues each step raised come later.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.0] Single European Act: 1986, in force 1987.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.1] Single market (one market with free movement of goods, services, people and money, so trade between members is easier): from 1 January 1993.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.2] Maastricht Treaty: signed 7 February 1992; in force 1 November 1993, creating the European Union.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.3] Euro (the shared currency): launched 1 January 1999 for banks and electronic payments; notes and coins from 1 January 2002.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.4] Lisbon Treaty: in force 1 December 2009.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.b] Widening means adding new member states. Each enlargement is listed by date, with the countries that joined.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.0] 1973: Ireland, the UK and Denmark, the first enlargement. 1981: Greece. 1986: Spain and Portugal.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.1] 1990: East Germany joined through reunification with West Germany, not as a new member state.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.2] 1995: Austria, Finland and Sweden.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.3] 2004: ten countries joined: Cyprus, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.4] 2007: Bulgaria and Romania, not 2004. 2013: Croatia, the most recent member.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.5] The UK left on 31 January 2020, so it is now a former member, leaving 27 members with about 450 million people (2024).
[geo-region-european-s4.15.b] The end of communism in Eastern Europe made the biggest enlargement possible, and it changed the EU for both new and old members.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.0] The Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989, opening the way for former communist states to seek EU membership.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.1] Copenhagen criteria (1993), the conditions for joining: stable democracy, rule of law, human rights and protection of minorities; a working market economy able to cope with competition; and the ability to take on the obligations of membership.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.2] Most of the 2004 entrants (such as Poland and Hungary), and both 2007 entrants, were former communist states, which is why the fall of communism led to this enlargement.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.3] Effects on newer members: economic growth, EU funds and emigration (workers leaving for richer members).
[geo-region-european-s4.15.4] Effects on older members: competition for jobs and investment, and migration from the newer members.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.5] Croatia joined the Schengen area (the area of passport-free travel between member states) in 2023. Bulgaria and Romania joined it fully on 1 January 2025.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.b] Enlargement brought Ireland both opportunities and competition. Ireland opened its doors early in 2004, which shaped who came and what followed.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.0] In 2004 Ireland, the UK and Sweden were the only existing members to give the new members' citizens immediate access to their labour markets.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.1] Polish was the most common language other than English and Irish spoken at home in Census 2016 and Census 2022, showing the size of the Polish community.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.2] Advantages: a larger market; a labour supply for the Celtic Tiger economy (Ireland's period of rapid growth); cultural diversity; export opportunities.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.3] Disadvantages: competition for foreign direct investment (companies from abroad setting up factories and offices) from lower-cost states; less EU regional funding for Ireland; pressure on wages and services.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.4] Example of investment competition: Dell announced in January 2009 that it would move its manufacturing from Limerick to Łódź, Poland, with the loss of about 1,900 jobs.
[geo-region-european-s4.17.b] Further expansion would affect both the EU and the countries waiting to join. The current candidate countries are named in a separate subsection.
[geo-region-european-s4.17.0] Benefits of further expansion for the EU: a larger single market with more consumers, which means more trade, and greater stability on its borders.
[geo-region-european-s4.17.1] Benefits for candidates: access to the single market and to EU funding, and, because they must meet the Copenhagen criteria, reforms to meet the Copenhagen criteria (the conditions for joining).
[geo-region-european-s4.18.b] Partition of Ireland (dividing the island into two political units) redrew a political boundary across a single island. Its present-day effects, including the Good Friday Agreement and Brexit, are in the next subsection.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.0] The Government of Ireland Act 1920 partitioned the island. Northern Ireland was established in 1921 with six of Ulster's nine counties, so Ulster itself was divided.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.1] Cavan, Donegal and Monaghan went to the Irish Free State.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.2] The Anglo-Irish Treaty was signed in December 1921.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.3] Boundary Commission (1925), the body set up to review the border: its report was not published, and the border was left unchanged.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.4] The border created a nationalist minority in the north (people who wanted a united Ireland) and a small Protestant minority in the south.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.5] Impact: nationalists in Northern Ireland faced discrimination, which fuelled the civil rights movement from 1968 and then the Troubles (the conflict in Northern Ireland).
[geo-region-european-s4.18.6] Communities, parishes and farms were divided by the border, so everyday life was cut across.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.b] Yugoslavia was a multi-ethnic federal state that fell apart in the 1990s. Its break-up shows how new borders can leave cultural groups on the wrong side.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.0] Yugoslavia was a multi-ethnic federal state. Its new borders followed the old internal republic lines, leaving Serb, Croat and Bosniak minorities on the 'wrong' side of them.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.1] Slovenia and Croatia declared independence in 1991, breaking away from the federal state; Bosnia and Herzegovina followed in 1992.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.2] War and ethnic cleansing (forcing a group out of an area by violence) followed in Bosnia and Herzegovina.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.3] Srebrenica, July 1995: about 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were killed by Bosnian Serb forces.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.4] The Dayton Agreement (1995) divided Bosnia and Herzegovina into two entities: the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and Republika Srpska.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.5] Montenegro became independent in 2006, a further split of the old state. Kosovo declared independence on 17 February 2008; Serbia does not recognise it.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.b] After the Second World War, borders in central Europe were moved by the victorious powers, and millions of people moved with them.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.0] In 1945 Poland's western border moved to the Oder–Neisse line. The USSR took Poland's eastern lands, so Poland shifted westward.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.1] Millions of Germans were expelled westwards, and Poles were resettled from the east into the lands Germany had lost.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.2] The Berlin Wall was built in 1961, dividing Germany and Berlin, so the city was split in two.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.3] Germany was reunified on 3 October 1990, so the division ended, after the Berlin Wall fell in 1989.
[geo-region-european-s4.21.b] The collapse of communism also split two multi-national states. One split left minorities behind; the other was peaceful.
[geo-region-european-s4.21.0] The USSR (the Soviet Union) broke up in 1991 into 15 independent states, such as Estonia and Latvia.
[geo-region-european-s4.21.1] Ethnic Russians became minorities in newly independent states. In Estonia and Latvia they make up roughly a fifth to a quarter of the population.
[geo-region-european-s4.21.2] Czechoslovakia split peacefully into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on 1 January 1993, the 'Velvet Divorce'.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.b] Cyprus is a divided island that is also an EU member, so a political boundary there has a direct effect on how EU law applies.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.0] The Turkish invasion of July 1974 divided Cyprus between Greek Cypriots in the south and Turkish Cypriots in the north.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.1] The UN buffer zone, the Green Line (a strip that keeps the two sides apart), runs through Nicosia.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.2] The 'Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus' is recognised only by Turkey, so almost no other state accepts it.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.3] 2004 Annan Plan referendum: about 65% of Turkish Cypriots voted Yes, and about 76% of Greek Cypriots voted No.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.4] Cyprus joined the EU in 2004, one of ten countries that year,, but EU law is suspended in the north, so the division continues inside the EU.
[geo-region-european-s4.23.b] A non-European example of a political boundary redrawn along religious lines.
[geo-region-european-s4.23.0] Partition of India, August 1947: India and Pakistan (including East Pakistan, now Bangladesh) were created along religious lines.
[geo-region-european-s4.23.1] Around 15 million people were displaced, and hundreds of thousands were killed in communal violence (violence between religious communities).
[geo-region-european-s4.24.b] The examples can be pulled together into three conclusions, which are useful for ending a 30-mark answer.
[geo-region-european-s4.24.0] Boundaries drawn across cultural groups create minorities, displacement and conflict, as in Ireland, Yugoslavia and India.
[geo-region-european-s4.24.1] Boundaries drawn around cultural groups, as in Belgium, can reduce conflict but can harden division.
[geo-region-european-s4.24.2] Shared institutions, such as power-sharing and EU membership, can soften the effect of a border.

CANDIDATES geo-region-european-s3: 3. The complexity of regions (i)
[geo-region-european-s3.0.b] Ireland is a single island but two political regions: the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland. Their economic, political and cultural activities interact across a border. Both regions exist because of the 1921 partition (the division of the island into two political units), and their cultural groups are not evenly matched, so it helps to know the starting facts about each.
[geo-region-european-s3.0.0] Northern Ireland was established in 1921 with six counties: Antrim, Armagh, Down, Fermanagh, Londonderry (Derry) and Tyrone. It is part of the United Kingdom, so it is governed within the UK, not by the Irish state.
[geo-region-european-s3.0.1] The Irish Free State was established in 1922. Ireland left the Commonwealth (the association of former British territories) and was declared a republic in 1949.
[geo-region-european-s3.0.2] The Republic has about 5.15 million people (Census 2022, the official population count) and Northern Ireland about 1.9 million (Census 2021), so the two regions differ greatly in size.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.b] Northern Ireland's population is divided into two main cultural groups. Their identity, religion, schooling and language differ, and this is the background to the political interaction that follows.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.0] Nationalists, mostly Catholic, identify as Irish. Unionists (people who want Northern Ireland to stay in the UK), mostly Protestant, identify as British. Some people identify as Northern Irish instead.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.1] In Census 2021, by religion (or religion brought up in), Catholic was about 46% and Protestant and other Christian about 43%. This was the first census in which Catholics outnumbered Protestants.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.2] In the Republic, Census 2022 gave Catholic about 69% and no religion about 14%, which shows the Republic's population is far more uniformly Catholic.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.3] Most children in Northern Ireland attend schools of their own community, and housing is often segregated by community (the two groups live in separate areas), so everyday contact between the groups is limited.
[geo-region-european-s3.1.4] Irish and Ulster-Scots (a Scots-based language tradition of Ulster) are officially recognised under the Identity and Language (Northern Ireland) Act 2022.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.b] The two communities disagreed about whether Northern Ireland should be in the UK or united with the Republic, and the disagreement turned violent. The Good Friday Agreement of 1998 set up a new way for them to share power.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.0] The Troubles, from the late 1960s to 1998, killed over 3,500 people and left the two communities divided by violence and political deadlock (no side able to make decisions).
[geo-region-european-s3.2.1] The Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement was signed on 10 April 1998. A referendum (a public vote) approved it on 22 May 1998: about 71% Yes in Northern Ireland and about 94% Yes in the Republic.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.2] The Republic changed Articles 2 and 3 of its Constitution, which had claimed Northern Ireland as part of the national territory, so the Republic gave up that claim.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.3] Power-sharing: a Northern Ireland Assembly (its elected parliament) in which ministers from both communities share government, so neither side can govern alone.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.4] The North/South Ministerial Council and other cross-border bodies link the Irish government with the Northern Ireland executive (its government), so the two regions co-operate on shared issues.
[geo-region-european-s3.2.5] Consent principle: Northern Ireland remains in the UK unless a majority of its people vote otherwise. Under the Agreement, people may hold Irish or British citizenship or both.
[geo-region-european-s3.3.b] Power-sharing needs both communities to take part, so it is fragile. The Assembly shows how political interaction can break down and recover.
[geo-region-european-s3.3.0] The Assembly has been suspended several times, meaning it stopped working and its powers were paused, for example in the two-year collapse before 2024.
[geo-region-european-s3.3.1] After a two-year collapse it was restored in February 2024, when Michelle O'Neill became the first nationalist First Minister.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.b] After 1998, the border mattered less in economic life and the two regions worked together more. Conflict had held this back, so peace let activity grow across the whole island.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.0] Conflict deterred investment and tourism; after 1998, falling violence allowed both to recover on the island as a whole.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.1] Cross-border bodies include InterTradeIreland and Waterways Ireland. Tourism Ireland markets the whole island abroad, so visitors see one destination, not two.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.2] A Single Electricity Market for the whole island has operated since 2007, so electricity is bought and sold as one all-island market.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.3] The Dublin–Belfast Enterprise rail service and the M1/A1 road corridor link the two main cities, which makes cross-border travel and trade easier.
[geo-region-european-s3.4.4] The International Fund for Ireland was set up in 1986. The EU PEACE programme began in 1995 as PEACE I; the current PEACE PLUS programme runs from 2021 to 2027. Both fund peace-building and co-operation.
[geo-region-european-s3.5.b] Culture and sport do not follow the border either. Several organisations cover the whole island, although some cultural symbols still divide communities.
[geo-region-european-s3.5.0] The GAA (Gaelic Athletic Association), the Irish rugby team, the Catholic Church and the Church of Ireland are organised on an all-island basis, so they treat Ireland as one unit.
[geo-region-european-s3.5.1] The Catholic and Church of Ireland archbishops of Armagh are each Primate of All Ireland (the senior bishop of their church across the island).
[geo-region-european-s3.5.2] The Irish language and Irish sport cross the border, while parades and flags remain sources of tension between communities, because they express one community's identity.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.b] Brexit (the UK leaving the EU) shows the three processes interacting at the border. It affected trade, politics and identity on the island all at once.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.0] In the UK referendum on EU membership, 23 June 2016, about 52% voted Leave overall; about 56% voted Remain in Northern Ireland and about 62% Remain in Scotland.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.1] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition period (when old rules still applied) ended on 31 December 2020. The 2016 referendum did not itself end membership.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.2] The Irish–Northern Irish land border is about 499 km long and became the UK–EU land border, which raised the question of customs checks between the two regions.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.3] The Northern Ireland Protocol kept Northern Ireland aligned with many EU single-market rules for goods, avoiding checks at the land border (a hard border). The Windsor Framework (February 2023) revised it.
[geo-region-european-s3.6.4] The Common Travel Area between Ireland and the UK has existed since 1923. Irish passport holders in Northern Ireland keep EU free movement, the right to live and work in the EU.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.b] In Belgium the language communities and the political regions do not coincide, so politics is organised around language. Belgium is the main European example of cultural groups and political regions interacting.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.0] Belgium became independent in 1830. Its population is about 11.8 million (2024).
[geo-region-european-s3.7.1] Flanders (about 6.8 million) is Dutch-speaking in the north. Wallonia (about 3.7 million) is French-speaking in the south. Brussels-Capital (about 1.2 million) is officially bilingual, so both languages are used there.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.2] A German-speaking community in the east has fewer than 80,000 people.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.3] Dutch speakers are about 6 in 10 of the population and French speakers about 4 in 10.
[geo-region-european-s3.7.4] French was long the language of government, courts and universities, so the language of the minority (French, about 4 in 10) once held the main public institutions.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.b] Language differences in Belgium were sharpened by a shift in wealth between north and south. Politics then split along language lines.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.0] Economic reversal: Wallonia's coal and steel industries (Liège, Charleroi and the Sambre–Meuse valley) declined, while Flanders grew through the port of Antwerp, Europe's second-largest port, and modern industry.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.1] The Catholic University of Leuven split in 1968; its French-speaking section moved to a new town, Louvain-la-Neuve, in Wallonia, so even education separated by language.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.2] There are no nationwide parties: each party stands in one language community only, so no party represents voters in all of Belgium.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.3] Political consequences: Belgium went 541 days without a new government in 2010–11, which shows how hard it is to form a government when each party stands in one language community only.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.4] The Flemish nationalist N-VA has been the largest party since 2010, and its leader Bart De Wever became Prime Minister in February 2025.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.5] Belgium's federal solution (power divided between a national government and regional ones) gives its language communities and regions considerable self-government.
[geo-region-european-s3.8.6] Brussels hosts the main institutions of the EU and the headquarters of NATO (a military alliance).
[geo-region-european-s3.9.b] Other European countries also have cultural groups tied to regions. Some share power peacefully, while others have faced independence movements.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.0] Switzerland has four national languages: German (about 6 in 10), French (about 2 in 10), Italian (under 1 in 10) and Romansh (under 1%).
[geo-region-european-s3.9.1] Switzerland has 26 cantons (its regional units) with wide powers. Power is shared between language groups and cantons, which keeps the country stable.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.2] Basque (Euskara) is unrelated to any other European language. The Basque Country lies in both Spain and France.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.3] ETA, a Basque separatist group, declared a definitive end to violence in 2011 and dissolved in 2018.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.4] Catalonia has its own language, Catalan. It held an independence referendum on 1 October 2017 that Spain's courts declared illegal. Spain imposed direct rule (control from the central government) using Article 155 of its constitution.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.5] The Basque Country and Catalonia are autonomous communities of Spain (regions with self-government) and among its wealthiest regions.
[geo-region-european-s3.9.6] Scottish independence referendum, 18 September 2014: about 55% voted No.
[geo-region-european-s3.10.b] The examples above share one pattern, and a general statement of it can round off a 30-mark answer.
[geo-region-european-s3.10.0] Where cultural and political regions do not match, tension arises.
[geo-region-european-s3.10.1] Outcomes range from devolution or federalism (Belgium, Spain) and power-sharing (Northern Ireland) to violent conflict (the Troubles). Devolution means a central government passes some powers to a region.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.b] Political union is the EU's move beyond economic co-operation towards shared political institutions and decisions.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.0] The Maastricht Treaty created the EU and planned the single currency (its dates are in the s4 notes).
[geo-region-european-s3.11.1] The draft EU Constitution was rejected by French and Dutch voters in 2005, so it did not come into force.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.2] The Lisbon Treaty created a permanent President of the European Council and a High Representative for Foreign Affairs. It extended qualified majority voting and the powers of the European Parliament.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.3] Qualified majority voting: a decision passes when enough member states, representing enough of the EU population, support it. Unanimity requires every member state to agree, so one state can block a decision.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.4] European Parliament: 720 MEPs (Members of the European Parliament, elected by voters) after the 2024 elections; Ireland elects 14.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.5] Competing visions: a federal Europe with stronger central power, or a Europe of nation states co-operating on shared aims.
[geo-region-european-s3.11.6] Eurosceptic parties (parties sceptical of the EU) have grown across Europe, opposing further political integration.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.b] Economic union is the EU's shared market and shared currency. Not every member state takes part in both.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.0] The single market removes barriers to trade, services, capital and labour between member states, so goods, services, money and workers can move freely.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.1] 21 member states use the euro in 2026. Bulgaria joined on 1 January 2026, replacing the lev. Croatia joined in 2023.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.2] Euro countries: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia and Spain.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.3] Not in the euro: Denmark (legal opt-out, a formal exemption); Sweden (voted No in a 2003 referendum and stays out by not joining the exchange-rate mechanism); Poland, Czechia, Hungary and Romania.
[geo-region-european-s3.12.4] The European Central Bank in Frankfurt sets one interest rate for the euro area. Since November 2014 its Single Supervisory Mechanism has supervised the big banks.
[geo-region-european-s3.13.b] A state must meet set economic tests before joining the euro, which keeps weaker economies from harming the shared currency. Once in, people gain several benefits.
[geo-region-european-s3.13.0] Convergence (Maastricht) criteria: low inflation (slow price rises); government deficit (spending above income in a year) no more than 3% of GDP (the value of a country's output); government debt no more than 60% of GDP.
[geo-region-european-s3.13.1] Two more criteria: a stable exchange rate, held for two years in ERM II (a system that keeps a currency steady against the euro); and low long-term interest rates.
[geo-region-european-s3.13.2] Benefits for people in the eurozone: no currency exchange costs; easy price comparison between countries; no exchange-rate risk in trade and travel; generally low interest rates and inflation.
[geo-region-european-s3.14.b] The euro area met serious financial trouble after 2010. A bailout (emergency loans to a government) was needed in some countries.
[geo-region-european-s3.14.0] Ireland's EU/IMF bailout of about €85 billion was agreed in November 2010 and exited in December 2013.
[geo-region-european-s3.14.1] Greece received three bailouts (2010, 2012 and 2015), ending in 2018, which shows the crisis there lasted longer than in Ireland.
[geo-region-european-s3.14.2] NextGenerationEU, agreed in 2020, is a recovery fund of about €750 billion financed by joint EU borrowing, so member states borrow together.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.b] Sovereignty is the power to make decisions for a state. Membership pools some of it; some areas stay national.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.0] Pooling sovereignty means member states share decision-making in areas such as trade and the single market.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.1] Taxation, defence and foreign policy remain largely national, and decisions in these areas are mostly made by unanimity.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.2] Brexit is an example of national sovereignty being reasserted against pooled decision-making.
[geo-region-european-s3.15.3] Article 7 procedures, which could suspend a member's voting rights, were opened against Poland (2017) and Hungary (2018) over rule-of-law concerns (whether governments respect courts and legal limits).
[geo-region-european-s3.16.b] Ireland's own history in the EU gives specific examples of sovereignty being pooled, tested and defended.
[geo-region-european-s3.16.0] Crotty v An Taoiseach (1987): Ireland's Supreme Court ruled that significant EU treaty changes need a referendum.
[geo-region-european-s3.16.1] Irish voters rejected the Nice Treaty (2001) and approved it in 2002. They rejected the Lisbon Treaty in June 2008 and approved it in October 2009.
[geo-region-european-s3.16.2] Ireland's corporation tax (tax on company profits) rate is 12.5%. Under the OECD agreement, a 15% minimum rate applies to large firms (turnover over €750 million) from 2024.
[geo-region-european-s3.16.3] Apple ruling: on 10 September 2024 the EU Court of Justice ruled that Ireland must recover €13 billion, plus interest, in unpaid tax from Apple as illegal state aid (help from a state that unfairly favours one company).
[geo-region-european-s3.16.4] Ireland is militarily neutral and not in NATO. It is outside the Schengen area (the EU zone without passport checks), linked to its Common Travel Area with the UK.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.b] The EU faces a mix of social and economic challenges, and exams ask for both kinds.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.0] Social challenges: an ageing population; migration and asylum pressures; youth unemployment; rising populism (politics that claims to speak for ordinary people against elites) and euroscepticism; regional inequality.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.1] Economic challenges: public debt; competitiveness; energy security; the cost of enlargement; the economic effects of Brexit.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.2] Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine began on 24 February 2022. It caused an energy price shock and a large refugee movement into the EU.
[geo-region-european-s3.17.3] The challenges of political union and sovereignty include disagreement over how far to deepen integration, and the slowness of decisions that need unanimity.
[geo-region-european-s3.18.b] Enlargement means admitting new member states, and it is debated for and against. The benefits of further expansion are in the s4 notes.
[geo-region-european-s3.18.0] Candidate countries (officially recognised as being on the path to join, so they are the likely future members):Ukraine and Moldova (June 2022); Bosnia and Herzegovina (December 2022); Georgia (December 2023).
[geo-region-european-s3.18.1] Other candidates: Albania, Montenegro, North Macedonia and Serbia; Turkey (candidate since 1999, with talks frozen).
[geo-region-european-s3.18.2] Arguments against enlargement: the cost of accession (joining) and of EU funding, slower decision-making with more members, and doubts over whether candidates are ready.
