TARGET geo-economic-environment-s1: 1. Renewable and non-renewable resources in the economy
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.b] A resource is anything in the environment that people use to meet their needs. Resources are classed by whether nature can replace them, and this decides whether a country can keep using them for ever.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.0] Resources include energy, minerals, water, soil, forests, fish, land and scenery, so the word covers far more than fuels.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.1] Non-renewable (stock, finite) resources formed over millions of years, so they are used up far faster than they form and will eventually run out. Examples are coal, oil, natural gas, peat, metal ores and uranium.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.2] Renewable (flow) resources are replaced naturally, so they will not run out however much is used. Examples are wind, solar, hydro (running water), wave, tidal and geothermal heat (heat from inside the Earth).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.0.3] Some resources are renewable only if managed sustainably (used no faster than they are replaced): forests, fish stocks, soils, groundwater and biomass (plant material used as fuel). Overuse turns them into exhausted resources, as happens with fish stocks. There is more on this in Sustainable economic development.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.b] Three kinds of fuel are non-renewable. Fossil fuels and peat formed from once-living material, and uranium is a finite mineral.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas and peat) formed from the remains of plants or marine organisms buried and compressed over millions of years. Burning them releases the carbon they stored.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.1] Peat is non-renewable because a bog (wet ground where dead plants build up as peat) grows only about 1 mm a year, far slower than it is cut.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.1.2] Nuclear energy from uranium is an alternative to fossil fuels with low carbon emissions, but it is not renewable because uranium is finite.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.b] Energy is the resource that the rest of the economy runs on. This is why a country's energy supply matters so much to its wealth and security.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.0] Primary energy is energy in its raw form, such as oil, gas or wind. Electricity is a secondary form made from primary sources, for example by burning gas or by turning turbines with wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.1] Renewables give a far larger share of electricity than of total energy, because much of total energy is oil used for transport and heating, which renewables supply little of.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.2] Energy powers industry, transport, farming and homes, so resources are central to the economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.3] Energy security means a secure and affordable supply that is not disrupted by price shocks (sudden large rises in price) or supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.2.4] A country that imports its energy pays a large import bill (the money spent buying energy from abroad) and is exposed to price shocks and supply cuts.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.b] Ireland has few fossil fuels of its own, so it depends heavily on imported energy. The mix of fuels it uses shows where that dependence lies.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.0] Import dependence (the share of energy bought from abroad): Ireland imported close to 90% of its energy in the years before Corrib gas came ashore in 2015.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.1] The share fell after Corrib began and has risen again as Corrib's output declines. In the early 2020s Ireland still imported about three-quarters of its energy (SEAI, the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.2] Early 2020s primary energy mix (SEAI): oil just under half and the largest single source; natural gas about a third; renewables a little over a tenth; coal and peat small and falling.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.3.3] Adding oil, gas, coal and peat shows that fossil fuels still supply most of Ireland's primary energy, while renewables are growing.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.b] Oil is Ireland's largest single source of primary energy, and Ireland produces none of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.0] All of Ireland's oil is imported, which adds to the import bill and leaves the country exposed to price shocks.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.1] Oil is used mainly for transport and home heating.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.4.2] Whitegate, Co. Cork, is Ireland's only oil refinery (the plant that turns crude oil into fuels).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.b] Natural gas is Ireland's second-largest primary energy source and its largest source of electricity. Home gas fields supplied it for decades but are now running down.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.0] Kinsale Head gas field, off Co. Cork, was discovered in 1971 and began production in 1978 as Ireland's first commercial gas field. Production ended in 2020 when the field was depleted (used up), so it is no longer a working field.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.1] Corrib gas field, off north-west Co. Mayo, was discovered in 1996 and first flowed in December 2015. Its gas is processed at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.2] For a time after 2015 Corrib supplied over half of Ireland's gas. Its output is now declining, which is why import dependence has risen again.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.3] Most of Ireland's gas now comes from Britain through interconnector pipelines (cross-border links) from Moffat, Scotland.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.4] Gas is the largest single source of Irish electricity, because gas-fired power stations generate more of it than any other fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.b] Ireland's own coal fields are small and long closed. Moneypoint, the big coal-burning station, ran on imported coal and has now stopped burning coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.0] Ireland's native coalfields are small and long closed. They are Arigna, Co. Roscommon, where mining ended in 1990 and which is now the Arigna Mining Experience, and Castlecomer, Co. Kilkenny.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.1] Moneypoint, Co. Clare, on the Shannon Estuary, is an ESB (Electricity Supply Board) coal-fired station opened in 1985. At about 900 MW (megawatts, a measure of power output) it is Ireland's largest power station.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.2] Moneypoint burned imported coal, brought in by large ships to its deep-water jetty (a landing pier where water is deep enough for big ships).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.6.3] Moneypoint stopped burning coal in 2025.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.b] Peat, cut from the bogs of the Midlands, was Ireland's own fuel and powered stations for decades. It has now ended, and the cutaway bogs (bogs from which the peat has been removed) are being given new uses.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.0] Bord na Móna, the State peat company, was founded in 1946.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.1] Peat-fired stations at Lanesborough (Lough Ree) and Shannonbridge (West Offaly) closed in December 2020. Bord na Móna ended all peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.2] Edenderry, Co. Offaly, switched from peat to biomass, a renewable fuel of wood and energy crops.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.3] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo: the peat station closed in 2003 and was replaced by wind farms on the cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.4] Cutaway bogs are reused. Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly, has turned a cutaway bog into parkland, wetlands and walking and cycling trails.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.b] Hydroelectric power uses falling water to turn turbines (machines that spin to drive generators). The Shannon Scheme at Ardnacrusha was an early large scheme, and little potential is left for more.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.0] Ardnacrusha, on the River Shannon near Limerick, was built by the German firm Siemens and opened in 1929 as the Shannon Scheme. The ESB was set up in 1927 to run it, and for some years it supplied most of the country's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.1] Other stations: Poulaphouca on the River Liffey, Co. Wicklow; Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid on the River Lee, Co. Cork; and Cathaleen's Fall at Ballyshannon on the River Erne, Co. Donegal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.2] Turlough Hill, Co. Wicklow, opened in 1974 as an ESB pumped-storage station. Water is pumped uphill when demand is low and released through turbines at peak times, so it stores energy for when it is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.8.3] Little potential is left for large new hydro schemes, because the best sites were already used.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.b] Wind is Ireland's main renewable source. The country's position on the Atlantic edge of Europe gives it an advantage.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.0] Wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s. Renewables, mostly wind, supplied about two-fifths.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.1] Strong, frequent Atlantic winds make the western coasts and uplands the best sites for wind farms.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.2] Bellacorick wind farm, Co. Mayo (1992), was Ireland's first commercial wind farm. Oweninny wind farm was built on the same cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.3] Galway Wind Park, Connemara, completed in 2017, is one of Ireland's largest onshore (on land) wind farms.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.9.4] Arklow Bank Wind Park, off Co. Wicklow, opened in 2004 as Ireland's first offshore (at sea) wind farm, with seven turbines. Offshore wind is planned to grow strongly, with at least 5 GW targeted by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.b] Ireland's other renewable sources are smaller than wind. Some are growing quickly and some are still at an early stage.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.0] Solar power has grown rapidly since the early 2020s, through large solar farms on farmland and panels on rooftops. SEAI grants support home installations.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.1] The Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is Ireland's wave-energy test site, where wave machines are trialled.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.2] Geothermal energy is limited to low-temperature ground-source heat pumps (devices that take heat from the ground to warm buildings).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.3] Tidal energy harnesses the rise and fall of the sea, and it is at an early stage in Ireland.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.10.4] Biomass (wood and energy crops) and biofuels (fuels made from plant material) are the remaining renewable sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.b] Governments set targets to push renewables up and cut emissions. Ireland missed its 2020 target and has set tougher ones for 2030 and 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.0] Ireland's 2020 EU target was 16% of gross final energy consumption (all the energy used by final users such as homes, industry and transport) from renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.1] Ireland reached only about 13.5% and missed the target. It was one of the furthest from its target of any EU member state.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.2] Climate Action Plan: 80% of electricity from renewables by 2030, and at least 5 GW (gigawatts) of offshore wind by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.3] Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021: cut greenhouse gas emissions by 51% by 2030, compared with 2018, and reach climate neutrality (net zero, meaning emissions no higher than what is removed) by 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.b] Ireland's renewable share fell short of its 2020 target for six linked reasons. Several come from how Irish people live and travel, and some from delays in building.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.0] Heavy dependence on cars and road freight for transport, which runs on oil, so renewables supply little of it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.1] Oil used for home heating in a dispersed rural settlement pattern (homes spread out, so gas networks and shared heating are hard to provide).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.2] Slow planning and grid connection for wind farms, which delayed new capacity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.3] No large hydro potential left for new schemes, because the best sites were already used.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.4] A late start on offshore wind, which left Ireland with less renewable capacity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.5] Strong economic and population growth raising energy demand, which made the renewable share harder to raise.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.b] Interconnectors are cables or pipelines that link Ireland's energy network to other countries. They help security of supply because power and gas can flow in when Ireland needs them.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.0] The East-West Interconnector, opened in 2012, links Co. Dublin to north Wales by electricity cable.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.1] The Celtic Interconnector, from Co. Cork to Brittany, France, is under construction and due to link Ireland to the EU mainland grid in the late 2020s.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.13.2] Gas also arrives by interconnector pipelines from Scotland, which is how most of Ireland's gas now reaches it.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.b] Knowing which sectors use most energy shows where demand is highest and where savings or renewables matter most.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.0] Transport is the largest energy-using sector in Ireland (SEAI).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.1] Heat for homes and businesses comes next, then electricity generation for industry and services.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.14.2] Data centres (buildings full of computer servers) used about a fifth of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office). Most are in the Dublin region.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.b] Europe depends heavily on imported energy, and its main oil and gas comes from the North Sea and from suppliers outside the EU.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.0] The EU imports close to three-fifths of its energy (early 2020s, Eurostat, the EU's statistics office).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.1] The North Sea, shared by the UK and Norway, is Europe's main oil and gas province (an area rich in oil and gas).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.15.2] Norway is not an EU member but is one of the EU's main gas suppliers, by pipeline.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.b] Relying on one supplier for a vital fuel is risky. Europe learned this when Russia, its biggest gas supplier, invaded Ukraine.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.0] Before 2022 Russia supplied about two-fifths of the EU's imported gas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.1] Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022. Gas and electricity prices soared across Europe.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.2] REPowerEU plan, May 2022: cut dependence on Russian fossil fuels by saving energy, importing more liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled to a liquid so ships can carry it, much of it from the USA) and speeding up renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.16.3] Russia's share of EU gas imports has since fallen sharply.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.17.b] National energy mixes differ widely across Europe. France relies on nuclear power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.17.0] Nuclear power (electricity made from splitting uranium atoms) supplies about two-thirds of France's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.b] Germany is changing its energy mix by moving to renewables and ending nuclear power, though it still burns coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.0] The Energiewende (energy transition) is Germany's long-term switch to renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.1] After the Fukushima disaster in Japan in March 2011 (a nuclear accident), Germany decided to phase out nuclear power. Its last three reactors closed on 15 April 2023.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.2] Germany still burns lignite (brown coal) from huge open-cast mines (mines dug from the surface) in the Rhineland. A 2020 law sets 2038 at the latest as the end of coal-fired power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.19.b] Poland shows a country whose power still rests on coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.19.0] Coal generates well over half of Poland's electricity (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.b] Denmark shows a country that has built its electricity supply around wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.0] Wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity (2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.1] Denmark was an early leader in wind power and is home to the turbine maker Vestas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.b] Two European countries that are not EU members get nearly all their electricity from renewable sources, thanks to their landscapes.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.0] Hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of Norway's electricity, from its mountains, high rainfall and glacial lakes, which give plenty of falling water.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.21.1] Geothermal and hydro supply almost all of Iceland's electricity, and about 9 in 10 Icelandic homes are heated geothermally.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.b] The EU sets renewable targets for all its members. It met its 2020 target and has raised the goal for 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.0] The EU target of 20% renewable energy by 2020 was achieved, at about 22%.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.1] The revised Renewable Energy Directive (2023, an EU law binding on member states) requires at least 42.5% renewable energy by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.2] Renewables supplied about a quarter of EU gross final energy consumption in 2023, so the EU still has far to go to reach 42.5%.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.b] Despite the growth of renewables, fossil fuels still supply most of the EU's energy. Final energy (the energy used by consumers) is spread across several sectors.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.0] Fossil fuels (oil, gas and coal) still supply most of the EU's energy. Oil is the largest single source, mainly for transport.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.23.1] Transport, households and industry each take roughly a quarter to a third of final energy. Services take a smaller share.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.b] Energy production and consumption are not spread evenly across Europe. Which role a country plays depends on its industry and its natural resources.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.0] Core industrial countries, such as Germany and France, consume most of the energy, because their large industry and populations need so much.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.1] Resource-rich countries, such as Norway, export energy, because they produce more than they need.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.24.2] Peripheral countries, such as Ireland, depend on imports, because they have few fuels of their own.

CANDIDATES geo-rocks-weathering-s2: 2. Human interaction with the rock cycle
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.b] People take rock, minerals and energy out of the earth's crust, and in doing so they become part of the rock cycle. The syllabus asks you to study one of four options: mining, extraction of building materials (quarrying), oil and gas exploitation, or geothermal energy production.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.0] Extraction removes rock far faster than natural weathering (the breakdown of rock in place) and erosion (the wearing away and carrying off of rock) can, and it exposes fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.1] Extraction creates new deposits of waste, which are stored on the surface, for example spoil heaps (piles of waste rock) and tailings (fine waste left after ore is processed).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.0.2] People therefore act as an exogenic process (one that works on the surface of the crust from outside): removing rock is the human equivalent of erosion, and dumping waste is the human equivalent of deposition.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.b] Mining removes valuable minerals from the crust. The method depends on how deep the ore (rock containing a metal worth extracting) lies, and the ore must then be processed to separate the useful mineral from the rest.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.0] Mined materials include, for example, metal ores (zinc, lead, copper, gold), coal and salt.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.1] Ore bodies form where minerals are concentrated by igneous activity (the movement and cooling of magma) or by hot fluids moving through rock, which is why ore is found in particular places rather than everywhere.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.2] Exploration comes first, so money is not wasted on a poor site: geological surveys and test drilling show whether the ore is worth mining before a mine is developed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.3] Open-cast (open-pit) mining removes the overburden (the soil and rock lying above the ore) and works ore that lies near the surface, because digging down from above is cheapest there.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.4] Underground (shaft) mining is used because deep ore cannot be reached from the surface; it goes through shafts and tunnels; room and pillar is one underground method.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.5] Processing crushes and grinds the ore and separates the mineral from the waste rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.1.6] Waste rock is stored in spoil heaps, and fine waste, called tailings, is stored in tailings ponds.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.b] A named case study with specific facts carries a mining answer. Learn one Irish mine, one open-pit mine abroad, and Aberfan as an example of what can go wrong with mining waste.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath: an underground zinc and lead mine, Europe's largest zinc mine, in production since 1977 and owned by the Swedish company Boliden; the ore lies in Carboniferous limestone (limestone formed in the Carboniferous period).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.1] Bingham Canyon copper mine, Utah, USA: one of the largest open-pit mines in the world.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.2] Super Pit gold mine, Kalgoorlie, Western Australia: a large open-pit gold mine.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.3] Aberfan, south Wales, 21 October 1966: a colliery (coal mine) spoil tip built over springs on a hillside collapsed into the village after heavy rain, burying Pantglas Junior School; 144 people were killed, 116 of them children.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.2.4] Aberfan was a flow slide of saturated waste, a mass movement process (the downhill movement of loose material under gravity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.b] Mining brings real economic benefits, but it also changes the land and water around the mine. A good answer gives both sides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.0] Landscape scars and subsidence (the sinking of the ground surface) occur above and around underground workings.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.1] Spoil heaps and tailings take up land, so they remove it from farming or wildlife.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.2] Avoca, Co. Wicklow: centuries of copper mining, now closed, left acid mine drainage (acidic water draining from old workings) that discolours and pollutes the Avoca River with heavy metals (toxic metals such as copper).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.3] Dust, noise and heavy traffic affect people living near the mine, for example on local roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.3.4] Positive impacts are jobs, exports and local spending, so the mine supports the local economy.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.b] Governments and mining companies try to limit the damage, both while the mine runs and after it closes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.0] Environmental licensing and monitoring control the handling of waste and the quality of water leaving the site, so pollution can be caught and stopped.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.1] Tailings ponds are lined to stop seepage (waste liquid leaking into the ground) and capped (sealed over) when they are closed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.4.2] Lisheen, Co. Tipperary: a zinc and lead mine that closed in 2015; the site was rehabilitated (restored to a safe, usable condition).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.b] Quarries work rock and sand close to the surface for cement, aggregate (crushed rock used in concrete), building stone and roofing. This option is called quarrying on the Ordinary Level paper, but it is the same option.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.0] Limestone is quarried, for example, for cement, aggregate and lime for agriculture.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.1] Sand and gravel come from glacial and fluvio-glacial deposits (material dropped by ice and by glacial meltwater) and are used in concrete.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.2] Granite and other dimension stone (stone cut into blocks of a set size) are cut from the rock for building.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.5.3] Slate is quarried for roofing, such as Valentia slate, and marble for decoration.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.b] Quarrying follows a fixed order, working from the top of the site down into the rock and then preparing the rock for sale.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.0] 1. Remove the overburden: the soil and glacial till (the mixed clay and stones left by ice) above the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.1] 2. Cut benches (stepped ledges) into the rock face.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.2] 3. Drill and blast the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.3] 4. Crush and screen the rock, grading it by size.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.6.4] Dimension stone is cut to size rather than blasted, because blasting would shatter the blocks.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.b] Cement is one of the main uses of quarried limestone, and making it links directly back to the rock cycle through the carbon locked in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.0] Limestone is crushed and mixed with shale, then heated in a kiln (a very hot furnace) to make clinker; the clinker is ground with gypsum to make cement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.1] Heating limestone releases carbon dioxide, because calcium carbonate gives lime and carbon dioxide.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.2] The carbon dioxide returns to the atmosphere carbon that had been locked in the rock for hundreds of millions of years.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.7.3] Irish Cement's works at Platin, near Drogheda, Co. Meath, and at Castlemungret, Co. Limerick, are both based on Carboniferous limestone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.b] Each Irish material has a place you can name. Use these in your answer in place of general statements such as a quarry in Ireland.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.0] Sand and gravel: large pits in glacial deposits around Blessington, Co. Wicklow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.1] Granite: quarried at Ballyknockan, Co. Wicklow, from the Leinster granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.2] Marble: green Connemara marble is quarried in Connemara, Co. Galway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.8.3] Slate: quarried on Valentia Island, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.b] A quarry sits in the countryside, so its effects fall on neighbours, farmland and water as well as on the rock itself.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.0] Permanent scars, and loss of farmland and habitat, because the ground is removed rather than just covered.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.1] Dust, noise and blasting vibration, and heavy lorries on rural roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.2] Lowering of the local water table (the level below which the ground is saturated with water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.9.3] Positive impacts are jobs and the materials needed for housing and roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.b] Quarries are controlled through the planning system, and the land can be given a new use once the rock is worked out.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.0] Planning permission comes with conditions on working hours, and limits on dust and noise, so neighbours are protected.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.1] Earth banks and trees screen the quarry from view, so it is less of an eyesore.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.10.2] Worked-out quarries are rehabilitated as lakes, wildlife habitats, landfill or amenity areas (places for public recreation).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.b] Oil and gas are fossil fuels that form from living things buried in sediment, which makes them part of the sedimentary side of the rock cycle. They only become a usable resource where the right rock layers hold them in place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.0] The remains of tiny marine organisms are buried in sea-floor sediment.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.1] Heat and pressure over millions of years turn these remains into oil and gas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.2] Oil and gas migrate upward through porous (full of tiny holes), permeable (letting fluids pass through) reservoir rock, such as sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.11.3] They are trapped beneath impermeable cap rock (rock that fluids cannot pass through), such as shale, in a trap: an anticline (an upfold of rock layers), a fault (a crack along which rock has moved) or a salt dome (a rising mass of salt that pushes up the layers above).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.b] Finding and producing oil and gas takes several stages. Ireland has two gas fields to use as case studies, and Corrib shows how a project can cause conflict.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.0] Seismic surveys (which use sound waves to map rock layers) locate possible traps, and exploratory wells test them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.1] Production, which starts once a well finds oil or gas, uses production platforms or subsea wells, with pipelines to an onshore terminal.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.2] Kinsale Head gas field, off the Co. Cork coast: discovered 1971, in production from 1978; Ireland's first commercial gas field; production ended in 2020 when the field was depleted.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.3] Corrib gas field, off the coast of north-west Co. Mayo: discovered 1996, and gas first flowed in December 2015; gas is piped ashore to a processing terminal at Bellanaboy, Co. Mayo, originally operated by Shell.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.12.4] Opposition at Corrib: the Shell to Sea campaign objected to a high-pressure raw-gas pipeline near homes, and five local men, the Rossport Five, were jailed in 2005 for refusing to obey a court order not to obstruct the pipeline work.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.b] Offshore drilling can go badly wrong. The Deepwater Horizon spill is the international example to learn, alongside the North Sea as a major producing region.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.0] The Deepwater Horizon oil rig, leased by BP in the Gulf of Mexico, exploded on 20 April 2010, killing 11 workers.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.1] About 4.9 million barrels of oil spilled before the well was sealed.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.2] The spill damaged, for example, fishing, tourism and wildlife along the US Gulf coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.13.3] The North Sea, shared by the UK and Norway, is a major oil and gas province (a region with large oil and gas reserves).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.b] Fracking is a newer way of getting gas from rock that does not flow freely, and it is controversial. Like all oil and gas use, it brings benefits and costs that you must weigh.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.0] Hydraulic fracturing (fracking) of shale (a fine-grained rock that traps gas) releases gas; concerns include groundwater pollution and induced earthquakes (quakes caused by human activity).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.1] Ireland banned onshore fracking by law in 2017.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.2] Positive impacts are energy supply and security, revenue and jobs, since a country with its own gas depends less on imports.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.3] Negative impacts are oil spills, damage to marine life and fishing, and conflict with local communities.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.14.4] Burning fossil fuels returns stored carbon to the atmosphere, contributing to climate change.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.b] Geothermal energy is heat from inside the earth. It is available everywhere deep enough, but it is much easier to use where the heat is close to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.0] Heat comes from the earth's interior, from radioactive decay (the breakdown of unstable atoms, which gives off heat) and residual heat (heat left over from the earth's formation).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.1] Temperature rises with depth at about 25 to 30 °C per kilometre; this is the geothermal gradient.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.15.2] Heat is greatest near plate boundaries and hotspots (places where hot rock rises from deep in the earth), where magma (molten rock) lies close to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.b] Where magma is near the surface, the rock heats groundwater to very high temperatures. This high-temperature (high-enthalpy) system can drive power stations and heat whole towns.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.0] Groundwater is heated by hot rock near magma.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.1] Production wells bring steam and hot water to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.2] Steam drives turbines (wheels spun by the steam) to generate electricity, and hot water heats buildings through district heating (hot water piped to many buildings from one source).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.16.3] Cooled water is pumped back down a reinjection well, so the water supply is returned to the ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.b] Not every country has hot rock near the surface. Two other approaches use lower heat or create the conditions for heat to be collected.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.0] Ground-source heat pumps use the steady warmth of shallow ground to heat homes. This is the low-temperature type, suited to places far from plate boundaries.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.17.1] Enhanced geothermal systems pump water into hot dry rock (hot rock with no natural water in it) to fracture it, so that the water picks up heat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.b] Iceland is the main international case study because geothermal heat is central to its energy supply. Larderello and Wairakei add historical and international examples.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.0] Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (a plate boundary where plates move apart) above a hotspot, which gives it geothermal heat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.1] About 9 out of 10 homes in Iceland are heated by geothermal water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.2] Geothermal power provides between a quarter and a third of Iceland's electricity; most of the rest is hydro (hydroelectric power).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.3] Hellisheiði is Iceland's largest geothermal power station, and Reykjavík has a geothermal district heating system.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.4] The Blue Lagoon is a pool of warm, mineral-rich waste water from the Svartsengi power station, now a tourist attraction.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.5] Larderello, Tuscany, Italy: the world's first geothermal electricity (1904) and first commercial geothermal power station (1913).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.18.6] Wairakei, New Zealand: geothermal power station opened in 1958.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.b] Ireland is far from a plate boundary, where geothermal heat is greatest, so its geothermal potential is low-temperature only.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.0] Ireland is far from a plate boundary, so it has only low-temperature geothermal potential.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.1] Warm springs at Mallow, Co. Cork, show that heated groundwater exists.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.19.2] Ground-source heat pumps are the main use, because they need only shallow ground, for example for heating homes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.b] Geothermal energy is clean and dependable, but it can only be used in suitable places and brings some problems of its own.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.0] Advantages: renewable (it does not run out), low carbon emissions, reliable because it does not depend on the weather, and cheap to run once built.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.1] Disadvantages: it is limited to suitable locations, and drilling costs are high.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.2] Hydrogen sulphide gas is released, giving a rotten-egg smell.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.3] Minerals are deposited in pipes as the hot water cools.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s2.20.4] Basel, Switzerland: an enhanced geothermal project triggered small earthquakes in 2006 and was abandoned.

CANDIDATES geo-rivers-s4: 4. Human interaction: river processes
[geo-rivers-s4.0.b] Rivers are useful, so people change them. Every change alters how the river's processes work: its velocity (speed of flow), erosion (wearing away of bed and banks), transport (carrying of material), deposition (dropping of material) and flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.0] Reasons for interfering include making electricity, supplying water, irrigation (watering crops), navigation (letting boats travel), draining farmland and protecting towns from flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.1] The three measures studied here are hydro-electric dams, canalisation and flood control, each with Irish and international examples.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.b] A hydro-electric dam turns the energy of falling water into electricity. It does this by holding the river back and then releasing it in a controlled way.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.0] The dam stores water in a reservoir (an artificial lake behind the dam), and the water is then released so that it falls through turbines (wheels that spin and drive generators).
[geo-rivers-s4.1.1] The drop in height of the water (the head) gives the power, so a greater drop means more energy from the same water.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.b] Upstream of a dam the river meets still water in the reservoir. This changes its processes completely, because a river can only carry material while it keeps moving.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.0] Velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load (the sand, silt and stones it was carrying) there.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.1] The reservoir slowly fills with sediment, a process called sedimentation, which reduces its capacity (how much water it can hold).
[geo-rivers-s4.2.2] A delta (a fan of deposited sediment) can build where the river enters the reservoir, because that is where the flow slows and drops its load.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.b] Below the dam the river has lost its sediment and its natural rhythm of floods and low flows. Both changes alter erosion and deposition.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.0] Water released from the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam. This is called clear-water erosion.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.1] The floodplain (the flat land beside the river) and delta receive no new silt, so deltas and coasts erode because nothing replaces the material lost.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.2] Discharge (the volume of water flowing past a point) is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.3] Less flooding means less deposition on the floodplain, because floodwater is what normally lays down silt there.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.4] Where water is diverted out of the old channel, the bypassed channel carries much less water.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.5] Dams block migrating salmon and eels, which cannot pass the wall. Fish passes (routes around the dam) and trapping and transporting fish around dams are responses.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.b] The Shannon Scheme is one power station at Ardnacrusha, near Limerick, fed by a weir and a canal. It is not a series of dams.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.0] It was built 1925–1929 by the German firm Siemens for the Irish Free State, and it was Ireland's first large hydro-electric station.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.1] Parteen Weir (a low barrier across the river) diverts most of the Shannon's water into a headrace canal to Ardnacrusha. A tailrace (the channel carrying used water away) returns it to the river near Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.2] Lough Derg's level is controlled by Parteen Weir, so the lough acts as the scheme's reservoir.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.3] The original channel between Parteen and Limerick, the Old River Shannon, carries much less water because most of it is diverted.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.4] Salmon and eel stocks declined. The ESB built a fish pass, and it traps silver eels (adult eels migrating downstream) upstream and transports them past the dam.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.b] Ardnacrusha is not the only Irish example. Four more dams and power stations can be named in an answer.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.0] Poulaphouca dam, River Liffey: created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, Co. Wicklow. It generates electricity and supplies drinking water to Dublin.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.1] Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid dams, River Lee, Co. Cork.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.2] Cathaleen's Fall power station, River Erne, at Ballyshannon, Co. Donegal.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.b] The Aswan High Dam is the main international case study. It holds back the Nile in a huge reservoir to give Egypt control over a river that once flooded and shrank unpredictably.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.0] Aswan High Dam, River Nile, Egypt, was completed in 1970. Its reservoir is Lake Nasser, which extends into Sudan.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.1] Benefits are flood control, year-round irrigation (which allows more than one crop a year), hydro-electricity, and protection from drought.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.b] The dam's effects follow from the same processes as any dam: trapped sediment, clear-water erosion and regulated flow. They then spread into farming, fishing, health and settlement.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.0] Silt is trapped in Lake Nasser, so farmers downstream rely on artificial fertilisers instead of the natural silt that floods once left.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.1] The river bed below the dam is scoured (worn away) by clear water, which has spare energy to erode.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.2] The Nile delta coast is eroding, e.g. at the Rosetta promontory (a headland on the delta coast), because no new sediment arrives to replace what the sea removes.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.3] The sardine fishery off the delta in the Mediterranean declined sharply.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.4] Irrigated land suffers waterlogging (soil saturated with water) and salinisation (a build-up of salt in the soil).
[geo-rivers-s4.7.5] Perennial irrigation canals (canals that hold water all year) spread the disease schistosomiasis (bilharzia).
[geo-rivers-s4.7.6] Nubian people were displaced, and the temples of Abu Simbel were moved to higher ground.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.b] The Three Gorges Dam is a second international example, built on the Yangtze to produce power and to reduce floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.0] Three Gorges Dam, Hubei province, China, is the world's largest power station by installed capacity.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.1] It was completed in 2006 and fully operational in 2012, and over one million people were relocated.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.2] It aims to reduce flood peaks downstream, because the Yangtze floods of 1998 killed thousands.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.3] It traps sediment that once reached the river's lower course and delta, so those areas receive less of the sediment they depended on.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.b] Canalisation modifies an existing river channel. It is not the same as building a canal.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.0] Methods are straightening, deepening and widening the channel, sometimes lining it with concrete, cutting through meanders (bends in the river), dredging (scooping sediment from the bed) and building locks and weirs for navigation.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.1] Purposes are navigation, faster drainage of land, and flood control.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.b] Making a river straighter and smoother makes the water move faster. Almost every other effect follows from that rise in velocity.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.0] A straighter, shorter course has a steeper gradient (slope) and less friction (drag from the bed and banks), so velocity rises.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.1] Water and floods are passed downstream faster, so flood peaks downstream are higher and arrive sooner.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.2] Faster flow has more energy, so it erodes the bed and banks.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.3] Where the channel widens or the flow slows again, deposition increases and dredging is needed to keep the channel clear.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.4] Meanders, oxbow lakes (cut-off loops of old meander), pools and riffles (deeper and shallower stretches of the bed) are lost, and the river is cut off from its floodplain.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.b] Canalisation has been used for drainage and navigation in Ireland and for shortening and straightening big rivers abroad.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.0] Arterial drainage (deepening and straightening main rivers to drain land) was carried out by the Office of Public Works (OPW) under the Arterial Drainage Act 1945, e.g. the Moy and Boyne schemes. Rivers were deepened and straightened mainly to drain farmland, not to protect towns.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.1] Arterial drainage effects were changes to fish spawning beds, bank erosion and downstream flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.2] Mississippi: cut-offs (new short channels across bends) made by the US Army Corps of Engineers shortened the river.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.3] Rhine: the Upper Rhine was straightened in the 19th century under Johann Gottfried Tulla, which sped flood waves downstream and caused the bed to erode downward.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.4] Navigation in Ireland: locks and weirs on the Shannon Navigation control water levels and slow the flow.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.b] Flood control measures respond to the causes of river flooding, so it helps to know what those causes are.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.0] Prolonged or intense rain, and saturated ground (soil that is already full of water and cannot absorb more), send water into rivers faster than they can carry it.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.1] Urban surfaces and drains speed runoff (water flowing over land into rivers). Deforestation and drainage of bogs and farmland upstream add to it.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.2] Building on floodplains puts homes in the path of floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.b] Hard engineering uses built structures to hold floodwater back or move it away. Each one changes river processes, and some pass the problem on downstream.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.0] Embankments and levees (raised banks) confine flow to the channel, so deposition raises the bed and the banks must be raised again.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.1] With embankments and levees the floodplain gets no silt, and failure gives sudden, severe floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.2] Flood walls and demountable (removable) barriers protect towns.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.3] Dams and reservoirs store floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.4] Dredging and deepening increase channel capacity, but the flow is faster and downstream flooding can increase.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.5] Relief (bypass) channels take floodwater around the town.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.b] Soft engineering works with the river instead of against it. It slows water or gives it space, so natural processes carry on.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.0] Floodplain zoning uses planning controls on building, so that homes are kept off land that floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.1] Afforestation (planting trees) upstream works through interception (leaves catching rain), which slows runoff.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.2] Wetlands and bogs can be restored, and washlands (land that is allowed to flood) give the river more room.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.3] Flood forecasting and warning give people time to prepare.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.4] Effect on processes: natural flooding and floodplain deposition continue.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.b] Irish floods show hard engineering, dam management and flood-risk planning in practice.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.0] Cork, November 2009: the River Lee flooded when the ESB released large volumes from Inniscarra dam during very heavy rain because the reservoir was full.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.1] The releases were not the sole cause, because the rain was exceptional. The University College Cork and the city water treatment works were flooded, and many homes lost their water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.2] Clonmel, Co. Tipperary: an OPW flood relief scheme on the River Suir uses demountable barriers that are put in place when floods are forecast.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.3] December 2015: Storm Desmond caused prolonged flooding on the Shannon, in towns such as Athlone.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.4] CFRAM (Catchment Flood Risk Assessment and Management) is the OPW programme that maps flood risk.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.b] The Mississippi is the international case study for levees. It shows both what they protect and how they can fail.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.0] The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 led to the US federal levee and floodway system run by the US Army Corps of Engineers.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.1] The 1993 flood breached many levees along the upper Mississippi and Missouri.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.2] In August 2005 Hurricane Katrina caused levee and floodwall failures in New Orleans, and about 80% of the city flooded.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.3] Levees stop flood silt reaching the delta, which contributes to the loss of Louisiana's coastal wetlands.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.b] The Netherlands shows a soft-engineering response to flooding: instead of building higher walls, it gives the river space.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.0] Room for the River (Ruimte voor de Rivier), Netherlands, started after near-disastrous high water on the Rhine and Maas in 1993 and 1995.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.1] Measures include moving dykes (embankments) back, lowering floodplains and digging side channels so the river can spread out safely.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.b] Exams expect a judgement, so weigh what these measures achieve against what they cost.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.0] Benefits are protection of homes, electricity, navigation and water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.1] Costs are problems moved downstream, sediment starvation (rivers and deltas losing the sediment they need), habitat loss, high cost, and a false sense of security.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.2] There is a trend towards working with natural processes.

CANDIDATES geo-graph-skills-s1: Figure interpretation
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.b] Every figure question starts the same way: read the labels and units before reading any value. Most lost marks come from a wrong unit, year or column, because a correct reading of the wrong one still scores nothing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.0] Check the title, both axis labels (the words along the horizontal and vertical lines), the key or legend (the box explaining colours or symbols), the units (%, thousands, millions, €m, hectares, mm, °C, per 1,000), the years shown and the source line. Each one changes what a number means.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.1] Read a bar or line value by running a ruler from the top of the bar or point across to the vertical axis, estimating between gridlines, and quoting the value with its unit.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.2] In a table, find the row and the column and read the cell where they meet. Check the totals row before using a figure, so you know whether a number is a part or the whole.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.3] Compare two years by reading neighbouring columns. To spot the one that did not change, read across the whole row, because the unchanged value is the one that stays the same between years.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.4] For highest and lowest, scan every value, not just the first and last, because the extreme can sit in the middle. For a ranking, list the values from largest to smallest.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.5] To count how many items are above an average line, count the bars or points whose tops lie above it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.6] For 'twice as much', double the smaller value and look for the value that matches it.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.0.7] An infographic (a single page combining headline numbers, icons, small charts and county maps) has several panels. Read each panel's own label and unit: headline figures are often totals, while panels may show percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.b] A description states what the figure shows. Using exact trend words and quoting numbers turns a vague answer into a complete one.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.0] Trend vocabulary: increase, rise or growth; decrease, fall or decline; steady, constant or no change; fluctuate (go up and down); peak (highest point); trough (lowest point); gradual or rapid (sharp); levelling off (the change slows and flattens).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.1] The overall trend is the long-term direction. A short-term change, such as a single year's dip, is described separately so it does not hide the main pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.2] To describe a trend, state the overall direction, the start and end values with their years and units, and the period of fastest change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.3] Name any peak, trough or reversal (a change of direction), and quantify the change as the difference, the percentage change, or both.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.1.4] Point out anomalies: values that break the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.b] Once a trend is described, the next marks come from comparing it with another, saying why it happened and judging whether the figure can be trusted.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.0] To compare two series (two lines or two sets of bars), say which is higher, when the lines cross, whether the gap widens or narrows, and the difference in a given year. For example, net migration = immigration minus emigration.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.1] To explain a trend, link the pattern to one cause from the relevant chapter, in one clause. Explaining means saying why the figure looks as it does, not just what it shows.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.2] To evaluate a figure, check whether an axis that does not start at zero or has uneven intervals exaggerates change, because a small rise then looks large.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.3] Also check whether unevenly spaced dates distort the trend, whether the sample (the group measured) is small, and whether the source is reliable and recent.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.2.4] Percentages can hide small totals: a large percentage rise may be a small change in numbers, because a small starting number is easy to double.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.b] These worked values come from the invented (illustrative) population of six towns, in thousands: Town A 18, Town B 32, Town C 25, Town D 41, Town E 12, Town F 29. Each statistic has a short method that you can repeat on any data set.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.0] Total (sum): add every value. 18 + 32 + 25 + 41 + 12 + 29 = 157 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.1] Difference: subtract the smaller value from the larger and say which is larger. Town B (32) exceeds Town C (25) by 7 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.2] Mean (average) = total of the values ÷ number of values: 157 ÷ 6 = 26.2 thousand, to one decimal place (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.3] Median (the middle value): put the values in order (12, 18, 25, 29, 32, 41); with an even number of values, average the middle two: (25 + 29) ÷ 2 = 27 thousand (illustrative).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.4] Mode: the most frequent value. The six towns have no mode because no value repeats.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.3.5] Range = highest value minus lowest value: 41 − 12 = 29 thousand (illustrative), which shows how spread out the values are.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.b] Percentages let you compare parts of different-sized wholes, but the method matters: dividing by the wrong number, or mixing up a percentage with a percentage point, gives a wrong answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.0] Percentage of a total = part ÷ total × 100. Illustrative: Town D's share of the six towns is 41 ÷ 157 × 100 = 26.1%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.1] Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100. A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.2] Always divide by the old (earlier) value, because the change is measured against where you started. Illustrative Country P, broadband households: 20% in 2000 and 90% in 2025, so the percentage change is (90 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 350%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.3] Percentage points: when both values are already percentages, the change is a difference in percentage points. A rise from 20% to 30% is a 10 percentage-point increase, but a 50% increase in the percentage change, (30 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.4] When a question asks by what percentage a percentage changed, the subtraction is the usual reading: give the answer in percentage points and add the percentage change if unsure. Use the percentage-change formula when the values are numbers rather than percentages.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.5] Missing value X: parts of a whole add to 100% (pie chart or percentage table) or to a stated total (stacked bar, a bar split into parts, or a total row), so X = total minus the sum of the known parts.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.6] Illustrative Country Q: if all segments except 'other' total 85%, then other = 100 − 85 = 15%, because the parts of a pie chart must reach 100%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.4.7] To find a number from a percentage: number = percentage ÷ 100 × total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.b] Raw numbers are hard to compare between places of different size, so geographers use rates, densities and ratios. How you present the answer matters too.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.0] Rate per 1,000 = number ÷ population × 1,000, which lets places of different size be compared. A birth rate is births ÷ population × 1,000.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.1] Density = number ÷ area, given as people per km².
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.2] Ratio: compare two values as a ratio, e.g. 2 : 1. Illustrative: Town D (41) is more than three times the size of Town E (12), a ratio of about 3.4 : 1.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.3] 'Twice as much' means two times the other value; 'half' means divided by two.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.4] Round to the precision the data uses, or to one decimal place where needed, and keep the unit and the multiplier (thousands, millions) in every answer.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.5.5] In a calculation question, show the working: a correct answer with working is safe, and the working can earn marks if the answer slips.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.b] When you are asked to draw a graph from a small table of data, marks are given for each of these rules, so each one is worth doing even when the shape of the graph is easy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.0] Use graph paper, a sharp pencil and a ruler; draw the graph large enough to fill most of the space.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.1] The title says what the data is, where and when, taken from the wording of the question.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.2] The horizontal (x) axis shows categories or time; the vertical (y) axis shows values. Label both axes with units.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.3] The scale starts at zero, is evenly divided, and its interval is chosen so the largest value fits (e.g. steps of 5 or 10 units).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.4] On a line graph, time intervals are spaced in proportion to the years. If the data is for 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2015, the last gap is half the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.5] Plot every value accurately. Bars have equal widths and equal gaps; points are joined with ruled straight lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.6.6] Use a key or legend (a box saying what each colour or shading means) whenever more than one colour or shading is used, and shade or colour each category distinctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.b] Illustrative data: population of six towns, in thousands, from the totals block above. Following the rules in order shows how each mark is earned.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.0] Graph choice: a bar chart, because the values are one measure for separate towns.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.1] Title: 'Population of six towns (thousands), illustrative'.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.2] x-axis: 'Town', with Towns A to F in order.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.3] y-axis: 'Population (thousands)', from 0 to 45 in steps of 5, so the largest value (41) fits.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.4] Six bars of equal width with equal gaps, each drawn to its value: A 18, B 32, C 25, D 41, E 12, F 29.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.7.5] A single data series (one set of values) needs no key; check each bar top against the scale before finishing.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.b] The question 'draw a suitable graph' is answered by naming the graph type, then giving one sentence of justification. The type depends on what the data is. Each example below is illustrative.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.0] Values for separate categories, e.g. the populations of six towns: bar chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.1] Change over time, e.g. the percentage of an urban population from 1980 to 2020: line graph (a bar chart is usually also accepted).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.2] Parts of a whole, or percentage shares adding to 100%, e.g. car sales by fuel type in one year: pie chart or divided bar.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.3] Two related variables for the same places, e.g. income and birth rate for twenty countries: scatter graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.4] Three components that add to 100%, e.g. sand, silt and clay at a soil site: triangular graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.5] Age and sex structure of a population: population pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.6] Monthly temperature and rainfall for one place: climate graph.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.8.7] Values for areas on a map, e.g. population density by region: choropleth map (a map with areas shaded by value).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.b] Bar charts show amounts for separate categories. They come in several forms, depending on whether you are comparing categories, groups within a category, or parts of a total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.0] Simple bar chart: separate categories, one bar per category, equal widths and equal gaps; vertical or horizontal.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.1] Multiple (clustered or comparative) bar chart: two or more bars side by side per category, e.g. males and females, or two years; a key is needed to tell the bars apart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.2] Compound (stacked or divided) bar chart: each bar is split into components, and the top of the whole bar is the total.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.3] To read a component in a compound bar, take the top of its segment minus the bottom of its segment.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.4] Percentage compound bar: every bar reaches 100%, so the segments show shares rather than totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.9.5] Illustrative Region R, tree planting: total 2,000 hectares, of which conifers 1,350 ha. Broadleaf = 2,000 − 1,350 = 650 ha, a share of 650 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 32.5%.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.b] A histogram looks like a bar chart but is used for continuous data, while a line graph is the standard way to show one variable changing over time.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.0] Histogram: like a bar chart but for continuous data grouped in classes (e.g. age groups or rainfall amounts), drawn with no gaps between bars because one class runs straight into the next.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.1] Line graph (trend graph): one variable over time; plot the points at the correct years and join them with ruled lines.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.2] Multiple line graph: two or more series on the same axes, with a key so each line can be identified.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.3] The gradient (steepness) of a line shows the rate of change: a steeper section means faster change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.4] Illustrative Country P, households with broadband: 2000 20%, 2005 38%, 2010 61%, 2015 74%, 2020 86%, 2025 90%. The rise is continuous; the fastest change is 2005 to 2010 (+23 percentage points), and the rise slows to +4 points from 2020 to 2025 as the line levels off.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.10.5] Illustrative Country P: the change from 2000 to 2025 is +70 percentage points, which is not the same as the 350% percentage change above, because one is a subtraction and the other is measured against the old value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.b] A pie chart shows how a whole is split into parts. Because a full circle is 360° and the parts add to 100%, each 1% takes up 3.6° of the circle.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.0] Pie chart: parts of a whole. Degrees = percentage × 3.6, because 360° ÷ 100 = 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.1] For raw numbers rather than percentages: degrees = value ÷ total × 360. Do not multiply raw numbers by 3.6.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.2] Drawing steps: draw a circle with a compass, draw a radius (a line from the centre to the edge) to 12 o'clock, then measure each angle clockwise with a protractor, largest segment first.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.3] Shade, key or label each segment with its percentage, and check that the angles add to 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.4] Illustrative Country Q, electricity sources (gas 40%, wind 25%, solar 10%, coal 5%, hydro 5%, other 15%): angles 144°, 90°, 36°, 18°, 18° and 54°, totalling 360°.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.5] Two pie charts compare the shares in two years, not the totals, unless the circles are drawn in proportion to the totals.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.11.6] Divided (percentage) bar: a single bar split into shares that add to 100%, an alternative to a pie chart.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.b] A pictogram uses icons to stand for quantities. A population pyramid is a special graph showing how many people of each age and sex a country has.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.0] Pictogram: each icon stands for a fixed quantity stated in the key, so check the key first; a part-icon is a fraction of that quantity.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.1] Population pyramid: a back-to-back horizontal bar chart, which is a histogram on its side.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.2] Age groups, usually in 5-year bands, sit on the central vertical axis: 0 to 4 at the bottom and the oldest group at the top.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.3] Males are drawn on the left and females on the right. The horizontal axis shows the percentage (or number) of the population, not age, scaled outward from zero on both sides.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.4] Reading a pyramid: a wide base means a high birth rate; a narrow top means low life expectancy; a narrowing base means a falling birth rate; bulges and indentations suggest baby booms, migration, war or famine.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.5] Describe the dependent groups (the young and the old, who rely on others) and the working-age group when reading a pyramid.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.6] Drawing one band (illustrative, Country Z): males aged 20 to 24 are 3.5% and females 3.4%, so at the 20 to 24 level draw a bar 3.5 units long left of the centre axis and one 3.4 units long to the right.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.12.7] The shapes of pyramids and the stages of demographic transition (how a country's birth and death rates change over time) are taught fully elsewhere. There is more on this in Changing population characteristics.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.b] A climate graph combines two kinds of data for one place on a single set of axes: rainfall as bars and temperature as a line, across 12 months.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.0] Months J to D run along the bottom axis. Rainfall is shown as bars against a left-hand axis in mm.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.1] Mean (average) monthly temperature is shown as a line, plotted at the middle of each month, against a right-hand axis in °C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.2] From the graph, read the hottest and coldest months, the wettest and driest months, and the seasonal pattern (rain all year, or a wet and dry season).
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.3] Calculations from the graph: annual temperature range = maximum mean monthly temperature minus minimum; mean annual temperature = sum of the 12 monthly means ÷ 12; total annual rainfall = sum of the 12 bars.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.4] Illustrative Station M, mean monthly temperature (°C) J to D: 5, 5, 7, 9, 12, 14, 16, 16, 14, 11, 8, 6. Rainfall (mm) J to D: 110, 80, 85, 60, 65, 70, 70, 85, 90, 115, 110, 120.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.5] Illustrative Station M results: warmest in July and August (16°C); coldest in January and February (5°C); annual range 16 − 5 = 11°C; mean annual temperature 123 ÷ 12 = 10.3°C.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.6] Illustrative Station M: total rainfall 1,060 mm; wettest month December (120 mm); driest month April (60 mm). Rain falls in every month with a winter maximum, and the mild winters and cool summers resemble a cool temperate oceanic pattern.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.13.7] Identifying a climate type from a climate graph is taught elsewhere. There is more on this in Physical regions and Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.b] A scatter graph tests whether two measurements for the same set of places are related, for example whether land value changes with distance from a city centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.0] Scatter graph: two variables for the same set of places. The independent variable (the one that may cause the change) goes on the x-axis; each place is plotted as a point, and the points are not joined.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.1] Draw a line of best fit by eye through the middle of the points, so it shows the general direction.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.2] Positive correlation: both variables rise together. Negative correlation: one rises as the other falls. No correlation: no clear pattern. Strength is described as strong or weak.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.3] Outliers, or anomalies, are points that lie away from the pattern of the others.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.4] Correlation does not prove cause: two things can move together without one causing the other.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.5] Illustrative distance from a city centre and land value at eight sites: 1 km €900 per m²; 2 km €700; 3 km €620; 4 km €450; 5 km €400; 6 km €250; 7 km €260; 8 km €150.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.14.6] Illustrative: the result is a strong negative correlation, with the 7 km site a slight anomaly because it is higher than the 6 km site. A one-clause explanation: accessibility falls with distance from the centre.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.b] A triangular graph is used when a total is split into exactly three parts. Each point is placed using all three values at once.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.0] Triangular graph: shows three components that always total 100%, e.g. the percentage employed in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.1] Each side is a 0 to 100% scale. Read each component along the gridlines parallel to the side where its 0 is, following the direction in which its numbers run.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.2] Check that the three readings add to 100, which confirms you have read the scales correctly.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.3] To plot a point: find the first value on its scale, follow its gridline in, and mark where it meets the second value's gridline; check the point with the third value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.4] A point clustered near a corner is dominated by the component that corner represents.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.15.5] Illustrative employment structure: Country X is primary 10%, secondary 25%, tertiary 65%; it sits near the tertiary corner, typical of a developed economy. Country Y is primary 60%, secondary 15%, tertiary 25%; it sits near the primary corner, typical of a less developed economy.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.b] Statistical maps put values onto a map so you can see how they vary from place to place. The choropleth is the main one; four other types each show a different kind of data.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.0] Choropleth map: areas (counties, regions or countries) shaded by value class; darker shading means a higher value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.1] Classes must not overlap and must cover every value, e.g. 0 to 49, 50 to 99, 100 to 149, 150 and over. A key is essential.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.2] Choropleth maps suit densities and percentages. Their weakness is that they imply the value is even across each area and hide variation inside it, and the boundaries look like sudden changes.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.3] Illustrative regional population densities (people per km²): North 35, East 160, South 85, West 40, Midlands 60. North and West are the lightest shade and East the darkest; the empty 100 to 149 class still appears in the key.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.4] Dot map: each dot stands for a fixed number, showing the distribution of a value.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.5] Isoline map: lines join places of equal value, e.g. isobars, isotherms, isohyets or contours.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.6] Flow-line map: arrow width is proportional to the amount moving, e.g. migration, traffic or trade.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.16.7] Proportional symbols: circles or bars sized in proportion to the value, placed on the map.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.b] Sometimes a table is a better way to present data than a graph, and tables, graphs and maps all feed into the Geographical Investigation.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.0] Use a table rather than a graph when many exact values must be given. A table cannot show a trend at a glance.
[geo-graph-skills-s1.17.1] Graphs, maps and tables are expected in the presentation of results in the Geographical Investigation report. There is more on this in The Geographical Investigation (20%).
