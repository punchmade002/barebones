TARGET geo-economic-globalisation-s4: 4. How the global economy is organised
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.b] A basic processing unit is a factory carrying out one stage of production: processing a raw material, making a component (a part that goes into a finished product) or assembling a product. Multinational companies (MNCs, firms operating in many countries) place each stage in whichever country suits it best, which is why production is so widely spread.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.0] The Intel and Apple chains, such as those in 'One multinational company (MNC)', are examples of stages spread across several countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.1] Cheap labour suits labour-intensive assembly (work that needs many workers rather than machines), so firms put assembly where wages are low.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.2] Units are placed close to raw materials or to markets, which cuts the distance inputs or finished goods must travel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.3] A factory inside a trade bloc (a group of countries with shared trade rules) sells to the bloc's members without the barriers that apply to imports from outside.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.4] Tax breaks (reduced tax bills) and grants (money from government) lower the cost of operating in a country, so they attract factories.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.5] Falling transport and communication costs make distant factories practical, because a plant far away can still be supplied and managed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.0.6] Spreading production across many countries means that one disaster or strike does not stop all production, so the firm spreads its risk.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.b] Nike shows how far a firm can spread production: it designs and sells shoes but owns almost none of the factories that make them. Do not say Nike owns its Asian factories.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.0] Founded as Blue Ribbon Sports in 1964 by Phil Knight and Bill Bowerman; renamed Nike in 1971.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.1] Headquarters, design and marketing are in Beaverton, Oregon, USA, so the high-skill, high-value work stays at home.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.2] Footwear is made by independent contract factories (outside firms paid to make the product), mainly in Vietnam (the largest), China and Indonesia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.3] Nike sells its products worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.1.4] Sweatshop criticism in the 1990s (attacks on factories with very low pay and poor conditions), including of factories in Indonesia, led to factory monitoring and published supplier lists (lists of the factories Nike uses).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.b] Governments attract factories by creating special areas with easy trade rules. In an export processing zone, firms import components and export finished goods free of customs duty (a tax on goods crossing a border), and they receive tax breaks.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.0] Shannon Free Zone, Co. Clare, set up in 1959, was one of the world's first export processing zones and Ireland's pioneer. The duty-free shop at Shannon Airport was 1947, so do not swap the dates.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.1] Shenzhen, China, became a Special Economic Zone (a region with special business rules to attract foreign firms) in 1980.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.2] Maquiladoras are assembly plants along Mexico's border with the USA, such as those at Tijuana and Ciudad Juárez, set up from 1965.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.2.3] A maquiladora imports components duty-free and exports finished goods to the USA, so it is an example of an export processing zone at work.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.b] Spreading production across many countries brings risks as well as savings. The costs fall on supply chains, workers and the environment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.0] Long supply chains (the linked stages from raw material to finished product) are vulnerable to disruption, as the 2021 chip shortage, the Ever Given blockage and the Red Sea diversions showed. There is more on this in 'Patterns in world trade'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.1] Branch plants (factories owned by a firm whose headquarters is elsewhere) close when costs change, and their workers lose jobs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.2] Some plants pay low wages and have poor conditions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.3] Moving components and goods over long distances has an environmental cost.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.3.4] Responses include reshoring (moving production back to the home country) and friend-shoring (moving it to politically allied countries).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.b] At world scale, regions play different roles in the global economy. The core is the wealthy, powerful centre; the periphery is the poorer edge that supplies it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.0] Core regions have capital (money to invest), MNC headquarters, research and development, skilled labour, good infrastructure and political power.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.1] The core's examples are the USA, western Europe's Blue Banana (Europe's core belt of dense development) and Japan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.2] The periphery supplies raw materials, food and cheap labour, with weak infrastructure and out-migration of people (people leaving to find work elsewhere).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.4.3] Most of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America and parts of South Asia are periphery.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.b] Some countries sit between the core and the periphery. They are changing their role in the global economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.0] The semi-periphery is made up of newly industrialised countries (NICs, countries that have recently built large manufacturing industries) moving towards the core.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.5.1] South Korea did so earlier; China, India, Brazil and Mexico are doing so now.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.b] The core did not become rich by accident. An early start, overseas empires and a self-reinforcing cycle of growth built its lead.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.0] Britain industrialised first, from the late 18th century (the Industrial Revolution), followed by western Europe and the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.1] Colonial empires supplied the core with raw materials and markets, and profits were reinvested in the core, which strengthened it further.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.2] Cumulative causation, a model by Gunnar Myrdal (1957), says success attracts more investment, people and services, which increases success.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.3] Backwash effects drain capital and skilled people from the periphery to the core, so the core grows at the periphery's expense.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.6.4] The Blue Banana runs from south-east England and London through the Benelux countries and the Rhine valley to northern Italy. There is more on this in 'Socio-economic regions'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.b] The periphery's economy was shaped by outside rulers for their benefit, which left it badly placed after independence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.0] Colonial economies were built around exporting one or two raw materials, so they had little other industry.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.1] Railways ran from mines and plantations to ports, rather than linking regions to each other, because their purpose was export.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.2] Borders were drawn by the colonisers, not by the people who lived there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.7.3] After independence these economies depended on falling commodity prices (the prices of raw materials), were burdened by debt and suffered conflict.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.b] Core and periphery are not fixed. Some places have moved up the ladder, and the North-South model is a simple picture of the pattern.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.0] Spread effects (the benefits of growth passing outwards from the core) and investment can raise peripheral regions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.1] The Asian Tigers and China moved from periphery to semi-periphery or core in about two generations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.2] Ireland moved from the European periphery to a core-like economy through MNC investment. There is more on this in 'Irish Regions - Core and Peripheral'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.8.3] The Brandt Line, from the Brandt Report of 1980, is a simple North-South model (a line dividing richer northern from poorer southern countries) of the same pattern.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.b] Sub-Saharan Africa is the main region excluded from world manufacturing. It has a very small share of world manufacturing and exports mostly unprocessed raw materials.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.0] Nigeria: crude oil is most of its exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.1] Zambia: copper is most of its exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.2] The Democratic Republic of the Congo produces most of the world's cobalt and exports it raw despite its huge reserves, amid conflict.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.3] Sudan: civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces since April 2023 has caused one of the world's largest displacement crises, with people forced to flee their homes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.4] South Sudan became independent from Sudan in 2011, taking most of the oil fields.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.9.5] The 2023 war is within Sudan, not a war with South Sudan, because they have been separate countries since 2011.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.b] Many linked problems keep sub-Saharan Africa out of manufacturing. Each one raises costs or risks for firms, or limits what the countries can do for themselves.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.0] Poor infrastructure: roads and ports are inadequate and electricity is unreliable, which makes running a factory costly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.1] Landlocked countries (with no coast): Africa has 16, such as Chad, Niger, Mali and South Sudan, and they face high transport costs to reach ports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.2] Political instability, conflict and corruption scare off investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.3] Low levels of education and skills, and poor health, limit the workforce.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.4] Small, poor home markets give firms little reason to produce locally.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.5] Heavy debt absorbs money that could fund development.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.6] The colonial legacy: economies were set up to export raw materials, and that pattern persists.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.10.7] Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs (taxes on imports) on processed goods than on raw materials, so processing at home does not pay; coffee beans enter rich markets with low tariffs, while roasted coffee faces higher ones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.b] Several measures try to help excluded regions join world trade and industry. They target debt, trade barriers and infrastructure.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.0] The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative, begun in 1996, gives debt relief (cancelling or reducing debts) through the World Bank and IMF.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.1] The African Continental Free Trade Area, which removes trade barriers between African countries, began trading on 1 January 2021.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.2] Chinese and other foreign investment is building infrastructure.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.11.3] Fair trade (a system that pays producers a fairer price). There is more on this in 'Patterns in world trade'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.b] Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, MNCs, technology and the movement of people and ideas. It has changed what is traded, who trades and how goods move.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.0] About 80% of world trade is linked to the international production networks of MNCs (UNCTAD, 2013).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.1] Much of it is intra-firm trade, between parts of the same company or with its suppliers in global value chains (production split into stages across countries).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.2] Trade is increasingly in components rather than finished goods, because each stage of production crosses borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.3] Trade in services and digital trade has grown rapidly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.4] New trading powers, led by China, have entered world trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.5] The WTO (World Trade Organization) and trade blocs have lowered barriers, so goods cross borders more easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.6] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard steel boxes) and huge ports have cut the cost of shipping, so distant trade pays.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.12.7] E-commerce (online selling) brings small sellers into world markets, so they can reach buyers abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.b] Globalisation does not benefit everyone equally. Some groups and regions gain, others lose.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.0] Consumers gain cheaper goods, because production moves to low-cost countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.1] NICs and China gain jobs and growth, because factories locate there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.2] Manufacturing regions in the core lose jobs, because production moves abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.3] Excluded regions fall further behind, because they get little of the new investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.4] Workers in low-wage assembly can face poor and unsafe conditions, as the Rana Plaza collapse showed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.13.5] Transporting goods over long distances has environmental costs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.b] Since 2018 some governments have pushed back against open trade, raising barriers between major economies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.0] The US-China trade war from 2018, the US tariffs of 2025 and Brexit have raised barriers between major economies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.1] Governments are reshoring strategic industries such as chips (the microchips in electronics), bringing production back home so they do not depend on distant suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.14.2] Supply-chain worries grew after COVID-19.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.b] India's economy was tightly controlled before 1991, and it opened to globalisation only after a crisis.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.0] Five-year plans from 1951 directed investment and production, so the state, not the market, decided what was made.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.1] Protection (tariffs and rules that shut out imports) shielded Indian industries from foreign competition.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.2] The Licence Raj: firms needed government licences to produce and expand, which limited competition and growth.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.3] A balance-of-payments crisis (when a country cannot pay for its imports and debts) in 1991 forced change.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.15.4] Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh removed most licences, cut tariffs and opened India to foreign investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.b] Globalisation brought rapid growth, new industries and foreign investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.0] Bengaluru became known as the 'Silicon Valley of India'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.1] IT and business-process outsourcing (hiring firms abroad to run tasks such as customer service) grew rapidly; Tata Consultancy Services (founded 1968), Infosys (founded 1981) and Wipro grew, with Bengaluru as a major hub.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.2] Texas Instruments opened a design centre in Bengaluru in 1985.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.3] Maruti Suzuki, a joint venture (a company shared between partners) with Suzuki of Japan, made its first car in 1983.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.4] India is a leading maker of generic medicines (cheaper copies of branded drugs), earning it the name 'pharmacy of the world'.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.5] Make in India, launched in 2014, aimed to attract manufacturing; iPhones are assembled near Chennai and Bengaluru.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.6] Services produce over half of India's GDP (the value of everything it produces), and India is among the world's five largest economies (early 2020s). A growing middle class has emerged.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.16.7] Hundreds of millions of people have been moved out of extreme poverty, as growth created jobs and income.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.b] The gains from globalisation have been uneven, and it has brought costs for some people and places.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.0] Inequality exists between cities and rural areas, and between states.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.1] Small farmers are exposed to world prices, so they can lose when prices fall.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.2] Farmers' protests in 2020-21 were against three farm laws, which were repealed in November 2021.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.3] Most workers are still in the informal sector (jobs without contracts, regular pay or legal protection).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.17.4] Pollution and accidents at MNC plants: the Bhopal gas leak at the Union Carbide pesticide plant in December 1984 killed thousands; Union Carbide was a US MNC.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.b] China is a second developing economy, which opened to the world after 1978. It gained enormous growth but also costs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.0] Reforms under Deng Xiaoping from 1978 opened China to foreign trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.1] Special Economic Zones from 1980 attracted foreign investment, such as Shenzhen.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.2] China joined the WTO on 11 December 2001, which gave it easier access to world markets.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.3] China has been the largest goods exporter since 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.4] About 800 million people have been lifted out of extreme poverty since 1978 (World Bank).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.5] Inequality exists between the coast and the interior, because growth centred on coastal zones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.18.6] Pollution from rapid industrial growth and dependence on exports are costs of this model, because a fall in world demand would hit China's economy.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.b] Bangladesh shows both sides of globalisation: jobs from the garment industry, and the dangers of low-cost production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.0] Ready-made garments are most of Bangladesh's exports; it is the world's second-largest garment exporter after China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.1] The garment industry provides jobs, especially for women.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.2] Rana Plaza, Savar, near Dhaka, collapsed on 24 April 2013, killing over 1,100 garment workers. It was a building collapse, not a fire.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s4.19.3] The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh, signed in 2013 by clothing brands and unions, introduced factory inspections.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-globalisation-s3: 3. Patterns in world trade
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.b] Trade is the buying and selling of goods and services between countries. Exports leave a country and imports arrive in it, and data questions use these words constantly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.0] Visible trade is goods that can be seen and counted, such as cars, food and computer chips.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.1] Invisible trade is services, which cannot be held or counted in the same way, such as software, financial services, tourism and aircraft leasing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.2] Balance of trade: the value of exports minus the value of imports, which gives one number that shows whether a country sells more than it buys.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.0.3] A trade surplus means exports are greater than imports; a trade deficit means imports are greater than exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.b] The balance of trade is only part of the picture of a country's dealings with the world. The balance of payments is the wider record.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.0] Balance of payments: the record of all money flows between a country and the rest of the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.1] It includes trade in goods and services, plus investment flows (money put into businesses abroad), profits sent home, borrowing and transfers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.1.2] It is a wider measure than the balance of trade, which records only exports and imports, so the two are not the same thing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.b] A country that keeps buying more from abroad than it sells pays a price at home, while exports bring benefits. Each effect follows from where the money goes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.0] A persistent trade deficit (imports greater than exports year after year) means money flows out of the country to pay for the extra imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.1] The country may have to borrow or sell assets (things it owns, such as businesses or property) to pay for those imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.2] Home industries and jobs can be lost as cheaper imports replace domestic production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.3] The currency (the country's money) may weaken when a country persistently imports more than it exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.2.4] Exports bring jobs, tax revenue, foreign earnings and economic growth, because foreign buyers pay money into the country.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.b] A country that sells to many different countries is safer than one that sells mainly to one. This is the advantage of many export partners.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.0] A country with many export partners is less dependent on any one market.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.3.1] A recession (a fall in economic activity), tariff or crisis in one country, such as the US tariffs imposed in 2025, then does less damage to its overall trade, because the other markets keep buying.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.b] Governments can open their markets to imports or restrict them. Protectionism is the restricting approach, and international bodies try to set common rules.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.0] Protectionism restricts imports to protect home industries from foreign competition, so home producers face less pressure from cheaper foreign goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.1] Barriers to trade include tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on the quantity imported), subsidies (payments that help home producers compete with imports), and regulations.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.2] Trade blocs (groups of countries that agree trade rules among themselves) remove barriers between their own members, so members trade with each other more easily than with outsiders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.3] GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade) was signed in 1947 to reduce tariffs between countries, so goods cost less to trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.4] The World Trade Organisation (WTO) was set up on 1 January 1995, with headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland; it has over 160 member countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.5] The WTO sets the rules of world trade and settles disputes between members, so countries have a common referee.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.4.6] Fair trade gives producers in developing countries a guaranteed minimum price and a premium (an extra payment), shown by the Fairtrade mark.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.b] World trade has grown much faster than world output since 1950, and most countries now depend on imports and exports. Several changes made this possible.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.0] Falling trade barriers, through GATT and the WTO, made it cheaper to trade across borders.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.1] Containerisation (shipping goods in standard-sized steel boxes) began with the first container ship voyage, the Ideal X of Malcolm McLean, USA, in 1956; containers made loading and shipping faster and cheaper.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.2] Shanghai, China, has been the world's busiest container port since 2010.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.3] Air freight and information and communication technology (ICT) speed up trade in high-value goods and services, for example by moving them quickly and sharing information instantly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.4] Multinational companies (MNCs, firms that operate in many countries) trade components between their own plants in different countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.5.5] The entry of China and Eastern Europe into world trade added large new producers and markets, so there were more goods to sell and more buyers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.b] The three triad areas and the rest of the world are linked by shipping routes and by products whose parts come from many countries. Because they are so linked, a disruption in one place spreads worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.0] One product links many countries: the iPhone and Intel chip supply chains (the series of countries and firms that make a product's parts and assemble it) each pass through several countries. There is more on this in One multinational company (MNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.1] The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, and the Panama Canal, opened in 1914, are chokepoints (narrow passages that much shipping must use) on the shipping routes linking the triad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.2] In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given blocked the Suez Canal for about six days, holding up hundreds of ships.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.3] From late 2023 attacks on shipping in the Red Sea forced many ships to sail around the Cape of Good Hope, Africa.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.4] The 2008-09 global financial crisis caused world trade to fall sharply in 2009.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.6.5] COVID-19 (2020-21) disrupted supply chains, for example in the 2021 chip shortage.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.b] Most of the world's trade and production is concentrated in three areas. The trading triad is North America, Europe and the Pacific Rim, the three core economic areas of the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.0] The triad produces most of the world's manufactured goods, such as cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.1] It holds the headquarters of most multinational companies (MNCs), so company decisions are made there.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.7.2] Most world trade takes place among the three areas, and most foreign direct investment (money that firms invest in businesses and factories in other countries) flows between them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.b] The USA is the triad's North American member and the dominant economy within it. Its trade is shaped by its size, its trade bloc and its use of tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.0] The USA is the world's largest economy and its largest importer, so its demand matters to exporters everywhere.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.1] It has had a trade deficit in goods every year since the mid-1970s, which means it imports more goods than it exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.2] NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement: USA, Canada, Mexico) took effect on 1 January 1994.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.3] USMCA (United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement) replaced NAFTA on 1 July 2020, so the three countries kept a trade bloc.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.4] Mexico overtook China in 2023 as the largest source of US imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.5] From 2018 the USA put tariffs (taxes on imports) on many Chinese goods in the US-China trade war.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.8.6] In 2025 the USA raised tariffs on imports from most countries, which raised the price of goods entering the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.b] Europe is one of the three triad areas, and it trades as a bloc. The EU is a union of countries that trade freely with one another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.0] The EU is a single market (members trade as if in one market) of 27 member states, since the UK left the EU.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.1] The single market began on 1 January 1993, removing barriers to trade between members, so goods move freely between them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.2] The EU is the largest trading bloc by value, with strong trade with the USA and China, for example in goods sold between the blocs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.3] The euro, the shared currency, was introduced in 1999; euro notes and coins followed in 2002.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.9.4] In July 2025 the EU and USA agreed a 15% US tariff on most EU goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.b] In the triad, the Pacific Rim means mainly East and South-East Asia, together with Australia. India is not part of it.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.0] Members include, for example, Japan, China, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, the ASEAN countries and Australia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.1] ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) was founded in 1967 as a group of South-East Asian countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.2] APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) was founded in 1989.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.3] RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership) has 15 Asia-Pacific members, including China, Japan, South Korea, Australia and the ASEAN members.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.4] RCEP was signed in November 2020 and came into force from 2022; its members hold about 30% of the world's population and GDP (the total value of goods and services a country produces in a year).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.10.5] The Asian Tigers are South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, fast-growing Asian economies. China and Japan are not Tigers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.b] Japan is a Pacific Rim economy that grew after the Second World War by exporting manufactured goods. Its story shows how a country with few raw materials can grow by selling manufactured goods.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.0] After the Second World War Japan's growth was based on exporting manufactured goods, mainly cars and electronics.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.1] Japan has few raw materials, so it must import most of its oil, coal and iron ore.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.2] Japan was the world's second-largest economy from the late 1960s until China overtook it in 2010; Germany overtook Japan in 2023.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.3] Toyota, based in Toyota City, uses just-in-time production, which delivers parts as they are needed rather than storing them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.4] Other major Japanese companies are, for example, Honda, Sony and Nissan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.5] To avoid trade barriers, Japanese firms opened factories inside the EU and USA; Nissan's car plant at Sunderland, north-east England, opened in 1986, inside the European Economic Community (EEC, the EU's earlier name).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.11.6] Japan's growth has been slower since the 1990s, so it is no longer expanding as quickly as it did after the war.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.b] China moved from a closed economy to the world's largest exporter of goods, which is why it is called the workshop of the world. Government reforms opened it to trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.0] Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms, from 1978, opened China's closed economy to foreign trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.1] The first Special Economic Zones (SEZs, areas with special rules to attract foreign firms) were set up in 1980, including Shenzhen beside Hong Kong.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.2] Shenzhen is in mainland China and is not part of Hong Kong; it grew from a small town to over 17 million people (2020 census).

[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.4] China has been the world's largest exporter of goods since 2009, and it overtook Japan as the second-largest economy in 2010.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.5] China runs a huge trade surplus, because it sells so much abroad; meaning its exports greatly exceed its imports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.6] The Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, links China to Asia, Africa and Europe through ports, railways and roads.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.12.7] The trade war with the USA, begun in 2018, also affects China; the US side is in the North America notes.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.b] South Korea is the standard example of a newly industrialised country (NIC, a country that has recently built a large manufacturing economy). It went from poverty to high income by selling manufactured goods abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.0] South Korea was war-damaged by the Korean War (1950-53) and was one of the world's poorest countries in the early 1960s.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.1] Under President Park Chung-hee (1961-79) it industrialised through exports and rose from poverty to high income.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.2] Its economy is dominated by chaebol, family-controlled business groups such as Samsung (founded 1938), Hyundai, LG and SK.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.3] Ulsan is home to Hyundai's shipyards, among the world's largest, and to a Hyundai car plant, as an example of an industrial city built on exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.4] South Korea is a world leader in shipbuilding, cars, electronics and memory chips, and Samsung makes smartphones.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.13.5] South Korea is one of the Asian Tigers and joined the OECD (the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) in 1996.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.14.b] Not every region shares in the triad's trade. Some areas are left on the edges of world trade.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.14.0] Africa, much of Latin America and South Asia hold only a small share of world trade, mostly in raw materials. There is more on this in How the global economy is organised.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.b] An international division of labour means that countries and regions specialise in different stages of production and different types of work. The first pattern grew out of colonial rule.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.0] In the old, colonial pattern, colonies and developing countries exported raw materials and food, while Europe and later the USA manufactured goods and sold them back.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.1] Sudan's Gezira Scheme, from 1925, grew irrigated cotton for the British (Lancashire) textile mills.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.2] India exported raw cotton and tea to Britain under colonial rule, as an example of raw materials and food leaving a colony.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.15.3] Ghana (then the Gold Coast) exported cocoa; Cote d'Ivoire and Ghana together grow over half of the world's cocoa today, still mostly exported unprocessed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.b] From the 1960s-70s the pattern changed. Multinational companies began to split production, placing each stage where it suited them best.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.0] Multinational companies moved assembly and routine manufacturing to newly industrialised countries (NICs) and low-wage countries.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.1] The core (the rich, powerful economies) kept headquarters, research and development (R&D, work on new products and technology), design, finance and marketing.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.2] The periphery (the poorer countries on the edge of the world economy) still supplies raw materials.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.3] As an example, Apple's packaging carried the line 'Designed by Apple in California. Assembled in China.'
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.16.4] Intel's R&D is at Hillsboro, Oregon; its fabs (chip factories) include Leixlip in Ireland; assembly and test take place in Malaysia and Vietnam. There is more on this in One multinational company (MNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.b] The pattern is not fixed. Services are now divided between countries, and countries move up or spread to new places.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.0] Services are now divided too: Bengaluru (Bangalore), India, is an IT services hub for firms such as Infosys, Wipro and Tata Consultancy Services, and IT and call centres are located in India.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.1] China is moving into high-value products instead of only low-cost assembly.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.17.2] Low-cost assembly is spreading to Vietnam, Bangladesh and India, because firms look for cheaper places to assemble.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.b] Ireland is a very open economy: its exports are larger than its GDP. It has moved from supplying food to the UK to hosting high-value manufacturing and services by multinational companies (MNCs).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.0] Goods exports passed 200 billion euro in 2024 (CSO, the Central Statistics Office), a record.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.1] Most of this export value is produced by MNC plants, not by Irish-owned firms, so large exports reflect MNC production.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.2] Main goods exports: pharmaceuticals and chemicals (the largest category), medical devices (a cluster at Galway, with firms such as Medtronic and Boston Scientific), computer chips and electronics, and food and drink (beef, dairy and whiskey).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.3] Main services exports: computer services (Ireland is one of the world's largest exporters of them), financial services, aircraft leasing and tourism.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.4] Irish-based lessors (companies that own aircraft and lease them to airlines) manage over half of the world's leased commercial aircraft, from Dublin and Shannon.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.18.5] Ireland has a large goods trade surplus, because it exports far more goods than it imports, the largest of it with the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.b] Where Irish goods go reflects who makes them. The main market is the USA.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.0] The USA is the largest single market for Irish goods, taking about a third of them, which makes it Ireland's biggest buyer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.1] Other main markets, such as EU partners Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands, then the UK and China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.2] US pharmaceutical and technology MNCs export from their Irish plants back to the USA and worldwide, which is why the USA dominates.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.19.3] In early 2025 Irish exports to the USA rose sharply as US buyers stockpiled medicines (bought extra in advance) before new tariffs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.b] Ireland's trade with the UK changed after the UK left the EU. Brexit is the usual reason to give.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.0] The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition period (when the old trade rules still applied) ended on 31 December 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.1] Brexit brought customs checks (inspections of goods at the border) on trade with the UK and took the UK out of the EU single market.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.2] A weaker pound makes Irish goods dearer for UK buyers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.20.3] Irish firms have diversified into other markets, so they depend less on the UK.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.b] Ireland's export success depends on a narrow base, which brings risk.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.0] Ireland depends on a few US MNCs and on the US market, so a problem for those firms or that market hits Irish exports.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.21.1] US tariffs from 2025 expose Irish exports to the USA to new barriers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.b] Data-response questions give charts and tables of trade and ask you to calculate. The method is the same every time.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.0] Read values from the chart or table and state their units before calculating.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.1] Balance of trade for one country: subtract imports from exports; a negative result is a trade deficit.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.2] Difference between two countries: subtract the smaller value from the larger, so the answer is positive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.3] Total exports: add the values for every partner, so none is missed.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s3.22.4] Use a bar chart to compare values between countries and a line graph to show change over time, because each suits that kind of data.

CANDIDATES geo-economic-globalisation-s1: 1. One multinational company (MNC)
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.b] A multinational company (MNC) is a company based in one home country that runs production or service branches in several other countries. The syllabus asks you to study one MNC to understand how it is structured and organised, so the general pattern comes first.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.0] Definition: a company with its headquarters (the main control centre) in its home country and production or service branches in several host countries (the countries that receive the branches). It is also called a transnational corporation (TNC).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.1] Typical structure: headquarters, research and development (R&D, the work of inventing and designing new products) and top management stay in the home country as the core. Branch plants, regional headquarters, and sales and service offices are placed abroad.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.2] Decisions are made at headquarters, and branch plants compete with each other inside the company for investment, so a plant that is less efficient or more costly risks losing out to another.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.3] Production is split into stages: design and R&D; basic processing of raw materials and components; assembly and testing; distribution and sales. Each stage is placed where it is cheapest or most efficient, which spreads sourcing, basic processing and markets across the world.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.4] The largest MNCs have sales or market values (the total worth of the company's shares) greater than the gross domestic product (GDP, the value of all goods and services a country produces in a year) of many countries, and they control much of world trade and investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.0.5] The largest companies by market value in the mid-2020s are American technology companies: Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Alphabet (Google's parent) and Amazon.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.b] Foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment by a company in another country, made by building or buying a plant there. Ireland has a State agency whose job is to win this investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.0] IDA Ireland (Industrial Development Authority), set up in 1949, is the State agency responsible for attracting and keeping FDI, so it is the body that supports foreign companies both before and after they arrive.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.1] IDA client companies employ about 300,000 people in about 1,800 companies (2023-24), concentrated in the Dublin region, which has the largest labour pool, universities, Dublin Port and Airport, services and existing clusters of firms.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.1.2] Other Irish-based MNCs: Pfizer (Ringaskiddy, Co. Cork, and Grange Castle, Dublin), Google (European headquarters, Barrow Street, Dublin), Meta (international headquarters, Dublin), Microsoft (in Ireland since 1985) and Dell (Limerick).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.b] Ireland has no shortage of competition for foreign investment, so a company needs reasons to build here. These are the MNC-specific advantages of Ireland, and each one can be developed into an explained point.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.0] Low corporation tax (the tax a company pays on its profits) of 12.5%, rising to 15% for very large multinational groups (turnover over 750 million euro) from 2024 under the OECD global minimum tax agreement.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.1] A young, educated, English-speaking workforce, which suits high-skill and customer-facing work.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.2] EU membership, which Ireland joined in 1973 as the EEC (now the EU), gives access to the single market (one trading area with no tariffs between members).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.3] Since the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020, Ireland is the only euro-area country where English is the main language (Malta also has English as an official language).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.4] IDA grants and support for companies that invest here, which lower the cost of setting up.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.5] Good international links, including ports, airports and broadband, so goods, staff and data can move easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.6] Clusters of similar firms and suppliers already in the country, so a new plant can find skilled workers and local suppliers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.7] Universities and research links with industry, which supply graduates and shared research.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.2.8] A stable democracy with a pro-business government, which gives a company confidence its investment is safe.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.b] Intel is the main case study. It is a clear example of an MNC whose headquarters and design work stay in the home country while manufacturing is spread across several continents. Intel was founded in 1968, which is earlier than Apple (1976).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.0] Intel Corporation was founded in 1968 in California by Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore. Its headquarters are in Santa Clara, California, in Silicon Valley (the area of California where many technology companies are based).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.1] Intel made the first commercial microprocessor (a computer's processing chip), the Intel 4004, in 1971.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.2] Intel is best known for the processors inside most personal computers: the x86 family, sold as Pentium and Core, and Xeon for servers (powerful computers that serve other computers).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.3] Wafer fabrication plants ('fabs', factories that make chips on silicon wafers) are at Hillsboro, Oregon (Intel's main research and development fabs); Chandler, Arizona; Rio Rancho, New Mexico; Leixlip, Ireland; and Kiryat Gat, Israel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.4] Assembly and test plants are at Penang and Kulim in Malaysia, Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam, and Chengdu in China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.3.5] Sales offices are located around the world, close to the customers they serve.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.b] Ireland's part of Intel is a manufacturing branch plant at Leixlip, Co. Kildare. It is the country's largest private-sector investment.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.0] Intel came to Leixlip, Co. Kildare, in 1989.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.1] Its campus is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, with over 30 billion euro invested by 2023. It has several fabs and employs several thousand people.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.2] Fab 34 opened in 2023 and was the first fab in Europe to make chips in high volume using extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography (printing the tiny circuit patterns onto the wafer with a very short wavelength of light).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.3] Fab 34 makes the Intel 4 and Intel 3 generations, such as Core Ultra processors.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.4.4] In 2024 Intel sold a 49% share of the Fab 34 joint venture (a business owned jointly by two companies) to Apollo Global Management, an American investment firm, for about 11 billion US dollars, to raise money for its other projects.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.b] A fab has very particular needs: easy transport, a big site, skilled people and huge supplies of water and electricity. Leixlip met them, which is the answer to the Ordinary question on the advantages of Ireland for Intel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.0] Leixlip is on the M4 motorway west of Dublin, giving road access to the capital, so staff and goods can travel easily.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.1] A large, flat greenfield site, which is undeveloped land, so there was room to build very large plants.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.2] Maynooth University is nearby in Co. Kildare, and Trinity College Dublin and University College Dublin are in the city, giving access to graduates and research links.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.3] A large labour pool in the Dublin area, so Intel can recruit many workers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.5.4] A water and electricity supply; fabs use huge amounts of both, so a plant cannot work without a dependable supply.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.b] For the syllabus you must study one product of the MNC. Intel's product is the microprocessor, and knowing how it is made explains why Intel needs the inputs and locations described next.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.0] A microprocessor, or chip, is the 'brain' of a computer, because it carries out the computer's instructions.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.1] It is made by building billions of tiny transistors (tiny electronic switches) in layers on a thin disc of pure silicon called a wafer.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.6.2] Wafers are processed in a dust-free clean room, because dust would ruin the tiny circuits.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.b] Sourcing means where a company gets the materials and parts it needs. A fab depends on a few specialist inputs, and some come from a single supplier.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.0] Silicon, refined from quartz sand, is bought as silicon wafers from specialist wafer makers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.1] Chemicals, specialty gases and metals, which are used up in processing the wafers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.2] Huge volumes of ultra-pure water and electricity, because the fab uses them continuously.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.7.3] Manufacturing machines, especially lithography machines (which print the circuit patterns onto wafers) from ASML, based at Veldhoven in the Netherlands. ASML is the only maker of EUV lithography machines, which cost hundreds of millions of euro each.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.b] Intel splits chip making into stages and puts each where it suits best. Following the chain from wafer to computer shows how the stages are spread across several continents.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.0] 1. Stage 1: wafers are processed in fabs at Leixlip, Hillsboro, Chandler and Kiryat Gat.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.1] 2. Stage 2: finished wafers are flown to assembly and test plants in Penang and Kulim (Malaysia), Ho Chi Minh City (Vietnam) and Chengdu (China), where they are cut into individual chips, packaged and tested.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.2] 3. Stage 3: the chips go to computer makers' assembly plants, mostly in Asia.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.3] Chips are small, light and very valuable, so they are moved by air.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.8.4] The Leixlip plant makes processed wafers only. Cutting, packaging and testing of the chips are not done in Ireland.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.b] A market is where a product is sold, and a market can be a country, a region or a type of customer. Intel's main customers are computer makers, server makers, governments and businesses.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.0] Computer manufacturers are the main customers: Lenovo (China), HP and Dell (USA).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.1] Makers of servers for data centres and cloud computing (storing and running data and programs on remote computers).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.2] Governments and businesses worldwide, because they also buy computers and servers.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.3] Markets are in North America, Europe and Asia, with China one of the largest.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.9.4] Two markets outside Ireland can be named as countries, for example the USA and China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.b] Intel faces pressures from rivals, costs and supply chains. The exam asks for one difficulty, so learn several to choose from and give each in one explained sentence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.0] Competition from AMD (USA); TSMC (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company), the world's largest contract chip maker; Samsung (South Korea); and Nvidia (USA), the leader in chips for artificial intelligence.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.1] Loss of Apple as a customer: Apple began replacing Intel chips in its Mac computers with its own M1 chip in November 2020.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.2] Very high cost of building new fabs.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.3] Falling profits: about 15,000 global job cuts were announced in August 2024, with further cuts in 2025 under the new chief executive, Lip-Bu Tan.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.4] In 2025 the US government took a stake of about 10% in Intel.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.5] Risk that production moves abroad if Ireland's tax advantage narrows (the 15% minimum rate for very large groups) or if costs rise.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.6] High energy costs and pressure on Ireland's electricity grid, since fabs need large amounts of power.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.10.7] Dependence on Asian supply chains: the global chip shortage of 2021, caused by COVID-19 disruption, halted car and electronics production worldwide.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.b] Intel's presence helps Ireland in several ways. These benefits are described without numbers because no checked figures exist for them.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.0] Direct jobs on the Leixlip campus, and indirect (spin-off) jobs with local suppliers and services, which grow because Intel buys goods and services locally.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.1] Corporation tax paid to the State, and exports from the plant.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.11.2] Skills development and research links with universities.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.b] Apple is the alternative case study to Intel. Its product is the iPhone, and it is a good example of a company that designs in one country and has others make its products. Apple was founded in 1976, later than Intel (1968).
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.0] Apple was founded in 1976 by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak. Its headquarters are at the Apple Park campus in Cupertino, California, which opened in 2017.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.1] Products: the iPhone (2007), iPad (2010), Mac computers and Apple Watch (2015), plus services such as the App Store, iCloud and Apple Music.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.12.2] Design, software and management are based in California, which makes California the core of the company.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.13.b] Apple's Irish base is in Cork. It is mainly a European headquarters and operations centre, not a phone factory.

[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.14.1] The chief contract manufacturer is Foxconn (Hon Hai Precision Industry), a Taiwanese company.

[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.16.1] A growing share is assembled in India, around Chennai and Bengaluru, by Foxconn and the Indian Tata group. This reduces dependence on China.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.17.b] Apple sells its iPhones in almost every region, through its own outlets and through phone companies.
[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.17.0] iPhones are sold worldwide. The Americas, Europe and China are the largest regions.

[geo-economic-globalisation-s1.19.b] Use map evidence to justify a location, and give one reason against a location. The map question asks for two reasons an MNC might locate in a mapped area, with map evidence, and one reason against.
