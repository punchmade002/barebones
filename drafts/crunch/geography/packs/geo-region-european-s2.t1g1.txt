TARGET geo-region-european-s2 (part 1 of 2): 2. A Peripheral European Region: the Mezzogiorno
[geo-region-european-s2.0.b] The Mezzogiorno is the Mediterranean, peripheral region you can study as a contrast to the core Paris Basin. It is southern Italy and its islands, which together form the peripheral south of the country.
[geo-region-european-s2.0.0] It is made up of eight regions: Abruzzo, Molise, Campania, Puglia (Apulia), Basilicata, Calabria, Sicily and Sardinia. This is why the region is described as the South and its islands, not one single area.
[geo-region-european-s2.0.1] The name Mezzogiorno means midday, a reference to the strong midday sun of the south.
[geo-region-european-s2.0.2] It covers about 40% of Italy's area but holds only about one-third of its population: about 20 million people (2020s), out of an Italian population of about 59 million (2024).
[geo-region-european-s2.0.3] Rome and the region of Lazio lie north of the Mezzogiorno and are not part of it, so never include them when you describe the region.
[geo-region-european-s2.0.4] Sicily and Sardinia are autonomous regions with a special statute (their own legal status), which gives their regional governments extra powers. This reflects their separate island identity.
[geo-region-european-s2.1.b] Peripheral means far from the economic core, where the main markets, industry and wealth are. Distance, relief (the shape and height of the land), history and politics combine to keep the South behind the North.
[geo-region-european-s2.1.0] Distance: the South is far from the EU's core markets and from the industrial North of Italy, which raises the cost of moving goods.
[geo-region-european-s2.1.1] Relief and climate: the land is mostly mountainous or hilly and summers are dry, which limits farming, transport and settlement.
[geo-region-european-s2.1.2] History: the South, formerly the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, became part of a united Italy in 1861, and the North–South gap widened after unification. Land stayed in large estates, which kept farming backward.
[geo-region-european-s2.1.3] Organised crime and emigration drain investment and people from the South, so the region loses both money and workers.
[geo-region-european-s2.1.4] The North–South divide is known as the questione meridionale (the southern question). The Mezzogiorno produces only about 22% of Italy's GDP (the value of all goods and services made), and its GDP per head is a little over half that of the Centre-North (recent years).
[geo-region-european-s2.1.5] Unemployment in the South is roughly two to three times the North's rate (recent years).
[geo-region-european-s2.1.6] Contrast: the Paris Basin, studied in region s1, is a core European region; the Mezzogiorno is the peripheral counterpart.
[geo-region-european-s2.2.b] You may be asked to draw an outline map of a European region and mark named features. These are the Mezzogiorno features you can draw and name.
[geo-region-european-s2.2.0] Rivers: the Volturno in Campania, the longest river of the southern mainland; the Ofanto in Puglia; the Basento in Basilicata.
[geo-region-european-s2.2.1] Urban centres: Naples, Bari, Taranto, Brindisi, Palermo, Catania and Reggio Calabria.
[geo-region-european-s2.2.2] Relief: the Apennines (the mountain chain down the peninsula), the Sila in Calabria, Mount Etna and Vesuvius.
[geo-region-european-s2.2.3] Lowlands: the Tavoliere di Puglia around Foggia, the Campanian plain and the plain of Catania.
[geo-region-european-s2.2.4] Agricultural and industrial areas: the Tavoliere (durum wheat) and the Bari–Brindisi–Taranto industrial triangle, which is the South's own industrial area.
[geo-region-european-s2.3.b] The climate has hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, with rain concentrated in the cooler months. This pattern drives the region's water problems and its farming.
[geo-region-european-s2.3.0] Summers are hot and dry because the subtropical high-pressure belt (a zone of sinking, dry air) moves north over the region.
[geo-region-european-s2.3.1] Lowland summer (July) averages around 25°C or more. This heat is why summers need irrigation.
[geo-region-european-s2.3.2] Winters are mild and wet, with rain brought by Atlantic depressions (low-pressure weather systems); most rain falls between October and March.
[geo-region-european-s2.3.3] Coastal winter (January) averages about 9–12°C.
[geo-region-european-s2.3.4] Annual rainfall is under 600 mm on parts of the Puglia lowlands and Sicily, but much higher on the Apennines. This is why irrigation is needed on the lowlands, while the high Apennines are wetter.
[geo-region-european-s2.3.5] The sirocco is a hot, dry, dusty wind from the Sahara that adds to the dryness of summer.
[geo-region-european-s2.3.6] Summer drought causes water shortage, while intense autumn and winter downpours cause flash floods (sudden floods after heavy rain).
[geo-region-european-s2.3.7] Farmers respond with irrigation (supplying water to crops) and drought-tolerant crops.
[geo-region-european-s2.4.b] Mountains and hills cover most of the region, and the lowlands are small and mostly coastal. This shapes where people farm and live.
[geo-region-european-s2.4.0] The Apennines run the length of the peninsula, forming its spine.
[geo-region-european-s2.4.1] The highest point of the Apennines is Corno Grande (Gran Sasso, Abruzzo), at 2,912 m. This is why the interior is so hard to farm or cross.
[geo-region-european-s2.4.2] The Calabrian uplands are dominated by high ground, including the Sila plateau. Because the high ground leaves little flat land, farming and settlement are limited.
[geo-region-european-s2.4.3] The Tavoliere di Puglia, around Foggia, is the largest plain of peninsular southern Italy. Because it is flat, it suits large-scale farming, such as durum wheat.
[geo-region-european-s2.4.4] The Campanian plain around Naples and the plain of Catania, Sicily's largest plain, are the other main lowlands.
[geo-region-european-s2.4.5] Mount Etna in Sicily, about 3,300 m, is Europe's highest active volcano. This is why the island has both a volcanic hazard and fertile volcanic soil.
[geo-region-european-s2.4.6] Steep slopes limit farming, transport and settlement to small areas and push villages onto hilltops.
[geo-region-european-s2.5.b] The region is geologically restless, so earthquakes and volcanoes are part of life in the South. Here are the local examples; they are also a source of fertile soil.
[geo-region-european-s2.5.0] The region lies where the African and Eurasian plates meet, which is why it has earthquakes and volcanoes.
[geo-region-european-s2.5.1] Messina and Reggio Calabria were destroyed by the earthquake and tsunami of 28 December 1908, with tens of thousands killed.
[geo-region-european-s2.5.2] Irpinia, Campania, 23 November 1980: thousands killed.
[geo-region-european-s2.5.3] L'Aquila, Abruzzo, 6 April 2009: magnitude 6.3, about 300 deaths.
[geo-region-european-s2.5.4] Vesuvius buried Pompeii and Herculaneum in AD 79 and last erupted in 1944. Because it is still active, it remains a hazard to nearby Naples.
[geo-region-european-s2.5.5] Etna and Stromboli are active volcanoes. This is another sign of the plate boundary beneath the region.
[geo-region-european-s2.5.6] Campi Flegrei is a volcanic caldera (a large crater formed by collapse) west of Naples, where the ground rises and falls, called bradyseism, around Pozzuoli.
[geo-region-european-s2.6.b] Soils vary sharply with bedrock (the solid rock under the soil) and slope, and this decides which land can be farmed well.
[geo-region-european-s2.6.0] Thin, stony soils cover much of the upland, where the bedrock is close to the surface. This is why upland farming is poor.
[geo-region-european-s2.6.1] Terra rossa is red clay soil that forms on limestone, for example in Puglia.
[geo-region-european-s2.6.2] Fertile volcanic soils on Vesuvius and Etna, in Campania and Sicily, support vineyards and orchards.
[geo-region-european-s2.6.3] Alluvial soils (soils deposited by rivers) on the lowlands are used for intensive farming. Because rivers deposit fresh fertile material, these soils suit intensive farming.
[geo-region-european-s2.6.4] Calanchi are gullied badland slopes on clay, for example in Basilicata. Because clay erodes easily in heavy rain, it forms gullies, so the land is of little farming use.
[geo-region-european-s2.6.5] Soil erosion is worsened by deforestation, overgrazing and the winter downpours that wash bare soil away.
[geo-region-european-s2.6.6] Sarno, Campania, 5–6 May 1998: saturated volcanic ash slid as mudflows, killing about 160 people.
[geo-region-european-s2.7.b] Rivers follow the climate: they carry water in the wet winter and shrink in the dry summer. This makes them poor sources of water and transport.
[geo-region-european-s2.7.0] Rivers are short and steep, so they flood quickly after rain.
[geo-region-european-s2.7.1] Winter: heavy rain turns the rivers into torrents. This is why rivers flood so quickly.
[geo-region-european-s2.7.2] Summer: many channels run dry. This is why rivers cannot supply irrigation water in the season crops need it most.
[geo-region-european-s2.7.3] Fiumare are wide, dry, stony riverbeds in Calabria and Sicily that flood after heavy rain.
[geo-region-european-s2.7.4] Few rivers are navigable (deep and steady enough for boats), so they are of little use for transport.
[geo-region-european-s2.7.5] The Apulian Aqueduct (Acquedotto Pugliese), built in the early 20th century, carries water from the Sele river springs in Campania across the Apennines to Puglia, which has little water of its own.
[geo-region-european-s2.8.b] Before reform, most land was held in large estates, which kept productivity (output per worker or hectare) low.
[geo-region-european-s2.8.0] Latifundia are large estates owned by absentee landlords, who lived away from them.
[geo-region-european-s2.8.1] The latifundia were worked by landless day labourers (braccianti) paid wages.
[geo-region-european-s2.8.2] The main uses of the estates were extensive wheat growing (low output per hectare) and sheep grazing.
[geo-region-european-s2.8.3] Small hill farms were fragmented into scattered plots, which were hard to farm efficiently. So the farmers could not use their land efficiently.
[geo-region-european-s2.8.4] Productivity was low on both the estates and the small hill farms.
[geo-region-european-s2.9.b] After the Second World War the state tried to fix the old estate system and develop the South. It did this through land reform and a development fund.
[geo-region-european-s2.9.0] Land reform laws of 1950 expropriated (took by law) parts of the large estates and gave plots to peasants.
[geo-region-european-s2.9.1] The Cassa per il Mezzogiorno (Fund for the South) ran from 1950 to 1984. Its aim was to develop the South with state money.
[geo-region-european-s2.9.2] Its work included land reclamation (making wet or unusable land farmable), drainage, irrigation dams, aqueducts and roads. These works improved land and water supply for farming.
[geo-region-european-s2.9.3] It built new settlements on reclaimed lowlands for the new smallholders (small farm owners).
[geo-region-european-s2.9.4] Malaria was eradicated from the lowlands after the Second World War, which made them safe to settle.
[geo-region-european-s2.9.5] From the late 1950s the Cassa also funded industry.
[geo-region-european-s2.10.b] Modern farming in the South is specialised: it grows Mediterranean crops suited to the climate and soils, often for markets beyond the region.
[geo-region-european-s2.10.0] Durum wheat is grown on the Tavoliere di Puglia around Foggia, Italy's main durum wheat area, for pasta. The dry summers and flat land suit it.
[geo-region-european-s2.10.1] Puglia is Italy's leading olive-oil region. Olives are drought-tolerant, so they suit the dry climate.
[geo-region-european-s2.10.2] Sicilian blood oranges are grown on the plain of Catania near Etna.
[geo-region-european-s2.10.3] Lemons are grown on the Amalfi coast and around Sorrento. These crops are specialised and sold beyond the region.
[geo-region-european-s2.10.4] Irrigated market gardening (growing vegetables for sale) on the Campanian plain includes San Marzano tomatoes grown near Vesuvius.
[geo-region-european-s2.10.5] Puglia and Sicily are major wine producers. Vines are drought-tolerant, so they suit the dry summers.
[geo-region-european-s2.10.6] Specialised Mediterranean produce is grown for markets, and agri-food firms (food-processing businesses) process it.
[geo-region-european-s2.11.b] Despite reform, southern farming faces natural, economic and social problems.
[geo-region-european-s2.11.0] Drought and water shortage reduce yields where land is not irrigated. This is why irrigation matters.
[geo-region-european-s2.11.1] Soil erosion removes topsoil from the hills. Steep slopes and winter downpours make this worse.
[geo-region-european-s2.11.2] Xylella fastidiosa, a bacterial disease of olive trees, was detected in the Salento (southern Puglia) in 2013 and has killed millions of olive trees.
[geo-region-european-s2.11.3] Ageing farmers and rural depopulation leave fewer people to work the farms. This is why farms are harder to keep going.
[geo-region-european-s2.11.4] Caporalato is illegal gangmaster hiring and exploitation of farm labourers, many of them migrants. This is why farm work can be unsafe and underpaid.
[geo-region-european-s2.11.5] Many farms depend on EU Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) support (payments to farmers), so they are exposed if it changes.
[geo-region-european-s2.12.b] Forests were cut down in the past, and the dry climate still threatens what remains.
[geo-region-european-s2.12.0] Historic deforestation of the Apennines has left slopes exposed to erosion. Without tree roots to hold the soil, rain washes it away.
[geo-region-european-s2.12.1] Reafforestation (replanting trees) of the Sila plateau in Calabria was carried out under the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno.
[geo-region-european-s2.12.2] Summer wildfires destroy woodland in the dry season. The dry heat makes fires spread easily.
[geo-region-european-s2.13.b] Fishing in the South is based on Mediterranean ports such as Mazara del Vallo in Sicily, and overfishing now limits catches.
[geo-region-european-s2.13.0] Mazara del Vallo, Sicily, is one of Italy's main fishing ports.
[geo-region-european-s2.13.1] Traditional tuna trapping, the mattanza, took place in Sicily at Favignana; it has now largely ended.
[geo-region-european-s2.13.2] Overfishing (catching fish faster than stocks recover) in the Mediterranean reduces catches.
[geo-region-european-s2.14.b] The South has some energy resources and is also a landing point for imported gas, with growing renewable power.
[geo-region-european-s2.14.0] The Val d'Agri oilfield in Basilicata is Italy's largest onshore oilfield and one of the largest onshore oilfields in western Europe. This makes Basilicata a significant energy source.
[geo-region-european-s2.14.1] Gas pipelines from Algeria land at Mazara del Vallo, and gas from Libya lands at Gela, both in Sicily.
[geo-region-european-s2.14.2] The Trans Adriatic Pipeline (TAP), carrying gas from Azerbaijan, lands in Puglia and began flowing in 2020.
[geo-region-european-s2.14.3] Puglia and Sicily are among Italy's leading regions for wind and solar power, which suit their sunny, open land.
[geo-region-european-s2.15.b] Industry in the South has stayed weak compared with the North. Several causes reinforce each other.
[geo-region-european-s2.15.0] Distance from markets raises the cost of transporting goods to buyers. This is why firms in the South struggle to compete.
[geo-region-european-s2.15.1] Poor infrastructure: weak roads, railways and ports make transport slow and costly. This is why firms find it costly to move goods.

[geo-region-european-s2.16.1] Petrochemical plants (making chemicals from oil and gas) were built at Brindisi and at the Priolo–Augusta complex near Siracusa in Sicily, and a refinery at Gela in Sicily. These plants were state-led, part of the growth-pole policy.

[geo-region-european-s2.17.1] Fiat's Melfi plant in Basilicata opened in the early 1990s and is now part of Stellantis. It brought a major car plant to inland Basilicata.
[geo-region-european-s2.17.2] Fiat's plant at Termini Imerese, Sicily, closed in 2011. The closure shows how fragile southern manufacturing jobs are.
[geo-region-european-s2.18.b] Some of the South's industry has brought serious problems as well as jobs.
[geo-region-european-s2.18.0] In 2012 a court ordered parts of the Taranto steelworks seized because of pollution linked to illness in Taranto.
[geo-region-european-s2.18.1] Lavoro nero is undeclared work in the informal, or black, economy, outside tax and labour rules, so workers lack protection and the state loses tax.
[geo-region-european-s2.19.b] Current policy tries to close the North–South gap using EU and Italian funds and new growth sectors.
[geo-region-european-s2.19.0] For EU cohesion policy (EU funding to reduce regional inequality) 2021–27, most of the South, including Campania, Puglia, Basilicata, Calabria and Sicily, is classed as less developed regions, with GDP per head below 75% of the EU average.
[geo-region-european-s2.19.1] A single Special Economic Zone for the South (ZES Unica) began in January 2024, offering tax credits and simplified permits.
[geo-region-european-s2.19.2] Italy's post-Covid National Recovery and Resilience Plan reserves at least 40% of its territorially allocated funds for the South.
[geo-region-european-s2.19.3] Aerospace is a growth sector: Leonardo builds Boeing 787 fuselage sections at Grottaglie, near Taranto. This shows the South moving beyond heavy industry.
[geo-region-european-s2.19.4] Agri-food is also a growth sector, building on the region's specialised produce. This is because local produce can be processed locally for added value.
[geo-region-european-s2.20.b] Exams ask you to explain secondary activity (manufacturing) using factors such as labour and markets. For each factor, give the fact and the challenge it creates in the Mezzogiorno.
[geo-region-european-s2.20.0] Labour: there is a large pool of unemployed workers, but low incomes limit local spending.
[geo-region-european-s2.20.1] Raw materials: heavy industry depends on inputs brought in from outside, such as gas by pipeline.
[geo-region-european-s2.20.2] Markets: the small local market and distant European markets mean firms face high transport costs.
[geo-region-european-s2.20.3] Infrastructure: slow motorway upgrading and limited high-speed rail make transport costly and slow.
[geo-region-european-s2.20.4] Government policy: the Cassa's growth poles and the ZES Unica attract investment, but capital-intensive plants create few jobs.
[geo-region-european-s2.21.b] Tourism is a tertiary (service) activity that suits the South's sun, scenery and history.
[geo-region-european-s2.21.0] Visitors come for the climate, the coast, the volcanoes of Vesuvius and Etna, the heritage sites and the food.
[geo-region-european-s2.21.1] UNESCO sites (World Heritage Sites): Pompeii and Herculaneum (1997); the Amalfi Coast (1997); the Valley of the Temples, Agrigento (1997); the trulli of Alberobello, Puglia (1996); the sassi of Matera (1993); the historic centre of Naples (1995).
[geo-region-european-s2.21.2] Taormina, Sicily, is a coastal tourist town. It is a coastal town, so it draws visitors for the sea and scenery.
[geo-region-european-s2.21.3] The Costa Smeralda in north-east Sardinia was developed as a luxury resort from the 1960s by the Aga Khan. This was planned luxury tourism, so it differs from the mass tourism elsewhere.
[geo-region-european-s2.21.4] Agriturismo is farm-stay tourism, bringing visitors into the countryside. This is why it suits the countryside, such as farms in hill areas.
[geo-region-european-s2.22.b] Tourism brings benefits, but it also creates problems.
[geo-region-european-s2.22.0] Seasonality: most visitors come in the summer months, leaving tourist jobs short-term. This is why tourism gives uneven year-round income.
[geo-region-european-s2.22.1] Coastal overdevelopment: abusivismo, illegal building including on coasts, damages the landscape.
[geo-region-european-s2.22.2] Crime: organised crime can affect businesses and tourist areas.
[geo-region-european-s2.22.3] Poor transport: slow road and rail links limit access to the South's attractions.
[geo-region-european-s2.23.b] Transport links open markets and bring investors and tourists, so poor links keep the region remote. The region's remoteness is partly a transport problem.
[geo-region-european-s2.23.0] The Autostrada del Sole (A1, Milan–Rome–Naples) was completed in 1964. It linked the South to the industrial North, which opened routes for goods and people.
[geo-region-european-s2.23.1] The A2 Salerno–Reggio Calabria motorway has been notorious for decades of slow, costly upgrading. This slow progress is why it counts as a transport problem.
[geo-region-european-s2.23.2] High-speed rail reaches Naples and Salerno; the Naples–Bari line is being upgraded.
[geo-region-european-s2.23.3] Gioia Tauro, Calabria, is Italy's largest container transhipment port (where containers are moved between ships). Because ships transfer cargo there, it is a hub for sea trade.
[geo-region-european-s2.23.4] Ferries cross the Strait of Messina between Villa San Giovanni/Reggio Calabria and Messina. They link Sicily to the mainland, so island goods and visitors can move.
[geo-region-european-s2.23.5] Ferries sail from Bari and Brindisi to Greece and Albania. This is why these ports matter for trade with Greece and Albania.
[geo-region-european-s2.23.6] A bridge across the Strait of Messina has been proposed, approved and challenged repeatedly over many years, so do not assume it is built.
[geo-region-european-s2.24.b] Services provide jobs in the South, but not the same kind as in the North.
[geo-region-european-s2.24.0] Public-sector employment (jobs paid by the state) is a large source of jobs in the South. This is because private industry is weak.
[geo-region-european-s2.24.1] Informal employment, such as lavoro nero (undeclared work), is also large.
[geo-region-european-s2.24.2] Private services are weaker than in the North.
[geo-region-european-s2.25.b] Population is concentrated on the coastal lowlands and in cities, and thins out in the interior.
[geo-region-european-s2.25.0] Campania has about 400 people per km², one of Italy's most densely populated regions. This is because of its fertile volcanic lowlands and the city of Naples.
[geo-region-european-s2.25.1] Basilicata has about 55 people per km² and only about 540,000 people (2020s). This is because it is largely mountainous and remote.
[geo-region-european-s2.25.2] Dense coastal lowlands and cities contrast with sparse interior uplands.
[geo-region-european-s2.25.3] Fertile volcanic lowlands attract settlement; thin, stony soils and dry uplands do not.
[geo-region-european-s2.25.4] Cities offer jobs in services and industry, drawing people from the interior.
[geo-region-european-s2.26.b] Poverty and lack of jobs have pushed people out of the South for well over a century.
[geo-region-european-s2.26.0] Large-scale emigration from the late 19th century went to the USA and Argentina. People left because of poverty and lack of work.
[geo-region-european-s2.26.1] After the Second World War, emigration went to the industrial cities of northern Italy (Turin, Milan) and to Germany, Switzerland and Belgium.
[geo-region-european-s2.26.2] Out-migration continues, as young graduates leave the region for work elsewhere in Italy and abroad. This is because jobs in the South are limited.
[geo-region-european-s2.27.b] Fewer births and continued departures mean the population is ageing, while some migrants arrive across the sea.
[geo-region-european-s2.27.0] Fertility (births per woman) was once high but is now very low: Italy's rate is about 1.2 children per woman (2023), among the lowest in the EU. This is why the population is ageing.
[geo-region-european-s2.27.1] Sardinia's fertility rate is among the lowest in Italy.
[geo-region-european-s2.27.2] Inland villages are ageing and depopulating, with few young residents. This is because young people leave and few children are born.
[geo-region-european-s2.27.3] €1 house schemes in depopulating villages, for example Sambuca di Sicilia, sell houses for €1 to attract new residents.
[geo-region-european-s2.27.4] Lampedusa, an Italian island south of Sicily, is a main landing point for migrants crossing the Mediterranean. It lies south of Sicily, so it is close to the sea route from Africa.
[geo-region-european-s2.28.b] Standard Italian is shared across the country, but the South also has strong dialects and minority languages.
[geo-region-european-s2.28.0] Standard Italian is the official language.
[geo-region-european-s2.28.1] Strong regional dialects include Neapolitan and Sicilian.
[geo-region-european-s2.28.2] Arbëreshë is spoken by Albanian-speaking communities in Calabria and Sicily, settled from the 15th century. This is why Albanian is still spoken there.
[geo-region-european-s2.28.3] Griko is a Greek dialect spoken in the Salento (Puglia) and southern Calabria.
[geo-region-european-s2.28.4] Sardinian is spoken in Sardinia.
[geo-region-european-s2.28.5] Catalan is spoken in Alghero, Sardinia.

[geo-region-european-s2.29.1] The Feast of San Gennaro, patron saint of Naples, includes the ritual of the liquefaction of his blood.
[geo-region-european-s2.30.b] Culture in the South combines folk traditions, regional identity and football. Religion and language, covered above, are part of it too.
[geo-region-european-s2.30.0] The tarantella is a fast folk dance of southern Italy, named after Taranto.
[geo-region-european-s2.30.1] Neapolitan song includes 'O sole mio'.
[geo-region-european-s2.30.2] SSC Napoli won Serie A (Italy's top football league) in 1987, 1990, 2023 and 2025.
[geo-region-european-s2.30.3] The art of the Neapolitan pizza maker was added to UNESCO's intangible heritage list in 2017.
[geo-region-european-s2.30.4] Sicily and Sardinia are autonomous regions with a special statute, giving their regional governments extra powers.
[geo-region-european-s2.30.5] Clientelism, the exchange of votes for favours, and organised crime both influence local politics.
[geo-region-european-s2.31.b] Naples is the South's largest city. Its rapid, unplanned growth left serious problems.

CANDIDATES geo-region-concept-s4: 4. Socio-economic regions
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.b] Geographers group places into regions by how wealthy and developed they are, not just by landscape. A socio-economic region is defined by its level of wealth and development, judged by several indicators at once, because no single figure tells the whole story.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.0] The indicators include income or GDP per head (the value of what an area produces, divided by its population), unemployment, the mix of primary, secondary and tertiary jobs, migration, age structure, education and services.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.1] Primary jobs take materials from the land and sea (farming, fishing, forestry, mining); secondary jobs make goods in factories; tertiary jobs provide services. The mix of these three is a clue to how developed a region is.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.2] The core–periphery idea: wealth, people and investment (money put into businesses and infrastructure) concentrate in a core, while the periphery (the outer, distant area) loses people and capital to it.
[geo-region-concept-s4.0.3] Cumulative causation means each advantage in the core attracts more, so the gap tends to widen unless governments or the EU intervene.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.b] A core region has a strong, growing economy, high incomes, and a concentration of population, investment and decision-making. Everything else about it follows from that economic strength.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.0] It has high GDP per head and low unemployment, because jobs are plentiful.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.1] It has a large tertiary (services) sector, including finance, company headquarters and high-tech industry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.2] Most foreign investment goes to the core, because firms from abroad want its markets, workers and transport links.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.3] Net in-migration (more people arriving than leaving) of young, educated workers gives it a young population, because they move to where the jobs are.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.4] It has dense, well-developed infrastructure (the transport networks that move people and goods): motorways, airports, ports and rail lines.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.5] It has universities and research institutions, which supply skilled workers, and usually the capital city with the seat of government.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.6] Causes: accessibility, a large market and labour force, an early start, government and capital functions, and agglomeration, where firms attract more firms to the same place because they gain from being near suppliers, customers and workers.
[geo-region-concept-s4.1.7] Problems of success: congestion, high house prices, urban sprawl (the city spreading outwards over farmland) and pressure on services.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.b] Three examples show what a core looks like at national and European scale. Each is a place where population, government and business cluster.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.0] Greater Dublin Area: Dublin, Kildare, Meath and Wicklow. County Dublin had about 1.45 million people in Census 2022, when the State's population was about 5.1 million.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.1] Dublin, the capital, has the seat of government, Ireland's main port (Dublin Port) and busiest airport (Dublin Airport). Its docklands host the European headquarters of many technology firms, e.g. Google. There is more on this in the Irish Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.2] Paris Basin, France: Île-de-France, the region around Paris at the basin's core, has about 12 million people. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.2.3] The Blue Banana: the curved belt of Europe's main core, running from south-east England and London through the Benelux countries (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg) and the Rhine valley to northern Italy (Milan).
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.b] A peripheral region is distant, physically or economically, from the core, with a weaker economy and fewer opportunities. Its problems tend to feed each other, so people leave and the region falls further behind.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.0] Remote location and poor access to markets and services, so it is costly to sell goods or reach shops, hospitals and schools.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.1] Difficult physical conditions: mountains, high rainfall, poor soils and a short growing season, which limit what farmers can produce.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.2] Dependence on primary activity (taking materials from land and sea): small farms, fishing and forestry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.3] Low incomes and higher unemployment, so people look elsewhere for work, or underemployment (people working fewer hours or in lower-skilled jobs than they want).
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.4] Net out-migration of young adults, so the people left behind are older and rural populations fall.
[geo-region-concept-s4.3.5] Fewer services, and small, scattered settlements, which means services costly to provide.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.b] Because the periphery tends to fall further behind, governments and the EU act to slow the loss of people and jobs.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.0] EU regional and cohesion funds (money meant to narrow the gap between richer and poorer regions) and state agencies channel investment into the periphery.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.1] Better roads and broadband, and decentralisation of government offices (moving them out of the capital), help peripheral areas keep people and jobs.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.2] Tourism builds on the scenery and culture of peripheral areas, so it brings jobs without needing a nearby core.
[geo-region-concept-s4.4.3] There is more on each example in the case-study chapters.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.b] These four examples are far from their country's or Europe's core, in the west, south and far north.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.0] West of Ireland: the Northern and Western regional assembly area. Co. Leitrim has the smallest population of any Irish county.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.1] The Mezzogiorno, southern Italy. There is more on this in the European Regions chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.2] Northern Norway: Arctic, remote and reliant on fishing.
[geo-region-concept-s4.5.3] North-west Scotland: the Highlands and Islands.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.b] A less-developed region is defined by how far its development falls below an average, and the EU uses a precise cut-off to decide which regions get the most help.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.0] A less-developed region is one whose level of development is well below the national or EU average.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.1] In EU regional policy, a less-developed region has GDP per head below 75% of the EU average, and receives the most EU aid.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.2] Most less-developed regions in Europe are also peripheral: the Mezzogiorno, and much of eastern Europe such as Romania and Bulgaria.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.3] Peripheral is not the same as less-developed, because a peripheral region can lie in a rich country, such as the West of Ireland or northern Norway.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.4] Ireland was a single Objective 1 region (the category receiving the highest level of EU aid) until 1999. From 2000 to 2006 only the BMW (Border, Midland and Western) region kept Objective 1 status.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.5] At a global scale, less-developed regions are developing countries. India is the continental example. There is more on India in its own chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.6.6] The Mezzogiorno's development history began with the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno (Fund for the South) in 1950.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.b] A region of industrial decline grew on heavy, traditional industry and has since lost much of it. The loss then spreads through the local economy.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.0] Its traditional industries include coal mining, iron and steel, shipbuilding and textiles.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.1] Causes: coal seams exhausted or too costly to work; cheaper imports and competition from newly industrialised countries (countries that built up their factory industry more recently, often with lower costs).
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.2] Further causes: coal replaced by oil and gas; out-of-date plant (old machinery and factories); and automation (machines doing work people once did), which needs fewer workers.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.3] Effects: high unemployment, especially among older male workers; derelict factories, mines and spoil heaps (waste rock dumped from mining); and polluted land.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.4] Further effects: out-migration, falling services, and a poor image that deters new investment.
[geo-region-concept-s4.7.5] Responses: retraining; clearing and cleaning derelict land; attracting new light industry and services; universities and technology parks; reuse of industrial heritage for tourism and culture; EU funds.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.b] Most examples are old coal and steel regions of north-west Europe. The Irish examples are port and shipbuilding areas.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.0] Sambre–Meuse valley, Wallonia, Belgium: coal mining and steel along the Sambre and Meuse, e.g. Charleroi on the Sambre and Liège on the Meuse.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.1] Ruhr, Germany (North Rhine-Westphalia): Europe's largest coal and steel region, so its decline was large, with cities such as Essen, Dortmund and Duisburg.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.2] Prosper-Haniel in Bottrop, the last hard-coal mine in Germany, closed in 2018. The Zollverein coal mine complex in Essen became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001 and is now a cultural centre, an example of reusing industrial heritage.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.3] South Wales coalfield, including the Rhondda valleys: coal mining collapsed in the late 20th century.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.4] Nord–Pas-de-Calais, France, is a further European example.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.5] Belfast, for example: the Harland and Wolff shipyard, which built the Titanic (launched 1911), declined greatly in the late 20th century.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.6] Dublin docklands: port jobs fell with containerisation (goods shipped in standard metal containers, which need far fewer dock workers). The International Financial Services Centre (IFSC), established in 1987 on derelict dockland, regenerated the area.
[geo-region-concept-s4.8.7] There is more on Irish economic evidence in the Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Economic Activities chapter.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.b] The clearest difference between the two region types is the kind of work people do. Compare them sector by sector: primary, secondary and tertiary.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.0] Primary, for example: large, commercial, efficient farms on good land in the core; small, fragmented farms on poor land in the periphery, with more fishing and forestry.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.1] Secondary: concentrations of manufacturing and multinational investment (factories owned by firms based in other countries) in the core; few, small and often grant-aided (helped by state money) factories in the periphery.
[geo-region-concept-s4.9.2] Tertiary: finance, headquarters, government, universities, and major ports and airports in the core; tourism and public services in the periphery.

CANDIDATES geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1: 1. The geography of volcanoes
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.b] A volcano is an opening (vent) in the Earth's crust, the thin rocky outer layer, through which magma, gases and fragments escape, together with the landform built around it. Magma is molten rock below the surface; lava is magma that has reached the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.0] Magma chamber: the reservoir of magma beneath the volcano, where the molten rock collects before it rises.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.1] Main vent: the pipe (conduit) that carries magma up to the crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.2] Secondary (parasitic) vent: a smaller opening on the flanks (sides) of the cone, which lets magma escape away from the summit.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.3] Crater: the hollow at the top of the main vent.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.4] Cone: the hill of the volcano, built up over many eruptions from alternating layers of lava and ash.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.5] Lava flow: lava moving across the surface away from the vent, such as the flows drawn down the side of the cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.0.6] Diagram: draw a labelled cross-section showing the magma chamber, main vent, secondary vent, crater, cone of alternating lava and ash layers, lava flow, ash cloud and volcanic bombs, with the crust around the chamber.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.b] An eruption throws out liquid, gas and solid material. Each one is a hazard in its own right, so you need to know the names and what each does.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.0] Lava: molten rock that flows from the vent, so it can bury land and buildings around the volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.1] Gases: water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide, which are released as the magma rises and the pressure on it falls.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.2] Pyroclastic material (tephra): solid fragments of rock thrown into the air. It includes ash, lapilli (cinders), volcanic bombs and pumice.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.3] Ash: the finest pyroclastic particles, which are light enough to fall over wide areas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.4] Volcanic bombs: large lumps of lava thrown out of the vent while still partly molten.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.5] Pumice: a light, porous rock (full of tiny holes) formed from frothy lava.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.6] Pyroclastic flows: fast, very hot avalanches of gas, ash and rock moving down the slopes, so they destroy anything in their path.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.1.7] Lahars: volcanic mudflows of ash and water, for example when an eruption melts snow or ice.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.b] The silica content of magma (silica is the mineral material that makes rock) controls how runny the lava is, how much gas it traps, and so how the volcano erupts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.0] Basic (basaltic) lava is low in silica and hot, so it is runny (low viscosity, meaning it flows easily).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.1] Gases escape easily from basic lava, so eruptions are gentle (effusive, meaning lava pours out rather than blasting).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.2] Acidic lava is high in silica and cooler, so it is viscous (thick and sticky).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.3] Acidic lava traps gas, so pressure builds up and eruptions are explosive.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.2.4] Low-viscosity lava spreads widely and builds shield volcanoes (broad, gently sloping); viscous lava piles up into steep cones. The names and shapes of the landform types belong to the volcanic and plutonic landforms notes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.b] Volcanoes are grouped by how likely they are to erupt again. The groups depend on how long it has been since the last eruption and whether another is expected.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.0] Active: a volcano that has erupted recently and may erupt again soon. Examples: Mount Etna, Sicily, Italy; Kilauea, Hawaii; Stromboli, Italy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.1] Dormant (sleeping): a volcano that has not erupted for a long time but could erupt again. Example: Mount Fuji, Japan, last erupted in 1707.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.3.2] Extinct: a volcano not expected ever to erupt again. Example: Slemish, Co. Antrim, the remains of an extinct volcano, linked with St Patrick.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.b] Most volcanoes lie in narrow belts that follow plate boundaries (the lines where the huge slabs of crust, called plates, meet), not at random. Each type of boundary gives a different result.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.0] Constructive (divergent) boundaries: basic magma rises to fill the gap where plates separate, mostly under the sea along mid-ocean ridges (underwater mountain chains).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.1] Iceland is where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level; the North American and Eurasian plates are moving apart there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.2] Destructive (convergent) boundaries: oceanic crust is subducted (pushed down beneath another plate), the descending plate melts, and acidic magma rises to form explosive volcanoes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.3] Destructive boundaries form volcanoes in fold-mountain chains (mountains crumpled up where plates collide), such as the Andes where the Nazca plate is subducted beneath the South American plate.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.4] They also form volcanoes in island arcs (curved chains of volcanic islands) such as Japan and Indonesia.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.4.5] Continental-continental collision and transform (conservative) boundaries, where plates slide past each other, have few or no volcanoes, so magma rarely reaches the surface there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.b] A belt of volcanoes circles the Pacific Ocean, because most of the plate boundaries around that ocean are destructive.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.0] The Ring of Fire is a belt of volcanoes and earthquakes around the edges of the Pacific Ocean, mainly along destructive boundaries.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.1] It contains about three-quarters of the world's active volcanoes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.5.2] Examples: Mount St Helens (Cascade Range, Washington State, USA); Mount Fuji, Japan; Mount Pinatubo, the Philippines; Krakatoa, Indonesia; volcanoes of the Andes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.b] A hot spot is a plume of magma rising from deep in the mantle (the hot layer beneath the crust), away from plate boundaries. It explains volcanoes in places where no plates meet.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.0] As a plate moves over a stationary hot spot, a chain of volcanoes forms, with the volcanoes getting older with distance from the hot spot, because each one is carried away from the magma supply.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.1] Hawaii sits in the middle of the Pacific plate over a hot spot, so it is not on a plate boundary. The Pacific plate moves north-west, so the islands get older to the north-west.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.2] The active volcanoes Kilauea and Mauna Loa are on the Big Island in the south-east, over the hot spot.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.6.3] Other hot spots: Yellowstone, USA; Iceland, which is on a constructive boundary and also over a hot spot.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.b] Ireland has no active volcanoes now, but it had huge eruptions in the past. These were linked to the opening of the North Atlantic Ocean, which is the syllabus's national setting.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.0] Ireland lies in the middle of the Eurasian plate, far from a plate boundary, so it has no active volcanoes today.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.1] About 60 million years ago, North America and Greenland separated from Europe as the Atlantic opened. The crust stretched and cracked.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.2] Basic lava poured out of long fissures (cracks in the ground) in north-east Ireland, flow upon flow, in fissure eruptions. These lavas formed the Antrim basalts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.3] Basalt is an extrusive (volcanic) rock, meaning it formed from lava on the surface. The Antrim basalts came from fissures, not from a single cone volcano.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.7.4] Landforms from these lavas include the Antrim plateau and the Giant's Causeway.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.b] Eruptions can kill people and damage land, buildings, travel and even the climate. Named examples show how large the damage can be.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.0] Loss of life, and destruction of buildings, farmland and infrastructure by lava, pyroclastic flows and lahars. In AD 79 Vesuvius, near Naples, Italy, buried the Roman towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum under ash and pyroclastic flows.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.1] In 2018, lava from Kilauea's lower East Rift Zone destroyed hundreds of homes in the Leilani Estates area of Hawaii.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.2] Ash fall can collapse roofs under its weight, for example when thick ash builds up on them, destroy crops and contaminate water supplies.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.3] Ash clouds disrupt air travel. In April 2010 an eruption under the Eyjafjallajökull ice cap, Iceland, closed much of European airspace for about a week, and about 100,000 flights were cancelled.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.4] Volcanic tsunamis (huge sea waves): the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa, Indonesia, caused tsunamis that killed about 36,000 people.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.5] The undersea Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai volcano, Tonga, erupted on 15 January 2022 and caused a tsunami across the Pacific.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.6] Gases and ash can cool the global climate for a year or two. After Mount Pinatubo, the Philippines, erupted in 1991, sulphur dioxide in the upper atmosphere cooled global average temperatures by about 0.5°C over the following year or so.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.8.7] Economic losses and evacuation of populations, because homes, farms and businesses are lost and people must leave the danger zone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.b] Volcanoes are dangerous, but they also give people land, energy, income and materials. This is why many people live close to them.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.0] Fertile soils from weathered volcanic ash and lava, which break down into minerals plants can use. On the slopes of Mount Etna and Vesuvius they support vineyards and orchards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.1] Geothermal energy (heat from underground) and hot water: in Iceland geothermal energy heats about nine in ten homes and generates electricity.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.2] Tourism: volcanoes, geysers (jets of hot water and steam) and hot springs attract visitors, such as the Blue Lagoon and the geysers at Geysir, Iceland.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.3] Minerals and building materials, such as sulphur, pumice and basalt, which can be quarried or mined from volcanic rock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.9.4] New land: Surtsey, an island that emerged from the sea off southern Iceland in 1963.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.b] Mount St Helens is the full case study to learn, with date, place, plate setting, events, effects and response. The eruption was sideways, not vertical, which makes it unusual.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.0] Place: Cascade Range, Washington State, USA. The Juan de Fuca plate is subducted beneath the North American plate, a destructive boundary.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.1] For weeks before 18 May 1980, magma pushed the north flank (side) outwards into a bulge.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.2] A magnitude 5.1 earthquake triggered the collapse of the north flank as a huge landslide.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.3] The collapse released a sideways (lateral) blast that flattened forests.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.10.4] The summit lost about 400 m of height, leaving a horseshoe-shaped crater.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.b] The eruption did great damage, but the number of deaths was low because people had been kept away from the danger zone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.0] Ash fell across several US states.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.11.1] Lahars (mudflows of ash and water) flowed down river valleys, including the Toutle River, destroying bridges and homes.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.b] Volcanoes usually give warning signs, because magma must move upwards before an eruption. Scientists measure these signs to judge when a volcano is becoming dangerous.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.0] Seismometers (instruments that record earthquakes) pick up swarms of small earthquakes as magma rises.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.1] Ground deformation: tiltmeters (which measure changes in slope), GPS and satellite radar measure swelling or bulging of the cone.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.2] Gas emissions: changes in the amount and type of gas, especially sulphur dioxide.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.3] Temperature: thermal imaging and satellites detect heating of the ground or of crater lakes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.4] Changes in groundwater and springs, for example because rising magma heats the water underground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.5] Studying the deposits and records of past eruptions shows how often a volcano erupts and which areas are at risk. Hazard maps show these areas.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.12.6] Scientists can often forecast that an eruption is likely, but not its exact time, size or style.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.b] Eruptions cannot be stopped, but their effects can be limited. The main approaches are to watch the volcano, keep people out of danger, and sometimes to fight the lava itself.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.0] Monitoring by volcano observatories, so that warning signs are picked up early.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.1] Hazard zoning keeps settlement out of high-risk zones, so fewer people and buildings are in the path of an eruption.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.2] Evacuation plans and warning systems, and public education.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.3] Diverting or slowing lava flows with barriers and channels, or cooling it with water.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.4] Heimaey, Iceland, 1973: the Eldfell eruption on Heimaey, Westman Islands, threatened the harbour. About 5,000 residents were evacuated overnight, mostly by the fishing fleet. Sea water was pumped onto the advancing lava to cool and halt it, saving the harbour.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1.13.5] Pinatubo, the Philippines, 1991: scientists from the US Geological Survey and the Philippine volcano institute monitored earthquakes, gas emissions and swelling, and forecast the eruption. Tens of thousands of people were evacuated, including from the US Clark Air Base, saving thousands of lives.
