TARGET geo-urban-s8: 8. The future of urbanism
[geo-urban-s8.0.b] The world keeps urbanising, which means a growing share of people live in towns and cities rather than the countryside. All the issues facing cities of the future follow from this trend. The UN figures below are projections for the future, not facts.
[geo-urban-s8.0.0] About 30% of the world's population was urban in 1950 and about 55% in 2018 (UN, World Urbanization Prospects 2018).
[geo-urban-s8.0.1] The UN projects about 68% of the world's population to be urban by 2050. Write it as a projection, not as a fact.
[geo-urban-s8.0.2] Close to 90% of the increase in urban population is expected in Asia and Africa. India, China and Nigeria together account for over a third of it.
[geo-urban-s8.0.3] In 2018 there were 33 megacities (cities with over 10 million people).
[geo-urban-s8.0.4] Tokyo was the largest urban agglomeration (a city together with its built-up surrounding area) in 2018, with about 37 million people. The UN projected that Delhi would overtake it around 2028.
[geo-urban-s8.0.5] Ireland is urbanising too: Census 2016 counted about 63% of Ireland's population as urban.
[geo-urban-s8.1.b] Demand for homes rises with population growth, smaller households and migration, and supply has not kept pace in successful cities. The result is that housing costs more than many people can pay.
[geo-urban-s8.1.0] High rents and house prices in Dublin and other successful cities put homes beyond the reach of many households, because so many people want to live where the jobs are.
[geo-urban-s8.1.1] Homelessness is a visible result: over 15,000 people were in emergency accommodation in 2025.
[geo-urban-s8.1.2] Housing for All (2021), the Irish government's housing plan, aims for about 33,000 new homes a year on average to 2030.
[geo-urban-s8.1.3] Rent Pressure Zones, introduced from 2016, limit rent increases in the areas where they apply, so tenants in high-demand areas face smaller rises.
[geo-urban-s8.1.4] Higher-density apartments near transport use land and transport more efficiently, because many people live on a small site close to public transport.
[geo-urban-s8.1.5] Public and cost-rental housing provide homes at below-market rents, helping households who cannot afford private rents.
[geo-urban-s8.1.6] The developing-world form of this issue is slum growth (crowded informal housing), because new arrivals cannot afford formal homes. There is more on this in Developing-world cities.
[geo-urban-s8.2.b] Car dependence causes congestion and emissions, and cities are responding with mass transit, restrictions on cars and new neighbourhood designs. Sustainable mobility means getting people around in ways that cause less pollution.
[geo-urban-s8.2.0] Car dependence produces congestion (traffic jams) and vehicle emissions in growing cities, because more people mean more cars on the same roads.
[geo-urban-s8.2.1] Dublin plans MetroLink (a planned metro rail line, with no date or cost yet) and BusConnects (a redesign of the bus network).
[geo-urban-s8.2.2] The NTA's Greater Dublin Area Transport Strategy 2022–2042 is the long-term transport plan for the region. The NTA is the National Transport Authority.
[geo-urban-s8.2.3] The Grand Paris Express will add about 200 km of new automatic (driverless) metro lines and 68 stations in the Paris region.
[geo-urban-s8.2.4] Copenhagen is a cycling city: cycling is the most common way of commuting to work or study.
[geo-urban-s8.2.5] London's Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ), an area where the most polluting vehicles must pay a charge, began in 2019 and was extended London-wide in August 2023.
[geo-urban-s8.2.6] Paris is promoting the 15-minute city, where everyday needs can be met within 15 minutes on foot or by bike. The idea is associated with Carlos Moreno and was promoted by Mayor Anne Hidalgo in her 2020 re-election campaign; it is not yet fully built.
[geo-urban-s8.2.7] Vauban in Freiburg, Germany, is a low-car eco-district built from the late 1990s on a former military barracks. Homes are built to high energy standards and residents are encouraged to live without a car. Electric vehicles reduce emissions from the cars that remain on the roads.
[geo-urban-s8.3.b] Cities produce most of the world's emissions and are exposed to sea-level rise, flooding and heat waves, so climate action and adaptation are needed together. Mitigation means cutting emissions; adaptation means coping with changes already under way.
[geo-urban-s8.3.0] Heat waves are intensified in cities by the urban heat island effect, where buildings and paved surfaces make the city warmer than its surroundings.
[geo-urban-s8.3.1] Cork city's low-lying centre suffers repeated tidal flooding; a flood-relief scheme for the River Lee is planned and disputed.
[geo-urban-s8.3.2] The Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 sets a legally binding target to cut Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions by 51% by 2030 from 2018 levels, and net zero (no net emissions) by 2050.
[geo-urban-s8.3.3] Adaptation responses include flood defences, green roofs (roofs planted with vegetation) and green infrastructure (parks, trees and other planted space that soak up water and cool the city).
[geo-urban-s8.3.4] Mitigation responses include energy-efficient buildings, district heating (one central plant heating many buildings) and renewable energy.
[geo-urban-s8.3.5] Masdar City in Abu Dhabi, UAE, was begun in 2006 as a planned low-carbon city; its original zero-carbon target has been scaled back.
[geo-urban-s8.4.b] Sprawl is low-density outward spread of a city onto surrounding land. It wastes land and energy, so planners promote compact growth instead.
[geo-urban-s8.4.0] Compact growth policy directs new housing into existing towns and cities rather than onto greenfield land (land not built on before), which saves countryside.
[geo-urban-s8.4.1] The National Planning Framework (NPF, 2018) sets a target of 40% of new homes within the built-up footprint of existing settlements.
[geo-urban-s8.4.2] The NPF expects half of growth to be in the five cities.
[geo-urban-s8.4.3] Brownfield regeneration reuses old industrial and urban land for new housing, so new homes do not need new land.
[geo-urban-s8.4.4] Height and density are debated: taller buildings save land but can change the character of a neighbourhood.
[geo-urban-s8.4.5] Green belts are planned areas of open land around cities that limit sprawl, because building on them is restricted.
[geo-urban-s8.5.b] Developed-world cities have more older residents, while fewer workers pay taxes to support services. Cities must therefore be redesigned for an older population.
[geo-urban-s8.5.0] Older people need accessible housing, accessible transport and health care close to home, because mobility often becomes harder with age.
[geo-urban-s8.5.1] Age-friendly design makes streets, buildings and services usable by older people, so they can stay independent.
[geo-urban-s8.5.2] Fewer workers paying taxes place pressure on the funding of services, since a smaller working population supports a larger older one.
[geo-urban-s8.6.b] Smart cities use sensors and data to manage traffic, energy and waste, but raise risks of cost, privacy and inequality.
[geo-urban-s8.6.0] Sensors and data are used to manage traffic, energy use and waste collection, so services respond to what is actually happening in the city.
[geo-urban-s8.6.1] Songdo in South Korea is a smart city built from the 2000s on land reclaimed from the Yellow Sea near Incheon.
[geo-urban-s8.6.2] Smart Dublin is a programme of the four Dublin local authorities to trial smart-city technology.
[geo-urban-s8.6.3] Risks include high cost, threats to privacy because so much data is collected, and a digital divide between those who can and cannot use digital services.
[geo-urban-s8.7.b] The COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 made remote and hybrid working (part at home, part in the office) common, which changed how city centres are used.
[geo-urban-s8.7.0] Remote and hybrid working reduce commuting, leaving offices and shops empty in city centres because fewer workers travel in.
[geo-urban-s8.7.1] City centres are converting offices to homes and leisure uses, so empty buildings find a new purpose.
[geo-urban-s8.7.2] Remote-working hubs in towns allow people to work close to home; Our Rural Future (2021–2025), the government's rural development policy, supports them.
[geo-urban-s8.8.b] Deindustrialised cities, where factories have closed, lose population and jobs, leaving vacant land and buildings. Not every future city grows.
[geo-urban-s8.8.0] Detroit fell from about 1.85 million people in 1950 to about 640,000 in 2020, and filed for bankruptcy in 2013. There is more on this in Urban problems in developed-world cities.
[geo-urban-s8.8.1] Vacant land is reused for parks and urban farming, so empty plots become useful instead of derelict.
[geo-urban-s8.8.2] Managed decline accepts a smaller population and plans services and land use for it, rather than trying to bring back the old population.
[geo-urban-s8.9.b] Rich and poor areas drift apart, which can lead to segregation (different groups living separately) and conflict.
[geo-urban-s8.9.0] Gentrification, where wealthier people move into and renovate a district, pushes lower-income residents out.
[geo-urban-s8.9.1] Gated communities (housing estates with controlled entry) separate wealthy households from the rest of the city.
[geo-urban-s8.9.2] Immigrants concentrate in some districts, such as the banlieues (suburbs) of Paris; Seine-Saint-Denis saw riots in 2005.
[geo-urban-s8.9.3] Social mix and inclusive planning spread housing for different incomes across the city, so no area is only rich or only poor.
[geo-urban-s8.10.b] Cities grow beyond their administrative boundaries, so governance (who runs the city region) is fragmented.
[geo-urban-s8.10.0] Dublin is divided between four councils: Dublin City, Fingal, South Dublin and Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown, so no single body runs the whole city region.
[geo-urban-s8.10.1] A Citizens' Assembly (a group of ordinary citizens asked to consider an issue) reported in 2023 recommending a directly elected mayor for Dublin; Dublin does not yet have one.
[geo-urban-s8.10.2] Limerick elected its first directly elected mayor in June 2024, unlike Dublin, which does not yet have one.
[geo-urban-s8.10.3] The Métropole du Grand Paris, created in 2016, is a metropolitan governance body for the Paris region.
[geo-urban-s8.11.b] Growing cities need water, energy and waste services that must keep pace with demand.
[geo-urban-s8.11.0] Uisce Éireann (Irish Water) has proposed a pipeline to bring water from the River Shannon to the Dublin region; no date or cost is set.
[geo-urban-s8.11.1] Data centres (buildings full of computer servers) used about a fifth of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 (CSO), so they add large demand to the energy supply.
[geo-urban-s8.11.2] São Paulo's 2014–2015 Cantareira drought showed how a large city can run short of water. There is more on this in Developing-world cities.
[geo-urban-s8.11.3] Waste collection and disposal must keep pace with growing populations, because more people produce more waste.
[geo-urban-s8.12.b] Most urban growth to 2050 falls in Asia and Africa, in cities with slums, poor water and sanitation, and high climate vulnerability.
[geo-urban-s8.12.0] Megacities are growing fastest in Asia and Africa, because that is where close to 90% of the world's urban increase is expected.
[geo-urban-s8.12.1] Kolkata and Mumbai sit on low-lying land, so this makes them vulnerable to flooding: Kolkata on the Hooghly delta, and Mumbai's 2005 flood brought about 944 mm of rain in one day.
[geo-urban-s8.12.2] Informal work (jobs outside regular employment), slums and inadequate water and sanitation remain the core challenges of these cities.
[geo-urban-s8.12.3] Innovative low-cost solutions exist, such as Curitiba's bus rapid transit (a bus system that works like a metro), begun in 1974.
[geo-urban-s8.13.b] The National Planning Framework (NPF) plans for Ireland's growth, and many of the issues above come together in Irish cities.
[geo-urban-s8.13.0] The NPF (2018) projects about 1 million more people in Ireland by 2040.
[geo-urban-s8.13.1] Galway, Cork, Limerick and Waterford, the regional cities, are each to grow by at least 50% by 2040.
[geo-urban-s8.13.2] Dublin's MetroLink is planned, with no date or cost yet.
[geo-urban-s8.13.3] Cork's light rail (a tram-style system) is proposed in the Cork Metropolitan Area Transport Strategy (2020).
[geo-urban-s8.13.4] Housing for All targets are covered under issue 1, and the emissions targets under issue 3.
[geo-urban-s8.13.5] Data centres and electricity demand are covered under issue 11.
