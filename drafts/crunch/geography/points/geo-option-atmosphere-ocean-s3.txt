TARGET geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3: 3. Exchanges of water between oceans and atmosphere
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.b] Water is constantly moving between the oceans, the air and the land. This continuous circulation is the hydrological (water) cycle, and it is the starting point for understanding how the oceans and atmosphere exchange water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.0] The cycle is a closed system, meaning nothing enters or leaves: the total amount of water on earth stays the same, and water only moves between stores (the places where it is held).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.1] Two forces drive the movement: solar energy, which powers evaporation (liquid water turning to vapour), and gravity, which pulls water down as precipitation (rain, snow and so on) and runoff (water flowing over the land).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.2] Diagram: draw a labelled cycle from ocean to atmosphere to land and back, with the stores drawn as boxes and the flows as arrows.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.1.b] A store is a place where water is held for a time. The oceans dwarf all the others, and only a tiny part of the earth's water is fresh liquid water at the surface.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.1.0] Oceans hold about 97% of the earth's water; ice sheets and glaciers hold about 2%, most of it in Antarctica; groundwater (water stored underground in soil and rock) holds under 1%.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.1.1] Lakes, rivers, soil moisture, the atmosphere and living things together hold only a tiny fraction. The atmosphere holds about 0.001% of the earth's water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.1.2] About 2.5-3% of all water is fresh (not salty), and most of that is frozen, so liquid fresh surface water is a very small part of the total.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.b] Flows are the processes that move water from one store to another. Each has its own name, and the ocean-atmosphere exchange depends on the first few.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.0] Evaporation: liquid water turns to vapour (water as an invisible gas), mainly from the ocean because the oceans are by far the biggest store.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.1] Transpiration: vapour is released from plant leaves; evapotranspiration is transpiration and evaporation combined over land.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.2] Sublimation: ice turns directly into vapour without first melting.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.3] Advection: wind carries water vapour horizontally from sea to land, so rain can fall far from where the water evaporated.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.4] Condensation: vapour turns to liquid droplets, forming cloud, fog or dew, for example when air cools.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.2.5] Precipitation, interception (rain caught by leaves), infiltration (soaking into the soil), surface runoff, throughflow (water moving sideways through soil) and groundwater flow return water to the sea. There is more on these drainage-basin flows in the chapter Rivers - Fluvial Processes and Landforms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.3.b] The oceans are both the main source and the main destination of the water that moves through the air. This shows how much of the cycle happens over the sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.3.0] Roughly 85-90% of global evaporation comes from the oceans, and roughly three-quarters of global precipitation falls on them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.3.1] Evaporation over the oceans exceeds the rain falling back on them, so the surplus is carried over the land by wind, and returns to the sea through rivers and groundwater.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.4.b] Residence time is how long a water molecule stays in a store before moving on. It differs enormously between stores.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.4.0] Water stays in the atmosphere for only about 9 days on average, but in the ocean for thousands of years and in the Antarctic ice sheet for far longer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.4.1] The atmosphere's water is therefore recycled quickly, while ocean water changes slowly.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.b] The exchange is not the same everywhere. Where evaporation and precipitation differ, distinctive climates result, and human activity can change the balance.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.0] Evaporation exceeds precipitation over the subtropical oceans, which helps explain their high salinity (saltiness), because water leaves but the salt stays. There is more on this in The atmosphere and oceans as linked systems.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.1] Precipitation exceeds evaporation in the equatorial belt and in the mid-latitude westerly belt (the zone of prevailing westerly winds).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.2] Deforestation reduces evapotranspiration and so the recycling of rainfall; a large share of the Amazon's rain is water recycled from the forest by evapotranspiration.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.5.3] Global warming intensifies the water cycle. There is more on this in Climatic environments and climate change.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.b] Humidity describes how much water vapour the air holds. It matters because it decides whether air can form cloud, fog or dew.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.0] Humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air. Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapour in a volume of air, in g/m³.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.1] Relative humidity is the amount of water vapour present as a percentage of the most the air could hold at that temperature. Saturated air (air holding all it can) has a relative humidity of 100%.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.2] Warm air can hold about 7% more water vapour for each 1 °C rise in temperature, so warm air holds much more than cold air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.3] Cooling air raises its relative humidity without adding any water, because the most it can hold falls; warming lowers it. This is why relative humidity is usually highest at dawn and lowest in mid-afternoon.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.4] Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated as it cools; further cooling causes condensation. There is more on measuring humidity in The atmosphere and oceans as linked systems.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.7.b] The amount of vapour in the air affects how evaporation works, and so how the climate feels and how temperatures behave.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.7.0] High humidity in the tropics makes heat oppressive, because sweat evaporates slowly into air that is already full of vapour.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.7.1] Low humidity in deserts allows rapid evaporation and large daily temperature ranges (the difference between day and night temperature).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.b] Evaporation is the step that puts water into the atmosphere. Its rate depends on energy, on the state of the air and on whether there is water to evaporate.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.0] Evaporation is water molecules gaining enough energy to escape as vapour. It takes latent heat (hidden heat energy) from the surface, cooling it, and that energy is released again on condensation. There is more on this in Solar energy and its redistribution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.1] Higher temperature increases evaporation because it gives more energy; strong sunshine and low air pressure also increase it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.2] Drier air, with low relative humidity, increases evaporation because it can take up more vapour.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.3] Wind increases evaporation by removing moist air and replacing it with drier air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.4] A large supply of water, such as oceans, lakes or wet ground, allows evaporation to continue.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.9.b] Because evaporation needs heat, wind and water together, it is strongest in some parts of the world and weak in others.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.9.0] Evaporation is greatest over the subtropical oceans under clear skies and steady trade winds (the regular winds blowing towards the equator), and along warm currents such as the Gulf Stream; it is least over cold polar seas.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.9.1] Potential evapotranspiration (the water that could evaporate if supply were unlimited) far exceeds rainfall in hot deserts, which is the cause of drought. There is more on this in Climate and economic development.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.10.b] Condensation is the reverse of evaporation and is how clouds begin. Vapour will not simply turn to droplets in clean air, so it needs something to form on.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.10.0] Condensation is water vapour turning into liquid droplets when air cools to its dew point (the temperature at which it is saturated).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.10.1] Condensation needs condensation nuclei: tiny particles of dust, sea salt, smoke or pollen around which droplets form.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.b] Air only condenses when it is cooled to dew point, so the question is what cools it. There are four ways.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.0] Adiabatic cooling (cooling caused by expansion, with no heat lost) of rising air is the main cause of cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.1] Contact cooling by radiation occurs when air touches ground that has lost heat at night.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.2] Advection cooling occurs when warm, moist air moves over a cold surface.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.11.3] Mixing of air masses of different temperatures can bring air to dew point, because the blend is cooler than the warm air alone.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.b] Rising air cools even though no heat is taken from it. Understanding why explains cloud height and the rates at which air cools.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.0] Rising air expands because pressure falls with height, and it cools without losing heat to its surroundings; sinking air is compressed and warms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.1] Unsaturated air cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate (the rate of cooling with height), about 10 °C per 1,000 m.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.2] Once condensation begins, the latent heat released slows the cooling to the saturated adiabatic lapse rate, about 5-7 °C per 1,000 m, which varies with temperature.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.3] The height at which condensation begins is the condensation level, or cloud base.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.b] Whether rising air keeps rising depends on how its temperature compares with the air around it. This decides whether the sky gives towering storm cloud or flat layer cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.0] Unstable air: if rising air stays warmer than its surroundings, because the environment cools faster than the rising air does, it keeps rising and forms towering cumulus, showers and thunderstorms. The environmental lapse rate (the cooling of the surrounding air with height) averages about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.1] Stable air: if rising air becomes cooler than its surroundings, it sinks back, giving layer cloud or clear skies.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.2] A temperature inversion, with warmer air above cooler air, is very stable because the cooler air below cannot rise, and it traps fog and pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.13.3] Diagram: a temperature-height graph comparing the environmental and adiabatic lapse rates.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.b] Condensation also happens at ground level, where it gives dew, frost and fog. The type depends on how the air is cooled.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.0] Dew forms on surfaces cooled below dew point on clear, calm nights.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.1] Hoar frost forms when the dew point is below 0 °C, as vapour turns straight to ice crystals.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.2] Radiation fog forms on clear, calm nights, usually under anticyclones (areas of high pressure) in autumn and winter, in valleys and hollows; it burns off in the morning sun.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.3] Advection fog forms when warm, moist air moves over a cold sea or land. The Grand Banks off Newfoundland, Canada, are among the foggiest places on earth, where warm moist air from over the Gulf Stream crosses the cold Labrador Current.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.4] San Francisco's summer fogs are also advection fog: they form where moist Pacific air crosses the cold California Current.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.14.5] Hill fog is cloud on high ground, where air is forced up and cools; smog is fog mixed with smoke.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.b] Clouds form wherever air is lifted and cooled below its dew point. The ways air is forced upward give five causes of cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.0] Clouds are visible masses of tiny water droplets or ice crystals, formed when rising air cools below its dew point.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.1] Convection: uplift from surface heating, which warms the air so that it rises.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.2] Orographic (relief) uplift: air is forced up over high ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.3] Frontal uplift: warm air rises over cold air at a front (the boundary between two air masses).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.4] Convergence: air streams meet and, with nowhere else to go, are forced upward, as at the ITCZ (the Intertropical Convergence Zone, where the trade winds meet).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.5] Turbulence: mixing of air that lifts it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.b] Cloud names are built from a few Latin words that describe shape, height and rain. Once you know them, a name tells you what the cloud looks like and what weather it brings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.0] Luke Howard, an English pharmacist and amateur meteorologist, proposed the Latin cloud names cirrus, cumulus, stratus and nimbus in 1803.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.1] Cirrus means wispy and fibrous, a curl of hair; cumulus means heaped and puffy, a heap; stratus means a layered sheet.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.2] Nimbus or nimbo- means rain-bearing; alto- means middle level.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.16.3] The WMO's International Cloud Atlas sets out the ten main cloud types (the WMO is the World Meteorological Organization).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.17.b] High clouds sit above about 6,000 m and are made of ice crystals. All three types here are thin, wispy or veil-like clouds.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.17.0] Cirrus: thin wisps, often called mares' tails; fair weather, but often the first sign of an approaching warm front.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.17.1] Cirrostratus: a thin milky veil that produces a halo round the sun or moon; rain is likely within hours.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.17.2] Cirrocumulus: small ripples, known as a mackerel sky because the pattern looks like fish scales.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.18.b] Middle clouds sit between about 2,000 m and 6,000 m, which is why their names begin with alto-, meaning middle level.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.18.0] Altocumulus: grey or white patches in rolls.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.18.1] Altostratus: a grey sheet through which the sun shows as if through frosted glass; it thickens ahead of a warm front.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.19.b] Low clouds are the ones that most often sit over Ireland and bring its drizzle and steady rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.19.0] Stratus: a grey, featureless layer, so it has no towering shape, that produces drizzle and hill fog.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.19.1] Stratocumulus: a lumpy grey layer, the commonest cloud over Ireland, usually dry or giving light drizzle.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.19.2] Nimbostratus: a thick, dark rain cloud giving long periods of steady rain or snow at warm fronts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.b] Some clouds do not stay in one layer. Their base is low but their tops reach high, because strong rising air builds them upward.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.0] Cumulus: fair-weather heaps with flat bases on sunny days, which can grow into showers if the air keeps rising.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.1] Cumulonimbus: a towering cloud with an anvil-shaped top spreading at the tropopause (the top of the lowest layer of the atmosphere), bringing heavy showers, hail, thunder and lightning, and squalls (sudden strong gusts).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.2] Heights are approximate for mid-latitudes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.b] Clouds appear in a predictable order as a front approaches, so they can be used to forecast weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.0] Ahead of a warm front, cirrus is followed by cirrostratus, altostratus and nimbostratus in turn. There is more on this in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.1] Cumulonimbus marks a cold front.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.2] Diagram: a chart of the ten cloud types arranged by height.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.22.b] Clouds affect temperature on the ground as well as producing rain, and the effect depends on the type of cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.22.0] Low thick clouds cool the surface by reflecting sunlight; high thin clouds warm it by trapping long-wave radiation (heat given off by the earth); cloudy nights are milder.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.23.b] Cloud is not the same as rain. Droplets must first grow, by one of two processes, before they can fall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.23.0] Cloud droplets are far too small to fall; they must grow about a million times in volume to form a raindrop heavy enough to reach the ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.24.b] In cold clouds, ice crystals grow at the expense of water droplets. This is the main way rain forms in Ireland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.24.0] In clouds colder than 0 °C, supercooled droplets (liquid droplets still unfrozen below 0 °C) and ice crystals exist together. Vapour is deposited on the ice crystals, which grow at the droplets' expense, become heavy and fall as snow, melting into rain below the freezing level.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.24.1] This Bergeron-Findeisen process is the main one in mid-latitude clouds, including Ireland's.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.25.b] In clouds that are never cold enough for ice, droplets grow by colliding with each other instead.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.25.0] In warm clouds, which are above 0 °C throughout and are common in the tropics, larger droplets fall faster, collide with and absorb smaller ones (coalesce) until they are heavy enough to fall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.b] Precipitation can fall as liquid or ice. The form depends on the size of the drops and on the temperature of the air they fall through.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.0] Rain: liquid drops, such as the steady rain from nimbostratus.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.1] Drizzle: very small drops falling from stratus.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.2] Snow: ice crystals clumped as flakes, falling when the air is near or below freezing to the ground.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.3] Sleet: melting snow, or rain and snow mixed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.4] Hail: layered ice balls formed when strong updrafts in cumulonimbus carry frozen drops up and down repeatedly, adding layers until they are too heavy; hail is mostly a warm-season phenomenon.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.5] Freezing rain and glaze: rain that freezes on contact with cold surfaces.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.b] Convectional rain comes from air that is heated from below and rises. It is the daily rain of the equator.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.0] 1. The ground is heated strongly, warming the air above it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.1] 2. The warm air expands, rises and cools adiabatically.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.2] 3. When it reaches dew point, cumulus and then cumulonimbus clouds form.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.3] 4. Heavy showers, often with thunder, follow, usually in the afternoon, because that is when the ground is hottest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.4] This type is typical of the equatorial belt every day, and of Irish summer afternoons occasionally, especially inland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.5] Diagram: a column of rising air above heated ground, topped by cumulonimbus.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.b] Relief rain forms when moist air is forced up over mountains. It leaves a wet windward side and a dry leeward side.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.0] 1. Moist air from the sea is forced to rise over a mountain barrier.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.1] 2. It cools, condenses and gives heavy rain on the windward slope (the side facing the wind).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.2] 3. On the leeward side (the sheltered side) the air descends, is compressed and warms, so clouds evaporate and a rain shadow (a dry area) results.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.3] 4. A warm, dry föhn-type wind may blow down the lee side. There is more on this in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.4] Ireland's west coast mountains receive this rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.5] In India, the Western Ghats force the Arabian Sea branch of the monsoon to rise, giving heavy rain on the Konkan and Malabar coasts, with the Deccan in rain shadow; Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in the Khasi Hills, Meghalaya, are among the wettest places on earth.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.28.6] Diagram: a cross-section of a mountain barrier showing the windward wet slope and the leeward rain shadow.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.b] Frontal rain falls where warm air meets cold air in a depression (a low-pressure weather system). It is Ireland's main source of rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.0] 1. In a depression, warm, moist tropical air is forced to rise over cold polar air at the warm front, giving gentle ascent and long, steady rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.1] 2. At the cold front the warm air is undercut, causing sharp ascent and short, heavy showers.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.2] Frontal rain is Ireland's main source of rain. There is more on depressions in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.30.b] Rain also falls where winds meet, because air that cannot go sideways is forced upward.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.30.0] Where air streams meet they are forced to rise, as where the trade winds converge at the ITCZ, giving heavy rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.b] World rainfall follows the pressure belts, which are bands of rising or sinking air arranged by latitude. Rising air gives rain and sinking air gives dry conditions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.0] Equatorial belt (about 0-10°): heavy rain all year, over 2,000 mm a year, from low pressure, rising air, convergence at the ITCZ, convection and high evaporation from warm seas and forests. Manaus, central Amazon, Brazil, receives about 2,300 mm a year.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.1] Tropics with a wet and dry season (about 10-20°, savanna and monsoon lands): rain falls in summer when the ITCZ moves over them, and the winter is dry.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.2] Subtropical high-pressure belts (about 20-30°): very low rainfall and the world's great hot deserts, from sinking, warming, stable air and clear skies.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.3] The Atacama, Chile, is the driest non-polar desert on earth, and the Namib receives much of its moisture as fog from the cold Benguela Current.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.4] Mid-latitudes (about 40-60°): moderate to heavy rain from depressions in the westerlies, wettest on west coasts facing onshore winds (Ireland), decreasing into continental interiors.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.5] Polar regions: very low precipitation, since cold air holds little vapour and sinking air lies under the polar high pressure. Antarctica is the driest continent, a cold desert.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.31.6] Diagram: a pole-to-pole profile of rainfall under the three-cell model (the pattern of three circulation cells in each hemisphere).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.b] The latitude pattern is changed locally by five factors, each of which can make a place wetter or drier than its latitude suggests.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.0] Relief and rain shadow: the leeward side of a mountain barrier receives much less rain. There is more on this in the block on relief (orographic) rainfall.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.1] Distance from the sea: continental interiors are dry because they lie far from the moisture of the oceans.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.2] Prevailing winds: onshore winds bring rain, while offshore winds bring dry air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.3] Ocean currents: warm currents add moisture and instability; cold currents chill the air and make it stable, so rain is rare though fog is common on the coasts named above.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.32.4] Seasonal migration: the pressure belts move north and south with the overhead sun, shifting the rain belts with them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.b] A rainfall regime is the pattern of rain through the year. Five regimes are worth knowing.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.0] Equatorial: rain all year because the air is warm and rising every day, often peaking after the equinoxes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.1] Monsoon: a very wet summer and a dry winter, as in India.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.2] Mediterranean: a wet winter and a dry summer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.3] Continental interiors: a summer maximum, because summer heating drives convection.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.4] Irish (oceanic): rain all year, with a maximum in autumn and winter.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.b] Ireland shows how wind, depressions and relief combine to make the west wet and the east drier.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.0] The west is wetter than the east, the mountains are wettest, and the west has more wet days, because moist Atlantic air arrives there first.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.1] Annual rainfall is about 750 mm in the driest parts of the east, around Dublin, about 1,000-1,250 mm across much of the west, and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.2] Wet days number about 150 a year on the east and south-east coasts, and up to about 225 a year in parts of the west.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.34.3] Causes: prevailing south-westerly winds, Atlantic depressions reaching the west first, relief uplift on the western mountains, and the rain shadow in the east.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.35.b] Variability is how much rainfall changes from year to year. It is a problem for farming and water supply.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.35.0] Rainfall is least reliable where totals are lowest. In the Sahel, annual rainfall is roughly 200-600 mm, falling in one short summer wet season when the ITCZ moves north.
