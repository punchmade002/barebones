TARGET geo-option-global-interdependence-s1: 1. Views of development and underdevelopment
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.b] People disagree about what 'development' means, and so about what counts as progress and who gets to set the standard. Every model in this subsection starts from a different answer.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.0] Economic development means growth in a country's output and income (the goods and services it produces and the money earned), usually measured by GNP per head (gross national product, the total income of a country's people, divided by the population).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.1] Human development means health, education, freedom and choice: people's ability to live lives they value, whatever the size of the national income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.2] Sustainable development means meeting today's needs without harming the future, so growth must not use up resources or damage the environment that later generations will need.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.3] Who defines development matters: definitions are usually set by rich countries and international institutions, which decides which goals and measures count.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.4] Measures follow views: GNP per head reflects the growth view and the HDI (Human Development Index, a score built from health, education and income) reflects the human view. There is more on this in the Economic Development notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.0.5] 'Underdevelopment' is itself a loaded word with two meanings. It can mean 'not yet developed', the modernisation view (poor countries are behind on a common path), or 'made poor by others', the dependency view (poor countries were held back by richer ones). Both meanings must be explained.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.b] Ideas about development have not stood still. Each era produced its own model and its own goals, usually reacting to the failures of the one before.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.0] Colonial era: colonies were treated as suppliers of raw materials for the ruling country, so their wealth flowed out, and colonial rule was justified by a 'civilising mission' (the claim that Europeans were bringing progress to others).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.1] 1950s–60s: growth and modernisation. Because the aim was catching up, development meant GNP growth and industrialisation, following Rostow's model (covered below).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.2] 1960s–70s: dependency theory emerged in Latin America, arguing that poverty was caused by the structure of the world economy rather than by the poor countries themselves.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.3] 1970s: the basic needs approach, a reaction to growth that left the poor behind, put food, water, shelter, health and education ahead of GNP growth.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.4] 1980s–90s: the neoliberal Washington Consensus (neoliberal means trusting free markets over state control). The term was coined by the economist John Williamson in 1989 for market reforms promoted by the IMF, World Bank and US Treasury.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.5] These reforms were privatisation (selling state-owned firms to private owners), free trade and cuts in state spending.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.6] From 1990: human development, measured by the HDI, and the idea of development as freedom.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.7] From 1987: sustainable development, which links growth to environmental limits because resources are finite.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.1.8] 2000s–2010s: goal-based views (the Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals, sets of targets agreed by the UN) and rights-based views of development (development as people's entitlement).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.b] Environmental determinism is the oldest idea here. It holds that the physical environment, especially climate, decides how far a society can develop.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.0] Temperate climates (mild, moderate temperatures) were said to produce energetic, advanced peoples, while tropical climates were said to produce poor and backward societies.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.1] The theory was developed by 19th- and early 20th-century geographers. Ellen Churchill Semple, in Influences of Geographic Environment (1911), and Ellsworth Huntington, in Civilization and Climate (1915), argued that climate determined how 'civilised' a people could become.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.2] It was used to justify colonialism and racial hierarchies (rankings of peoples), presenting European rule as the natural order.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.3] Critique: it ignores history, including colonialism, slavery and trade rules, as well as politics and people's choices.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.4] Critique: it is contradicted by Singapore, which is tropical yet one of the world's richest countries, and by poverty in temperate places.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.5] Critique: its assumptions about the abilities of different peoples were racist, and they are rejected today.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.2.6] Reply: possibilism, associated with the French geographer Paul Vidal de la Blache, holds that the environment offers possibilities and people choose which to use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.b] Few geographers now say the environment decides everything, but some still argue that physical geography shapes development. This version keeps the idea without the racism.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.0] Jeffrey Sachs, in The End of Poverty (2005), stressed malaria, poor soils and a landlocked location (no coast, so no easy access to sea trade) as obstacles that aid could help overcome.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.1] Jared Diamond, in Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997), argued that geography (crops, animals and the east–west axis of Eurasia) explains why some societies gained power first.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.2] Botswana is landlocked, yet it became independent in 1966 as one of the world's poorest countries. Diamonds and stable government made it an upper-middle-income country, so physical limits can be overcome.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.3.3] Physical factors raise the costs of development without fixing its outcome, which is why Botswana could succeed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.b] Modernisation theory holds that all societies pass along one path from traditional to modern, as Western Europe and North America did. Poor countries are behind and need investment, technology, industry and Western values to catch up.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.0] Walt W. Rostow, an American economist and adviser to Presidents Kennedy and Johnson, set out his five-stage model in The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-Communist Manifesto (1960).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.1] Stage 1, traditional society: because output is low, people live on subsistence farming (growing food mainly for the family's own use) and limited technology.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.2] Stage 2, preconditions for take-off: investment in transport and education, new ideas, and trade opening to outside contact.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.3] Stage 3, take-off: investment rises to over 10% of national income and a few leading industries grow fast.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.4] Stage 4, drive to maturity: growth spreads across the economy and diversifies into many industries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.5] Stage 5, age of high mass consumption: because the economy is mature, most people enjoy cars, durable goods (long-lasting items such as appliances) and services.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.4.6] Rostow dated Britain's take-off to the late 18th century (about 1783–1802). He is not used to date any other country's take-off.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.b] Applying Rostow to Ireland is a textbook interpretation, not Rostow's own work. The model can be applied to Irish development as follows.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.0] Traditional stage: a farming economy with heavy emigration, including net emigration (more people leaving than arriving) of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.1] Preconditions: T. K. Whitaker's Economic Development and the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958), and an opening to foreign investment, with the IDA (the Industrial Development Authority, which attracts foreign firms) set up in 1949.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.2] Take-off: industrial growth in the 1960s and 1970s, free secondary education from 1967, and EEC (the European Economic Community, now the EU) membership from 1 January 1973.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.3] Drive to maturity: growth in the 1980s and 1990s led by multinational companies (firms operating in many countries).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.4] High mass consumption: the Celtic Tiger, from the mid-1990s to 2007.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.5.5] Limits of the model: the crash from 2008 and the EU–IMF bailout of November 2010 show that the stages can be reversed.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.b] South Korea is an East Asian success story whose industrialisation seems to fit Rostow's stages, because it climbed from poverty to wealth within decades.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.0] The Korean War (1950–53) left South Korea among the world's poorest countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.1] Export-led industrialisation (building factories to sell goods abroad) was planned under Park Chung-hee from the 1960s.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.2] Large family conglomerates (chaebol), such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG, led the growth, and heavy investment in education supplied skilled workers.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.3] South Korea joined the OECD (a club of mainly wealthy countries) in 1996 and was reclassified as a developed country by UNCTAD (the UN body for trade and development) in 2021.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.6.4] Its rapid industrialisation seems to fit Rostow's stages, which is why it is used as evidence for the model.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.b] Modernisation has been widely criticised, because many countries did not follow the predicted path. Each criticism below is a separate point to make in an evaluation.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.0] Eurocentric (judging everything from a European viewpoint): it assumes the Western path is the only path and that Western society is the goal.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.1] Linear and stage-bound: countries can skip, stall or go backwards. Zimbabwe, with land seizures from 2000 and hyperinflation (prices rising out of control) peaking in 2008, and Syria, where civil war from 2011 reversed decades of progress, show this.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.2] It ignores colonialism and unequal trade as causes of poverty, treating poor countries as simply late starters.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.3] It assumes wealth will trickle down from growth to the poor, which often does not happen, because owners and governments keep most of the gain.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.4] It is growth-centred and environmentally unsustainable: mass consumption for everyone is impossible on current resources.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.5] It ignores culture, inequality and gender, so growth alone can leave women and minorities behind.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.7.6] Many African states that followed modernisation advice did not take off.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.8.b] Modernisation was not only an idea. It shaped what governments and international bodies actually did, and it was top-down: decided from above rather than by local people.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.8.0] Large prestige projects, such as dams and steelworks, were built to bring industry quickly, and were often built with little local participation.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.8.1] The Washington Consensus described above applied the same top-down thinking through markets, imposing reforms from outside.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.8.2] Debt and aid followed these policies, because poor countries borrowed to pay for such projects. There is more on this in the Empowering People notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.b] Dependency theory turns modernisation upside down. It holds that poor countries are poor because of how they were drawn into the world economy, not because of their own backwardness.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.0] The rich core (metropolis) countries develop by draining wealth from the poor periphery (satellite) countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.1] Development of the core and underdevelopment of the periphery are two sides of one process, because the core's wealth and the periphery's poverty are linked.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.2] Origins: Latin American economists and thinkers. Raúl Prebisch, an Argentine economist, headed the UN Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLA), set up in 1948.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.3] With Hans Singer, Prebisch argued in 1950 that the terms of trade (the price of exports compared with the price of imports) of primary-product exporters (sellers of raw materials and farm produce) tend to decline. This is the Prebisch–Singer thesis.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.4] Andre Gunder Frank, in The Development of Underdevelopment (1966), argued that Latin America was underdeveloped by its links with the core, through metropolis–satellite chains.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.9.5] Dependency is structural, through trade, investment and debt. It is not the same as dependence on aid.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.b] Dependency theorists point to specific mechanisms that keep the periphery poor. Each one is a separate point you can make.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.0] Colonial extraction of raw materials, using slavery where needed, moved wealth from colonies to the ruling country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.1] Dependence on one or two primary exports (raw materials or farm produce) whose prices are low and unstable, so incomes are low and hard to plan.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.2] Tariff escalation, where rich countries tax processed goods more heavily than raw materials, which keeps poor countries exporting raw materials, and rich-country subsidies (government payments to their own producers) that undercut producers elsewhere.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.3] Profit repatriation: multinationals send profits home, so the money does not stay in the host country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.4] Debt repayments drain income, so less is left to fund development.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.5] Brain drain: skilled people leave for the core, which means the periphery loses the doctors, engineers and teachers it trained.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.10.6] Local elites who benefit from the link with the core and resist change.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.b] Commodities are raw materials and farm products sold on world markets. These examples show countries tied to the core through one or two of them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.0] For example, Brazil's colonial economy moved through export cycles of brazilwood, sugar, gold, coffee and rubber, each tied to demand in the core.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.1] Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grow over half of the world's cocoa, mostly exported as beans rather than processed chocolate, so the higher-value processing happens elsewhere.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.2] Zambia depends on copper, which is most of its exports, so its income rises and falls with one commodity.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.11.3] Sudan's Gezira Scheme, from 1925, grew cotton for the Lancashire mills, so the farmland served the core's factories.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.b] Wallerstein developed dependency ideas into a three-part picture of the world, which allows for countries that change position.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.0] Immanuel Wallerstein's The Modern World-System (first volume 1974) divides the world into core, semi-periphery and periphery.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.1] Core countries control high-value production and finance, so they keep the biggest share of the profit. Periphery countries supply cheap raw materials and labour.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.2] Semi-periphery countries sit between the two, and countries can move between zones. South Korea and China have moved up.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.12.3] China began reforms in 1978, joined the WTO (World Trade Organization) in 2001 and has been the world's second-largest economy since 2010.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.b] Dependency theory explains some poverty well, but later events exposed its weaknesses.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.0] It cannot explain how the Asian Tigers (South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore) and China developed through trade with the core.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.1] Countries that cut their links with the world economy through self-reliance (depending on their own resources) stayed poor.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.2] It blames outside forces, so it can excuse corruption and poor government at home.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.13.3] The remedy, withdrawal from world trade, has rarely worked, because countries lose markets and technology.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.b] In the 1970s some thinkers turned away from GNP growth and asked what poor people actually needed, and what technology would suit them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.0] The basic needs approach of the 1970s put food, water, shelter, health and education first, not GNP growth.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.1] The ILO World Employment Conference (1976, held by the International Labour Organization, the UN body for work) put basic needs at the centre of development policy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.2] E. F. Schumacher's Small Is Beautiful (1973) argued for small-scale, appropriate technology that local people can afford, use and repair.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.14.3] Schumacher founded the Intermediate Technology Development Group in 1966, now called Practical Action.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.15.b] The human view judges a country by how people live, not by how much it produces.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.15.0] Amartya Sen, an Indian economist and Nobel Prize winner in Economics (1998), argued in Development as Freedom (1999) that development means widening people's choices and capabilities (what people are able to do and be).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.15.1] The first Human Development Report (by UNDP, the UN Development Programme) appeared in 1990. The HDI was created by Mahbub ul Haq with Sen. There is more on how it works in the Economic Development notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.15.2] The human view judges progress by people's lives, not by national output alone.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.16.b] Two further alternatives change who decides and what is protected: local people take part in planning, and growth stays within environmental limits.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.16.0] Bottom-up (grassroots) development puts participation, co-operatives (businesses owned by their members), NGOs (non-governmental organisations, such as charities) and women's groups at the centre, in contrast to top-down projects. There is more on this in the Empowering People notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.16.1] The Brundtland Report, Our Common Future (1987), set out sustainable development. There is more on its application in the Sustainable Development notes.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.16.2] The Sustainable Development Goals are 17 goals adopted in 2015, with a target year of 2030.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.17.b] If development is more than money, then GNP is the wrong ruler. These two proposals measure or describe progress differently.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.17.0] Bhutan's Gross National Happiness was put forward in the 1970s by King Jigme Singye Wangchuck as a goal ahead of GNP.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.17.1] Hans Rosling, a Swedish doctor and statistician, with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund in Factfulness (2018), argued that dividing the world into 'developed' and 'developing' is out of date and replaced it with four income levels. Most people now live in the middle-income levels.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.b] The pictures people see shape what they believe about a place. Media and charity images of Africa have been narrow, and that has consequences.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.0] Famine, war, disease and helpless victims, especially children, dominate media and charity images of Africa. Good news and ordinary life are rarely shown.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.1] Stereotypes follow, such as the belief that 'Africa is a country', when it is a continent of many different countries.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.2] Donors can take a 'saviour' view of themselves, and the people shown can come to see themselves as helpless.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.3] Investors and tourists may lose confidence in countries shown only as crisis zones, so the images can cost those countries income.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.18.4] Audiences can tire of the same images, leading to donor fatigue (people giving less because the appeals feel repetitive).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.b] Coverage of this famine shows both the power and the cost of one image of Africa. It raised huge sums but also shaped how a whole continent was seen.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.0] A BBC television report by Michael Buerk in October 1984 brought the famine to world attention.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.1] Band Aid's single 'Do They Know It's Christmas?' (1984) and the Live Aid concerts (July 1985), organised by Bob Geldof, raised huge sums.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.2] The famine came during civil war in northern Ethiopia, and government policies worsened it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.19.3] The powerful coverage fixed an image of Africa as helpless and dependent, and hid the political causes: civil war and government policy.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.b] Aid agencies, writers and speakers have pushed back against the single story of crisis.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.0] Dóchas, the Irish association of development NGOs, drew up a Code of Conduct on Images and Messages that its member organisations sign.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.1] The code requires images to respect the dignity of the people shown, avoid stereotypes and tell the full context.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.2] NGOs use dignified images and the people's own voices in their appeals, so the people shown speak for themselves.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.3] Kenyan writer Binyavanga Wainaina's satirical essay 'How to Write About Africa' (Granta, 2005) mocked Western clichés about the continent.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.20.4] Nigerian novelist Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's talk 'The Danger of a Single Story' (2009) argued that a single story about a place distorts it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.21.b] Positive examples from sub-Saharan Africa (the part of the continent south of the Sahara desert) replace the image of helplessness with specific achievements.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.21.0] Rwanda: after the genocide from 7 April 1994, in which about 800,000 people, mostly Tutsi, were killed in about 100 days, the country rebuilt. Since the 2013 election women have held over 60% of the seats in the lower house of parliament, the highest share of any country.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.21.1] Kenya: M-Pesa, a mobile-phone money transfer service launched in 2007 by Safaricom (part-owned by Vodafone), lets people without bank accounts send, receive and save money by text message. Most Kenyan adults use it.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.21.2] Botswana: its growth from diamonds under stable government is the counter-image to the landlocked-state argument above.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.b] Words used for poorer countries carry hidden assumptions. A loaded term suggests a judgement while sounding neutral.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.0] 'Underdeveloped' and 'backward' imply that poor societies are lagging behind a normal path.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.1] 'Primitive' implies that poor societies are at an earlier stage of human history.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.2] 'Developing' implies one direction of travel, towards the Western model, so it assumes every country wants the same goal.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.3] 'Less economically developed country' (LEDC) still measures other countries against the developed norm.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.4] 'Global South' groups countries by their position in the world economy rather than by wealth.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.5] 'Majority world' is a term popularised by the Bangladeshi photographer Shahidul Alam, because most of the world's people live there.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.22.6] The UN created the 'least developed countries' category in 1971. Most of its members are in sub-Saharan Africa, and it is an official term.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.b] These labels began as politics, not as a ranking of wealth. Their meaning drifted after the Cold War (the rivalry between the USA and the Soviet Union) ended.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.0] 'Third World' was coined by the French demographer Alfred Sauvy in 1952, by comparison with the 'Third Estate' of the French Revolution (the ordinary people, outside the clergy and nobility).
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.1] It was originally a Cold War political term, not a ranking of wealth. The First World was the USA-led capitalist West and the Second World the Soviet-led communist bloc.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.2] The Third World was the rest: mostly newly independent former colonies that did not align with either bloc.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.3] The Bandung Conference, Indonesia, in April 1955 brought together leaders of newly independent Asian and African states. The Non-Aligned Movement (countries refusing to join either bloc) was founded at Belgrade in 1961.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.4] The Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991, ending the Second World and leaving the terms without their original meaning.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.23.5] The terms became a ranking, with first meaning best, and a byword for poverty, because people forgot their Cold War origin. They lump together very different countries, from Singapore to the poorest states.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.b] The North–South model divides the rich, industrialised North from the poor South. It replaced the three worlds as the main way of picturing global inequality.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.0] The Brandt Report, North–South: A Programme for Survival (1980), came from a commission chaired by the former West German Chancellor Willy Brandt.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.1] The Brandt Line runs roughly along 30°N and dips south to include Australia and New Zealand in the North.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.2] The Brandt Line is a 1980 concept, not a current UN or World Bank classification.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.3] The gap in income, health and power between most of Africa and Europe remains very large, which is why the idea still has some use.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.4] World Inequality Report 2022: the richest 10% of people own about 76% of global wealth, and the poorest half about 2%. Poverty and deprivation also exist within the North.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.24.5] Global institutions remain weighted towards wealthy states: the five permanent members of the UN Security Council (the body with power to decide on international action) are the USA, the UK, France, Russia and China.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.b] Since 1980 many countries south of the Brandt Line have grown rich or powerful, so a simple two-part split no longer fits the world.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.0] Many 'South' countries are now high-income or industrial powers, such as Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan and Hong Kong, so 'South' no longer means poor.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.1] China is the world's second-largest economy, and India is among the five largest.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.2] The BRIC term (Brazil, Russia, India, China) was coined in 2001 by Jim O'Neill of Goldman Sachs, and the first BRIC summit was held in 2009. South Africa joined in 2011, forming BRICS. Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the United Arab Emirates joined in January 2024, and Indonesia in January 2025.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.3] The G20, set up in 1999, includes China, India, Brazil, South Africa, Indonesia, Mexico and Saudi Arabia, giving the South a stronger voice.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.4] Most of the world's people now live in middle-income countries, as Hans Rosling showed in Factfulness, so a rich/poor split misdescribes them.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.25.5] In 2016 the World Bank announced that it would stop dividing countries into 'developing' and 'developed' in its main data tables, keeping its four income groups.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.26.b] Eurocentric thinking sees the world from a European point of view and judges other societies by European standards, as if Europe's path, values and history were the norm. Maps show it clearly.
[geo-option-global-interdependence-s1.26.0] The Mercator projection (a way of flattening the globe onto a map) was designed by the Flemish map-maker Gerardus Mercator in 1569 for navigation, because lines of constant compass bearing are straight on it. Its effect is to place Europe at the top centre of the world map.
