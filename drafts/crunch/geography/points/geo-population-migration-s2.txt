TARGET geo-population-migration-s2: 2. Overpopulation
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.b] Overpopulation is about the balance between people and what they have to live on, not about how crowded a place looks. Two places with the same number of people can be very differently placed.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.0] Overpopulation: the population of an area is too large for its resources, technology and economy to support an acceptable standard of living.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.1] It is a balance between people and resources, not a matter of numbers or density (people per square kilometre) alone.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.2] Underpopulation: too few people to develop the resources fully, so some resources go unused.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.3] Optimum population: the number that gives the highest standard of living with existing resources and technology.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.4] The Netherlands has over 500 people per km² (2020s) and is densely populated but not overpopulated, because its wealth, technology and trade let it support them well.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.5] The Sahel (the dry belt south of the Sahara) is thinly populated but overpopulated, because its fragile resources, drought and poverty cannot support its people.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.6] Niger, a Sahel country, has about 27 million people (2023) in a very large, mostly desert country, so little of the land can feed anyone.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.b] Thinkers have long argued about whether population or resources win out. Three views are worth knowing, each with a date and a title.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.0] Malthus, 'An Essay on the Principle of Population' (1798): population grows geometrically (doubling again and again) while food grows arithmetically (by a fixed amount each time), so famine, disease and war act as positive checks that cut numbers back.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.1] Ireland's Great Famine is often cited in support of Malthus.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.2] Boserup, 'The Conditions of Agricultural Growth' (1965): population pressure drives farming innovation, so food supply can rise to meet demand.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.3] The Green Revolution (the spread of high-yield crops and modern farming from the mid-1960s) is often cited in support of Boserup.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.4] Club of Rome, 'The Limits to Growth' (1972): argues that growth is limited by finite resources.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.b] Resources are what a population lives on. Where they are not developed, are degraded or shrink, the same population becomes too many.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.0] Sahel: desertification (fertile land turning to desert), drought, overgrazing, fuelwood cutting and the shrinking of Lake Chad all cut what the land can support.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.1] Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s, because of irrigation demand and drought, which is a resource shrinking under the same population.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.2] Pre-Famine Ireland: over-reliance on one resource, the potato, and subdivision of holdings (farms split among sons) into ever-smaller plots, so many families had too little land.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.3] Developing resources raises carrying capacity (the number of people an area can support): India's Green Revolution and Ireland's industrial growth since the 1960s.
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.b] Ireland shows how one fragile resource can leave a large population exposed. Here the checked facts are the whole-island figures and the blight.
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.0] The population of the whole island was about 8.2 million in 1841; use this whole-island figure, not the 26-county one.
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.1] The rural poor depended heavily on the potato, so they had no other food when it failed.
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.2] Holdings were subdivided among sons into ever-smaller plots, and landlords were often absentee (living elsewhere).
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.3] Conacre meant renting a small plot for one season.
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.4] Potato blight (Phytophthora infestans, a disease of potato plants) struck in 1845, and the Great Famine ran from 1845 to 1849.
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.5] About 1 million people died and about 1 million emigrated, leaving a whole-island population of about 6.6 million by 1851.
[geo-population-migration-s2.4.b] Desertification (fertile land turning to desert) is the clearest case of resources shrinking under pressure. It happens because a poor, fast-growing population overuses a dry land.
[geo-population-migration-s2.4.0] The Sahel is a semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, from Senegal and Mauritania through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad to Sudan.
[geo-population-migration-s2.4.1] Rainfall is about 200–600 mm a year, falls in a short summer rainy season and is very unreliable, so crops and grass can fail.
[geo-population-migration-s2.4.2] Causes of desertification: overgrazing (too many animals for the grass), overcultivation with fallow periods (rests that let soil recover) abandoned, cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood, and drought.
[geo-population-migration-s2.4.3] Wind and rain then erode the bare soil, because nothing is left to hold it in place.
[geo-population-migration-s2.5.b] People and governments have tried to reverse desertification by holding water and soil in place and by bringing back trees.
[geo-population-migration-s2.5.0] The Great Green Wall, an African Union initiative launched in 2007, aims to restore a belt of land across the Sahel.
[geo-population-migration-s2.5.1] Stone lines (diguettes) along contours (lines of equal height) and zaï planting pits in Burkina Faso trap water and soil, so crops can grow on degraded ground.
[geo-population-migration-s2.5.2] Farmer-managed natural regeneration of trees in Niger lets farmers protect and regrow trees that spring from existing roots and stumps.
[geo-population-migration-s2.6.b] Attitudes and customs affect how many children people have, and so how fast a population grows against its resources.
[geo-population-migration-s2.6.0] Factors include religion and attitudes to contraception (methods of avoiding pregnancy), the status and education of women, early marriage, preference for sons, children as farm labour and old-age security, and the prestige of large families.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.b] Niger shows what happens when several of these cultural factors act together.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.0] Early marriage of girls is common, so women start having children young and have more of them over their lives.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.1] Female literacy is low, which limits women's choices and their use of family planning.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.2] Large families carry prestige, so having many children raises a family's standing.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.3] Total fertility rate (the average number of children a woman has in her lifetime) is about 6 (early 2020s), among the highest in the world, with population growth over 3% a year.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.4] Median age (the age that splits the population in half) is about 15, so the population is very young, and Niger is among the lowest-ranked countries in the UN Human Development Index (a ranking of countries by health, education and income) (2020s).
[geo-population-migration-s2.8.b] Within India, two states show how education and the status of women change family size.
[geo-population-migration-s2.8.0] Son preference and the low status of women in some Indian states keep families having children until they have sons.
[geo-population-migration-s2.8.1] Kerala: total fertility rate about 1.8 (National Family Health Survey 2019–21), literacy about 94% (Census 2011), the highest of any state, with high female literacy and good health care.
[geo-population-migration-s2.8.2] Bihar: total fertility rate about 3.0 (National Family Health Survey 2019–21), literacy about 62% (Census 2011), the lowest of any state.
[geo-population-migration-s2.8.3] India's total fertility rate is about 2.0 (2019–21), down from about 6 in the 1950s.
[geo-population-migration-s2.9.b] Ireland shows culture changing fertility within living memory, as law and attitudes shifted.
[geo-population-migration-s2.9.0] Before the 1970s, Catholic teaching, large families and illegal contraception kept fertility high.
[geo-population-migration-s2.9.1] Births peaked at about 74,000 in 1980.
[geo-population-migration-s2.9.2] Contraception was legalised by the Health (Family Planning) Act 1979, on prescription, so couples could plan family size legally.
[geo-population-migration-s2.9.3] The total fertility rate (average children per woman) was about 4 in the early 1970s, fell below replacement (the level at which each generation just replaces itself) around 1990 and was about 1.5 in 2023.
[geo-population-migration-s2.10.b] Income and family size are linked in both directions. Poor families tend to have more children, and large families make it harder to escape poverty.
[geo-population-migration-s2.10.0] Low-income countries have high birth rates: children are economic assets (they work and earn), infant mortality is high so parents have extra children, and there are no pensions, so children support parents in old age.
[geo-population-migration-s2.10.1] Rising incomes bring falling fertility, through the cost of raising children, women's paid work and education.
[geo-population-migration-s2.10.2] Poverty also limits the ability to develop resources or import food, so a poor country cannot easily make up for a shortage.
[geo-population-migration-s2.10.3] Contrast low-income Niger with high-income Ireland, the EU and Japan.
[geo-population-migration-s2.11.b] High-income countries show where rising incomes lead: low fertility, long lives and, in Japan, an ageing and shrinking population.
[geo-population-migration-s2.11.0] Ireland: total fertility rate about 1.5 (2023); life expectancy (average years a person lives) about 82–83 years.
[geo-population-migration-s2.11.1] EU: total fertility rate about 1.4 (2023).
[geo-population-migration-s2.11.2] Japan: about 29% aged 65 or over (2023); population falling from a peak of about 128 million around 2008–2010.
[geo-population-migration-s2.11.3] Bangladesh is an exception: its fertility fell sharply while it was still poor.
[geo-population-migration-s2.12.b] Bangladesh shows that fertility can fall without wealth when policy, education and women's work change.
[geo-population-migration-s2.12.0] Independent since 1971, so its fertility fall happened within a few decades of nationhood.
[geo-population-migration-s2.12.1] Total fertility rate fell, for example, from about 6–7 in the early 1970s to about 2 in the 2020s.
[geo-population-migration-s2.12.2] Reasons: a national family planning programme with community health workers visiting homes, NGOs (non-governmental organisations, such as charities), girls' education and millions of women employed in the garment industry.
[geo-population-migration-s2.12.3] Population density is over 1,100 people per km² (2020s), one of the highest of any large country; it is used here for its fertility fall, not as overpopulation.
[geo-population-migration-s2.13.b] Technology changes both sides of the balance. It lowers deaths, raises food output and lowers births, and where it is missing, it keeps carrying capacity low.
[geo-population-migration-s2.13.0] Medical technology (vaccines, antibiotics, clean water) cut death rates far faster than birth rates fell, causing the population explosion of the 20th century; this is DTM (demographic transition model) stage 2 in Changing population characteristics.
[geo-population-migration-s2.13.1] Penicillin (an antibiotic) was discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928.
[geo-population-migration-s2.13.2] Smallpox was declared eradicated by the World Health Organization in 1980.
[geo-population-migration-s2.13.3] Agricultural technology: the Green Revolution, with high-yield seeds, irrigation and fertilisers, increased carrying capacity.
[geo-population-migration-s2.13.4] Contraceptive technology lowered birth rates, because couples could choose family size.
[geo-population-migration-s2.13.5] Lack of technology: subsistence farming (growing food mainly for the family's own use) keeps carrying capacity low in the Sahel.
[geo-population-migration-s2.14.b] India shows technology raising carrying capacity, but with costs that you must also give.
[geo-population-migration-s2.14.0] Green Revolution from the mid-1960s: high-yield wheat bred in Mexico by Norman Borlaug, introduced in India under M. S. Swaminathan.
[geo-population-migration-s2.14.1] Food-grain output rose, for example, from about 51 million tonnes (1950–51) to about 330 million tonnes (2022–23).
[geo-population-migration-s2.14.2] Core areas: Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh.
[geo-population-migration-s2.14.3] Side effects: falling water tables (the underground water level, which drops when too much is pumped for irrigation), soil degradation and debt among small farmers.
[geo-population-migration-s2.15.b] Governments can try to change birth rates directly. Policies that aim to cut births are called anti-natalist, and they can succeed but carry a human cost.
[geo-population-migration-s2.15.0] India launched the world's first national family planning programme in 1952.
[geo-population-migration-s2.15.1] Forced sterilisations (operations that end a person's ability to have children) during the Emergency, 1975–77, caused a backlash against family planning.
[geo-population-migration-s2.15.2] China's one-child policy was introduced in 1979–80.
[geo-population-migration-s2.16.b] China's policy cut fertility sharply, but its enforcement and its effects on the population show the human cost.
[geo-population-migration-s2.16.0] Enforced through fines, loss of jobs and benefits, and in places forced abortions and sterilisations.
[geo-population-migration-s2.16.1] Exceptions existed for many rural families whose first child was a girl and for ethnic minorities, so it did not apply to every family.
[geo-population-migration-s2.16.2] Effects: fertility fell well below replacement; the sex ratio at birth (boys born per 100 girls) rose above 115 in the 2000s, through son preference and sex-selective abortion, leaving millions more men than women.
[geo-population-migration-s2.16.3] The '4-2-1' problem: four grandparents, two parents and one child per family, so one child may support six older people.
[geo-population-migration-s2.16.4] Rapid ageing followed, because fewer young people replaced older ones.
[geo-population-migration-s2.16.5] Replaced by a two-child policy from 2016 and a three-child policy in 2021.
[geo-population-migration-s2.16.6] China's population fell in 2022, the first fall since 1961.
[geo-population-migration-s2.17.b] When there are too many people for the land, the first effects are on food and on the land itself.
[geo-population-migration-s2.17.0] Food shortages, malnutrition (not enough food or the wrong nutrients) and famine.
[geo-population-migration-s2.17.1] Environmental damage: soil erosion, desertification, deforestation, overgrazing and water shortages, because people strip the land to get food and fuel.
[geo-population-migration-s2.17.2] The Sahel shows both: famines in the late 1960s–1970s and the early-to-mid 1980s, and desertification.
[geo-population-migration-s2.18.b] Too many people for the jobs and services available means poverty and strain on public provision.
[geo-population-migration-s2.18.0] Unemployment (no work) and underemployment (part-time or low-productivity work, below what a person could do), poverty and low incomes.
[geo-population-migration-s2.18.1] Pressure on schools, hospitals, housing, water and sanitation, because these cannot expand as fast as the population.
[geo-population-migration-s2.18.2] A high young dependency ratio: many children per worker, so each worker supports more people who do not earn.
[geo-population-migration-s2.19.b] When an area cannot support its people, some leave and others compete for what remains.
[geo-population-migration-s2.19.0] Rural-to-urban migration to shanty towns (areas of poor, self-built housing on the edge of cities). There is more on this in Rural-to-urban migration in developed and developing regions.
[geo-population-migration-s2.19.1] Emigration, as people leave the country in search of work or food.
[geo-population-migration-s2.19.2] Conflict over land and water, because shrinking resources are fought over.
[geo-population-migration-s2.20.b] The speed of population growth affects how fast a country can develop. Rapid growth tends to hold development back.
[geo-population-migration-s2.20.0] Savings go on feeding and educating more people rather than investment, so there is less money for factories and infrastructure.
[geo-population-migration-s2.20.1] Per-capita income (income per person) gains are cancelled out, because the extra output is shared among more people.
[geo-population-migration-s2.20.2] Jobs cannot keep up with population growth, and the environment is degraded.
[geo-population-migration-s2.20.3] The 'poverty trap' of high fertility keeps families and countries poor.
[geo-population-migration-s2.21.b] When growth slows, development can speed up. Fewer children means a bigger share of the population is of working age.
[geo-population-migration-s2.21.0] The demographic dividend is a large working-age population with fewer dependants (children and older people who rely on others).
[geo-population-migration-s2.21.1] East Asian economies such as South Korea and China, and Ireland from the 1990s, benefited.
[geo-population-migration-s2.21.2] Ireland's dividend: the large generation born around 1980 (births peaked at about 74,000 that year) entered the workforce in the late 1990s and 2000s, helping the Celtic Tiger economy.
[geo-population-migration-s2.22.b] Very slow growth or falling population also harms development, because the population ages.
[geo-population-migration-s2.22.0] Ageing, labour shortages, a shrinking tax base (fewer workers paying tax) and high pension and health costs.
[geo-population-migration-s2.22.1] Examples: Japan, Italy and South Korea, all countries with very low fertility.
[geo-population-migration-s2.22.2] Italy: total fertility rate about 1.2 (2023).
[geo-population-migration-s2.22.3] South Korea: total fertility rate about 0.7 (2023), the lowest in the world.
