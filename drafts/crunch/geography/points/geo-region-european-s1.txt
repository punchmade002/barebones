TARGET geo-region-european-s1: A Core European Region: the Paris Basin
[geo-region-european-s1.0.b] The Paris Basin is a large, low sedimentary lowland (a basin floored by layers of rock laid down as sediment) in northern France, centred on Paris. Knowing exactly where it starts and stops matters, because the edging hills are not part of it.
[geo-region-european-s1.0.0] It is open to the English Channel in the north-west, so the basin has a sea outlet on one side and is closed in by higher land elsewhere.
[geo-region-european-s1.0.1] Older uplands ring the basin and mark its edge, and they are not part of the lowland: the Armorican Massif (west), the Massif Central (south), the Vosges (east) and the Ardennes (north-east).
[geo-region-european-s1.0.2] The basin covers several French administrative regions (the main divisions used to govern France). Île-de-France, the region containing Paris, is its core.
[geo-region-european-s1.0.3] France has 13 regions in mainland France (since 2016); Île-de-France is one of them.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.b] A core region is one where people, wealth and decision-making are concentrated. The Paris Basin earns that status from the concentration of people, wealth and functions around Paris, and from the links that radiate out from it.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.0] Paris is the capital, so government, finance and head offices (the headquarters of large firms) are concentrated there.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.1] The region holds the largest concentration of population, wealth, industry and services in France.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.2] Fertile farmland surrounds the city and supplies its food, so the city does not depend on distant supplies.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.3] The national transport network is focused on the region, so routes across France lead in and out of it.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.4] The basin has a central position in the EU.
[geo-region-european-s1.1.5] The reasons reinforce each other: the large market draws industry and services, these draw migrants, and the transport links carry goods and people in and out.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.b] Exams ask for an outline map of a European region with named features on it. These are the items you can draw and name, and where each one belongs.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.0] River: draw the Seine and name it; the Marne, Oise and Loire may be added as named rivers.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.1] Urban centres: name Paris with two others from Rouen, Le Havre, Reims, Orléans, Troyes, Chartres or Amiens.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.2] Placement on rivers: Paris, Rouen and Le Havre lie on the Seine, with Le Havre at its mouth and Rouen upstream of it. Troyes is on the Seine upstream of Paris. Orléans is on the Loire. Épernay, not Reims, is on the Marne.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.3] Placement elsewhere: Chartres lies in the Beauce (a lowland plain) and Amiens is on the Somme.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.4] Relief: shade the Beauce (lowland plain) and the Brie plateau, and mark the edging uplands on the rim, labelled as bounding the region.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.5] Agricultural area: the Beauce for cereals, and Champagne for vines.
[geo-region-european-s1.2.6] Industrial area: the Lower Seine between Rouen and Le Havre.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.b] The climate is transitional, between cool temperate oceanic conditions in the west (mild, wet, moderated by the sea) and more continental conditions in the east (bigger swings between summer and winter). This shapes what can be grown.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.0] The annual temperature range (the gap between the warmest and coldest month) increases from west to east, so inland winters are colder and summers warmer.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.1] Paris: average January temperature about 5°C; average July temperature about 20°C.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.2] Paris: annual rainfall about 600–650 mm, spread fairly evenly through the year, so rainfall is moderate and there is no dry season.
[geo-region-european-s1.3.3] Champagne lies near the northern limit of commercial vine growing, so its warm summers are essential to the crop.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.b] The basin is a saucer of sedimentary rock layers, with the youngest rocks at the centre and the oldest at the rim. The resulting low, gentle relief matters for land use.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.0] Layers of limestone, chalk, clay and sand dip (slope) gently towards Paris, which sits near the centre on the youngest rocks.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.1] The land is mostly below about 300 m, so the relief is low and gently rolling, which suits large-scale farming.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.2] On the eastern side, resistant layers form escarpments (long ridges with a steep face) that face outwards (east), while their dip slopes (the gentle back slopes) fall back in towards Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.3] Clay vales (low, wet valleys cut in soft clay) lie between the escarpments.
[geo-region-european-s1.4.4] Only the relief and its effect on land use are needed here. There is more on how cuestas (escarpments) form in Landforms from sedimentary structures.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.b] Soil type decides what a farmer can grow, and the basin has a clear pattern: rich loam on the plateaux, thin chalk soil in Champagne, and heavy clay in the vales.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.0] Limon, a fine, wind-blown loam, covers the Beauce, Brie and Picardy plateaux.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.1] Limon is deep, fine, well-drained, fertile and easily worked, so it is among the most fertile soils in Europe.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.2] Champagne's chalk gives thin calcareous (lime-rich) soils. The dry chalk plain east of Reims was once called 'Champagne pouilleuse' (poor Champagne).
[geo-region-european-s1.5.3] Fertilisers and modern farming turned that dry chalk plain into productive cereal land, so soil weakness was overcome by human effort.
[geo-region-european-s1.5.4] Heavier, wetter clay soils in the vales and in the west are suited to grass rather than crops, because they are hard to work and waterlogged.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.b] The Seine and its tributaries (rivers that flow into it) form the main drainage system, and they converge on Paris. The gentle land that makes them useful also makes them a flood risk.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.0] The Seine rises on the Langres plateau in Burgundy and flows about 775 km north-west through Troyes, Paris and Rouen to the English Channel at Le Havre.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.1] Its tributaries are the Aube, Yonne, Marne and Oise.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.2] Gentle gradients make the rivers navigable, so boats and barges can use them.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.3] The lower Seine meanders (winds in loops) between Paris and Rouen.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.4] The Loire crosses the south of the region and flows through Orléans.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.5] Permeable chalk and limestone (rock that lets water soak in) store groundwater in aquifers (underground water stores) and give dry valleys, which are valleys with no river in them now.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.6] The gentle gradient and low relief let floodwater spread slowly across the flat valley floor. The Great Flood of Paris (January 1910) and a major flood in June 2016 (which closed the lower galleries of the Louvre) both show this risk.
[geo-region-european-s1.6.7] Upstream reservoirs regulate the flow of the rivers; Lac du Der-Chantecoq regulates the Marne.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.b] The Paris Basin is the main arable region (growing crops rather than keeping animals) of France, and its physical conditions give it a head start in farming.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.0] France is the EU's largest agricultural producer and its largest wheat producer.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.1] Low, gently rolling relief allows large fields to be worked with machinery.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.2] Limon soils are fertile and easily worked, giving high yields.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.3] Warm summers ripen cereals and grapes, and the long growing season extends the time crops have to develop.
[geo-region-european-s1.7.4] Dry harvest weather lets crops be gathered in good condition, because wet grain is harder to store.
[geo-region-european-s1.8.b] The Beauce is the best example of the basin's arable farming, where flat land and limon soil are put to full use.
[geo-region-european-s1.8.0] The Beauce, south-west of Paris around Chartres, is known as the granary of France (a granary is a store for grain).
[geo-region-european-s1.8.1] Crops: wheat, barley and maize, with sugar beet and oilseed rape grown on the plateaux.
[geo-region-european-s1.8.2] Many cereal farms exceed 100 hectares, which is large enough for big machines to work efficiently.
[geo-region-european-s1.8.3] Large, highly mechanised farms achieve high yields on the flat limon land, because flat ground suits big machines.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.b] Viticulture (growing grapes for wine) is the basin's best-known specialist farming. It depends on chalk soils, slopes and a protected name.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.0] Vineyards lie on the chalk slopes around Reims and Épernay; Épernay is on the Marne.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.1] The vines grow on the slopes of the escarpment, on thin chalk soils.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.2] Only sparkling wine from this protected (AOC) area may be called Champagne. AOC is a legal label that protects a product's name and place of origin.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.3] The Champagne houses (the firms that make and sell the wine) store wine in chalk cellars. Moët & Chandon is based in Épernay.
[geo-region-european-s1.9.4] The houses add value: grapes grown on the slopes become a branded product sold under the protected name, which links farming to manufacturing.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.b] Where the soil is too wet and heavy for crops, farmers turn to grass and animals. Near Paris, the huge city market favours fresh produce instead.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.0] Wetter clay soils and grassland support dairy farming (milk production) in Normandy (west), which produces Camembert cheese.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.1] Brie, east of Paris, is also a dairy area, so it gives its name to Brie cheese.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.2] Market gardening (growing vegetables and flowers for sale) is practised on the edge of Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.10.3] Proximity to Paris lets fresh produce reach a very large urban market quickly, which matters because fresh goods spoil.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.b] Physical conditions are only part of the story. Changes to farm layout, organisation and EU money also explain why the basin farms so productively.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.0] Remembrement: after the Second World War, fragmented holdings (many small scattered plots) were consolidated into larger farms.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.1] Mechanisation allows fewer workers to farm larger areas, which is also why the rural population falls.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.2] Farm co-operatives (farmers' groups) and agribusiness firms (large commercial food companies) buy, process and market farm produce.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.3] France receives the largest share of CAP spending (the EU's Common Agricultural Policy) of any member state, which supports French farm incomes.
[geo-region-european-s1.11.4] The Paris market is a large outlet for farm produce within reach of the basin.
[geo-region-european-s1.12.b] Forestry and fishing are small activities here compared with farming.
[geo-region-european-s1.12.0] Large forests grow on the poorer sandy soils near Paris, which are less useful for crops, so trees took the land. They include the Forests of Fontainebleau (on sands and sandstone), Compiègne and Rambouillet.
[geo-region-european-s1.12.1] These state forests are used for timber and recreation.
[geo-region-european-s1.12.2] Fishing is minor in this region and no port is named for it.
[geo-region-european-s1.13.b] France has few local fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas), so it relies on nuclear power, and the basin's rivers and coast host many of the stations.
[geo-region-european-s1.13.0] Nuclear power supplies about two-thirds of France's electricity (recent years).
[geo-region-european-s1.13.1] Nuclear stations sit on rivers and the coast, which supply the cooling water they need: Nogent-sur-Seine on the Seine; Dampierre and Saint-Laurent on the Loire near Orléans; Paluel and Penly on the Normandy Channel coast.
[geo-region-european-s1.13.2] France has few local fossil fuels, so oil is imported as crude (unrefined) and refined on the Seine estuary.
[geo-region-european-s1.13.3] Oil refining and petrochemicals (chemicals made from oil) are located at Gonfreville-l'Orcher near Le Havre.
[geo-region-european-s1.14.b] The largest market and labour force in France drew manufacturing to Paris, and firms with head offices and research staff stayed close to it.
[geo-region-european-s1.14.0] Market: the Paris region is the largest market in France, so firms locate close to their customers.
[geo-region-european-s1.14.1] Labour: it has the largest labour force in France, so factories can find workers.
[geo-region-european-s1.14.2] Capital: the head offices of large firms and their finance are based in Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.14.3] Research: universities and research institutes provide the skills and ideas that industry needs.
[geo-region-european-s1.15.b] Food and drink processing is manufacturing that turns farm output into products. It sits close to the farms that supply it.
[geo-region-european-s1.15.0] Food and drink processing uses local farm output: sugar beet and cereals from the plateaux, and grapes from the Champagne vineyards.
[geo-region-european-s1.15.1] Processing close to the source saves the cost of moving bulky crops such as sugar beet to distant factories.
[geo-region-european-s1.16.b] Infrastructure (the roads, rails, ports and other networks that support industry) is a major reason firms locate here. Paris sits at the centre of the network.
[geo-region-european-s1.16.0] Paris is the hub of road, rail, river and air transport in France, so firms there are well connected.
[geo-region-european-s1.16.1] Firms can move raw materials and finished goods by road, rail, river and air from a single location.
[geo-region-european-s1.16.2] The Seine and the Lower Seine allow bulk goods to reach factories by water.
[geo-region-european-s1.16.3] Good transport lowers the cost of getting inputs in and products out, so transport links reinforce the location advantage of the region.
[geo-region-european-s1.17.b] Car making shows both the strength of Paris as an industrial region and a shift in where factories are built.
[geo-region-european-s1.17.0] Renault has a plant at Flins, and PSA (now Stellantis) has a plant at Poissy; both are west of Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.17.1] Renault's Boulogne-Billancourt plant, on Île Seguin in the Seine, closed in 1992.
[geo-region-european-s1.17.2] The closure is an example of industry leaving the inner city for suburban and provincial sites.
[geo-region-european-s1.18.b] Between Rouen and Le Havre, the Seine becomes a corridor of ports and heavy industry. It links the basin to overseas markets.
[geo-region-european-s1.18.0] The Lower Seine, between Rouen and Le Havre, is an industrial area built around its ports, oil refining and petrochemicals.
[geo-region-european-s1.18.1] Le Havre is France's largest container port, and it sits at the mouth of the Seine where the river meets the English Channel.
[geo-region-european-s1.18.2] Rouen is France's main grain-exporting port, so it links the basin's cereal surplus to overseas markets.
[geo-region-european-s1.18.3] Since 2021 the ports of Le Havre, Rouen and Paris form one body, HAROPA.
[geo-region-european-s1.19.b] Modern industry here is based on research and knowledge, and Paris-Saclay is the named example.
[geo-region-european-s1.19.0] Paris-Saclay is a research and high-technology cluster (a group of related firms and institutions in one place) on the Saclay plateau, south-west of Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.19.1] It combines universities, research institutes and company laboratories.
[geo-region-european-s1.19.2] Its research base, such as its universities and laboratories, supports the high-technology firms located in the cluster.
[geo-region-european-s1.20.b] Decentralisation means moving activity out of the capital. From the 1950s the French state tried to limit Paris's dominance and spread industry to the provinces (regions outside Paris).
[geo-region-european-s1.20.0] Decentralisation from the 1950s–60s restricted new industry in Paris and steered it to the provinces.
[geo-region-european-s1.20.1] DATAR, the regional planning agency, was set up in 1963.
[geo-region-european-s1.20.2] Eight métropoles d'équilibre (balancing metropolises) were named in 1964, including Lyon, Marseille and Lille, to counter the pull of Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.20.3] The 1965 regional plan (schéma directeur) for Paris led to five new towns: Cergy-Pontoise, Évry, Marne-la-Vallée, Melun-Sénart and Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines.
[geo-region-european-s1.20.4] Result: because factories were steered away, Paris lost its share of manufacturing but kept its head offices and research.
[geo-region-european-s1.21.b] Paris is the primate city of France (a city far larger and more important than any other), and its services are the basis of its economic dominance in the region.
[geo-region-european-s1.21.0] Government, finance, head offices, universities and international organisations are concentrated in Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.21.1] The OECD and UNESCO have their headquarters in Paris, as international organisations that add to its service role.
[geo-region-european-s1.21.2] La Défense, west of the city, is one of Europe's largest purpose-built business districts.
[geo-region-european-s1.21.3] Île-de-France has about 12 million people (2020s), about 18% of France's population (France about 68 million, 2024).
[geo-region-european-s1.21.4] Île-de-France produces about 30% of France's GDP (the total value of goods and services produced).
[geo-region-european-s1.21.5] Concentrated services draw international business, which in turn needs more of the same services, so the centre keeps growing.
[geo-region-european-s1.22.b] Rungis shows how farming (primary activity) and services such as trade and distribution (tertiary activity) connect.
[geo-region-european-s1.22.0] Rungis International Market, south of Paris, opened in 1969, replacing Les Halles in central Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.22.1] It is one of the world's largest wholesale fresh-food markets.
[geo-region-european-s1.22.2] It links primary and tertiary activity: farm produce from the basin is sold wholesale there and then distributed to the city's shops and restaurants.
[geo-region-european-s1.23.b] Tourism is a major tertiary activity in the region, with attractions to suit many interests. Heavy visitor numbers also create problems.
[geo-region-european-s1.23.0] Visitors come for heritage, culture, shopping, events, theme parks, Champagne and the countryside.
[geo-region-european-s1.23.1] The Eiffel Tower was built for the 1889 Exposition Universelle. The Louvre is the world's most visited art museum.
[geo-region-european-s1.23.2] Notre-Dame Cathedral was damaged by fire in April 2019 and reopened in December 2024. The Palace of Versailles is a major heritage attraction in the region.
[geo-region-european-s1.23.3] Disneyland Paris, at Marne-la-Vallée, opened in 1992 and is Europe's most visited theme park, so it is a major draw for visitors.
[geo-region-european-s1.23.4] Paris hosted the 2024 Olympic and Paralympic Games.
[geo-region-european-s1.23.5] Champagne Hillsides, Houses and Cellars became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015; the Champagne houses in Reims and Épernay offer cellar tours.
[geo-region-european-s1.23.6] Problems: congestion, seasonal pressure on sites (crowding in peak months), and wear on heritage sites from large visitor numbers.
[geo-region-european-s1.24.b] Transport is central to the region's development. The network is built around Paris, with new lines extending it.
[geo-region-european-s1.24.0] The road and rail network is radial (star-shaped), centred on Paris, so routes spread out from the capital like spokes.
[geo-region-european-s1.24.1] The Boulevard Périphérique ring road, completed in 1973, encircles the city and carries traffic around it, so through traffic can avoid the centre.
[geo-region-european-s1.24.2] The TGV's first high-speed line, Paris–Lyon, opened in 1981.
[geo-region-european-s1.24.3] The Channel Tunnel opened in 1994; Eurostar links Paris with London, so the region is joined to Britain by rail.
[geo-region-european-s1.24.4] The RER is the regional express rail network across the city region, so suburbs are linked to the centre.
[geo-region-european-s1.24.5] The Grand Paris Express is about 200 km of new automatic metro lines with 68 new stations.
[geo-region-european-s1.24.6] Line 14 was extended north to Saint-Denis Pleyel and south to Orly airport in 2024.
[geo-region-european-s1.25.b] Air and river links add to the road and rail network, and together they keep Paris attractive for business.
[geo-region-european-s1.25.0] Charles de Gaulle Airport (Roissy, opened 1974) is France's busiest airport; Orly is the second Paris airport, so air links serve the whole region.
[geo-region-european-s1.25.1] The Port of Paris at Gennevilliers is one of Europe's largest river ports.
[geo-region-european-s1.25.2] Transport links let firms in the region reach markets and suppliers quickly, which sustains the concentration of head offices and services.
[geo-region-european-s1.25.3] The same network connects the basin to the Channel ports and to the rest of Europe, so the region gains from both road and river access.
[geo-region-european-s1.26.b] The language of the region is tied to Paris's history as a centre of power.
[geo-region-european-s1.26.0] The language of the region is French.
[geo-region-european-s1.26.1] Standard French developed from the dialect of the Paris region, which became the standard because Paris was the centre of government.
[geo-region-european-s1.26.2] Immigrant languages are spoken in the suburbs, because of post-war migration.
[geo-region-european-s1.27.b] Religion in the region has changed from a Catholic tradition to a mixed and largely secular society.
[geo-region-european-s1.27.0] The region is traditionally Roman Catholic, but church attendance is now low.
[geo-region-european-s1.27.1] The 1905 law separating church and state (laïcité) keeps religion out of government and public institutions.
[geo-region-european-s1.27.2] There is a large Muslim minority, mainly of North African origin.
[geo-region-european-s1.27.3] Secularisation (the decline of religion's role in society) has reduced the role of the church in public life.
[geo-region-european-s1.28.b] Culture covers more than religion and language. Politics, sport and the arts all reflect Paris's role as the centre of France.
[geo-region-european-s1.28.0] Politics: France is a highly centralised state run from Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.28.1] Paris Saint-Germain won the UEFA Champions League in 2025.
[geo-region-european-s1.28.2] Stade de France, in Saint-Denis, was built for the 1998 FIFA World Cup.
[geo-region-european-s1.28.3] The French Open tennis is played at Roland-Garros, Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.28.4] The Tour de France traditionally finishes on the Champs-Élysées.
[geo-region-european-s1.28.5] Paris is a centre of art, fashion and cuisine, which also draws tourists.
[geo-region-european-s1.29.b] Exams ask for the factors that influenced the development of one urban area. Here each factor is matched to Paris.
[geo-region-european-s1.29.0] Site: Paris began on the Île de la Cité, an island at a crossing point of the Seine.
[geo-region-european-s1.29.1] Situation: Paris lies near the centre of the Paris Basin, where the Seine's main tributaries, the Marne, Oise, Aube and Yonne, converge (the Marne joins just upstream of the city and the Oise just downstream).
[geo-region-european-s1.29.2] Transport: the city is the hub of a radial road and rail network and of the river port at Gennevilliers.
[geo-region-european-s1.29.3] Administration: as capital and seat of a centralised state, Paris concentrates government, finance and head offices.
[geo-region-european-s1.29.4] Primary activity: the rich limon plateaux around the city supplied food to a growing population.
[geo-region-european-s1.29.5] Industry and services: the largest market and labour force attracted manufacturing and services, including the business district of La Défense.
[geo-region-european-s1.29.6] Planning: the 1853–1870 rebuilding, the post-war estates and the new towns each shaped the city's growth.
[geo-region-european-s1.30.b] Paris grew in stages, from planned rebuilding in the nineteenth century to suburban estates and regional planning in the twentieth.
[geo-region-european-s1.30.0] Haussmann rebuilt central Paris between 1853 and 1870 under Napoleon III, so the city gained wide boulevards, uniform apartment blocks, parks and sewers.
[geo-region-european-s1.30.1] Grands ensembles, large high-rise social housing estates built in the suburbs (banlieues) in the 1950s–70s, housed a growing population; Sarcelles is an example.
[geo-region-european-s1.30.2] Growth spread beyond the Périphérique ring road into the surrounding suburbs.
[geo-region-european-s1.30.3] The five new towns built under the 1965 plan are Cergy-Pontoise, Évry, Marne-la-Vallée, Melun-Sénart and Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines.
[geo-region-european-s1.30.4] Métropole du Grand Paris was created on 1 January 2016, joining Paris with about 130 surrounding communes (the smallest units of local government in France).
[geo-region-european-s1.30.5] The Grand Paris Express metro network is the latest large-scale project for the city region.
[geo-region-european-s1.31.b] Rapid growth and concentration have brought serious problems to Paris and its suburbs.
[geo-region-european-s1.31.0] Congestion on roads into and around the city, because so many people and jobs are concentrated there.
[geo-region-european-s1.31.1] High housing costs, which push people out to the suburbs.
[geo-region-european-s1.31.2] Social segregation (different groups living apart) and deprivation in the banlieues: Seine-Saint-Denis, north-east of Paris, is the poorest département (a French county-sized administrative area) in mainland France.
[geo-region-european-s1.31.3] Riots in the banlieues began in Clichy-sous-Bois in October 2005 and spread across France.
