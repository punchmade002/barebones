TARGET geo-economic-development-s1: 1. Measuring economic development
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.b] Economic development is one part of development, and growth alone does not show whether people are better off. Geographers need both ideas, and the gap between them is the reason broader measures such as the HDI exist.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.0] Economic development means growth in a country's output (the goods and services it produces) and income, with rising living standards for its people.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.1] Development is wider than income: it also includes health, education, political freedom, equality and quality of life. A country can grow richer without improving these, which is why income alone is not enough.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.2] Developed (high-income) economies have high output and incomes per head, meaning per person; developing economies are low- and middle-income.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.3] The World Bank classes countries as low, lower-middle, upper-middle or high income by GNI per head (gross national income divided by population). The thresholds change each year, so do not quote any.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.4] Newly industrialised countries (NICs) sit between developed and developing, because their economies have recently shifted towards manufacturing. South Korea, Singapore and Taiwan are examples; with Hong Kong they are called the Asian Tigers.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.b] These totals all measure the value of a country's output, but they differ in whether they count output by location or by ownership. Telling them apart is the key skill for the Ireland case below.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.0] Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of all goods and services produced within a country's borders in one year, whoever owns the firms. It is a measure of location.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.1] Gross national product (GNP) is GDP plus income earned abroad by the country's residents and firms, minus income sent out of the country to foreign owners as profits, interest and dividends (payments to shareholders).
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.2] GNP therefore measures the output owned by the nation's residents, while GDP measures the output produced on its soil. This is the difference between ownership and location.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.3] Gross national income (GNI) is the term the World Bank and UN now use, and it is essentially the same as GNP. Data tables may use either term, so treat them as the same measure.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.4] Totals are converted to US dollars for comparison. Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts for price differences between countries, so a dollar buys more in a low-cost country and PPP makes the comparison fairer.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.5] Per capita (per head) figures divide the total by the population, and are needed to compare countries of different size. A large total GNP, as in India, can hide a low GNP per head because it is shared among so many people.
[geo-economic-development-s1.2.b] Growth is the percentage change in output each year, and it shows whether an economy is expanding or shrinking. Governments aim for it because of what it brings.
[geo-economic-development-s1.2.0] The GDP growth rate is the percentage change in output per year. Two quarters (six months) of falling output is the usual definition of recession, when the economy is shrinking.
[geo-economic-development-s1.2.1] Economic growth brings more jobs, higher incomes, more tax revenue (money the government collects) to fund services and infrastructure (roads, water, power and other basic networks), and less emigration because people find work at home.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.b] Output per head is the most common single measure of development. It is popular because it is easy to use and to compare.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.0] It is simple and gives one comparable figure, so countries can be ranked at a glance.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.1] It is available for almost every country, collected in a standard way, so like is compared with like.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.2] It allows change over time to be tracked, so a country's progress or decline can be seen.
[geo-economic-development-s1.3.3] It is closely linked to a government's ability to fund health, education and other services, because higher output means more tax revenue.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.b] A single average cannot show how well people really live. Each weakness below is a way the figure can mislead.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.0] It is an average, so it hides inequality between rich and poor, and between regions.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.1] It ignores the informal economy (work done for cash or unrecorded) and subsistence farming (growing food for the family, not for sale), which are large in developing countries. It also ignores unpaid work such as caring, housework and volunteering.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.2] It counts harmful spending, such as pollution clean-up, disaster repair and crime, as if it added to well-being, because spending raises output whatever its cause.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.3] It ignores environmental damage and the depletion (using up) of resources.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.4] Exchange-rate swings (changes in the value of one currency against another) distort dollar comparisons unless PPP is used.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.5] It says nothing directly about health, education, freedom or happiness, which is why a broader measure was needed.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.b] Ireland is the clearest case where GDP and GNP give very different pictures of the economy. Foreign-owned firms produce a great deal in Ireland, but much of the profit leaves.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.0] Multinationals (firms operating in many countries) own much of Irish output and send their profits abroad, so Irish GNP has long been well below GDP. GNP is lower because profits flow out to foreign owners.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.1] Irish GDP growth for 2015 was about 26%, first published in July 2016. The US economist Paul Krugman called it "leprechaun economics" because it was not believable as real growth.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.2] The jump came mainly from multinationals relocating intellectual property (patents and other intangible assets, meaning things of value you cannot touch) and aircraft-leasing assets to Ireland, not from Irish people becoming richer.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.3] From 2017 the Central Statistics Office (CSO) published modified gross national income (GNI*), which removes the profits of redomiciled firms (firms that moved their legal base to Ireland), depreciation (loss of value through wear and age) on foreign-owned intellectual property and leased aircraft.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.4] In the early 2020s Ireland's GNI* was only a little over half of its GDP, which shows how large the distortion is.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.5] Ireland has the second-highest GDP per head in the EU, after Luxembourg (Eurostat, early 2020s), so GDP overstates Irish prosperity. Use GNI* as the better guide to Irish living standards.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.b] The HDI combines health, education and income into one value. It was designed to measure people's well-being, not just the economy.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.0] The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) first published the HDI in its Human Development Report in 1990.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.1] Mahbub ul Haq, a Pakistani economist, created the HDI working with Amartya Sen, an Indian economist who won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1998.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.2] A long and healthy life is measured by life expectancy at birth (how many years a newborn can expect to live).
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.3] Knowledge is measured by mean (average) years of schooling for adults aged 25 and over, and expected years of schooling for children starting school.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.4] A decent standard of living is measured by GNI per head at PPP dollars. The income indicator is GNI per head, not GDP.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.5] Each dimension is turned into an index from 0 to 1, and the three are combined into one value between 0 and 1. The closer the value is to 1, the higher the development.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.6] The four categories are very high (0.800 and above), high (0.700 to 0.799), medium (0.550 to 0.699) and low (below 0.550).
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.7] Older textbooks use adult literacy and gross enrolment (the share of children enrolled in school) for education; these were replaced by mean and expected years of schooling in 2010.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.b] HDI is highest in the richer regions and lowest in parts of Africa. One named example of each category is enough for an exam answer.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.0] Very high HDI: Europe (especially Scandinavia and Switzerland), North America, Australia and Japan. Norway ranked 1st (0.957) in the Human Development Report 2020, using 2019 data. Ireland and Switzerland were joint 2nd (0.955) in the same report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.1] Switzerland ranked 1st (0.967) in the Human Development Report 2023/24, using 2022 data. Ireland was in the top ten in that report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.2] High HDI: Brazil, at about 0.76 in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.3] Medium HDI: India, 134th of 193 countries (0.644) in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.4] Low HDI: mostly sub-Saharan Africa (the countries south of the Sahara desert), including Niger, Chad, South Sudan, Somalia and the Central African Republic.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.5] Niger was last of 189 countries (0.394) in the 2020 report, and Somalia was last of 193 (0.380) in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.b] The HDI was built to fix the weaknesses of income per head, so its strengths mostly come from being broader and people-centred.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.0] It is broader than income alone, combining health, education and income.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.1] It focuses on people and outcomes rather than output.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.2] It shows countries whose income is high but whose human development lags, such as oil-rich states, and countries that achieve good health and education on modest incomes.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.3] It can be tracked over time, showing progress or decline.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.4] It helps governments and aid agencies target spending, for example on schooling or health, because it shows which area is weakest.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.b] Like any single number, the HDI leaves things out and hides differences. The UNDP has tried to answer some of these criticisms.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.0] As a national average it hides inequality between rich and poor, men and women, regions and ethnic groups.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.1] It leaves out political freedom, human rights, gender equality, environmental sustainability, safety and happiness.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.2] Data are poor or out of date in some developing countries, and the figures are published about two years after the data year.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.3] The three dimensions are weighted equally, which is a judgement: nobody can prove they matter equally.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.4] Very high-income countries cluster close to 1, so small differences between them mean little.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.5] The UNDP introduced three newer measures in 2010 to answer some of these criticisms: the inequality-adjusted HDI, the Gender Inequality Index and the Multidimensional Poverty Index, the last developed with Oxford University's Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.b] GNP and HDI measure different things, so a country can rank very differently on each. Using both, with other indicators, gives the fullest picture.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.0] GNP measures output and income; HDI measures human outcomes in health, education and living standards.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.1] Equatorial Guinea has far higher GNI per head than its HDI rank suggests, because of oil wealth; the gap between its income rank and its HDI rank is large.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.2] Oil-rich Gulf states generally rank higher on income than on HDI.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.3] Cuba ranks higher on HDI than on income, because of strong public health care and education.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.4] GNI per head is one of the four HDI indicators, so the two measures overlap; the HDI adds life expectancy and schooling to it.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.b] No single number captures development, so geographers use a set of social and economic indicators. Data-response questions give these in tables.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.0] Social indicators: life expectancy, infant and under-five mortality (deaths of babies and young children), literacy rate, years of schooling, doctors per 1,000 people, access to safe water and sanitation, and daily calorie intake.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.1] Economic indicators: share of workers in the primary sector (farming and other raw-material work, high in developing economies), unemployment rate, energy use per head, and internet and phone access.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.2] Several indicators used together give a more reliable picture than any one measure, because each one can mislead on its own.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.b] Life expectancy at birth varies by decades around the world. The reasons are mostly health, living conditions and conflict.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.0] Access to health care: doctors, hospitals and clinics are scarce in many poorer countries, so illness goes untreated.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.1] Vaccination and clean water and sanitation prevent disease; where they are lacking, life expectancy is lower.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.2] Infectious disease: malaria caused about 600,000 deaths in 2022, over nine-tenths of them in Africa (WHO World Malaria Report 2023).
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.3] HIV/AIDS lowered life expectancy in southern Africa in the 1990s and 2000s, in Botswana, Zimbabwe and South Africa; it has since recovered with antiretroviral drugs (medicines that control the virus).
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.4] Lifestyle: diet, smoking, alcohol and obesity shorten life in some rich countries.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.5] War and conflict, and income and poverty, reduce life expectancy.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.6] Life expectancy at birth is about 84 years in Japan and about 82 to 83 years in Ireland, among the highest in the EU. It is below 60 in several sub-Saharan African countries, including Chad, Nigeria and Lesotho (early 2020s).
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.b] How long children stay in school depends on what the state provides and on what families can afford. Each reason below keeps children out of the classroom.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.0] Government spending on schools and teachers largely determines how many children are educated.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.1] Poverty forces children to work or stay at home, because families need the income or help.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.2] Distance to schools is a barrier in rural areas.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.3] Fees, uniforms and books are costs some families cannot pay.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.4] Cultural attitudes to girls' education and early marriage keep girls out of school.
[geo-economic-development-s1.13.5] Conflict closes schools.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.b] Education is both a result of development and a cause of it. A skilled workforce brings benefits that feed back into the economy.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.0] An educated workforce attracts foreign investment (money from overseas firms) in high-tech and service industries, which need skilled workers.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.1] Higher productivity (output per worker) and wages raise tax revenue, which funds further services.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.2] An educated workforce produces more innovation and more new firms.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.3] Educated women tend to have healthier, smaller families.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.4] Ireland has one of the highest shares of 25 to 34-year-olds with third-level education in the EU (early 2020s, Eurostat). Free secondary education was introduced in 1967.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.5] Universities and technological universities supply graduates to multinationals in pharmaceuticals, medical devices and information technology.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.b] The unemployment rate is the share of people who want work but cannot find it. A high rate signals a weak economy and causes problems of its own.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.0] Effects of high unemployment: lost income and poverty, emigration of young people, lower tax revenue and higher welfare costs (payments to people without work), and social problems.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.1] Ways to reduce unemployment: attracting investment, retraining and education, support for small firms, infrastructure spending and promoting tourism, each of which creates jobs or prepares people for them.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.2] Ireland's unemployment rose to about 15% in 2012 after the 2008 crash and fell below 5% by 2022 (CSO).
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.b] Data-response questions ask for a chart drawn from a table and a calculated difference. The values below are illustrative only, not real figures.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.0] A bar chart needs a title, labelled axes with units, a suitable scale, accurate bars and a key.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.1] A pie chart needs the same title and key, with each slice's share of the whole shown clearly.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.2] To calculate a difference, subtract the lower value from the higher and state the unit: for example, an illustrative country with a life expectancy of 80 years and another with 60 years differ by 20 years.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.3] There is more on the detailed graph method in the Tables, Graphs and Data Skills chapter.
