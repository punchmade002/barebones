TARGET geo-economic-environment-s4: 4. Sustainable economic development
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.b] Economic activity such as farming, fishing, mining and industry always affects the environment. Sustainable development is the framework within which that environmental impact is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.0] The Brundtland Report, "Our Common Future" (UN World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987), defined it as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.0.1] It has three pillars: environmental protection, economic growth and social fairness. Sustainable development balances all three rather than maximising one, so a plan that boosts profit but wrecks the environment is not sustainable.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.b] Five working rules turn the definition into practice. Each one protects the environment so that it can keep supporting people in the future.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.0] Renewable resources (ones that regrow or refill naturally) such as fish, forests and water should be used no faster than they regenerate, otherwise they are used up.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.1] Non-renewable resources (ones that cannot be replaced once used) should be used efficiently and recycled, so that they last longer.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.2] Pollution should be kept within what the environment can absorb, so that air, water and soil are not overloaded.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.3] The polluter pays: those who cause environmental damage bear the cost of it, which gives them a reason to cause less.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.1.4] The precautionary principle: act to prevent serious harm even before the harm is proven, because waiting for proof may be too late.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.b] Sustainable development became an international aim, with world meetings setting out plans for governments to follow.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.0] The Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro (1992) was a later, separate event from the Brundtland Report. It produced Agenda 21, an action plan for sustainable development.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.2.1] The UN Sustainable Development Goals were adopted in 2015: 17 goals with a target year of 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.b] In Ireland, the main body that controls the environmental impact of industry is the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). It was created by law and works independently of the government departments it advises.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.0] It was set up under the Environmental Protection Agency Act 1992, and the EPA began work in 1993.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.3.1] It is an independent public body (a State organisation not run by a government minister) with headquarters at Johnstown Castle, Co. Wexford.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.b] The EPA's most important power is licensing. Activities with a high risk of pollution may not operate unless the EPA allows it and sets the conditions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.0] Large industries need an EPA licence: pharmaceutical and chemical plants, power stations, intensive pig and poultry farms, cement works and mines. Waste facilities also need one.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.1] The licence sets emission limits for the activity, which means the maximum pollution it may release into air, water or land.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.4.2] Integrated pollution control means one licence covers air, water, waste and noise, instead of separate permits for each. This lets the EPA look at the whole impact of a site at once.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.b] To control pollution, the EPA first has to measure it. It tracks the state of the environment and reports what it finds.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.0] The EPA monitors water quality in rivers, lakes, estuaries (where rivers meet the sea), groundwater (water held underground in rock and soil) and bathing waters.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.1] It runs a national air quality network and produces a greenhouse gas inventory (a count of emissions of gases that cause climate change) and projections of future emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.2] Drinking water is supplied by Uisce Éireann.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.3] The State of the Environment report is published every four years, so the public and government can see whether things are improving.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.4] The EPA advises government and the public and funds environmental research.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.5.5] The EPA took over radiological protection in 2014, including the control of radon (a natural radioactive gas that can seep up from the ground).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.b] A licence is only useful if it is obeyed, so the EPA checks and punishes breaches.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.0] Inspections check that licensed activities keep within their emission limits.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.1] The Office of Environmental Enforcement, set up within the EPA in 2003, prosecutes polluters (takes them to court).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.6.2] The EPA oversees the environmental work carried out by local authorities (the county and city councils).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.b] The EPA cannot solve every problem alone. Some pollution is hard to see, track or assign to one source.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.0] The EPA cannot inspect everywhere, so some breaches go unchecked.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.1] Diffuse pollution (pollution that comes from many scattered sources rather than one pipe or chimney) from thousands of farms is hard to police.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.7.2] Local authorities and other bodies share responsibility for environmental control, so the EPA is one part of a wider system.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.b] Control is best done before a project starts. An environmental impact assessment is the study that looks at a project's likely effects in advance.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.0] An environmental impact assessment is a study, required before planning permission is decided for large projects, that predicts a project's effects on the environment and how to reduce them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.8.1] The developer's report (the developer is the company or body proposing the project) was called an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS). It is now an Environmental Impact Assessment Report (EIAR).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.b] Environmental impact assessment is not optional for big projects. It comes from EU law and is built into Irish planning rules.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.0] The EU Environmental Impact Assessment Directive, 1985, is applied in Irish planning law.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.9.1] Assessment is required for projects above set sizes, such as motorways, power stations, wind farms, mines and quarries, large intensive farms, pipelines, ports and airports. Small projects below the set sizes do not need one.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.b] An EIAR has to describe the project, the place it will be built and every kind of effect it could have, then say how harm will be reduced.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.0] A description of the project and of the existing environment (the baseline, meaning the condition of the site before anything is built, which later effects are measured against).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.1] Likely effects on people, plants and animals, soil, water, air, climate, landscape, archaeology and heritage, traffic and noise.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.2] Alternatives considered, such as other designs or sites, and the mitigation measures (steps to reduce or prevent harm) proposed to reduce effects.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.3] A non-technical summary for the public, so ordinary people can understand the report.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.10.4] The developer writes the report, often with consultants. Neither the EPA nor the planning authority writes it.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.b] The assessment follows a set order. It ends with a decision-maker who weighs the report against what the public and experts say.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.0] 1. The developer prepares the EIAR.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.1] 2. The public and bodies such as the National Parks and Wildlife Service make submissions (written comments and objections).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.11.2] 3. The planning authority or An Coimisiún Pleanála decides whether to grant permission, often with conditions. The board was renamed in 2025 from An Bord Pleanála, the national planning appeals board.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.b] Short questions ask why environmental impact studies are needed. There are four reasons, and Ireland's experience at Derrybrien shows what happens when the law is not followed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.0] They prevent damage before it happens, which is cheaper than paying for clean-up afterwards.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.1] They require developers to consider alternatives, so a less harmful design or site may be chosen.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.2] They give the public a say in decisions, through submissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.3] They are a legal requirement.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.12.4] At Derrybrien, Co. Galway, a peat slide on 16 October 2003 occurred during wind-farm construction. In 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland a lump sum of €5 million plus €15,000 a day because no proper environmental impact assessment had been carried out.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.b] Fish are a renewable resource, but only if people leave enough of them to breed. This idea is the basis of sustainable fishing.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.0] Fish are renewable only while catches stay below the rate at which the stock (the population of one kind of fish) breeds and grows.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.13.1] The maximum sustainable yield is the largest catch that can be taken year after year without reducing the stock.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.b] Overfishing means taking fish faster than stocks can replace themselves. Several causes push fleets to do this.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.0] Modern technology, including sonar (finds fish using sound), satellite navigation, factory ships (which process the catch at sea) and huge nets, lets fleets find and take fish faster than stocks can recover.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.1] Too many boats chase the same stock, so the catch is shared out among more boats and each one fishes harder.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.2] Subsidies (payments from government) keep fleets fishing when stocks are falling.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.3] Young fish are caught before they have had the chance to breed, so the stock cannot renew itself.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.4] Discards: dead or unwanted fish are thrown back into the sea, which wastes the catch.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.14.5] Illegal fishing takes fish outside the quota system (the limits on how much each country or boat may catch).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.b] The Grand Banks is the classic example of a fishery destroyed by overfishing. It shows how both fish stocks and jobs can be lost.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.0] The Grand Banks, off Newfoundland, Canada, were one of the world's richest cod fisheries for centuries.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.1] Catches rose sharply with factory trawlers (large boats that fish with nets and process the catch on board) from the 1960s, and the stock collapsed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.2] Canada declared a moratorium (ban) on northern cod fishing on 2 July 1992.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.3] About 30,000 people in Newfoundland lost their jobs, the largest single lay-off in Canadian history.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.15.4] The stock has recovered only slowly, and partly, in the decades since.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.b] North Sea herring is a second example, with a happier ending. It shows that a stock can recover when fishing is stopped and then tightly limited.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.0] North Sea herring was overfished in the 1960s and 1970s.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.1] The fishery was closed from 1977 to the early 1980s, which gave the stock time to breed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.16.2] The stock recovered after the fishery reopened under strict quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.b] In EU waters, fish stocks are managed through the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP). Here it matters as a conservation tool that tries to keep catches sustainable.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.0] The EU Common Fisheries Policy, 1983, sets total allowable catches (TACs, the maximum amount of each stock that may be caught) and divides them into national quotas.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.1] Conservation rules include minimum mesh sizes (so small fish escape the net), minimum landing sizes, closed areas and seasons, and vessel decommissioning (scrapping boats to reduce fleet size).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.2] The CFP reform of 2013 aims to fish at maximum sustainable yield and introduces a landing obligation that ends discards, phased in from 2015 to 2019.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.3] Monitoring uses satellite tracking, the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority (set up in 2007) and fishery protection patrols by the Irish Naval Service.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.4] Ireland's exclusive economic zone (the sea area over which a country controls resources) extends 200 nautical miles from the coast, within which these rules apply.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.17.5] Further measures include marine protected areas (areas of sea where fishing is restricted) and aquaculture (fish farming) as an alternative to wild catch.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.b] Fishing matters to Irish coastal communities, which depend on the fish stocks around the coast for their living.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.0] Mackerel is the most valuable Irish catch, landed mainly at Killybegs, Co. Donegal, Ireland's largest fishing port.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.1] Killybegs is the base of the pelagic fleet (boats that fish species living in open water, not on the seabed) that catches mackerel and herring.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.2] Ireland has six State Fishery Harbour Centres: Killybegs, Castletownbere, Dingle, Rossaveel, Dunmore East and Howth.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.18.3] Irish fishing communities depend on these stocks, so conservation limits cause hardship. There is more on this in Conflicts between economic interests and environmental interests.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.b] Mining that happened before modern controls can go on polluting for generations. Avoca is the Irish example.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.0] Copper mining at Avoca, Co. Wicklow, over centuries left acid mine drainage (acidic water that runs out of old mine workings) when mining ended.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.19.1] The acid drainage still discolours and pollutes the Avoca River long after the mines closed, which shows why closure plans are needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.b] Today a mine has to work within licence rules. Tara Mines shows what controlled mining looks like.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.0] Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath, is an underground zinc and lead mine, Europe's largest zinc mine, producing since 1977 and owned by Boliden.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.1] It operates under an EPA licence, which sets limits on its emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.2] Tailings (the waste left after the metal has been extracted) are stored in a lined tailings facility, so they do not leak into the ground, and the water is monitored.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.20.3] Restoration plans are in place for the site.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.b] A responsible mine plans for its end before it opens. Lisheen shows rehabilitation (restoring a site to a safe, useful condition) after closure.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.0] Lisheen, Co. Tipperary, a zinc and lead mine, closed in 2015 and the site has been rehabilitated.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.21.1] A new mine needs an environmental impact assessment report (EIAR) and a closure and restoration plan backed by a financial bond (money set aside in advance) before it opens, so the clean-up is paid for even if the company fails.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.b] Peat was long treated as a fuel to be dug out. The scale of that harvesting damaged Ireland's bogs.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.0] Bord na Móna, founded in 1946, carried out large-scale industrial harvesting of peat for power stations and fuel.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.22.1] Harvesting removed raised bogs (domed peatlands formed over thousands of years), released the carbon they had stored and destroyed their habitats.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.b] Peat harvesting has ended, and the focus has moved to repairing the damage and finding new uses for the land.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.0] The Lanesborough and Shannonbridge peat stations closed in December 2020, and Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.1] Cutaway bogs (bogs where the peat has been removed) are being rehabilitated and rewetted, meaning water is returned to them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.2] Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly: a cutaway bog turned into parkland, wetlands and trails.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.3] Bellacorick, Co. Mayo: the peat station closed in 2003 and wind farms now stand on the cutaway bog.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.23.4] A just transition (a change that is fair to the workers affected) is needed for Midland workers who lost jobs in peat.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.b] The Aral Sea shows how diverting water for farming can destroy a whole environment and the livelihoods that depended on it.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.0] The Amu Darya and Syr Darya, which feed the Aral Sea in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, were diverted from the 1960s to irrigate (water by channels) cotton in the Soviet Union.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.1] The sea shrank to a fraction of its size and split into separate lakes, and its salinity (salt content) rose.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.2] The fishing industry collapsed, and ports such as Moynaq (Uzbekistan) were left stranded far from the water.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.3] Dust storms spread salt and pesticides from the exposed seabed, harming local health.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.24.4] The Kok-Aral dam (Kazakhstan, 2005) raised the level of the small northern sea, and fishing there has partly revived.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.25.b] Burning fossil fuels pollutes the air and adds to climate change, so a sustainable approach changes the energy source.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.25.0] Switching to renewable energy (energy from sources that do not run out, such as wind) reduces the environmental impact of power generation. There is more on this in Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.b] Farming can be paid and encouraged to protect the environment, not just to produce food.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.0] Agri-environment schemes pay farmers for conservation: REPS (Rural Environment Protection Scheme, 1994), now succeeded by ACRES.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.1] Origin Green, Bord Bia's sustainability programme for Irish food and drink, was launched in 2012.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.2] Nitrates rules limit the amount of nitrogen fertiliser and manure that farms can apply, because the excess can run off and pollute water.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.3] BurrenLIFE (2005 to 2010) paid Burren farmers to manage species-rich limestone grassland through winter grazing (winterage); it continued as the Burren Programme.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.26.4] Results-based farming in the Burren rewards farmers for measured environmental outcomes, not just for following set practices.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.b] Forests and tourist sites can be used without being destroyed if how they are used is controlled.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.0] Forests are replanted after felling, using mixed native broadleaves (a variety of native trees such as oak, rather than one species).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.1] Forests are not planted on deep peat.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.2] Visitor limits and ecotourism (tourism designed to protect nature) reduce pressure on fragile sites.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.27.3] At Skellig Michael, Co. Kerry, access is controlled through licensed boats and visitor limits.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.b] The best way to deal with waste is to create less of it, which is why there is an order of priority.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.0] Reduce, reuse and recycle, in that order of priority.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.1] Levies (charges) and deposit schemes (a refundable payment on containers) give people and businesses an incentive to reduce waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.28.2] A circular economy keeps products and materials in use for as long as possible, instead of using them once and throwing them away.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.b] The polluter pays principle is put into effect through taxes and charges that put a price on pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.0] Irish carbon tax, introduced in 2010.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.1] Plastic bag levy, introduced in March 2002, a charge on plastic bags, so shoppers have a reason to use fewer.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.2] Landfill levy, charged on waste sent to landfill, so dumping costs more than reducing waste.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.29.3] EU Emissions Trading System, from 2005: companies pay for the emissions they release.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.b] Governments have set targets for cutting emissions, and the future of sustainable development depends on meeting them.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.0] The EU and Ireland aim for climate neutrality by 2050 (releasing no more greenhouse gases than are removed).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.1] Ireland's Climate Act 2021 sets a 51% cut in emissions by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.2] The Climate Action Plan sets 80% renewable electricity by 2030 and a retrofit (upgrading insulation and heating) of 500,000 homes to B2 energy rating by 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.3] Offshore wind, electric vehicles, public transport and the circular economy are the main routes to lower emissions.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.30.4] Cutting agricultural emissions, restoring bogs and planting forests as carbon sinks (places that absorb and store carbon).
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.b] Meeting these targets will not be easy. Several obstacles stand in the way.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.0] Cost: clean technology, retrofitting and rehabilitation are expensive.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.1] Short-term economic pressure favours quick profit over long-term protection.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.2] Jobs in traditional industries, such as peat and fishing, are at risk.
[geo-economic-environment-s4.31.3] Global cooperation is needed, because pollution and climate change do not stop at borders.
