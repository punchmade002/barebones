TARGET geo-sea-s2: 2. Human interaction: coastal processes
[geo-sea-s2.0.b] Coasts are a balance between erosion (wearing land away), transport (moving the material) and deposition (dropping it). Human activity can upset that balance, which is why this option looks at how people change coastal processes.
[geo-sea-s2.0.0] Human activity can interrupt longshore drift (the movement of sand and shingle along the shore by waves hitting at an angle), remove sediment (the sand and stones that make up beaches), destroy the vegetation that holds dunes together, or reflect wave energy back out to sea.
[geo-sea-s2.0.1] The effects often appear further along the coast, downdrift (in the direction the sediment travels), rather than where the work was done. This is because the sediment supply is cut off or changed for everywhere further along.
[geo-sea-s2.1.b] Sand dunes are held in place by plants, so anything that kills the plants lets the wind start to remove the dune. Recreation is one of the main ways people do this.
[geo-sea-s2.1.0] Trampling of sand dunes by walkers and sunbathers kills marram grass, the tough grass whose roots bind dune sand together.
[geo-sea-s2.1.1] With no roots binding it, the sand is removed by the wind, cutting blowouts (hollows) into the dunes.
[geo-sea-s2.1.2] A blowout can breach (break through) the dune ridge, letting the sea reach the land behind, which the dunes had been protecting.
[geo-sea-s2.1.3] Vehicles and parking on dunes and beaches destroy vegetation and compact the sand (press it down hard); caravan parks and holiday homes built on dunes do the same.
[geo-sea-s2.2.b] Heavy use of a coast, even for quiet recreation, damages both the land surface and the wildlife that depends on it.
[geo-sea-s2.2.0] Golf courses on dunes (links) stabilise some dunes, but replace natural habitats and lead to demands for defences to protect the course.
[geo-sea-s2.2.1] Footpath erosion and cliff-edge damage occur at busy viewing points, because so many feet wear away the surface.
[geo-sea-s2.2.2] Litter, pollution and disturbance of nesting and feeding birds follow heavy use.
[geo-sea-s2.3.b] Building a resort right at the shore does more than take up space. It removes the natural store of sand and leaves the beach with nowhere to move.
[geo-sea-s2.3.0] Hotels, apartments and promenades built on the beach or dune line remove the natural sediment store, the sand that would normally feed the beach.
[geo-sea-s2.3.1] They block the natural landward movement of dunes and beaches, so the beach narrows and defences become necessary; this is called coastal squeeze (the beach is squeezed between the sea and the buildings).
[geo-sea-s2.3.2] Example: the Spanish Mediterranean coast, including the Costa del Sol and Benidorm on the Costa Blanca, where high-rise resorts have been built along the shore since mass tourism grew in the 1960s.
[geo-sea-s2.4.b] Once people build on a coast, they often try to protect it from the sea. Coastal defences fall into two types, and the difference decides how they affect coastal processes.
[geo-sea-s2.4.0] Hard engineering builds structures to resist the sea; soft engineering works with natural processes.
[geo-sea-s2.4.1] Hard engineering is usually expensive and effective locally, and it can increase erosion elsewhere, because it changes how waves and sediment move along the rest of the coast.
[geo-sea-s2.5.b] A sea wall protects the land behind it but changes the processes in front of it.
[geo-sea-s2.5.0] Sea walls are concrete or stone walls, often curved (recurved) to throw waves back.
[geo-sea-s2.5.1] They reflect wave energy, so backwash (the water running back down the beach) scours (digs out) the beach in front, lowering and narrowing it, and the wall itself is undermined.
[geo-sea-s2.5.2] Erosion often increases at the ends of a sea wall, because the unprotected land beside it still takes the full force of the waves.
[geo-sea-s2.5.3] Sea walls are expensive to build and maintain, so they are usually used only where valuable land or buildings need protecting.
[geo-sea-s2.6.b] Rock armour is a cheaper alternative to a wall. Instead of reflecting waves, it uses gaps to soak up their energy.
[geo-sea-s2.6.0] Rock armour (rip-rap) is large boulders piled at the foot of a cliff or in front of a sea wall.
[geo-sea-s2.6.1] The gaps between the boulders absorb and break up wave energy.
[geo-sea-s2.6.2] It is cheaper than a sea wall and quick to place, because the boulders are simply piled up rather than built into a structure.
[geo-sea-s2.6.3] It can be unsightly and hard to walk over, and big storms may move the boulders.
[geo-sea-s2.6.4] In photographs, rock armour appears as a pile of loose boulders at the cliff foot or wall.
[geo-sea-s2.7.b] Groynes work by catching the sand that longshore drift carries along the beach. This helps the beach in one place but harms it further along.
[geo-sea-s2.7.0] Groynes are low wooden, rock or concrete barriers built out from the shore at right angles, across the beach.
[geo-sea-s2.7.1] They trap sand moving by longshore drift on their updrift side (the side the sand arrives from), widening the beach, which absorbs wave energy.
[geo-sea-s2.7.2] Effect on processes: the beach downdrift is starved of sediment, so erosion increases there.
[geo-sea-s2.7.3] Unlike rock armour, which lies along the foot of a cliff or wall, groynes run out across the beach at right angles to the shore.
[geo-sea-s2.8.b] Several other structures are used on coasts. Each either breaks up waves or interrupts the movement of sediment.
[geo-sea-s2.8.0] Gabions are wire cages filled with stones, which are stacked to form a protective barrier.
[geo-sea-s2.8.1] Revetments are sloping wooden, concrete or rock structures that break waves.
[geo-sea-s2.8.2] Offshore breakwaters and reefs are structures parallel to the shore, just offshore, that make waves break before they reach the beach.
[geo-sea-s2.8.3] Training walls and harbour piers (walls built to guide water or protect a harbour entrance) interrupt longshore drift and cause deposition in new places; the North Bull Wall, Dublin Bay, is an example.
[geo-sea-s2.9.b] Soft engineering rebuilds the natural defences of the coast, the beach and the dunes, instead of building against the sea.
[geo-sea-s2.9.0] Beach nourishment pumps or trucks sand or shingle onto a beach to widen it.
[geo-sea-s2.9.1] It looks natural and keeps the beach for tourism, so visitors still have sand to use.
[geo-sea-s2.9.2] It must be repeated, as waves remove the sand, and dredging (digging up) the source can harm the sea bed.
[geo-sea-s2.9.3] Dune regeneration uses sand fencing or brushwood to trap blown sand, replants marram grass, and fences off damaged areas.
[geo-sea-s2.9.4] It is cheap and natural, but takes years because the plants must grow, and it needs visitors to keep off the dunes so the new growth is not trampled.
[geo-sea-s2.10.b] Sometimes the cheapest option is to stop fighting the sea. Managed retreat gives up some land in a planned way.
[geo-sea-s2.10.0] In managed retreat (managed realignment), or 'do nothing', defences are removed or not replaced, and the sea is allowed to flood low-value land.
[geo-sea-s2.10.1] The flooded land becomes salt marsh (a muddy, plant-covered area flooded by the tide), which absorbs wave energy and provides habitat.
[geo-sea-s2.10.2] It is cheap and sustainable because natural salt marsh needs no upkeep, but landowners lose land.
[geo-sea-s2.11.b] The Mappleton scheme shows how a defence can move erosion downdrift.
[geo-sea-s2.11.0] At Mappleton, on the Holderness coast, England, a 1991 scheme of two rock groynes and rock armour at the cliff foot protected the village and the main coast road.
[geo-sea-s2.11.1] The scheme cost about £2 million, which shows how expensive hard engineering can be.
[geo-sea-s2.11.2] Result: the beach built up between the groynes, but the cliffs immediately south of the scheme, starved of sediment, eroded faster than before.
[geo-sea-s2.11.3] This is the textbook example of defence moving the problem downdrift.
[geo-sea-s2.12.b] In Dublin Bay a wall built for shipping changed where sand was deposited, and a whole island grew as a result.
[geo-sea-s2.12.0] The North Bull Wall was built in the 1820s to stop sand silting up the entrance to Dublin Port.
[geo-sea-s2.12.1] Sand then built up against and beside the wall, and North Bull Island grew from a sandbank into an island with dunes, salt marsh and mudflats.
[geo-sea-s2.12.2] It is an example of a human structure causing deposition, because the wall slowed the moving sand so it settled beside it.
[geo-sea-s2.13.b] Rosslare Strand is an Irish example of a resort coast that needs defending.
[geo-sea-s2.13.0] Rosslare Strand is a sandy resort coast on an eroding shoreline, so the resort needs defences to protect it.
[geo-sea-s2.13.1] It is protected by groynes and rock armour.
[geo-sea-s2.14.b] After the 1953 storm surge, the Dutch built defences that were balanced with conservation.
[geo-sea-s2.14.0] The North Sea storm surge of 1953 (a rise in sea level driven by storm winds) flooded the south-west of the Netherlands and killed about 1,800 people there.
[geo-sea-s2.14.1] The response was the Delta Works: dams, sluices (gates that control water flow) and storm-surge barriers that shortened and sealed the coast of Zeeland.
[geo-sea-s2.14.2] The Oosterschelde (Eastern Scheldt) barrier, completed in 1986, has movable gates that stay open in normal weather.
[geo-sea-s2.14.3] Because the tides still flow in, the estuary's ecosystem and shellfish industry are kept, which makes this defence balanced with conservation.
[geo-sea-s2.15.b] Defences protect single places, but a coast is one connected system. Management looks at the whole coast and balances competing needs.
[geo-sea-s2.15.0] Coasts are under pressure from erosion, development, tourism and rising sea levels.
[geo-sea-s2.15.1] Management tries to protect people and property while keeping natural processes and habitats working.
[geo-sea-s2.15.2] Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) plans a whole stretch of coast, a sediment cell (a length of coast where sediment moves as a closed system), as one unit.
[geo-sea-s2.15.3] ICZM balances defence, tourism, development and conservation, because work in one place affects places downdrift.
[geo-sea-s2.16.b] Laws and awards are tools for managing the coast. Laws limit what can be built or damaged, and awards encourage good management.
[geo-sea-s2.16.0] The EU Habitats and Birds Directives protect coastal sites through Special Areas of Conservation (SACs) for habitats and Special Protection Areas (SPAs) for birds, which together form the Natura 2000 network.
[geo-sea-s2.16.1] In Ireland, the National Parks and Wildlife Service oversees these sites, so there is a state body responsible for enforcing the protection.
[geo-sea-s2.16.2] Planning controls restrict building close to the shore.
[geo-sea-s2.16.3] Spain's Ley de Costas (Coastal Law) of 1988 was passed in response to overdevelopment and restricts building on the shore zone.
[geo-sea-s2.16.4] Blue Flag is an international beach award run by the Foundation for Environmental Education and administered in Ireland by An Taisce.
[geo-sea-s2.16.5] Its criteria cover water quality, environmental education and information, environmental management, and safety and services, encouraging councils to manage beaches well.
[geo-sea-s2.17.b] Popular dunes and cliffs can be kept open to visitors if the visitors are guided, so that trampling does less damage and people stay safe.
[geo-sea-s2.17.0] Dune management at busy beaches uses boardwalks and marked paths, fencing off eroded areas, marram replanting, moving car parks back from the dunes, information signs and seasonal wardens.
[geo-sea-s2.17.1] Example: Brittas Bay, Co. Wicklow, is a dune system heavily used by day-trippers from Dublin in summer; trampling and parking damaged the dunes, which are part of a protected Special Area of Conservation.
[geo-sea-s2.17.2] Cliffs of Moher, Co. Clare: a visitor centre, built into the hillside, opened in 2007.
[geo-sea-s2.17.3] Paths and a wall or barrier are set back from the cliff edge to control trampling and keep people safe.
[geo-sea-s2.18.b] North Bull Island, first met as a landform made by a wall, is also a protected site, with recreation and conservation pressing on the same place.
[geo-sea-s2.18.0] North Bull Island, Dublin Bay, has been a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve since 1981, and was extended in 2015 as the Dublin Bay Biosphere.
[geo-sea-s2.18.1] Its salt marsh and mudflats are a major wintering ground for Brent geese and other wildfowl (water birds) and waders (long-legged shore birds).
[geo-sea-s2.18.2] Pressures include the Royal Dublin and St Anne's golf courses on the island, cars driving onto the beach, and many visitors from the city.
[geo-sea-s2.18.3] It shows recreation, conservation and a human-made landform in one place.
[geo-sea-s2.19.b] Medmerry is the example of managed retreat used as a management measure, where the sea is let in on purpose.
[geo-sea-s2.19.0] Medmerry, West Sussex, England, was completed in 2013 as a managed realignment scheme.
[geo-sea-s2.19.1] The shingle bank was no longer maintained, a new embankment was built further inland, and the sea was let in to create intertidal habitat (land flooded and exposed by the tide).
[geo-sea-s2.19.2] This intertidal habitat absorbs wave energy, so it acts as a natural defence for the land behind the new embankment.
[geo-sea-s2.20.b] For a long answer you need to weigh the options against each other. Each type of measure has strengths and limits.
[geo-sea-s2.20.0] Hard defences protect a place, but they move erosion downdrift and are costly, so the problem is shifted rather than solved.
[geo-sea-s2.20.1] Soft measures are cheaper and more sustainable because they work with natural processes, but they are slower and less certain.
[geo-sea-s2.20.2] The best management works with the sediment system along a whole coast, because work in one place affects places downdrift.

[geo-sea-s1.15.b] Every coastal landform is either erosional (made by wearing rock away) or depositional (made by dropping material). Sorting them correctly is …
[geo-sea-s1.15.0] Erosional features: cliff, wave-cut notch, wave-cut platform, cave, sea arch, sea stack, sea stump, blowhole and headland.
[geo-sea-s1.15.1] Depositional features: beach, storm beach, sand spit, sand bar, lagoon, tombolo, sand dunes and salt marsh.
[geo-sea-s1.15.2] Matching a photograph, aerial photograph or diagram to these features uses the same two classes.
[geo-sea-s1.16.b] The average annual rate of coastal erosion is the total distance retreated divided by the number of years. It is a short calculation that need…
[geo-sea-s1.16.0] Step 1: subtract the earlier year from the later year to find the number of years.
[geo-sea-s1.16.1] Step 2: divide the total distance the cliff retreated by the number of years, so the answer is a distance per year.
[geo-sea-s1.16.2] Illustrative figures (invented): a cliff retreats 50 m between 1990 and 2015.
[geo-sea-s1.16.3] Years: 2015 minus 1990 gives 25 years.
[geo-sea-s1.16.4] Rate: 50 m divided by 25 years gives an average of 2 m per year.
