TARGET geo-region-european-s4: 4. The complexity of regions (ii)
[geo-region-european-s4.0.b] The Gaeltacht is the area where Irish is the community language, the language people use in everyday life. Its extent has shrunk as speakers have declined, and the decline began in the 1800s.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.0] Census 1851 (a count of the whole population) was the first census to count Irish speakers. It found about 1.5 million Irish speakers, about 23% of the population, and Irish-speaking areas then covered much of the west and south.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.1] The Great Famine (1845–49) and emigration (people leaving the country) hit the poor, western, Irish-speaking areas hardest, so the Irish-speaking population fell fastest where Irish was strongest.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.2] National schools, set up in 1831, taught through English, so children learned to read and write in a language that was not their home language.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.3] English became the language of law, trade, politics, the Church and emigration. Parents saw English as the route to work, so many chose not to pass Irish on to their children.
[geo-region-european-s4.0.4] Census 1891 shows the result: about 680,000 Irish speakers, about 14% of the population, down from about 23% in 1851.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.b] From the 1890s people tried to reverse the decline. The new state then gave the Gaeltacht its first official boundaries, and later redrew them.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.0] The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge), a revival movement, founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill, promoted the language, to stop its decline.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.1] The new state's language policy made Irish compulsory in schools and required for some public jobs, so that the state itself supported the language.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.2] Coimisiún na Gaeltachta (the commission set up to define the Gaeltacht) reported in 1926 and defined two types of Gaeltacht. Fíor-Ghaeltacht ('true' Gaeltacht): districts where 80% or more spoke Irish.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.3] Breac-Ghaeltacht ('partly' Gaeltacht): districts with 25–79% Irish speakers. The 1926 definitions together covered a large area across many western and southern counties.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.4] The Gaeltacht Areas Order 1956 (a government order, not a new law) shrank the official Gaeltacht to areas where Irish was still the community language, because Irish had weakened in the Breac-Ghaeltacht districts.
[geo-region-european-s4.1.5] The Department of the Gaeltacht (Roinn na Gaeltachta) was set up in 1956. Gaeltarra Éireann, the Gaeltacht industry body, followed in 1957. Later small extensions were made by order.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.b] Today's Gaeltacht lies in seven counties, and one of them, Meath, is an unusual case because its Gaeltacht was planned by the state.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.0] Gaeltacht areas lie in seven counties: Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford and Meath.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.1] The Meath Gaeltacht was built by planned colonies (settlements created by the state, not areas where Irish had survived): Ráth Chairn, settled in 1935 by families moved from Connemara, and Baile Ghib, settled in 1937.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.2] Galway (Conamara) has the largest Gaeltacht population; Donegal is second.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.3] Census 2022: the Gaeltacht population was about 106,000, and about 20,000 Gaeltacht residents speak Irish daily outside the education system, so only a minority use it as an everyday language.
[geo-region-european-s4.2.4] Nationally (Census 2022): about 1.87 million people (about 40%) said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.b] The state and communities have built institutions to protect Irish, from development agencies to broadcasting and official recognition.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.0] Údarás na Gaeltachta, the Gaeltacht development authority, was set up in 1980 to replace Gaeltarra Éireann. It creates jobs so people can stay in the Gaeltacht. The Gaoth Dobhair (Gweedore) industrial estate in Co. Donegal is an example of Gaeltacht industry.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.1] Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972. TG4 (originally Teilifís na Gaeilge) launched on 31 October 1996. Both give Irish a presence in the media.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.2] The Official Languages Act 2003 (amended 2021) gives Irish official status, which means public bodies must provide services in it.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.3] Irish became an official EU language in 2007 and gained full working-language status on 1 January 2022.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.4] The 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language, a long-term state plan, covers 2010 to 2030.
[geo-region-european-s4.3.5] Gaeltacht summer colleges give young people intensive immersion in Irish, so they use the language all day.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.b] The 2012 Act changed how the Gaeltacht is defined. Instead of a fixed map, boundaries now depend on how much Irish is actually spoken.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.0] The Gaeltacht Act 2012 defined the Gaeltacht by language use rather than by a fixed map, so boundaries now depend on language use.
[geo-region-european-s4.4.1] Gaeltacht Language Planning Areas: 26, so the Gaeltacht is now a set of planning units, each needing a language plan (a local plan to strengthen the use of Irish).
[geo-region-european-s4.4.2] Gaeltacht Service Towns, and Irish Language Networks outside the Gaeltacht, extend Irish use beyond the core areas.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.b] Even with these supports, the use of Irish as a daily language in the Gaeltacht has kept falling. A 2007 study set out how much daily use is needed to keep it alive.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.0] The Comprehensive Linguistic Study of the Use of Irish in the Gaeltacht (2007) found that Irish is viable as a community language only where at least 67% of people speak it daily.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.1] Its 2015 update warned that Irish could cease to be the community language in most Gaeltacht districts within about ten years.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.2] In-migration of English speakers (people moving in from outside) weakens Irish, because English becomes the language of daily life in the area.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.3] Suburban growth, such as Galway city's spread west, brings the Gaeltacht edge into contact with English-speaking suburbs.
[geo-region-european-s4.5.4] Tourism, emigration of young people and English-language media add further pressure on Irish as the language of the community.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.b] Responses target the pressures above, by keeping Irish speakers in the Gaeltacht and giving them reasons and ways to use the language.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.0] Housing with language conditions, so that Irish speakers can afford to stay in Gaeltacht areas.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.1] Jobs through Údarás na Gaeltachta and Gaeltacht industry, so that people do not have to leave to find work.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.2] Irish-medium schooling (teaching through Irish) and preschools, so children learn the language early.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.3] Language plans in each Language Planning Area.
[geo-region-european-s4.6.4] Family supports for Irish-speaking households, and Irish-language media such as TG4 and Raidió na Gaeltachta.
[geo-region-european-s4.7.b] Belgium is a brief comparison from outside Ireland: a language boundary fixed in law. There is more on its present-day relations in the notes on the next part of this topic.
[geo-region-european-s4.7.0] The language border between Dutch- and French-speaking areas was fixed by laws of 1962 and 1963, so the boundary between language regions became official.
[geo-region-european-s4.7.1] The first state reform came in 1970. Belgium became a federal state in 1993 (power shared between central and regional governments), with Flanders, Wallonia and Brussels-Capital as its regions. Brussels is officially bilingual.
[geo-region-european-s4.8.b] Dublin's growth has spread from the city into the surrounding counties, creating a city region (a city and the area that depends on it).
[geo-region-european-s4.8.0] Suburban expansion (new housing on the edge of the city) from the 1950s and 1960s led to the Advisory Regional Plan for Dublin (Myles Wright, 1967), which proposed new towns west of the city: Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown.
[geo-region-european-s4.8.1] Ballymun, an example of high-rise housing: seven 15-storey tower blocks built 1966–69 and demolished between 2004 and 2015 under a regeneration plan (a plan to rebuild and improve a run-down area).
[geo-region-european-s4.8.2] Census 2022: County Dublin had about 1.46 million people.
[geo-region-european-s4.8.3] The Greater Dublin Area (Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow) had about 2.1 million people in Census 2022, about 40% of the State's population.
[geo-region-european-s4.8.4] Commuter towns (towns whose residents travel to work in the city) in the surrounding counties, such as Navan, Drogheda, Naas and Newbridge.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.b] As Dublin grew, the areas that govern it were redrawn several times, from adding townships to splitting the county council.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.0] Rathmines and Pembroke townships were added to Dublin city in 1930, extending the city boundary.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.1] Local Government (Dublin) Act 1993: from 1 January 1994, Dublin County Council was replaced by Fingal, South Dublin and Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown County Councils. The county had grown too large to govern as one unit, and local government needed to be closer to the people.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.2] Dublin Corporation was renamed Dublin City Council in 2002 under the Local Government Act 2001.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.3] Census 2022 populations of the four Dublin local authorities: Dublin City about 590,000; Fingal about 330,000; South Dublin about 300,000; Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown about 235,000.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.4] The Dublin Regional Authority was set up in 1994.
[geo-region-european-s4.9.5] The Local Government Reform Act 2014 replaced the regional authorities, including the Dublin Regional Authority, with three regional assemblies from 2015: Northern and Western; Eastern and Midland; Southern.
[geo-region-european-s4.10.b] A growing city region brings problems, and the state has responded with planning, transport, regeneration and new forms of governance.
[geo-region-european-s4.10.0] Problems: urban sprawl (a city spreading outwards over farmland), long commutes, congestion, housing costs, one-off housing (single houses built in the countryside) and dormitory growth (towns that people sleep in but work elsewhere).
[geo-region-european-s4.10.1] The National Planning Framework (Project Ireland 2040), published in 2018, aims for half of future population growth in the five cities (Dublin, Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford) and to limit sprawl.
[geo-region-european-s4.10.2] Transport: the M50 orbital motorway (a ring road around the city) was built in stages from 1990, the Luas light rail opened in 2004, and DART serves the suburbs by rail.
[geo-region-european-s4.10.3] Regeneration: the IFSC (International Financial Services Centre) was set up in the Docklands in 1987; the Docklands and Ballymun are regeneration areas.
[geo-region-european-s4.10.4] The Citizens' Assembly reported in 2023, recommending a directly elected mayor (a mayor chosen by voters, not by councillors) for Dublin.
[geo-region-european-s4.10.5] Limerick elected Ireland's first directly elected mayor in June 2024, after a plebiscite (a public vote on one question) in 2019.
[geo-region-european-s4.11.b] Dublin is not the only place where boundaries moved. Other cities were extended or merged, and the EU divided Ireland into economic regions for funding.
[geo-region-european-s4.11.0] Cork city boundary extended on 31 May 2019, taking in suburbs such as Ballincollig, Glanmire and Blarney from the county.
[geo-region-european-s4.11.1] Limerick City and County Council and Waterford City and County Council were formed by merger in 2014.
[geo-region-european-s4.11.2] From 1999/2000 Ireland was divided for EU purposes into two NUTS 2 regions (the EU's standard statistical regions): the Border, Midland and Western (BMW) region, and the Southern and Eastern region.
[geo-region-european-s4.11.3] The BMW region kept Objective 1 (poorest-region) funding status for 2000–06, which meant it qualified for the highest level of EU support.
[geo-region-european-s4.11.4] The economic regions were drawn for EU funding, so their boundaries follow economic need rather than language or identity.
[geo-region-european-s4.12.b] The European Union began as a way to make war between European states impossible, by tying their economies together after the Second World War.
[geo-region-european-s4.12.0] Reasons: peace after two world wars; rebuilding after the war; coal and steel placed under shared control, so no state could secretly build up weapons; economic growth; the Cold War (the rivalry between the communist East and the capitalist West).
[geo-region-european-s4.12.1] Schuman Declaration, 9 May 1950 (now Europe Day), proposed pooling coal and steel production.
[geo-region-european-s4.12.2] The Treaty of Paris (1951) put the shared control of coal and steel into practice, because it set up the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC).
[geo-region-european-s4.12.3] The Treaty of Rome (1957, in force 1958) set up the European Economic Community (EEC) and Euratom (the European Atomic Energy Community).
[geo-region-european-s4.12.4] The six founders ('the Six') were Belgium, France, West Germany, Italy, Luxembourg and the Netherlands.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.b] Deepening means the members drew closer together, with more shared rules and institutions. Only the dates are given here; the issues each step raised come later.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.0] Single European Act: 1986, in force 1987.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.1] Single market (one market with free movement of goods, services, people and money, so trade between members is easier): from 1 January 1993.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.2] Maastricht Treaty: signed 7 February 1992; in force 1 November 1993, creating the European Union.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.3] Euro (the shared currency): launched 1 January 1999 for banks and electronic payments; notes and coins from 1 January 2002.
[geo-region-european-s4.13.4] Lisbon Treaty: in force 1 December 2009.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.b] Widening means adding new member states. Each enlargement is listed by date, with the countries that joined.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.0] 1973: Ireland, the UK and Denmark, the first enlargement. 1981: Greece. 1986: Spain and Portugal.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.1] 1990: East Germany joined through reunification with West Germany, not as a new member state.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.2] 1995: Austria, Finland and Sweden.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.3] 2004: ten countries joined: Cyprus, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.4] 2007: Bulgaria and Romania, not 2004. 2013: Croatia, the most recent member.
[geo-region-european-s4.14.5] The UK left on 31 January 2020, so it is now a former member, leaving 27 members with about 450 million people (2024).
[geo-region-european-s4.15.b] The end of communism in Eastern Europe made the biggest enlargement possible, and it changed the EU for both new and old members.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.0] The Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989, opening the way for former communist states to seek EU membership.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.1] Copenhagen criteria (1993), the conditions for joining: stable democracy, rule of law, human rights and protection of minorities; a working market economy able to cope with competition; and the ability to take on the obligations of membership.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.2] Most of the 2004 entrants (such as Poland and Hungary), and both 2007 entrants, were former communist states, which is why the fall of communism led to this enlargement.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.3] Effects on newer members: economic growth, EU funds and emigration (workers leaving for richer members).
[geo-region-european-s4.15.4] Effects on older members: competition for jobs and investment, and migration from the newer members.
[geo-region-european-s4.15.5] Croatia joined the Schengen area (the area of passport-free travel between member states) in 2023. Bulgaria and Romania joined it fully on 1 January 2025.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.b] Enlargement brought Ireland both opportunities and competition. Ireland opened its doors early in 2004, which shaped who came and what followed.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.0] In 2004 Ireland, the UK and Sweden were the only existing members to give the new members' citizens immediate access to their labour markets.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.1] Polish was the most common language other than English and Irish spoken at home in Census 2016 and Census 2022, showing the size of the Polish community.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.2] Advantages: a larger market; a labour supply for the Celtic Tiger economy (Ireland's period of rapid growth); cultural diversity; export opportunities.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.3] Disadvantages: competition for foreign direct investment (companies from abroad setting up factories and offices) from lower-cost states; less EU regional funding for Ireland; pressure on wages and services.
[geo-region-european-s4.16.4] Example of investment competition: Dell announced in January 2009 that it would move its manufacturing from Limerick to Łódź, Poland, with the loss of about 1,900 jobs.
[geo-region-european-s4.17.b] Further expansion would affect both the EU and the countries waiting to join. The current candidate countries are named in a separate subsection.
[geo-region-european-s4.17.0] Benefits of further expansion for the EU: a larger single market with more consumers, which means more trade, and greater stability on its borders.
[geo-region-european-s4.17.1] Benefits for candidates: access to the single market and to EU funding, and, because they must meet the Copenhagen criteria, reforms to meet the Copenhagen criteria (the conditions for joining).
[geo-region-european-s4.18.b] Partition of Ireland (dividing the island into two political units) redrew a political boundary across a single island. Its present-day effects, including the Good Friday Agreement and Brexit, are in the next subsection.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.0] The Government of Ireland Act 1920 partitioned the island. Northern Ireland was established in 1921 with six of Ulster's nine counties, so Ulster itself was divided.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.1] Cavan, Donegal and Monaghan went to the Irish Free State.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.2] The Anglo-Irish Treaty was signed in December 1921.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.3] Boundary Commission (1925), the body set up to review the border: its report was not published, and the border was left unchanged.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.4] The border created a nationalist minority in the north (people who wanted a united Ireland) and a small Protestant minority in the south.
[geo-region-european-s4.18.5] Impact: nationalists in Northern Ireland faced discrimination, which fuelled the civil rights movement from 1968 and then the Troubles (the conflict in Northern Ireland).
[geo-region-european-s4.18.6] Communities, parishes and farms were divided by the border, so everyday life was cut across.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.b] Yugoslavia was a multi-ethnic federal state that fell apart in the 1990s. Its break-up shows how new borders can leave cultural groups on the wrong side.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.0] Yugoslavia was a multi-ethnic federal state. Its new borders followed the old internal republic lines, leaving Serb, Croat and Bosniak minorities on the 'wrong' side of them.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.1] Slovenia and Croatia declared independence in 1991, breaking away from the federal state; Bosnia and Herzegovina followed in 1992.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.2] War and ethnic cleansing (forcing a group out of an area by violence) followed in Bosnia and Herzegovina.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.3] Srebrenica, July 1995: about 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were killed by Bosnian Serb forces.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.4] The Dayton Agreement (1995) divided Bosnia and Herzegovina into two entities: the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and Republika Srpska.
[geo-region-european-s4.19.5] Montenegro became independent in 2006, a further split of the old state. Kosovo declared independence on 17 February 2008; Serbia does not recognise it.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.b] After the Second World War, borders in central Europe were moved by the victorious powers, and millions of people moved with them.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.0] In 1945 Poland's western border moved to the Oder–Neisse line. The USSR took Poland's eastern lands, so Poland shifted westward.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.1] Millions of Germans were expelled westwards, and Poles were resettled from the east into the lands Germany had lost.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.2] The Berlin Wall was built in 1961, dividing Germany and Berlin, so the city was split in two.
[geo-region-european-s4.20.3] Germany was reunified on 3 October 1990, so the division ended, after the Berlin Wall fell in 1989.
[geo-region-european-s4.21.b] The collapse of communism also split two multi-national states. One split left minorities behind; the other was peaceful.
[geo-region-european-s4.21.0] The USSR (the Soviet Union) broke up in 1991 into 15 independent states, such as Estonia and Latvia.
[geo-region-european-s4.21.1] Ethnic Russians became minorities in newly independent states. In Estonia and Latvia they make up roughly a fifth to a quarter of the population.
[geo-region-european-s4.21.2] Czechoslovakia split peacefully into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on 1 January 1993, the 'Velvet Divorce'.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.b] Cyprus is a divided island that is also an EU member, so a political boundary there has a direct effect on how EU law applies.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.0] The Turkish invasion of July 1974 divided Cyprus between Greek Cypriots in the south and Turkish Cypriots in the north.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.1] The UN buffer zone, the Green Line (a strip that keeps the two sides apart), runs through Nicosia.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.2] The 'Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus' is recognised only by Turkey, so almost no other state accepts it.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.3] 2004 Annan Plan referendum: about 65% of Turkish Cypriots voted Yes, and about 76% of Greek Cypriots voted No.
[geo-region-european-s4.22.4] Cyprus joined the EU in 2004, one of ten countries that year,, but EU law is suspended in the north, so the division continues inside the EU.
[geo-region-european-s4.23.b] A non-European example of a political boundary redrawn along religious lines.
[geo-region-european-s4.23.0] Partition of India, August 1947: India and Pakistan (including East Pakistan, now Bangladesh) were created along religious lines.
[geo-region-european-s4.23.1] Around 15 million people were displaced, and hundreds of thousands were killed in communal violence (violence between religious communities).
[geo-region-european-s4.24.b] The examples can be pulled together into three conclusions, which are useful for ending a 30-mark answer.
[geo-region-european-s4.24.0] Boundaries drawn across cultural groups create minorities, displacement and conflict, as in Ireland, Yugoslavia and India.
[geo-region-european-s4.24.1] Boundaries drawn around cultural groups, as in Belgium, can reduce conflict but can harden division.
[geo-region-european-s4.24.2] Shared institutions, such as power-sharing and EU membership, can soften the effect of a border.
