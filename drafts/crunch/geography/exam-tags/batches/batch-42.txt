QUESTION geography-pp-2019-higher-q116 (LC Geography Higher 2019 — Part Two — Q16, 80 marks)

PART 16 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Human use of land changes soil characteristics such as humus content, structure, fertility and depth, either by degrading the soil through overgrazing and desertification or by improving it through conservation.
Aspect 1 – Overgrazing in the west of Ireland:
Overgrazing means keeping more animals on land than its vegetation can support.
From the 1980s EU headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in Connemara and the mountains of Mayo, Donegal and Kerry.
Grazing stripped the heather and grass, so roots died and less litter was returned, which lowered the humus content of the soil.
Falling humus weakened the crumb structure and reduced the soil's ability to hold water and nutrients.
Trampling by livestock compacted the soil, which is called poaching on wet Irish ground, and this reduced infiltration and increased runoff.
Bare soil was removed by rain-splash, sheet wash, rills and gullies, so the topsoil became thinner and less fertile.
On the hills exposed blanket peat eroded and silt washed into rivers, damaging salmon and trout spawning grounds.
In Iceland sheep grazing prevented birch regrowth, and the light volcanic andosols were blown and washed away, leaving erosion escarpments.
Stocking rates must stay within the carrying capacity of the land if the soil's cover, structure and depth are to be kept.
Aspect 2 – Desertification in the Sahel:
Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas, resulting from climatic variations and human activities.
The Sahel is a semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, from Senegal and Mauritania to Sudan, with highly unreliable rainfall of about 200 to 600 mm a year.
Population growth concentrated cattle, goats and sheep around wells, shortened fallow periods, spread cash crops such as groundnuts and cotton and increased fuelwood cutting.
Droughts in the 1970s and 1980s left the soil bare, so wind and the heavy rain of the short wet season removed the topsoil and humus.
Loss of topsoil and humus lowers fertility, so crop yields fall.
The bare surface forms a crust, so rain runs off instead of soaking in and water retention is reduced.
Wind-blown sand and dust remove the fine particles from the soil.
Poorly managed irrigation causes salinisation, which leaves salt crusts and can make the ground bare and sterile, as on the canal-irrigated land of Punjab and Haryana in India.
Human pressure and drought act together, because overuse leaves the soil bare and drought and storms then remove it.
Aspect 3 – Conservation methods:
Conservation methods protect soil from erosion and rebuild its fertility and structure.
Contour ploughing across a slope makes the furrows trap water instead of channelling it downhill, which reduces runoff and soil loss.
Terracing cuts steep slopes into level steps, as in the rice terraces of Banaue in the Philippines, which keeps soil in place and holds water.
Shelter belts of trees and hedges cut wind speed and reduce wind erosion, and they were promoted after the Dust Bowl of the 1930s in the Great Plains of the USA.
Crop rotation, fallow and green manure such as clover restore nutrients and humus, which improves crumb structure and fertility.
Reduced tillage and mulching leave crop residue on the surface to protect the soil.
In Burkina Faso farmers revived zaï pits filled with manure or compost, which catch rainwater and concentrate nutrients around the seed in crusted soil.
In Niger farmer-managed natural regeneration has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, as the trees shade the soil, cut wind speed and add leaf litter.
Liming raises the pH of acid soils and drainage improves gleys, so soil characteristics can be improved as well as protected.

QUESTION geography-pp-2019-higher-q117 (LC Geography Higher 2019 — Part Two — Q17, 80 marks)

PART 17 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Soil is made up of mineral matter, organic matter, water and air, and each of these influences how a soil develops, including its texture, structure, fertility and horizons.
Aspect 1 – Mineral matter:
Mineral matter is particles of weathered rock, from stones to clay, and it forms the skeleton of the soil and decides its texture.
A typical good loam is about 45% mineral matter by volume.
The parent rock controls the result, as limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils such as the rendzinas of the Burren.
Granite and sandstone give sandy, acidic, free-draining soils, as on the granite of the Wicklow Mountains.
As mineral matter weathers it releases nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium.
Clay particles are the chemically active part, and with humus they hold nutrient ions, so they influence fertility.
Texture controls drainage, so a sandy soil is easily leached while a clay soil becomes waterlogged, and a loam of roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay is best for farming.
Aspect 2 – Organic matter:
Organic matter is living organisms plus dead plant and animal remains, and it makes up about 5% of a good loam.
Decomposers break litter down into humus, a dark, sticky substance that is the end product of decomposition.
Humus binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients, releases nutrients slowly and is the main store of nitrogen in the soil.
Earthworms mix and aerate the soil and drag litter down, so the boundary between the A and B horizons of a brown earth is gradual.
Mull humus, which is mild and well mixed, forms under deciduous woodland and grassland and gives brown earths, whereas acid mor humus forms under conifers and heath and gives podzols.
Humification is slow in cold, acid or waterlogged conditions, so partly decomposed matter builds up as peat, which covers about a fifth of Ireland.
Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules of legumes such as clover add nitrogen to the soil.
Aspect 3 – Water:
Water enters the soil as precipitation and is held in the pores, and the dissolved nutrients form the soil solution from which roots absorb them.
Where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, as in Ireland, gravitational water moves down and leaches bases and nutrients, which makes the upper soil more acidic and less fertile.
Mild leaching gives brown earths, while intense leaching under conifers or heath produces podzols with a bleached ash-grey E horizon and an iron-rich B horizon.
Where evaporation exceeds precipitation, capillary action raises water and leaves salts and lime near the surface, which is part of the calcification that forms chernozems.
Too much water fills all the pores and causes waterlogging, so air is excluded and iron is reduced, giving the grey, mottled gley soils of the drumlin belt of Cavan, Monaghan and Leitrim.
Capillary water held in small pores is available to plants, whereas hygroscopic water is bound tightly to particles and cannot be used.
Water also drives weathering, since hydrolysis forms clay and the rate of chemical weathering sets soil depth, with deep soils in the hot, wet tropics.
Aspect 4 – Air:
Air fills the pores not occupied by water, and about 25% of a good loam is air by volume.
Soil air supplies oxygen for root respiration and for the aerobic organisms that decompose litter.
Soil air also supplies nitrogen for nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
Soil air is richer in carbon dioxide than the atmosphere, which forms weak carbonic acid in soil water and aids chemical weathering.
In poorly aerated, anaerobic soils decomposition is slow, so organic matter builds up as peat and iron is reduced to give grey colours.
Water and air share the pore spaces, so when one rises the other falls, and humus improves crumb structure, which opens pores for both.
Compaction by heavy machinery or by poaching destroys peds and reduces air space, so drainage and root growth suffer.

PART 18 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Humans alter biomes by removing, replacing or degrading vegetation and by changing soils, water and species, as shown by the felling of the Amazon rainforest, intensive agriculture and industrial development.
Aspect 1 – Felling of the Amazon rainforest:
Brazil's space agency INPE measures clearance by satellite, and close to a fifth of the original Amazon forest has been cleared.
Clearance in the Brazilian Amazon peaked at about 27,800 km² in 2004.
Cattle ranching is the largest single cause and accounts for most of the cleared land in the Brazilian Amazon.
Soya farming spread north from Mato Grosso, and much of the soya is exported as animal feed, including to Europe and China.
The Trans-Amazonian Highway, begun in the early 1970s, opened the forest to settlers, and BR-364 into Rondônia produced a fishbone pattern of clearings.
Cleared latosols lose their fertility within a few years because nearly all the nutrients are held in the vegetation, and erosion and a hard laterite crust follow.
Less evapotranspiration means less rainfall downwind and longer dry seasons, and the loss of habitat threatens many endemic species.
Burning and decay release the forest's stored carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
Indigenous peoples such as the Yanomami lose their land, and illegal gold mining pollutes rivers with mercury.
Aspect 2 – Intensive agriculture:
Intensive farming uses high inputs of capital, machinery, fertiliser, pesticides and water to get maximum output per hectare.
In Ireland hedgerows were removed and fields enlarged, wetlands and callows were drained and species-rich meadows were reseeded with ryegrass.
Earlier silage cutting by machine destroys the nests of ground-nesting birds such as the corncrake, which survives in small numbers in north-west Donegal, west Mayo and the Shannon Callows.
Heavy use of fertiliser and slurry raises nitrate levels in rivers and causes eutrophication of lakes.
Wild bees and farmland birds have declined as their habitats were lost.
The Aral Sea, on the border of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, was the world's fourth-largest lake in 1960.
From the 1960s Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya to irrigate cotton and rice, so the sea shrank to a fraction of its former size, its salinity rose and its fishing industry collapsed.
Dust storms carry salt and pesticide residues from the exposed seabed onto farmland and people.
The tallgrass prairie of North America has been almost entirely converted to farmland, and only a few per cent survives.
Aspect 3 – Industrial development:
Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from power stations, industry and vehicles form acid rain that is carried downwind across national borders.
Acid rain acidified thousands of lakes in southern Norway and Sweden from the 1960s to the 1980s, and fish died out in many of them.
In the Black Triangle, where Germany, Poland and the Czech Republic meet, lignite-burning power stations killed large areas of spruce forest in the Ore Mountains in the 1970s and 1980s, a process known as Waldsterben.
Bord na Móna, set up in 1946, harvested milled peat from the Midland raised bogs for power stations and briquettes, which removed the bog habitat.
Its cutaway bogs are now being rehabilitated, as at Lough Boora Discovery Park in Offaly, which has been converted to wetlands, woodland and amenity land.
The Athabasca oil sands near Fort McMurray in Alberta are mined by open-pit methods that strip away boreal forest and muskeg.
The mining leaves large tailings ponds of polluted water and causes high greenhouse gas emissions per barrel.
Oil spills in the Niger Delta have polluted creeks, mangrove forest and farmland, and a UNEP study of Ogoniland in 2011 found that clean-up could take 25 to 30 years.
Greenhouse gases from industry stress biomes through coral bleaching, thawing tundra permafrost and more drought and fire.
