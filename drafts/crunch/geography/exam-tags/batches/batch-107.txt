QUESTION geography-pp-2025-higher-q119 (LC Geography Higher 2025 — Part Two — Q19, 80 marks)

PART 19 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Religion is an important cultural indicator because it shapes the landscape, daily life and identity of groups, it has a distinct world distribution, and it can feed conflict.
Aspect 1 – Religion shapes landscape and daily life:
Religion shapes beliefs, values and moral codes and often marks group identity more strongly than nationality does.
It shows in the landscape through churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, holy wells, wayside shrines and cemeteries.
It shapes diet, such as halal food for Muslims, kosher food for Jews and vegetarianism among Hindus.
It shapes dress, such as the headscarf, the turban and the kippah.
It shapes the calendar through festivals such as Christmas, Easter, Eid, Diwali and Yom Kippur.
Religion is linked with language, as in Arabic and the Qur'an or Hebrew and Judaism.
Religion is linked with nationality, as in Catholicism with Irish or Polish identity and Orthodoxy with Serbian identity.
Census questions on religion make it easy to map, and Census 2022 in Ireland recorded about 69% Roman Catholic, down from about 78% in 2016.
Secularisation, the decline of religious practice and church influence, is occurring in much of Europe, and about 14% of people in Ireland had no religion in 2022.
Aspect 2 – Distribution and spread of world religions:
Pew Research figures for 2020 show about 2.3 billion Christians, about 2 billion Muslims and about 1.2 billion Hindus, so religions have very different distributions.
Islam was founded by the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca in the early 7th century, and the Hijra to Medina in 622 begins the Islamic calendar.
Buddhism was founded by Siddhartha Gautama in north-east India around the 5th century BC.
Christianity spread through the Roman Empire, which made it the official religion in the 4th century.
Islam spread with the Arab conquests from the 7th century and reached Spain within about a century, and later it spread by trade to Indonesia and West Africa.
Irish monks such as St Columbanus spread Christianity as missionaries in continental Europe in the early medieval period.
Colonialism spread Christianity to the Americas, Africa and the Philippines.
Christianity is divided into Catholic in southern Europe, Ireland and Latin America, Protestant in northern Europe and the USA, and Orthodox in eastern Europe, Russia and Greece, after the Great Schism of 1054 and the Reformation of 1517.
Islam is strong in the Middle East, North Africa and Pakistan, Indonesia has the world's largest Muslim population, and Mecca and Medina in Saudi Arabia are its holiest cities.
Aspect 3 – Religion and conflict:
Religion can feed conflict when it overlaps with national identity and political power, and Northern Ireland shows this.
The Plantation of Ulster from 1609 settled English and Scottish Protestants on confiscated land.
The Government of Ireland Act 1920 led to partition, and Northern Ireland was established in 1921 with a Protestant unionist majority.
Catholics faced discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting, and the civil rights movement began in 1968.
The Troubles from the late 1960s to 1998 killed over 3,500 people, including 13 civil rights marchers shot dead on Bloody Sunday in Derry on 30 January 1972.
The Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998 set up a power-sharing Assembly and Executive.
Peace walls still separate Catholic and Protestant areas of Belfast, and integrated schools teach fewer than one in ten pupils.
The partition of India in August 1947 created Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan and displaced around 15 million people.
Jerusalem contains the Western Wall, the Al-Aqsa Mosque and Dome of the Rock, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, so competing claims to this holy city feed conflict between religions.

QUESTION geography-pp-2025-higher-q120 (LC Geography Higher 2025 — Part Two — Q20, 80 marks)

PART 20 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Most nation states contain several cultural groups because of historical conquest and union, boundaries drawn without regard to culture, and migration, and states respond in different ways, as Spain, Northern Ireland and Canada show.
Aspect 1 – Spain, Basques and Catalans:
Basques and Catalans have their own languages, autonomy statutes and strong regional identities within Spain.
Basque and Catalan were suppressed under Franco between 1939 and 1975.
Spain's 1978 constitution created 17 autonomous communities.
The Basque Autonomous Community, under the Statute of Gernika of 1979, and Navarre collect their own taxes.
Catalonia's autonomy statute dates from 1979 and was revised in 2006, and Barcelona is its capital.
ETA, founded in 1959, killed over 800 people in its campaign for an independent Basque state and declared a definitive end to violence in 2011.
In 2010 Spain's Constitutional Court struck down parts of the 2006 Catalan statute, including its description of Catalonia as a nation, which fuelled the independence movement.
On 1 October 2017 a Catalan independence referendum that Spain's courts had declared illegal was held, and about 90% of those voting backed independence on a turnout of about 43%.
Spain imposed direct rule under Article 155 of its constitution, and nine Catalan leaders were jailed in 2019 and pardoned in 2021.
Aspect 2 – Northern Ireland and power-sharing:
Northern Ireland was created in 1921 around a unionist majority with a large nationalist minority, so two cultural groups live in one state.
The 2021 census recorded Catholics at about 46% and Protestants and other Christians at about 43%.
Discrimination against nationalists fuelled the civil rights movement from 1968.
The Troubles killed over 3,500 people between the late 1960s and 1998.
The Good Friday Agreement signed on 10 April 1998 redesigned the political structure for the two cultural groups.
It set up a power-sharing Assembly and Executive, so each main group has a role in government.
It also created a North/South Ministerial Council.
Referendums on 22 May 1998 returned about 71% Yes in Northern Ireland and about 94% Yes in the Republic.
The Assembly was restored in February 2024 with Michelle O'Neill, a nationalist, as First Minister for the first time.
Aspect 3 – Canada and other ways states respond:
States accommodate cultural groups through federalism, devolution, power-sharing and multiculturalism, or less successfully through assimilation and suppression.
In the UK, devolution in 1999 created the Scottish Parliament, the National Assembly for Wales and the Northern Ireland Assembly.
Canada adopted an official policy of multiculturalism in 1971, which recognises and supports several cultures within one state.
Quebec is French-speaking and its identity is distinct within Canada.
Quebec's Charter of the French Language, Bill 101, was passed in 1977 to protect the French language.
Quebec held sovereignty referendums in 1980, when about 60% voted No, and in 1995, when about 50.6% voted No.
In 2006 Canada's House of Commons recognised that the Québécois form a nation within a united Canada.
The Sámi, the indigenous people of the far north of Norway, Sweden and Finland, have elected parliaments in Norway since 1989, in Sweden since 1993 and in Finland since 1996.
France follows assimilation, expecting minorities to adopt one secular French identity.

MODEL ANSWER: Cultural identity in Switzerland is defined by many diverse factors that combine and cut across one another, and four of them are examined below.
Named region – Switzerland:
Switzerland is a landlocked European country of 26 cantons, with about 60% of its territory in the Alps.
It is a Willensnation, a nation formed by the will of its people rather than by one language, religion or ethnicity.
Aspect 1 – Language and regions:
Switzerland has four national languages, German (about 62%), French (about 23%), Italian (about 8%) and Romansh (about 0.5%).
The territoriality principle gives each canton or commune its own official language or languages, used in its schools and administration.
There are 17 German-speaking cantons, 4 French-speaking cantons, Italian-speaking Ticino and trilingual Graubünden, which uses German, Romansh and Italian.
Romansh became a national language by referendum in 1938 and a partly official language in 1996, and the isolation of Alpine valleys helped preserve it.
The Röstigraben, running roughly along the River Sarine in Fribourg, is the cultural divide between German- and French-speaking Switzerland and often shows in referendum results.
The public broadcaster SRG SSR runs separate services in German, French, Italian and Romansh, so each language group keeps its own media identity.
No language group wants to join its neighbouring country, because French, Italian and German speakers all identify with Switzerland.
Aspect 2 – Religion:
The Reformation divided Switzerland, as Ulrich Zwingli led it in Zurich from 1519 and John Calvin led it in Geneva from the 1530s.
Historically Protestant cantons include Zurich, Bern, Basel and Geneva, while historically Catholic cantons include Lucerne, Uri, Schwyz, Fribourg, Valais and Ticino.
Religious conflict led to the Sonderbund War of November 1847, a short civil war in which a league of Catholic, conservative cantons was defeated.
Religion cuts across language, because both German-speaking and French-speaking Switzerland contain Protestant and Catholic cantons.
This cross-cutting reduces conflict, because language and religion do not divide the same people into two opposing blocs.
In 2022 about a third of people were Roman Catholic, about a fifth were Reformed, about a third had no religion and about 6% were Muslim.
Debate over the place of Islam shows identity tensions, as a ban on building new minarets passed with about 57.5% on 29 November 2009.
Aspect 3 – Historical development and political boundaries:
The Federal Charter of 1 August 1291, an alliance of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden, began the Confederation, and 1 August is still the national day.
The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 recognised Swiss independence from the Holy Roman Empire.
The Congress of Vienna in 1815 recognised Switzerland's permanent neutrality and fixed its borders.
The federal constitution of 1848 created the modern federal state, in which the cantons keep wide powers over schools, police, taxes and churches.
Direct democracy gives citizens a voice, as they vote in referendums several times a year and a popular initiative needs 100,000 signatures.
The Federal Council has seven members from the main parties who govern collectively, and language regions are represented by custom.
Boundaries still shape identity, as the canton of Jura was created on 1 January 1979 when the French-speaking, mainly Catholic north voted to leave German-speaking, mainly Protestant Bern.
Aspect 4 – Music, festivals and traditions:
Yodelling and the alphorn are traditional Alpine music that symbolise Swiss mountain identity.
Schwingen, Swiss wrestling in sawdust rings, is held at Alpine festivals.
Basel Fasnacht is a carnival that begins at 4 a.m. on the Monday after Ash Wednesday, and it has been on UNESCO's intangible heritage list since 2017.
Sechseläuten in Zurich in April features a parade of the guilds and the burning of the Böögg, a snowman figure, to mark the end of winter.
The Fête des Vignerons in Vevey, held only about four or five times a century and most recently in 2019, celebrates the winegrowers' tradition.
The Montreux Jazz Festival, held since 1967, and the Locarno Film Festival, held since 1946, give Switzerland an outward-looking cultural identity.
Fondue, raclette, rösti and Gruyère cheese are typical foods shared across the language groups and add to a common identity.
Evaluation:
Federalism, direct democracy and cross-cutting language and religion mean that Switzerland's divisions do not line up, so its diverse cultural factors strengthen rather than break its identity.
