QUESTION geography-pp-2021-higher-q120 (LC Geography Higher 2021 — Part Two — Q20, 80 marks)

PART "20" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Language is an important cultural indicator because it marks who belongs to a group, carries its identity and history, and is used both to unite and to divide people.
Aspect 1 – Language, identity and power:
Language carries a group's history, literature, songs, place names and ways of thinking, so it marks who belongs to a group.
Many nation states were built around a single language, and language is easy to map from census questions, so it is used to define cultural regions.
The Gaelic League, founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill, led a language revival that fed the Irish independence movement.
Suppressing a language has been used to weaken a group, as Basque and Catalan were banned from public life and schools under Franco from 1939 to 1975.
The Welsh Not was a wooden board hung round the neck of children caught speaking Welsh in some 19th-century schools.
Kurdish was banned in public life in Turkey until 1991 and in broadcasting until the early 2000s.
Colonial languages became official languages in former colonies, such as English in India and Nigeria, French in Senegal and Portuguese in Brazil.
Languages cross borders, as French is spoken in France, Belgium, western Switzerland and Quebec, so a shared language can unite people in different states.
A language border can divide a state, as Belgium's does.
Aspect 2 – The Irish language as a cultural indicator:
Place names preserve the Irish-speaking past of areas that are now English-speaking, with elements such as baile, cill, dún, inis and cluain.
Examples include Clonmel (Cluain Meala), Kilkenny (Cill Chainnigh) and Dundalk (Dún Dealgan).
Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution makes Irish the national language and the first official language.
The 1851 census, the first count, recorded about 1.5 million Irish speakers, about 23% of the population.
Census 2022 showed that about 1.87 million people, about 40%, could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
Gaeltacht areas lie in seven counties, with the largest in Conamara and the Aran Islands in County Galway and others in Gaoth Dobhair in Donegal and Corca Dhuibhne in Kerry.
The Comprehensive Linguistic Study of 2007 found that Irish is viable as a community language only where at least 67% speak it daily.
Údarás na Gaeltachta, set up in 1980, grant-aids firms in the Gaeltacht and supports Irish-language community projects.
The Official Languages Act 2003 gave the public the right to deal with public bodies in Irish, and An Coimisinéir Teanga enforces it.
Irish became an official EU language in 2007, with full working-language status from 1 January 2022.
Aspect 3 – Threats to and survival of minority languages:
About 40% of the world's languages are endangered, and minority languages decline through conquest, the need for a dominant language for jobs and education, emigration and urbanisation.
When parents do not pass a language on, inter-generational transmission breaks.
Television, film, music and the internet are dominated by English, and about half of all websites are in English.
English loanwords such as le weekend enter other languages, so the pressure falls on small national languages as well as on minority languages.
France protects French through the Toubon Law of 1994, which requires French in advertising, product labels and public signage.
Quebec's Charter of the French Language, Bill 101 of 1977, made French the sole official language of Quebec.
Media protect minority languages, as TG4 was launched on 31 October 1996 in Baile na hAbhann and Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972.
Welsh has official status and is compulsory in schools up to age 16, and the Welsh-language channel S4C launched on 1 November 1982.
Census 2021 found that about 538,000 people in Wales could speak Welsh, but its share of the population had fallen since 2011, which shows the limits of revival.
Manx, whose last native speaker died in 1974, has been revived, with a Manx-medium primary school.

QUESTION geography-pp-2021-higher-q121 (LC Geography Higher 2021 — Part Two — Q21, 80 marks)

PART "21" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: People in Ireland express their culture and identity in everyday life through sport, traditional music and dance, and festivals and traditions.
Aspect 1 – Sport and the GAA:
The GAA was founded on 1 November 1884 in Hayes' Hotel, Thurles, County Tipperary, by Michael Cusack and others to preserve and promote native Irish games.
Maurice Davin was its first president.
It promotes hurling and Gaelic football, and the Camogie Association, founded in 1904, runs the women's form of hurling.
Clubs are based on parishes and teams represent counties, so the GAA reinforces local and county identity.
Players are amateurs, and supporters wear their county colours as everyday dress that shows county identity.
Members were once banned from playing foreign games such as soccer and rugby, and the ban was removed in 1971.
On Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920, British forces opened fire at a football match in Croke Park and killed 14 people.
Croke Park holds about 82,300 spectators and is one of Europe's largest stadiums.
Hurling was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2018, and the GAA has clubs among the Irish diaspora abroad.
The Ireland rugby team represents the whole island, and sporting loyalties in Northern Ireland divide between Irish and British teams.
Aspect 2 – Traditional music and dance:
Traditional instruments include the fiddle, uilleann pipes, tin whistle, flute, concertina, accordion, bodhrán and harp.
The harp is Ireland's national symbol and appears on coins and the presidential seal.
Sessions are informal gatherings where musicians play together and share tunes.
Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann was founded in 1951 to promote traditional music.
The first Fleadh Cheoil na hÉireann was held in Mullingar in 1951, and it is now held in a different town each August and draws hundreds of thousands of visitors.
Sean-nós is unaccompanied traditional singing in Irish and is strongest in Conamara.
Uilleann piping was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2017 and Irish harping in 2019.
Riverdance began as the interval act of the Eurovision Song Contest in Dublin on 30 April 1994.
It became a worldwide touring show that carried Irish dance to audiences abroad.
Aspect 3 – Festivals and traditions:
St Patrick's Day on 17 March is marked by parades around the world, as in New York, so it is a global expression of Irish identity in the diaspora.
Dublin's St Patrick's Festival began in 1996.
St Brigid's Day became a public holiday in 2023, the first named after a woman.
Samhain, on 31 October and 1 November, is the Celtic origin of Halloween.
Puck Fair in Killorglin, County Kerry, is held each August, when a wild goat is crowned king.
Ballinasloe Horse Fair in County Galway is held every October, is one of Europe's oldest horse fairs, and is also important to Irish Travellers.
The Rose of Tralee has been held since 1959 and is open to women of Irish birth or descent worldwide.
Lisdoonvarna Matchmaking Festival in County Clare is held every September.
In Northern Ireland, Orange Order parades on 12 July commemorate the Battle of the Boyne in 1690 and express unionist identity, but they can also divide communities.

QUESTION geography-pp-2021-higher-q122 (LC Geography Higher 2021 — Part Two — Q22, 80 marks)

PART "22" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Temperature is distributed unevenly over the earth's surface because of latitude, the distribution of land and sea, and the effects of ocean currents, altitude and winds.
Aspect 1 – Latitude:
Latitude is the main control, so temperatures generally fall from the equator to the poles and isotherms run broadly east–west.
At the equator the sun's rays strike nearly vertically and concentrate energy on a small area.
Towards the poles the same beam strikes at a low angle and is spread over a larger area.
Low-angle rays pass through more atmosphere, so more energy is absorbed, scattered and reflected before it reaches the ground.
Snow-covered polar regions reflect most of the little radiation they receive, as fresh snow reflects about 80 to 90%.
Between the equator and about 35 to 40 degrees north and south more energy is received than is radiated away, giving a surplus.
Poleward of that more energy is lost than received, giving a deficit, so the tropics would keep heating and the poles cooling without a poleward transfer of heat by winds and ocean currents.
The tilt of the axis, about 23.5°, makes the overhead sun migrate between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, giving seasons and changing day length.
The poles have months of continuous daylight in summer and continuous darkness in winter, while the equator has almost equal day and night all year.
The thermal equator, the line of highest mean temperature, lies mostly north of the geographical equator because the northern hemisphere has more land.
Aspect 2 – Land and sea:
Land heats and cools quickly, while the sea heats and cools slowly because water has a high specific heat, mixes, is transparent and evaporates.
Continental interiors therefore have hot summers, cold winters and a large annual range, while coasts and islands have a small range.
Ireland's annual range is only about 10 °C, with a mean January of about 4 to 7 °C and a mean July of about 14 to 16 °C.
Verkhoyansk in north-east Siberia has a mean January of about −45 °C and a mean July of about 15 °C, an annual range of over 60 °C.
Isotherms bend where they cross coasts.
The hottest places are subtropical deserts, not the equator, where cloud and rain limit heating, and Furnace Creek in Death Valley recorded 56.7 °C on 10 July 1913.
The coldest places are the interior of Antarctica and north-east Siberia in winter, and Vostok Station in Antarctica recorded −89.2 °C on 21 July 1983.
Cloudy regions reflect much insolation and keep nights warmer by trapping long-wave radiation, while clear-skied deserts have a very large daily range.
On 20 June 2020 Verkhoyansk recorded 38 °C, the highest temperature recorded in the Arctic.
Aspect 3 – Ocean currents, altitude and winds:
Warm currents raise coastal temperatures, as the North Atlantic Drift, the extension of the Gulf Stream, keeps Ireland and Britain mild.
January isotherms bend sharply northward over the north-east Atlantic because of the Drift.
Norway's coast stays ice-free in winter even north of the Arctic Circle at Hammerfest, and Murmansk in Russia is an ice-free Arctic port, because of the warm water of the Drift.
Cold currents lower coastal temperatures, as the Humboldt and Benguela currents do on the coasts of Peru and south-west Africa.
The coast of Labrador in Canada, at about the same latitude as Ireland, has long freezing winters and sea ice because the cold Labrador Current flows south past it.
Temperature falls by about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m of altitude, so mountains are cooler than lowlands at the same latitude.
Mount Kilimanjaro in Tanzania is about 5,900 m high and only about 3° south of the equator, yet it is snow-capped.
Onshore westerlies carry ocean warmth in winter and ocean coolness in summer over the west coasts of the mid-latitudes.
Winds from cold continental interiors bring cold spells to coastal areas such as Ireland.
South-facing slopes in the northern hemisphere receive more sun and are warmer, which is why vineyards are often planted on them.
