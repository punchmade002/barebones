PART "10B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Chosen option – Rural to urban migration in India:
Rural to urban migration in India is the movement of people from poorer rural states, mainly Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, to Mumbai, Delhi and Kolkata.
Push factors are small landholdings, debt, landlessness and drought, and pull factors are jobs in construction, factories and the informal sector.
About 31 per cent of Indians were urban in Census 2011, up from about 17 per cent in 1951.
Donor region – rural Bihar and Uttar Pradesh:
Bihar is India's densest state, at about 1,100 people per square kilometre, so many families cannot be supported by small farms.
Male out-migration leaves women, children and the old to do the farm work.
The donor region loses working-age labour, which lowers farm output and local development.
Remittances sent home fund houses, education and farm inputs, which raise living standards in the villages.
Villages that depend on migrants are vulnerable, as in March 2020 when millions of migrant workers walked or travelled home from the cities during the COVID-19 lockdown.
Fewer young adults remain, so rural schools and services decline.
Receiver region – Mumbai:
Greater Mumbai had about 18.4 million people in Census 2011, so it is a major receiver of migrants.
Migration puts huge pressure on housing, water, sanitation and transport.
About 65 million people lived in slums in India, and Dharavi in Mumbai is one of Asia's largest slums, covering about 2 square kilometres near the city centre.
Dharavi supports many small industries such as recycling, leather, pottery and garments, so migrants find informal work.
Cities gain a large supply of cheap labour for construction and factories.
Delhi, at about 16.3 million, and Kolkata, at about 14.1 million, also receive migrants, and Kolkata's slums are called bustees.
Urban growth is also fuelled by natural increase among the young migrants.
Informal work gives low pay and no legal protection, so receiver regions face poverty alongside growth.

PART "10C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Emmeloord in the Noordoostpolder:
Emmeloord is the central town of the Noordoostpolder in the Netherlands, which was drained in 1942.
Central place theory defined:
Walter Christaller, a German geographer, published central place theory in 1933 after studying southern Germany.
A central place is a settlement that provides goods and services to people in its surrounding area.
Threshold and range:
Threshold is the minimum number of customers needed to keep a service in business.
Range is the maximum distance people will travel to obtain a service.
A service locates where its threshold falls within its range, so it will not survive if too few people live within travelling distance.
Order of goods and the hierarchy:
Low-order goods such as bread and newspapers are bought often, have a low threshold and are found in every village.
High-order goods such as department stores, hospitals and universities have a high threshold and are found only in large centres.
This produces a hierarchy with many small, closely spaced low-order centres and fewer, more widely spaced high-order centres.
Each higher-order centre also supplies all the lower-order goods.
Assumptions and predictions:
The theory assumes a flat, uniform plain with an evenly spread population, equal purchasing power and equal ease of transport in all directions.
It assumes that consumers buy at the nearest centre.
Market areas are hexagonal, because circles overlap or leave gaps and hexagons fit together with no gaps.
Nesting follows set principles, with k equal to 3 for the marketing principle, 4 for the transport principle and 7 for the administrative principle.
Evidence and limitations:
In the Noordoostpolder, Emmeloord was planned as the central town with ten villages around it, including Marknesse, Ens, Kraggenburg and Bant.
The villages were spaced so that farmers could reach one by bicycle, giving a planned two-level hierarchy.
In parts of the Irish Midlands, market towns serving farming areas are regularly spaced, and Ireland's hierarchy runs from Dublin to Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford, then county towns and villages.
The theory is limited because relief, rivers and coasts are not uniform and transport routes are linear.
Car ownership, motorways, out-of-town shopping centres and online shopping change ranges and thresholds, and Dublin's primacy breaks the regular hierarchy in Ireland.

PART "11B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named Irish urban centre – Galway:
Galway began as a Norman town founded by the de Burgo family in the 13th century and has changed its functions several times.
Function 1 – Medieval port and trading town:
Galway was a port, governed by 14 merchant families known as the Tribes of Galway, and received a royal charter in 1484.
It traded wine with Spain and France, and the Spanish Arch, built in 1584, survives as part of the town walls.
This trading function declined after Galway surrendered to Cromwellian forces in 1652, and a long decline followed.
Function 2 – Education and industry:
Queen's College Galway was founded in 1845 and is now the University of Galway, giving the city an education function.
Digital Equipment Corporation opened a plant in Galway in 1971, which gave the city a manufacturing function.
That plant closed its manufacturing in 1993, showing how functions are lost over time.
Function 3 – Medical devices, tourism and culture:
Galway is now a centre of the medical devices industry, with multinationals such as Medtronic and Boston Scientific.
The National Spatial Strategy of 2002 named Galway a gateway, and it is the regional capital of the West.
Galway was European Capital of Culture in 2020, and the Galway Arts Festival and the Galway Races at Ballybrit attract visitors, giving it a tourism function.
Second Irish urban centre – Limerick:
Limerick began as a Viking settlement from about 922, and King John's Castle was built in the early 13th century on King's Island, giving it defence functions.
Limerick developed as a Georgian commercial port and later became an industrial city based on its port and flour milling.
Dell opened in Limerick in 1990 and announced in January 2009 that it was moving manufacturing to Poland with the loss of about 1,900 jobs.
The University of Limerick became a university in 1989, and Limerick was Ireland's first National City of Culture in 2014, so its functions moved towards education and culture.
General trends in Irish urban functions:
Dublin changed from a Viking trading port to an administrative capital, a Georgian city, a port and industrial centre, and then a services and technology centre in the Docklands, including the International Financial Services Centre in 1987.
Across Irish towns, defence functions were lost, industry moved from the inner city to the suburbs or closed, and services such as offices, tourism, education and health grew.

PART "11C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Change 1 – Population size:
The population of the State fell to about 2.8 million in Census 1961, the lowest ever recorded, because of heavy emigration.
It rose to about 3.4 million in 1981, about 4.2 million in 2006 and about 5.15 million in Census 2022, above 5 million for the first time since 1851.
The growth was due to natural increase and net immigration, and from 2016 to 2022 net migration of about 190,000 was slightly larger than natural increase of about 170,000.
Change 2 – Births and fertility:
The total fertility rate was about 4 in the early 1970s but fell below replacement level around 1990 and was about 1.5 in 2023.
Births peaked at about 74,000 in 1980 and were about 55,000 in 2023.
Free secondary education from 1967 and more women in third-level education led to smaller families.
The marriage bar was removed in 1973 and contraception was legalised by the Health (Family Planning) Act 1979, which gave women more choice about family size.
More women in paid employment, later marriage, secularisation and the cost of housing and childcare also lowered fertility.
Change 3 – Age structure and life expectancy:
Life expectancy at birth rose to about 82 to 83 years in the early 2020s because of better diet, housing, health services and vaccination.
Infant mortality fell to about 3 per 1,000 live births.
About 776,000 people were aged 65 and over in Census 2022, about 15 per cent of the population, up about 22 per cent since 2016.
The average age rose from 37.4 in 2016 to 38.8 in 2022, so the pyramid has changed from a young, wide-based shape to an ageing one.
Change 4 – Migration and nationality:
Net emigration of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961 was driven by economic stagnation and Britain's demand for labour.
The 1970s saw the first net immigration since the Famine, as emigrants returned and new factories created jobs.
Emigration returned in the 1980s and after 2008, but net immigration from 1996 to 2008 and again since the mid-2010s turned Ireland into a country of immigration.
About 630,000 non-Irish nationals lived in Ireland in Census 2022, about 12 per cent of the population, with Polish people the largest group at about 94,000.
Change 5 – Urbanisation and households:
Census 1971 was the first in which more people lived in towns than in rural areas, and by Census 2016 about 63 per cent were urban.
The Greater Dublin Area has about 40 per cent of the State's population, and households are now smaller with more people living alone.
