PART "24" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Climate characteristics such as temperature, rainfall and seasons vary from place to place, and they have also changed over time scales from a few years to millions of years.
Aspect 1 – Change with latitude and altitude:
From the equator to the poles temperature falls and the contrast between seasons grows, because the sun's rays strike at a lower angle at higher latitudes.
The equatorial belt under the rising air of the ITCZ is hot and wet all year, while hot deserts lie near 30° where air sinks in the subtropical highs.
The pressure belts shift with the seasons, which gives the Mediterranean its hot dry summers under the subtropical high.
Temperature falls with height, so high mountains have a vertical sequence of climates, and snow lies on high ground even near the equator.
Relief changes rainfall, because the windward side of a mountain range gets relief rain while the leeward side lies in a rain shadow, as the Deccan does in India.
In Ireland rainfall varies from about 750 mm in the driest east to over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
Climate boundaries are transition zones that shift, and the Sahel, between the Sahara and wetter land, moves with the ITCZ from year to year.
The Sahel receives roughly 200 to 600 mm of rain in one short summer wet season.
Aspect 2 – Distance from the sea and currents:
Climate also changes from coast to interior, because the sea heats and cools slowly while land responds quickly.
Ireland's annual temperature range is only about 10 °C, while Verkhoyansk in the continental interior of Siberia has a range of over 60 °C.
Maritime climates therefore have small temperature ranges, while continental climates have very cold winters and hot summers.
Warm currents such as the North Atlantic Drift warm west coasts, so Ireland and north-west Europe are mild for their latitude.
Cold currents cool and dry west coasts on desert margins, including the Canary Current by the Sahara, the Benguela Current by the Namib and the Humboldt Current by the Atacama.
Winds from the sea bring rain, so rainfall in Ireland is highest in the west where Atlantic depressions arrive first.
Aspect 3 – Ice Ages and long-term change:
Over millions of years climate has changed because of plate movement, mountain building and changes in ocean circulation.
The Quaternary glaciation began about 2.6 million years ago, and the earth has swung between long glacial periods and shorter warm interglacials, which over the last 800,000 years have followed a cycle of about 100,000 years.
Milankovitch cycles cause this, as eccentricity varies over about 100,000 years, obliquity over about 41,000 years and precession over roughly 19,000 to 26,000 years.
When northern summers are cool, snow survives the winter and ice sheets grow, and feedbacks amplify this as more ice raises albedo and colder oceans absorb more carbon dioxide.
Ice cores show carbon dioxide near 180 ppm at glacial maxima and about 280 ppm in interglacials.
At the Last Glacial Maximum about 20,000 years ago, ice covered much of northern Europe including Ireland, sea level was about 120 m lower and climate belts were pushed towards the equator.
Ireland had two main cold stages, the Munsterian and the Midlandian, and the Midlandian ice had gone by about 10,000 years ago.
Drumlins, eskers, corries and U-shaped valleys are evidence of this, and Antarctic ice cores from EPICA Dome C record about 800,000 years of past climate.
Aspect 4 – Recent and short-term change:
Climate has also changed over centuries and decades, from natural causes and since the Industrial Revolution from human activity.
The Medieval Warm Period, roughly AD 950 to 1250, was warm enough for Norse settlers led by Erik the Red to settle Greenland from about AD 985.
The Little Ice Age, from the 14th to the mid 19th century, brought advancing Alpine glaciers, and the River Thames in London froze often enough for frost fairs, the last in 1814.
Volcanic eruptions cool the earth for a year or two, as after Mount Pinatubo in June 1991 when global temperature fell by about 0.5 °C, and Tambora in 1815 led to 1816 being the year without a summer.
Carbon dioxide has risen from about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s, as measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958.
Global average surface temperature has risen about 1.1 to 1.2 °C since the late 1800s, and 2024 was the warmest year on record and the first more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels.
Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13 per cent per decade since satellite records began in 1979, and global sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018.
Ireland's average temperature has risen about 0.9 °C since 1900, with wetter winters, flooding risk and the risk of summer drought seen in 2018.

PART "1B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option (i) – Delta formation by deposition:
A delta is a landform of deposition that forms where a river enters a sea or lake.
Role of deposition:
A river carrying a heavy load of silt and clay enters the sea, where its velocity drops sharply.
The loss of energy means the river can no longer carry its load, so it deposits it at its mouth.
In salt water, fine clay particles clump together by flocculation and sink faster.
Heavier material is deposited first and the finest last, so the deposits are sorted into layers.
Deposits build up until they block the main channel, which forces the river to split into smaller channels called distributaries.
Calm water with a small tidal range and weak currents is needed so that the deposits are not washed away.
Topset beds are flat, coarser deposits laid down on top of the delta.
Foreset beds are steeply sloping deposits at the front, and they build the delta out into the water.
Bottomset beds are fine, flat layers deposited beyond the front of the delta.
Specific example – Mississippi delta, USA:
The Mississippi delta in Louisiana is a bird's-foot delta, formed where the river enters the Gulf of Mexico.
The Mississippi drains a very large basin, so it carries a heavy load of silt to its mouth.
The Gulf of Mexico has a small tidal range and weak currents, so the deposits are not removed.
Long fingers of deposits extend out into the Gulf along the distributaries.
The deposits form fertile land and wetlands that are used for farming and wildlife.
Diagram points in words:
In plan view, the river splits into distributaries that spread out into the water at the mouth.
In cross-section, flat topset beds lie on top, steep foreset beds slope at the front, and flat bottomset beds lie beyond the front.
Deltas can be arcuate like the Nile, bird's-foot like the Mississippi, or lake deltas like the one at Glendalough.

PART "1C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Prediction of earthquakes:
Earthquakes cannot be predicted exactly, but scientists can identify where they are likely and estimate how often they occur.
Mapping active faults and plate boundaries shows the regions most at risk, such as the Pacific Ring of Fire.
Studying the history and frequency of past earthquakes gives the likelihood of another one in a given area.
Seismic gap theory says that a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time holds the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next.
Foreshocks, the smaller tremors before a main shock, are monitored as a possible warning.
Ground deformation is measured with GPS, tiltmeters and lasers across faults to detect strain building up.
Radon gas release and changes in well-water levels are monitored as possible warning signs.
In Haicheng, China, in 1975 the city was evacuated after foreshocks, hours before a magnitude 7.3 earthquake, and many lives were saved.
In Tangshan, China, in 1976 no warning was given, and about 240,000 people died, which shows the limits of prediction.
Reducing the effects of earthquakes:
Early-warning systems detect the fast P waves and give seconds of warning before the destructive surface waves arrive.
Those seconds are enough to stop trains, shut off gas supplies and let people take cover, as in Japan's national system.
Base isolation places rubber and steel bearings under a building so that the ground moves beneath it.
Flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames with cross-bracing and shear walls sway rather than crack.
Tall buildings such as Taipei 101 use a tuned mass damper, a giant counterweight that offsets sway.
Strict building codes and land-use planning avoid soft ground and fault lines, because soft sediment can liquefy.
Older buildings can be retrofitted, and stricter codes and retrofitting followed the Kobe earthquake of 1995.
Emergency drills and education prepare people, such as Japan's national Disaster Prevention Day drills each 1 September.
Automatic shut-off valves on gas mains reduce the risk of fires after an earthquake.

[geo-rivers-s1.0.b] Before looking at how rivers shape the land, you need the vocabulary for the parts of a river system. These words are used to label maps and diagrams.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.2] Tributary: a smaller river that flows into a larger river, so it adds its water to the main river.
[geo-rivers-s1.0.6] Distributary: a channel that leaves the main river and flows away from it, found on deltas (fan-shaped landforms of deposited material at a river mouth).
[geo-rivers-s1.0.7] Estuary: the tidal lower course of a river, where fresh water meets salt water. The Shannon enters the Atlantic through the Shannon Estuary below Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s1.1.b] The long profile is the smooth, concave slope of a river from its source to its mouth. Because the slope changes along the way, the river is divided into three courses, and each course has its own landforms.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.2] Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried within the flow, giving the water its brown colour.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.4] Competence: the largest particle a river can carry. It rises sharply in flood because the faster, bigger flow can move larger particles.
[geo-rivers-s1.4.5] Capacity: the total load a river can carry. It also rises sharply in flood, so a flooded river carries far more material overall.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.b] A river drops its load when it no longer has the energy to carry it. Understanding why it drops it, and in what order, explains the shape of most depositional landforms.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.1] Deposition also happens when the load becomes too great for the river's energy to carry, so the excess is dropped even if the flow has not slowed.
[geo-rivers-s1.5.2] Heavier material is deposited first and the finest last, so deposits are sorted by size. This contrasts with glacial till (material dropped by ice), which is unsorted and angular.
[geo-rivers-s1.6.3] Deranged: no clear pattern; forms on poorly drained glacial deposits (material left by ice), e.g. the drumlin belt of Co. Cavan, with many small lakes and marshes between the drumlins.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.5] On an Ordnance Survey map, the contours (lines joining points of equal height) form a V that points upstream.
[geo-rivers-s1.7.7] Diagram: cross-section of a V-shaped valley; plan view of interlocking spurs with the river winding between them.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.b] A meander is a looping bend in a river. It is formed by both erosion and deposition, so it is classified under both.
[geo-rivers-s1.10.5] Diagram: plan view of a meander with the fastest-flow line, river cliff and point bar.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.3] Example: oxbow lakes on the lower Mississippi, USA.
[geo-rivers-s1.11.4] Diagram: plan sequence showing the cut-off and the formation of the oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.1] When the river floods, it spreads across the valley, loses velocity and deposits a layer of fine silt called alluvium.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.5] Example: levees along the lower Mississippi, USA, which have been artificially raised for flood control.
[geo-rivers-s1.12.6] Diagram: cross-section of a floodplain showing alluvium layers, levees and the raised channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.b] A delta is a landform of deposition at a river mouth. It builds up when the river drops its load faster than the sea or lake can remove it.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.0] A delta forms where a river carrying a heavy load enters a sea or lake and its velocity drops sharply.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.1] In salt water, fine clay particles clump together (flocculation) and sink faster.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.2] Deposits build up until they block the channel, forcing the river to split into smaller channels called distributaries.
[geo-rivers-s1.13.3] Conditions needed: a large load, and calm water with a small tidal range and weak currents, so that deposits are not removed.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.0] Topset beds: flat, coarser deposits laid down on top of the delta, because the heavier material drops first.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.1] Foreset beds: steeply sloping deposits at the front of the delta, built out into the water so the delta grows forward.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.2] Bottomset beds: fine, flat layers laid down beyond the front of the delta, because the finest material is carried furthest.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.3] Arcuate (fan-shaped) delta, e.g. the Nile delta, Egypt.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.4] Bird's-foot delta: long fingers of deposits along distributaries, e.g. the Mississippi delta, USA.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.5] Estuarine delta: fills a drowned river mouth (a river mouth flooded by the sea).
[geo-rivers-s1.14.6] Lake delta, e.g. the delta between the Upper and Lower Lakes at Glendalough, Co. Wicklow, which divided what was once a single lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.14.7] Diagram: plan view of a delta with distributaries; cross-section showing topset, foreset and bottomset beds.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.b] Two more depositional features occur near the mouth, or where the load is large: the estuary and the braided channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.0] Estuary: in the tidal river mouth, fresh and salt water meet, and deposition builds mudflats and salt marsh.
[geo-rivers-s1.15.2] Braided channel: the river splits into several channels around islands of deposited sand and gravel, called eyots or braid bars.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.b] Every fluvial landform is formed by erosion, by deposition, or by both. Sorting them this way is a skill that exams test.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.1] Erosion and deposition: meander, oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.2] Deposition: floodplain, levee, delta, point bar, estuary mudflats.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.2] 3. When the stress exceeds the strength of the rock, the rock fractures or slips suddenly along a fault (a crack in the crust along which rock can move).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.3] 4. The stored energy is released as seismic waves, which are vibrations that travel through the Earth and shake the ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.5] Foreshocks are smaller tremors before the main shock. Aftershocks are tremors after it, and can bring down buildings already weakened by the main shock.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.3] Seismic (shock) waves: waves of energy spreading out from the focus in all directions.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.4] Diagram: draw a cross-section showing the fault, the focus below ground, seismic waves spreading out, and the epicentre on the surface directly above the focus.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.1] S (secondary) waves are slower than P waves and arrive second.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.2] Surface waves travel along the surface, arrive last and cause most of the damage, because they shake the ground and the buildings on it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.5.b] Most earthquakes occur in narrow belts along plate boundaries, the lines where plates meet, because that is where plates lock and then slip. Each boundary type produces a different kind of earthquake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.5.0] The Pacific Ring of Fire, the horseshoe of plate boundaries around the Pacific Ocean, has about 90% of the world's earthquakes, including most of the most powerful ones.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.7.b] Where one plate is subducted (forced down) beneath another, the sinking plate generates earthquakes at a range of depths.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.7.1] Undersea earthquakes at subduction zones (where one plate sinks under another) can cause tsunamis, which are giant sea waves.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.0] These earthquakes are shallow and often very damaging.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.1] San Andreas Fault, California: the Pacific plate and North American plate slide past each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.4] East Anatolian Fault, Turkey, a transform fault: on 6 February 2023 a magnitude 7.8 earthquake killed over 50,000 people in Turkey and Syria, and many apartment blocks collapsed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.10.0] Building and bridge collapse, deaths and injuries, and ground rupture (the ground splitting open along the fault).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.10.1] Broken gas, water and power lines, and damaged roads and railways.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.0] Fires from broken gas mains and power lines, because leaking gas and sparks ignite easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.11.1] Tsunamis: the Tōhoku earthquake of 11 March 2011, magnitude 9.0 off north-east Japan, sent a tsunami that overtopped sea walls. About 18,000 people were killed or are missing.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.0] Magnitude: larger earthquakes release more energy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.1] Depth of focus: a shallow-focus earthquake causes more surface damage than a deep-focus one of the same magnitude.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.3] Rock and soil type: soft sediment shakes more and can liquefy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.5] Building quality and building codes (the rules a building must follow).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.6] Level of development and preparedness: wealth to prepare, respond and rebuild.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.b] Kobe shows how a moderate-magnitude earthquake with a shallow focus, close to a big city, can be devastating. Begin a case-study answer with the date, place, plate setting and magnitude.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.0] Place: the Great Hanshin earthquake, Kobe, Japan, 17 January 1995. Its epicentre was near the northern tip of Awaji Island, and its focus was shallow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.1] Many older wooden houses with heavy tiled roofs collapsed, because the heavy roofs sat on weak frames.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.2] Fires from broken gas mains burned large areas, because the shaking broke the pipes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.4] About 6,400 people died, and about 300,000 were left homeless.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.5] Response: the government faced criticism for a slow response. Stricter building codes and retrofitting (strengthening existing buildings) followed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.16.0] About 230,000 people died in 14 countries.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.b] Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely: their exact time, place and magnitude are not known. Scientists identify where they are likely and estimate how often they occur.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.0] Mapping active faults and plate boundaries, to show where earthquakes are likely.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.1] Studying the history and frequency of past earthquakes, to estimate how often they occur.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.2] Seismic gap theory: a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time has the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.3] Monitoring foreshocks, ground deformation (GPS, tiltmeters and laser measurements across faults), radon gas release and changes in well-water levels. Tiltmeters measure small changes in the slope of the ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.18.b] Three examples show how prediction and warning have worked, failed, and led to legal trouble.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.18.0] Haicheng, China, 4 February 1975: after foreshocks, the city was evacuated hours before a magnitude 7.3 earthquake, saving many lives.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.18.1] Tangshan, China, 28 July 1976: no warning was given, and the official death toll was about 240,000.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.b] Prediction cannot give a time, but warning systems can give a short notice once an earthquake has started or a tsunami has been triggered.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.0] Early-warning systems detect the fast P waves, giving seconds of warning before the destructive waves arrive, because P waves outrun the surface waves.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.1] Those seconds are enough to stop trains, shut off gas supplies and take cover.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.2] Japan has a national earthquake early-warning system.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.0] Deep foundations anchored to bedrock (solid rock below the soil), so the building does not sit on soft ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.1] Base isolation: rubber and steel bearings or rollers under the building, so the ground moves beneath it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.2] Flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames that sway rather than crack, with cross-bracing and shear walls (extra supports that stiffen the structure).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.3] Counterweights (tuned mass dampers) in tall buildings, which swing against the shaking. Taipei 101, Taiwan, has a giant tuned mass damper, a steel ball hung near the top.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.4] Automatic shut-off valves on gas mains, and flexible joints on pipes, so pipes bend rather than break and gas does not leak.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.5] Retrofitting older buildings, which means strengthening them to modern standards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.0] Strict building codes, so that new buildings can survive shaking, and land-use planning that avoids soft ground (which shakes more and can liquefy) and fault lines.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.1] Emergency drills, education and emergency supplies.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.3] Japan holds national Disaster Prevention Day drills each 1 September, the anniversary of the 1923 Great Kanto earthquake.
