PART 7B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named MNC – Intel Corporation:
Intel Corporation, founded in 1968 in California, has its headquarters at Santa Clara in Silicon Valley and runs plants in the USA, Ireland, Israel, Malaysia, Vietnam and China.
Two aspects are described and explained here, the sourcing of raw materials and components and the location of processing units.
Aspect 1 – Sourcing of raw materials and components:
Silicon, refined from quartz sand, is the main raw material and is bought in as silicon wafers from specialist wafer makers.
Intel buys wafers rather than making them because specialist suppliers can produce pure wafers at lower cost and on a large scale.
Chemicals, specialty gases and metals are also bought in, because each chip is built in many layers that need these inputs.
Manufacturing machines come from ASML at Veldhoven in the Netherlands, which is the only maker of extreme ultraviolet lithography machines.
These machines cost hundreds of millions of euro each, and as ASML is the sole supplier Intel has no alternative source.
Fabs need huge volumes of ultra-pure water and electricity, which are drawn from the local supply at Leixlip, so sites with reliable water and power are chosen.
Because components and materials come from several countries, Intel depends on long supply chains, which were disrupted by the global chip shortage of 2021.
Aspect 2 – Location of processing units:
The first stage takes place in wafer fabrication plants at Leixlip in Co. Kildare, Hillsboro in Oregon, Chandler in Arizona and Kiryat Gat in Israel.
Fabs are placed where skilled graduates are available, and Leixlip was chosen for its large flat greenfield site on the M4 motorway, its water and electricity supply and its Dublin-area graduate workforce.
Leixlip makes processed wafers only, because the cutting, packaging and testing of chips are not done in Ireland.
The second stage sends finished wafers by air to assembly and test plants in Penang and Kulim in Malaysia, Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam and Chengdu in China.
These plants are in Asia because that stage is labour-intensive and labour costs there are lower.
Chips are small, light and very valuable, so air freight is cheap relative to their value and the stages can be located far apart.
The third stage sends chips to computer makers' assembly plants, mostly in Asia, while Intel's headquarters and main research fabs stay in the USA at Santa Clara and Hillsboro.

PART 7C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named economy – Brazil:
Brazil, a Portuguese colony from the arrival of Cabral in 1500 until independence in 1822, is the developing economy examined.
Challenge 1 – Dependence on primary exports:
Colonial Brazil was organised to export raw materials to Portugal in a series of cycles, including brazilwood, sugar, gold, coffee and rubber.
Each cycle enriched Portugal and a small elite rather than the colony as a whole.
The economy was left with little manufacturing because raw materials were shipped out with little processing.
The Amazon rubber boom centred on Manaus collapsed by about 1912 when plantations in British South-East Asia, grown from Brazilian seeds, undercut it, which shows the risk of relying on one export.
Today soya, iron ore, oil, beef and coffee dominate exports, especially to China, so the colonial pattern of exporting raw materials continues.
A fall in commodity prices brought a recession in 2015–16.
Challenge 2 – Land and social inequality:
Huge estates called latifundia, originating in Portuguese land grants, are owned by a few families, while millions of rural workers are landless.
Brazil has one of the most unequal patterns of land ownership in the world.
The MST, founded in 1984, campaigns for land reform and occupies unused estates.
About 4 to 5 million enslaved Africans were taken to Brazil to work on plantations and in mines.
Slavery ended only in 1888, the last in the Americas.
The legacy of slavery is racial inequality, as Afro-Brazilians are over-represented among the poor and in favelas.
Challenge 3 – Indigenous peoples and regional inequality:
Indigenous peoples were killed by violence and European diseases and displaced from their lands by colonial settlers and plantations.
Many Indigenous communities still lack secure land rights and face poverty and loss of territory today.
When sugar declined the North-East was left poor while wealth and industry shifted to the South-East around São Paulo.
The North-East is still the poorest region, and SUDENE was set up in 1959 to promote its development.
Poverty in the North-East drives migrants to the favelas of southern cities such as São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro.

PART 8C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact 1 – CAP raised farm incomes and exports:
The Common Agricultural Policy, launched in 1962, has had a major impact on the Irish economy through farm prices, incomes and exports.
After Ireland joined on 1 January 1973 guaranteed prices raised farm prices and incomes sharply.
A guaranteed market opened for beef and dairy products.
Farms modernised with machinery, fertiliser, silage, land drainage and farm buildings, in what is remembered as the golden age of the 1970s.
Ireland was a net recipient of EU money from 1973 until about 2014, receiving over €40 billion more than it paid in, mostly through the CAP.
Milk quotas, introduced in 1984, capped output and held back Irish dairying for 30 years.
After the quotas ended on 1 April 2015, Irish milk output rose from about 5.5 billion litres in 2014 to about 8.3 billion litres in 2020.
Reforms such as the MacSharry reform of 1992 and the Fischler reform of 2003 decoupled payments from production, and cross-compliance now links payments to environmental and animal-welfare rules.
Impact 2 – Costs to farm structure and environment:
The CAP also had negative effects, because payments historically favoured bigger farmers, so most of the money went to a minority.
Many beef and sheep farms depend heavily on direct payments, which is a weakness if payments fall.
Intensification damaged water quality through nitrates and raised greenhouse gas emissions as the dairy herd grew.
The Nitrates Directive of 1991 limits the organic nitrogen spread per hectare, so EU environmental rules directly shape Irish farming.
The number of farms has fallen and farmers are ageing, with 33% of holders aged 65 or over and only 7% under 35 in the Census of Agriculture 2020.
Rural development and current policy:
LEADER, from 1991, funds rural enterprise such as farm diversification, rural tourism and crafts, which spreads income beyond farming.
Ireland's CAP Strategic Plan for 2023-2027 is worth about €9.8 billion, with about 45% for environmental and climate measures, which steers farm spending towards sustainability.
