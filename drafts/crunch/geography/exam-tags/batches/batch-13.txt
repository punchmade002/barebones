PART "4C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Primary activity – Farming in the West of Ireland:
The West Region of Galway, Mayo and Roscommon is a peripheral region where farming is the main primary activity.
Physical factor 1 – Climate:
The climate is cool temperate oceanic, with prevailing south-westerly winds bringing frequent depressions from the Atlantic.
Relief rainfall gives annual totals of about 1,000 to 1,250 mm on lowlands and over 2,000 mm on some mountains, against about 750 to 1,000 mm in the east.
Mild winters, with January means of about 4 to 7 °C because of the North Atlantic Drift, mean that grass grows well for much of the year.
Wet weather and waterlogged land make tillage difficult and hay hard to save, so most farming is grazing.
Exposure to Atlantic gales limits the land that can be farmed and the crops that can be grown.
Physical factor 2 – Relief:
Rugged uplands such as the Twelve Bens and Maumturks in Connemara and Mweelrea, Nephin and Croagh Patrick in Mayo are too steep, high and exposed for crops.
Hill sheep farming on the commonage of Connemara and the Mayo mountains is one specific primary activity.
A low, gently rolling limestone lowland in east Galway and Roscommon is more suitable for farming and machinery.
Physical factor 3 – Soils:
Blanket bog covers much of west Mayo and Connemara, where high rainfall keeps the ground waterlogged, so it is suited only to rough grazing.
Leached, acidic podzols with an iron pan lie on the uplands and have low fertility.
Heavy, poorly drained gley soils on the drumlins and clay lowlands restrict farming to grazing.
Better-drained, more fertile brown earths on the limestone of east Galway and Roscommon are the region's best farmland, where tillage is possible.
On the Aran Islands the soils are thin and rocky, and islanders made soil from layers of sand and seaweed on the bare limestone.
Combined effect:
These physical conditions give a short effective grazing season, and cattle rearing is the main enterprise, which is a second specific primary activity.
Suckler herds rear calves, and young store cattle are sold through local marts to farmers in the east to be fattened.
Sketch map points:
On a sketch map Galway, Mayo and Roscommon are outlined with the Atlantic coast to the west, the Twelve Bens and Mweelrea are marked as uplands, and the limestone lowland of east Galway and Roscommon is shaded.

PART "5B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Region – India:
India's population of over 1.4 billion is very unevenly distributed, with a national density of 382 people per km² in Census 2011.
Bihar is the densest state at about 1,100 people per km², while Arunachal Pradesh is the least dense at about 17.
Uttar Pradesh is the most populous state, with about 200 million people in Census 2011.
Dense areas – Ganges plain:
The Ganges plain is densely populated because the river deposits fertile alluvium in its floods.
The flat land is easy to farm, build on and connect by road and rail, and the Ganges and its tributaries supply water for irrigation.
The plain has a very long history of settlement, so numbers have built up over thousands of years.
Bihar and Uttar Pradesh on the plain are the densest and most populous states.
Dense areas – Coasts and deltas:
Deltas and coastal plains such as Kerala and West Bengal are densely populated because flat, fertile land and plentiful water support intensive farming.
Dense areas – Cities:
Around large cities such as Mumbai, Delhi and Kolkata population is dense because they offer jobs in industry and services.
Greater Mumbai had about 18.4 million people, Delhi about 16.3 million and Kolkata about 14.1 million in Census 2011.
Rural-to-urban migration from Bihar and Uttar Pradesh adds to the numbers in these cities.
Sparse areas – Thar Desert and Himalayas:
The Thar Desert in the north-west is sparsely populated because aridity makes farming very difficult.
The Himalayas in the north are sparsely populated because of cold temperatures, steep slopes and thin soils.
Sparse areas – North-east and plateaus:
The forested hills of the north-east, including Arunachal Pradesh, are sparsely populated because dense forest and rugged relief make farming and settlement difficult.
The central plateaus are sparsely populated compared with the plains, because the Deccan lies partly in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats.
Human factors:
Irrigation and the Green Revolution raised food output in areas such as Punjab, which helps support large rural populations.
Industrial regions such as Mumbai–Pune and the Damodar valley attract workers, and transport links concentrate people along the main routes.
Sketch map points:
On a sketch map the Ganges is drawn from the Himalayas across the northern plain to its delta on the Bay of Bengal, with dense population shaded along it.
Mumbai on the west coast, Kolkata on the Hooghly near the Bay of Bengal and Delhi on the Yamuna are marked as dense urban centres, with the Thar Desert shaded as sparse.

PART "5C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Social challenge – Ageing population:
Many EU member states face an ageing population, because people live longer and birth rates are below the replacement level of about 2.1 children per woman.
A shrinking share of people of working age pays taxes to support a growing number of pensioners, so pension costs rise.
Health care and long-term care costs rise as the elderly population grows, which puts pressure on public budgets.
Young people emigrate from poorer regions such as the Mezzogiorno of southern Italy to find work, which leaves older populations behind.
Governments respond by raising the retirement age and by encouraging the immigration of workers.
Social challenge – Migration and integration:
Since 2015 large numbers of refugees and migrants, many of them from Syria, have arrived in the EU.
This puts pressure on housing, schools and services in receiving countries, and members disagree over how to share responsibility.
Under free movement, workers from the eastern members have moved west since the 2004 enlargement, which creates labour shortages in the east and debate about integration in the west.
Economic challenge – Regional inequality and competition:
The EU has regional inequality, with a wealthy core around the Blue Banana and poorer peripheral and eastern regions.
As a result of enlargement, regional funds now go mainly to eastern Europe, and the gap between core regions and peripheral regions persists.
Enlargement in 2004 opened lower-cost production sites in the east, and in 2009 Dell moved manufacturing from Limerick to Łódź in Poland.
Competition from China is strong, the EU has a large trade deficit with it, and the EU applied duties of up to about 35% on Chinese-made electric cars from October 2024.
Economic challenge – External pressures:
The EU depends on imports of oil and gas, and Russian gas fell from about 45% of EU gas imports in 2021 to about 19% in 2024 after the invasion of Ukraine.
Brexit made trade with the UK more costly because of customs declarations, checks and paperwork, and Ireland lost about 26% of its mackerel quota.
In July 2025 the EU agreed to a US tariff of 15% on most EU goods, which affects exporters such as Irish pharmaceutical firms.
The eurozone debt crisis after 2008 forced Greece to accept austerity in return for bailouts, which showed the strain of sharing one currency among very different economies.
