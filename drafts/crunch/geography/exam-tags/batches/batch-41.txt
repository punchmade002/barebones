MODEL ANSWER: This answer examines the impact of global trading patterns on producer and consumer regions, using the multinational company Apple.
Aspect 1 – Producer regions in Asia and Africa:
Apple designs its products in California and owns very little manufacturing itself, so it outsources production to contract manufacturers.
Foxconn, a Taiwanese contract manufacturer, assembles iPhones in very large plants at Zhengzhou, known as iPhone City, and at Shenzhen in China.
These producer regions gain jobs, wages and export earnings from assembling iPhones.

Apple has been criticised for working conditions in supplier factories, and its dependence on assembly in China exposes producers to trade tensions between the USA and China.
Aspect 2 – Consumer regions in the USA, Europe and China:
iPhones are sold worldwide, and the Americas, Europe and China are the largest regions of sale.
Sales are made through Apple Stores, Apple's online store and mobile phone networks, so consumers in many regions can buy the same product.
The USA and Europe import iPhones that are assembled in Asia rather than making them at home.
Because assembly is concentrated in low-wage Asian plants, consumers in rich regions can buy advanced phones at affordable prices.
Containerisation and air freight make trade cheap and fast, and chips and phones are small, light and valuable enough to be moved by air.
The USA is the world's largest importer and has had a trade deficit in goods every year since the mid-1970s.
Cheaper imports can replace home production, so manufacturing jobs can be lost in consumer regions.

Apple's Cork campus, with about 6,000 staff, is its European headquarters and provides AppleCare customer support in many European languages for consumers.
Competition from Samsung and Chinese phone makers gives consumers a choice of rival products.
Aspect 3 – Changing trade and division of labour:
From the 1960s and 1970s multinationals moved assembly and routine manufacturing to low-wage and newly industrialised countries in the new international division of labour.
The core region of the USA keeps headquarters, research, design, finance and marketing, and Apple's headquarters are at Apple Park in Cupertino, California.

Ireland depends on a few US multinationals and on the US market, so US tariffs from 2025 expose Irish exports to new barriers.
Falling trade barriers under GATT and the World Trade Organisation, set up in 1995, together with containerisation, made this global pattern of production possible.

QUESTION geography-pp-2019-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2019 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART "14" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: This answer examines how aid programmes can help and hinder development, by looking at government and EU aid, NGO aid and the ways in which aid can harm recipients.
Aspect 1 – Government and EU aid that helps:
Bilateral aid is given directly from one government to another, while multilateral aid is pooled through bodies such as the EU and UN agencies.
Emergency aid saves lives after famine, floods or conflict by providing food, shelter and medical help.
Long-term development aid funds schools, clinics, wells and vaccination programmes, which improve health and education.
Infrastructure such as roads and power supplies helps local economies to grow.
Irish Aid began its bilateral programme in 1974, with partner countries including Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia and Vietnam.
Irish aid is untied, so recipients can spend it anywhere to get the best value.
The EU institutions and member states together, known as Team Europe, are the world's largest aid donor, giving over two-fifths of all official development aid.
ECHO, the EU's humanitarian aid office, was set up in 1992 to deliver emergency relief.
Uganda in 1998 became the first country to receive HIPC debt relief, and its savings went to schools, health and water.
Aspect 2 – NGO aid that helps:
NGOs are non-profit organisations that are independent of governments and are funded by donations, governments and the EU.
They provide emergency relief and long-term development in water, health, education and farming.
Concern Worldwide was founded in 1968 in response to famine in Biafra during Nigeria's civil war.
Trócaire was founded in 1973 by the Irish Catholic bishops, and its Lenten Trócaire box funds its work.
Bóthar gives farm animals to poor families, who pass on the gift of the first female offspring to another family.
Self Help Africa was founded in 1984 in response to the Ethiopian famine and supports smallholder farmers.
NGOs work close to communities, from the bottom up, at small scale and with appropriate methods.
NGOs are fast in emergencies and reach places that governments cannot.
Médecins Sans Frontières, founded in France in 1971, won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1999 for its international work.
Aspect 3 – How aid can hinder development:
Tied aid must be spent on goods and services from the donor country, so much of the money returns to donor firms.
UK aid for the Pergau Dam in Malaysia was linked to a British arms deal, and in 1994 the English High Court ruled that the aid was unlawful because it was not for sound development.
US food aid has long been required by law to be bought largely from US farmers and shipped largely on US ships.
Aid has been used to win political allies, trade deals and influence, as in Cold War aid.
Corruption and elite capture can divert aid money to powerful groups rather than the poor.
Recipients can come to rely on aid rather than building their own economies, and the Zambian economist Dambisa Moyo argued in Dead Aid in 2009 that aid breeds corruption and dependency in Africa.
In the mid-1990s Haiti cut its tariff on rice under pressure from lenders, and cheap subsidised US rice flooded in and ruined many Haitian rice farmers.
In 2010 former US President Bill Clinton publicly apologised for the rice policy, saying it had been a mistake.
Aid given as loans must be repaid, so it adds to the debt burden of poor countries.

MODEL ANSWER: This answer examines the causes and impacts of global warming, one of the global environmental issues named.
Aspect 1 – Causes of global warming:
The natural greenhouse effect occurs because greenhouse gases let incoming short-wave solar radiation through but absorb outgoing long-wave radiation, which keeps the Earth habitable.
The enhanced greenhouse effect occurs because human activity adds greenhouse gases, which trap more heat.
Carbon dioxide comes from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and cement production.
Fossil fuels supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy.
China produces and consumes about half the world's coal and has been the largest CO2 emitter since the mid-2000s.
Methane comes from cattle and sheep, rice paddies, landfill and gas leaks.
Nitrous oxide comes from nitrogen fertilisers and slurry.
CFCs and other fluorinated gases are also greenhouse gases.
Atmospheric CO2 was about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution and was over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s, as recorded at Mauna Loa in Hawaii.
Aspect 2 – Global impacts of global warming:
Global average surface temperature has risen by about 1.1 to 1.2 °C since the late 1800s.
2024 was the warmest year on record and the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels.
Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979.
Glaciers are retreating worldwide, which threatens water supplies.
Global sea level rose by about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018, because of thermal expansion of seawater and melting land ice.
Sea-level rise threatens low-lying countries and deltas such as Bangladesh, the Maldives, Tuvalu and Kiribati.
Heatwaves, droughts, floods and storms are becoming more intense, and the Sahel has suffered droughts.
The Great Barrier Reef in Australia has suffered repeated mass coral bleaching since the late 1990s.
Warming brings species shifts and extinctions, crop failures in vulnerable regions and climate refugees.
Aspect 3 – Causes and impacts in Ireland:
Ireland's average temperature has risen by about 0.9 °C since 1900.
Warmer temperatures bring wetter winters and more flooding.
They also bring a risk of summer drought, stronger storms and coastal erosion.
Storm Éowyn in January 2025 caused record damage and power cuts.
Low-lying coastal towns are threatened by rising seas.
Warming brings changes to farming and to marine species.
Agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, mainly methane and nitrous oxide from cattle, slurry and fertiliser.
Transport is the next largest source, at about a fifth of emissions.
Ireland's emissions per person are high for an EU country.
