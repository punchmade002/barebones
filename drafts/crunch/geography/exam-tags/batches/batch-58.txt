PART 4B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named region – Cork Harbour:
Secondary activity in the Cork Harbour region is mainly pharmaceuticals and chemicals, with Pfizer, Janssen and Novartis at Ringaskiddy and Eli Lilly at Kinsale.
Factor 1 – Deep-water port and industrial sites:
The Port of Cork has deep-water berths at Ringaskiddy, so large ships can bring in raw materials and carry out finished goods.
Whitegate, Cork Harbour, is Ireland's only oil refinery, opened in 1959, and it depends on crude oil imported by sea.
Pharmaceuticals are light, high-value goods that can also be exported quickly by air from Cork Airport.
Large flat sites at Ringaskiddy and Little Island are zoned for industry, so firms have room to build and expand.
These sites have water supplies and facilities for waste and effluent, which chemical plants need.
Ringaskiddy lies on the harbour shore, away from the city centre, which suits hazardous chemical industry.
Road links connect the harbour to Cork city and the national motorway network, so goods reach markets quickly.
Factor 2 – Skilled labour and government support:
University College Cork and Munster Technological University supply graduates in science and engineering.
Munster Technological University was formed in 2021 from Cork IT and IT Tralee, which widened the supply of trained workers.
IDA Ireland offers grants, serviced sites and advance factories to attract foreign firms to the region.
Corporation tax of 12.5% from 2003 made Ireland attractive to multinational firms.
An English-speaking base in the EU gives firms access to the single market.
Firms in the same industry gather together to share skilled workers, suppliers and research, so the cluster reinforces itself.
Pharmaceuticals and chemicals are Ireland's largest goods exports, so the Cork Harbour plants are important to the national economy.
Older heavy industry such as Irish Steel at Haulbowline closed in 2001, and the pharmaceutical plants replaced the lost jobs.

PART 4C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Social challenge – Migration and integration:
Conflict in Syria and instability elsewhere led to more than a million migrants and refugees reaching the EU in 2015.
Frontline Mediterranean states such as Greece and Italy received large numbers of arrivals and found it hard to cope.
Member states disagreed over how to share responsibility for asylum seekers, and some, such as Hungary, resisted quotas.
Schengen border-free travel came under strain when some states reintroduced border checks.
Integrating newcomers into housing, schools, jobs and language is difficult and can cause social tension and segregation.
Support for anti-immigration parties has grown in several member states.
Migrants also fill labour shortages and help to support ageing societies.
A second social challenge is an ageing population, because low birth rates and longer life expectancy raise pension and healthcare costs for a shrinking workforce.
Economic challenge – Debt, unemployment and trade:
After the 2008 financial crash, countries such as Greece, Ireland and Portugal had high public debt and needed bailouts.
The bailouts demanded austerity, with cuts in public spending that reduced demand and raised unemployment.
Euro members cannot devalue their currency, so weaker economies found it hard to regain competitiveness.
Youth unemployment stayed high in southern Europe, in countries such as Spain, Greece and Italy.
Peripheral members such as Greece trade much less within the EU than core members, so development remains uneven.
Brexit moved the UK from internal to external partner after it left on 31 January 2020, and trade became more costly through customs declarations and checks.
Russian gas fell from about 45% of EU gas imports in 2021 to about 19% in 2024 after the invasion of Ukraine, and the EU had to find costly new energy supplies.
The EU-USA agreement of July 2025 capped the US tariff on most EU goods at 15%, which affects exporters such as Irish pharmaceutical firms.

PART 5B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named region – India:
India is a continental region in south Asia that recorded about 11 million foreign tourist arrivals in 2019.
Factor 1 – Cultural and historical attractions:
The Taj Mahal at Agra is a marble tomb built in the 17th century by Shah Jahan, and it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
The Golden Triangle linking Delhi, Agra and Jaipur concentrates Mughal monuments and tourists on one route.
Rajasthan's forts and palaces, and Jaipur, the Pink City, draw large numbers of visitors.
India is the birthplace of Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism, so it attracts religious tourism.
Varanasi is a Hindu holy city on the Ganges with bathing ghats, and it draws pilgrims and tourists.
The Golden Temple at Amritsar is the holiest Sikh shrine, and Bodh Gaya in Bihar is where the Buddha reached enlightenment.
The Kumbh Mela at Prayagraj is a vast Hindu pilgrimage gathering that attracts huge crowds.
Festivals such as Diwali, and spiritual tourism based on yoga and Ayurveda, add to the cultural appeal.
Factor 2 – Government support and access:
The government launched the Incredible India marketing campaign in 2002 to promote the country abroad.
E-visas were introduced in 2014, which made entry quicker and easier for visitors.
Airport expansion has improved access to the main tourist destinations.
Low-cost airlines led by IndiGo, India's largest airline, make travel within the country cheaper.
The Supreme Court restricted polluting industry around the Taj Mahal to protect the monument that tourists come to see.
Domestic tourism is far larger than foreign tourism, and improved transport links help it to grow.
Tourism brings investment in hotels, roads and airports, which attracts further visitors.

PART 5C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named urban area – Paris, France:
Paris is the capital of France and lies near the centre of the Paris Basin in northern France.
Factor 1 – Site and situation:
Paris began on the Île de la Cité, an island at a crossing point of the Seine that was easy to defend and bridge.
Paris lies where the Marne, Oise, Aube and Yonne converge on the Seine, so routes naturally focus on it.
The Seine is navigable because of its gentle gradient, so it carried trade to Rouen and to Le Havre on the Channel.
Fertile limon plateaux around the city supplied food to a growing population.
Factor 2 – Transport and routeways:
Paris is the hub of a radial road and rail network, and the Boulevard Périphérique ring road was completed in 1973.
The first TGV high-speed line, Paris to Lyon, opened in 1981, and Eurostar links Paris to London through the Channel Tunnel.
Charles de Gaulle Airport, opened in 1974, is France's busiest airport.
The Port of Paris at Gennevilliers is one of Europe's largest river ports.
Factor 3 – Administration and services:
As capital of a centralised state, Paris concentrates government, finance and head offices.
La Défense, west of the city, is one of Europe's largest purpose-built business districts.
Île-de-France has about 12 million people, about 18% of France's population, and produces about 30% of French GDP.
The large market and labour force attracted manufacturing and services, so growth reinforced itself.
Factor 4 – Planning:
Haussmann rebuilt central Paris between 1853 and 1870 with wide boulevards, parks and sewers.
Grands ensembles such as Sarcelles housed a growing population from the 1950s, and the 1965 plan created five new towns including Cergy-Pontoise.
