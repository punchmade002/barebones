PART "11B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Galway:
Galway, at the mouth of the River Corrib on Galway Bay, is the regional capital of the West and its functions have changed greatly over time.
Function 1 – Port, trade and defence:
Galway began as a Norman town founded by the de Burgo family in the 13th century, with defence and port functions.
It was governed by 14 merchant families, the Tribes of Galway, and received a royal charter in 1484.
Its main function was trade, importing wine from Spain and France through its port.
The Spanish Arch, built in 1584, is a surviving part of the town walls and shows its defence function.
Galway surrendered to Cromwellian forces in 1652 and a long decline followed, so its trading function weakened.
Function 2 – Industry:
Digital Equipment Corporation opened a plant in Galway in 1971, giving the city a manufacturing function based on American investment.
Digital closed its manufacturing in Galway in 1993, so that industrial function was lost.
Galway is now a centre of the medical devices industry, with multinationals such as Medtronic and Boston Scientific.
Function 3 – Education and services:
Queen's College Galway was founded in 1845 and is now the University of Galway, which gives the city a major education function.
The National Spatial Strategy of 2002 named Galway a gateway, so services, health care and administration grew as it became the capital of the West.
Galway has a large hinterland across the West, so its shops and services draw customers from a wide area.
With about 86,000 people, Galway is one of Ireland's largest regional cities, which supports its retail and service function.
Growth has drawn in-migration for work and study, and the National Planning Framework of 2018 aims for at least 50% growth in Galway by 2040.
Function 4 – Tourism and culture:
Galway was European Capital of Culture in 2020, jointly with Rijeka in Croatia.
The Galway Arts Festival and the Galway Races at Ballybrit attract many visitors, so tourism and recreation are now important functions.

PART "11C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Challenge 1 – Housing supply and affordability:
Demand for homes rises with population growth, smaller households and migration, and supply has not kept pace in successful cities such as Dublin.
High rents and house prices put homes beyond the reach of many households.
Homelessness is a visible result, with over 15,000 people in emergency accommodation in Ireland in 2025.
Housing for All, launched in 2021, aims for about 33,000 new homes a year on average to 2030.
Rent Pressure Zones, introduced from 2016, limit rent increases in the areas where they apply.
Higher-density apartments near transport use land and transport more efficiently.
Public and cost-rental housing provide homes at below-market rents.
In developing-world cities the same challenge shows up as the growth of slums.
Challenge 2 – Transport and sustainable mobility:
Car dependence produces congestion and vehicle emissions in growing cities.
Dublin plans MetroLink and BusConnects to move more people by public transport.
The NTA's Greater Dublin Area Transport Strategy 2022–2042 is the long-term transport plan for the region.
Copenhagen is a cycling city, where cycling is the most common way of commuting to work or study.

Paris is promoting the 15-minute city, where everyday needs can be met within 15 minutes on foot or by bike.
Vauban in Freiburg, Germany, is a low-car eco-district built on a former military barracks, where residents are encouraged to live without a car.
Moving more journeys to public transport, cycling and walking reduces congestion and cuts emissions in the city.

PART "12B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Recent trends in migration:
About 1.3 million asylum applications were made in the EU in 2015, and a similar number in 2016, mainly from people fleeing war in Syria, Afghanistan and Iraq.
Over 1 million people crossed the Mediterranean to Europe in 2015, most reaching Greek islands such as Lesbos from Turkey.
The Central Mediterranean route from Libya to Italy, with Lampedusa as a main landing point, continued to carry irregular arrivals.
The Ukrainian refugee movement from 2022 added to these pressures, and over 4 million people held temporary protection in the EU in 2024.
Policy impact 1 – Sharing the burden between states:
The Dublin system makes the state of first entry responsible for an asylum claim, which put most pressure on frontier states such as Italy and Greece and broke down in 2015.
In September 2015 the EU agreed to relocate 160,000 asylum seekers from Italy and Greece.
Hungary, Poland and the Czech Republic refused to take part, and in 2020 the EU Court of Justice ruled that they had broken EU law.
The Pact on Migration and Asylum, adopted in May 2024, brought mandatory solidarity, under which each state either takes in relocated asylum seekers or pays into a fund.
Policy impact 2 – Stronger border control:
Hungary, under Viktor Orbán, built a fence on its border with Serbia in 2015.
After 2015 some states reintroduced temporary checks at internal borders within the Schengen area.
The EU-Turkey statement of March 2016 required irregular migrants reaching the Greek islands to be returned to Turkey, with €6 billion for refugees there, and arrivals in Greece fell sharply.
Deals with Libya and other North African states aimed to control departures before migrants reached EU borders, which is known as externalisation.
Frontex, the EU border and coast guard agency based in Warsaw, was strengthened.
Policy impact 3 – Humanitarian responses and legal routes:
Germany under Chancellor Angela Merkel took in the largest number of asylum seekers in the EU in 2015 to 2016.
The Temporary Protection Directive was activated for the first time on 4 March 2022, giving Ukrainians the immediate right to live and work without an asylum claim.
Policy is pulled in two directions, because labour shortages and ageing populations create a need for workers and legal routes such as the EU Blue Card, while public opposition and anti-immigration parties push for tighter control.

PART "12C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Niger and the Sahel:
Overpopulation means a population too large for its resources, technology and economy to support an acceptable standard of living, and Niger in the Sahel is a clear example.
Niger has about 27 million people in a large, mostly desert country, with most of them living in the south where rainfall allows farming.
Cause 1 – Resource degradation:
Desertification caused by overgrazing, overcultivation, fuelwood cutting and drought has reduced the land that can support people.
Sahel rainfall is only about 200 to 600 millimetres a year and is very unreliable, so crops often fail.
Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s because of irrigation demand and drought.
Cause 2 – Society and culture:
Early marriage of girls is common, female literacy is low and large families carry prestige.
Niger's total fertility rate is about 6 children per woman, with population growth of over 3% a year and a median age of about 15.
Cause 3 – Poverty and technology:
In a low-income country children are economic assets and there are no pensions, so families have many children, especially where infant mortality is high.
Medical technology such as vaccines and clean water cut death rates much faster than birth rates fell, which produced rapid growth in stage 2 of the demographic transition.
Subsistence farming without modern technology keeps carrying capacity low, and poverty limits the ability to develop resources or import food.
Effect 1 – Food shortages and environmental damage:
The Sahel suffered famines in the late 1960s and 1970s and in the early to mid 1980s, with malnutrition widespread.
Pressure on the land causes soil erosion, deforestation, desertification and water shortages.
Effect 2 – Poverty and pressure on services:
Unemployment and underemployment are high because jobs cannot keep up with population growth, so incomes stay low.
There is heavy pressure on schools, hospitals, housing, water and sanitation.
A high young dependency ratio means many children per worker, so savings go on feeding people rather than investment, which creates a poverty trap.
Effect 3 – Migration and conflict:
People leave rural areas for shanty towns in cities or emigrate to find work.
Pressure on scarce land and water can cause conflict.
The African Union's Great Green Wall, launched in 2007, aims to restore a belt of land across the Sahel.

[geo-population-migration-s2.0.0] Overpopulation: the population of an area is too large for its resources, technology and economy to support an acceptable standard of living.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.3] Optimum population: the number that gives the highest standard of living with existing resources and technology.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.5] The Sahel (the dry belt south of the Sahara) is thinly populated but overpopulated, because its fragile resources, drought and poverty cannot support its people.
[geo-population-migration-s2.0.6] Niger, a Sahel country, has about 27 million people (2023) in a very large, mostly desert country, so little of the land can feed anyone.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.1] Ireland's Great Famine is often cited in support of Malthus.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.2] Boserup, 'The Conditions of Agricultural Growth' (1965): population pressure drives farming innovation, so food supply can rise to meet demand.
[geo-population-migration-s2.1.3] The Green Revolution (the spread of high-yield crops and modern farming from the mid-1960s) is often cited in support of Boserup.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.b] Resources are what a population lives on. Where they are not developed, are degraded or shrink, the same population becomes too many.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.0] Sahel: desertification (fertile land turning to desert), drought, overgrazing, fuelwood cutting and the shrinking of Lake Chad all cut what the land can support.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.1] Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s, because of irrigation demand and drought, which is a resource shrinking under the same population.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.2] Pre-Famine Ireland: over-reliance on one resource, the potato, and subdivision of holdings (farms split among sons) into ever-smaller plots, so many families had too little land.
[geo-population-migration-s2.2.3] Developing resources raises carrying capacity (the number of people an area can support): India's Green Revolution and Ireland's industrial growth since the 1960s.
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.b] Ireland shows how one fragile resource can leave a large population exposed. Here the checked facts are the whole-island figures and the blight.
[geo-population-migration-s2.3.1] The rural poor depended heavily on the potato, so they had no other food when it failed.
[geo-population-migration-s2.4.b] Desertification (fertile land turning to desert) is the clearest case of resources shrinking under pressure. It happens because a poor, fast-growing population overuses a dry land.
[geo-population-migration-s2.4.0] The Sahel is a semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, from Senegal and Mauritania through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad to Sudan.

[geo-population-migration-s2.4.2] Causes of desertification: overgrazing (too many animals for the grass), overcultivation with fallow periods (rests that let soil recover) abandoned, cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood, and drought.
[geo-population-migration-s2.5.b] People and governments have tried to reverse desertification by holding water and soil in place and by bringing back trees.
[geo-population-migration-s2.5.0] The Great Green Wall, an African Union initiative launched in 2007, aims to restore a belt of land across the Sahel.

[geo-population-migration-s2.7.b] Niger shows what happens when several of these cultural factors act together.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.0] Early marriage of girls is common, so women start having children young and have more of them over their lives.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.1] Female literacy is low, which limits women's choices and their use of family planning.
[geo-population-migration-s2.7.2] Large families carry prestige, so having many children raises a family's standing.
