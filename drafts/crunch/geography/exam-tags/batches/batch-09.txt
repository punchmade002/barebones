MODEL ANSWER: Lithosphere: the rigid outer layer of the Earth made up of the crust and upper mantle.

CANDIDATES (shortlist):
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.0.0] The earth is made of concentric layers (layers one inside another, like the rings of an onion). From the outside in they are the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.1.b] The crust is the thin, solid outer layer of the earth, the part we live on. It exists in two types, which differ in thickness, density and rock type.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.b] The mantle is the thickest layer. Its upper part contains the layers on which the crust rests and moves.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.1] The mantle has an upper part and a lower part, and it is the upper part that holds the asthenosphere and the base of the lithosphere.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.2] Asthenosphere: the semi-molten (partly melted), plastic (able to flow slowly without breaking) layer in the upper mantle, beneath the lithosphere, on which the plates move. It is not liquid.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.4] Lithosphere: the rigid outer shell made of the crust plus the solid top of the upper mantle. It is about 100 km thick on average, thinner under the oceans and thicker under the continents.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.5] The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates (large slabs of rigid rock), which is why the rigid lithosphere can move as separate pieces over the plastic asthenosphere.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.b] The inside of the earth is hot, and that heat keeps the mantle moving. This movement is what later drives changes in the crust.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.0] Arthur Holmes, a British geologist, suggested in the late 1920s that mantle convection (slow circulation of heated rock in the layer beneath the crust) could move continents.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.4] Convection currents in the mantle (rising and sinking flows of heated rock) drag the plates along, because the plates sit on top of the moving rock.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.1] This process is subduction, and the zone where the plate sinks is the subduction zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.3] The place where the two plates meet at the surface is an ocean trench, a deep, narrow valley in the sea floor.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.b] When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks. This one collision produces a trench, fold mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes, which is why such coasts are so active.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.0] An ocean trench forms where the oceanic plate bends down, because the plate is forced into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.6] Diagram: cross-section with ocean trench, subducting plate, melting zone, rising magma, volcano, fold mountains and earthquake foci (the points underground where earthquakes start).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.1] An ocean trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands, called an island arc, form, and earthquakes are frequent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.2] The Mariana Trench, about 11 km deep, is the deepest place on earth; it lies where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the Philippine plate, and the Mariana Islands form an island arc.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.b] Continental crust is too light to sink into the mantle, so when two continents meet there is no subduction. The crust has nowhere to go but up.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.0] Landforms: ocean trenches, fold mountains, volcanic mountain chains and island arcs, such as the Andes and the Mariana Trench.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.0] Crust is created at ridges, carried across the ocean floor, and destroyed at subduction zones, so the ocean floor is continually recycled.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.1] Destructive boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, or modified where two continental plates collide. Examples: the Andes, the Mariana Trench and the Himalayas.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.2] Ridges, subduction zones, collision zones and transform faults are the boundaries where these belts form, because plates interact there.

This is because it has pores or cracks (joints) that water can flow through.

CANDIDATES (shortlist):
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.4] Chemical weathering changes or dissolves the minerals in the rock, so the rock itself is altered, not just broken.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.b] Carbonation is a chemical weathering process. It dissolves limestone, and it works along joints and bedding planes (the flat surfaces between layers of rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.0] Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.1] The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble, so it is carried away in solution (dissolved in water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.2] Word equation: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.4] Rock type (its mineral composition): limestone is vulnerable to carbonation, granite to hydrolysis, and quartzite (a very hard rock) resists both.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.b] Karst is a landscape on limestone shaped mainly by solution (rock dissolving in water), with few surface streams and many underground features. The name comes from the Kras plateau of Slovenia. Limestone suits this because of its chemistry and the way water moves through it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.1] Permeable rock lets water pass through it, mainly along cracks. Porous rock has tiny spaces between its grains that can hold water. Impermeable rock lets no water pass through.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.2] Limestone is permeable because water moves along its bedding planes (horizontal layers) and joints (vertical cracks), not through the solid rock itself.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.3] The ground water table is the upper surface of the zone where all cracks in the rock are saturated (completely full of water). Above it lies the zone of aeration (where cracks hold air). The table rises in wet weather and falls in dry weather.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.4] Percolation is the downward movement of water through soil and rock, which is how rainwater reaches the limestone's cracks.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.0] Grikes: deep, narrow vertical cracks, because carbonation widens the rock along joints, where water collects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.2] Karren (runnels): small grooves cut into clint surfaces because rainwater running across them slowly dissolves the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.3] Solution hollows (kamenitza or solution pans): small enclosed hollows on clint surfaces where rainwater collects and dissolves the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.4] Diagram: plan of clints and grikes; cross-section showing bedding planes and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.3] Diagram: cross-section from impermeable rock, through a swallow hole, underground to the resurgence.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.b] Below the surface, the same solution widens cracks in the limestone into hollow spaces that can be large enough to walk through.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.0] Caves and caverns: water dissolves passages along bedding planes and joints, and underground rivers enlarge them by erosion (wearing away rock). A cavern is a very large cave.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.1] Streams flowing off the impermeable shale and sandstone of Cuilcagh sink into swallow holes where they reach the limestone, flow through the caves and re-emerge as the Cladagh River.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.3] Mask-Corrib lowland: water from Lough Mask flows underground through the limestone to Lough Corrib near Cong, Co. Mayo.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.4] The Cong Canal, built in the mid-1800s to link the two lakes, never held water because it leaked into the permeable limestone. It became known as the dry canal.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.3] Rock type and drainage: impermeable granite and shale give many surface streams and bog. Permeable limestone takes drainage underground, leaving few rivers and dry surfaces.

MODEL ANSWER: Slope angle: Steeper slopes increase the gravitational force pulling material downhill.
Water content: Water in soil or rock adds weight and reduces friction, so material slides more easily.

CANDIDATES (shortlist):
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.b] Slopes are never completely still. Mass movement is the way weathered material shifts downhill, and everything else in this subsection (the processes, the factors and the case studies) is a variation on it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.0] Mass movement (also called mass wasting) is the downslope movement of weathered material, such as regolith (the loose broken rock and soil that covers solid rock), soil, rock, mud, peat or snow, under the influence of gravity.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.2] Gravity pulls material downslope. Friction (grip between surfaces), cohesion (particles sticking together) and plant roots resist it. Movement happens when the downslope force exceeds the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.b] The slowest mass movement, moving soil by a few millimetres to centimetres a year. It is too slow to watch, so you recognise it from its effects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.0] Soil particles are lifted at right angles to the slope by wetting (swelling) or by freezing (frost heave, the upward push of freezing ground).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.2] Evidence: terracettes (small steps on grassy slopes), bent tree trunks, tilted fences, walls and poles, soil piled up behind walls, and cracked roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.b] Solifluction is a slow flow of waterlogged soil in cold regions near ice. It works because frozen ground below stops water draining away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.b] Rockfall and landslide both move solid rock quickly. The difference is that rockfall is a free fall from a cliff, while a landslide is a slide along a plane.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.0] Rockfall: blocks loosened by freeze-thaw, or undercut by the sea or a river (worn away at the base), fall freely from a steep cliff. They collect as scree (also called talus) at the foot.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.0] Saturated material, often clay over or under permeable rock (rock that lets water through), slides along a curved (concave) slip plane and rotates backward.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.0] Mudflow: fine material (clay, silt, weathered rock or volcanic ash) saturated by heavy rain or snowmelt flows rapidly downhill like a liquid, often along valleys. It can bury settlements.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.1] Lahar: a volcanic mudflow of ash and water, from rain or from melted snow and ice on the volcano. It can travel very fast and far. The word is from Indonesia.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.2] Avalanche: a sudden, rapid slide of snow and ice down a steep mountain slope, triggered by heavy snowfall, rapid thaw or disturbance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.1] The peat gains weight, and its base loses grip on the underlying mineral soil or rock. The peat bursts and flows downhill as a black slurry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.b] Whether a slope moves, and how fast, depends on a set of factors. The first three are the slope angle, the water in the ground and what the slope is made of.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.0] Gradient (slope angle): the steeper the slope, the greater the downslope pull of gravity and the faster the movement. Gentle slopes favour creep; steep slopes favour falls and slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.1] Water content: some water adds cohesion, but saturation fills the pores (the tiny gaps between particles), adds weight, reduces friction and acts as a lubricant, so material flows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.3] Clay becomes slippery and plastic (easily moulded) when wet. Jointed or bedded rock dipping towards the slope slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.0] Vegetation: roots bind soil and plants absorb water, so bare slopes move more easily.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.1] Climate and weathering: freeze-thaw loosens rock, heavy seasonal rainfall adds water, and periglacial climates produce solifluction.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.2] Undercutting: erosion by the sea or a river at the base of a slope removes support, so the material above is left unsupported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.3] Loading: extra weight at the top of a slope, from rock, buildings or spoil (waste rock), increases the downslope force.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.5] Human activity also affects slope stability. There is more on this in Human interaction: mass movement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.3] The rock above slumps and rotates seaward in rotational slides, and clay flows out as mudflows. These have repeatedly blocked the coast road.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.13.b] South America gives a lahar triggered by a volcano and mudslides triggered by torrential rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.14.3] Marmolada glacier, Dolomites, Italy, 3 July 2022: during a heatwave a large section of the glacier collapsed as an ice avalanche, killing 11 people.

[geo-weather-climate-s1.0.2] Pressure (the weight of the air pressing down on a surface) is measured in hectopascals (hPa); 1 hPa equals 1 millibar (mb), so the two units give the same value and are never converted.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.0.3] Average sea-level pressure is about 1013 hPa, which gives a reference point for judging whether a pressure reading is high or low.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.2.0] Isobars join places of equal atmospheric pressure; they are usually drawn at fixed intervals, commonly every 4 hPa, so read the labels to confirm the interval.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.0] A depression (also called a low or cyclone) is marked L or Low with a pressure figure; it has closed isobars with pressure falling towards the centre, and usually has fronts attached. Its isobars are usually tightly packed.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.3.2] An anticyclone (also called a high) is marked H or High; it has closed isobars with pressure rising towards the centre, widely spaced isobars, and no fronts.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.b] The values in this example are invented for illustration and are not taken from any real chart. It shows how to answer the usual questions on pressure and wind from isobars.

[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.2] Pressure at the depression's centre is 976 hPa, the figure written on the L, and the isobar interval is 4 hPa, the gap between neighbouring isobars such as 980 and 984.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.3] A point midway between the 988 and 992 hPa isobars is about 990 hPa, because it lies halfway between the two values.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.4.4] Winds are stronger over the west of Ireland than over central Europe, because the isobars are closer together over the west.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.b] Wind is named from the direction it blows from, and its strength follows the spacing of the isobars. Once you know these rules you can judge the wind anywhere on a chart.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.3] Near the surface the wind blows nearly parallel to the isobars, angled slightly in towards a low and out from a high.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.5.4] Buys Ballot's law: stand with your back to the wind, and low pressure is on your left in the northern hemisphere.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.b] A front is the boundary between two air masses of different temperature and humidity. Frontal symbols point in the direction the front is moving.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.0] Warm front: a line with semicircles on the side it moves towards (red on colour charts). Warm air rises gently over cold air, bringing a long period of steady rain and thickening cloud, then rising temperature after it passes.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.2] Occluded front: triangles and semicircles alternating on the same side (purple on colour charts). It forms where a cold front catches up with a warm front, lifting the warm air off the ground, and it brings cloud and rain.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.3] An occluded front is usually found near the centre of a mature depression (one that is fully developed).
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.4] Stationary (quasi-stationary) front: triangles and semicircles alternating on opposite sides.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.5] Worked example W3 (drawing practice): in a box, draw each of the four symbols above, placing the symbol on the side the front moves towards.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.6] Draw a curved line with three triangles for cold and three semicircles for warm. Alternate them on one side for occluded and on opposite sides for stationary.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.7] Diagram cue: a plan of a depression shows the warm front ahead, the warm sector (the area of warm air between the fronts), the cold front behind and the occlusion near the centre.
[geo-weather-climate-s1.6.8] The isobars are kinked where they cross the fronts. There is more on the weather as a depression passes in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
