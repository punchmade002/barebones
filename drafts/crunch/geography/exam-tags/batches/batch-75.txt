QUESTION geography-pp-2022-higher-q120 (LC Geography Higher 2022 — Part Two — Q20, 80 marks)

PART "20" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Political and physical boundaries shape cultural groups by uniting some people, splitting others and creating minorities, as partition in Ireland, the divided Kurds and the Pyrenees and colonial borders show.
Aspect 1 – Political boundary in Ireland:
Partition drew a political border across the island of Ireland that created two states and two minorities.
The Government of Ireland Act 1920 created two administrations on the island.
Northern Ireland was established in May 1921 with six of Ulster's nine counties, and the Anglo-Irish Treaty of December 1921 created the Irish Free State in the other twenty-six counties.
Cavan, Donegal and Monaghan went to the Irish Free State in 1922.
The Boundary Commission of 1925 was not published and the border was left unchanged.
The border is about 499 km long and follows old county boundaries rather than physical features, so it cuts through communities and farms.
Partition created two majorities and two minorities, including a large nationalist minority in Northern Ireland.
Discrimination against nationalists fuelled the civil rights movement from 1968, and the Troubles killed over 3,500 people.
The Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998 set up power-sharing and the consent principle, and people in Northern Ireland may be Irish, British or both.
In the Brexit referendum on 23 June 2016 Northern Ireland voted about 56% Remain, and the Windsor Framework of 2023 keeps the land border free of checks.
Aspect 2 – Boundaries dividing the Kurds:
The Kurds number 30 million or more and are the largest ethnic group without a state of their own.
Their mountainous homeland, Kurdistan, is divided between Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria.
The Treaty of Sèvres of 1920 provided for a possible Kurdish state.
The Treaty of Lausanne of 1923 replaced it and drew the borders of modern Turkey with no Kurdish state.
Turkey long denied a separate Kurdish identity and banned Kurdish in public life until 1991 and in broadcasting until the early 2000s.
The PKK, founded in 1978, began an armed campaign in 1984 that cost about 40,000 lives.
On 16 March 1988 Saddam Hussein's forces attacked the Kurdish town of Halabja with chemical weapons and killed about 5,000 people.
Iraq's 2005 constitution recognised the Kurdistan Region as autonomous, with its own government in Erbil.
On 25 September 2017 about 93% voted for independence in a referendum that Iraq's government rejected, and Iraqi forces then retook Kirkuk.
The Kurds keep their identity through the Kurdish language and Newroz, the Kurdish New Year on 21 March.
Aspect 3 – Physical and colonial boundaries:
Physical boundaries often fit cultural divides but can also cut through a cultural group, and colonial boundaries ignored cultures altogether.
The Treaty of the Pyrenees of 1659 used the Pyrenees mountains as much of the France–Spain border.
The Basques live on both sides of the Pyrenees, so the physical boundary splits them between two states.
Basque and Catalan were suppressed under Franco from 1939 to 1975.
ETA, founded in 1959, killed over 800 people in its campaign for an independent Basque state, declared an end to violence in 2011 and dissolved in 2018.
Spain responded with autonomy under its 1978 constitution, and the Statute of Gernika of 1979 gave the Basque Autonomous Community its own taxes.
The Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885 set the rules for the European division of Africa.
Many African borders are straight lines that ignore ethnic territories, splitting some groups and joining rival ones.
These superimposed colonial borders are a cause of later conflict and secession.
The Somali people were split between Somalia, Ethiopia, Kenya and Djibouti by colonial borders.
The Maasai are divided between Kenya and Tanzania by the colonial border.

QUESTION geography-pp-2022-higher-q121 (LC Geography Higher 2022 — Part Two — Q21, 80 marks)

PART "21" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Cultural identity in Switzerland is defined by many diverse factors, including its mountain and political boundaries, languages, religions and migration, and together they make it a Willensnation, a nation formed by the will of its people.
Aspect 1 – Physical and political boundaries:
Physical geography and political history shaped Swiss identity and created a federal state in which diversity is managed locally.
About 60% of Switzerland is in the Alps, and the mountains isolated valleys, which helped preserve local dialects and Romansh.
The Federal Charter of 1 August 1291 was an alliance of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden, and 1 August is the Swiss national day.
The Congress of Vienna in 1815 recognised Switzerland's permanent neutrality and fixed its borders.
The Sonderbund War of November 1847 was a short civil war in which a league of Catholic conservative cantons was defeated.
The federal constitution of 1848 created the modern federal state, now of 26 cantons with wide powers over schools, police, taxes and churches.
The canton of Jura was created on 1 January 1979 after referendums in which the French-speaking, mainly Catholic north voted to leave the German-speaking, mainly Protestant canton of Bern.
Willensnation means a nation formed by the will of its people rather than by a common language or ethnicity.
Aspect 2 – Language:
Switzerland has four national languages, so identity is based on shared institutions rather than one language.
German is spoken by about 62% of the population, French by about 23%, Italian by about 8% and Romansh by about 0.5%.
Ticino is the only Italian-speaking canton, and Graubünden is trilingual in German, Romansh and Italian.
The territoriality principle means each canton has its own official language or languages, used in its schools and administration.
Romansh became a national language by referendum in 1938.
The Röstigraben is the cultural divide between German- and French-speaking Switzerland, and it is often visible in referendum results.
The public broadcaster SRG SSR runs separate services in German, French, Italian and Romansh.
No language group wants to join its neighbouring country, because French, Italian and German speakers each identify with Switzerland.
Aspect 3 – Religion:
The Reformation created a religious geography that cuts across the language regions.
Ulrich Zwingli led the Reformation in Zurich from 1519, and John Calvin led it in Geneva from the 1530s and 1540s.
Zurich, Bern, Basel, Geneva, Vaud and Neuchâtel were historically Protestant.
The central cantons of Lucerne, Uri, Schwyz, Zug and Unterwalden, and Fribourg, Valais, Ticino and Jura, were historically Catholic.
Both German- and French-speaking Switzerland contain Protestant and Catholic cantons, so religion cuts across language and reduces conflict between language groups.
In 2022 about a third of people were Roman Catholic, about a fifth Reformed, about a third had no religion and about 6% were Muslim.
On 29 November 2009 a popular initiative banning the building of new minarets passed with about 57.5%.
In March 2021 a ban on face coverings in public passed with about 51%.
Aspect 4 – Migration:
Migration has added new cultural groups and made immigration a central issue of Swiss identity.
Foreign nationals make up about a quarter of the permanent resident population, about 27% in 2023.
The largest foreign groups are Italians, Germans, Portuguese and French.
After 1945 many Italian guest workers came on seasonal permits that restricted family reunification, a restriction abolished in 2002.
Large communities from Kosovo and the rest of former Yugoslavia and from Turkey have settled, and most Swiss Muslims have roots there.
Almost 400,000 cross-border commuters from France, Italy and Germany work in Switzerland, many in Geneva, Basel and Ticino.
The Schwarzenbach Initiative of 1970, which sought to cap the foreign population, was rejected by about 54%.
The initiative against mass immigration of 9 February 2014 passed with about 50.3%.

MODEL ANSWER: Climate characteristics influence economic development by deciding what can be farmed, how severe drought and desertification are and where tourists go, although wealth and technology can reduce the limits it sets.
Aspect 1 – Agriculture:
Temperature, the amount and timing of rainfall and its reliability decide what can be grown and how profitable farming is.
Ireland's rainfall and mild temperatures give a long grass-growing season, so grass is the most important crop, used for dairy, beef and sheep.
Grass grows when soil temperature is above about 6 °C, which is for most of the year in Ireland, and longest in the south and south-west.
Tillage is concentrated in the drier south and east, for example Co. Wexford, Co. Carlow and Co. Kildare, where cereals can be harvested in dry conditions.
The very wet west and north-west suit beef and sheep rearing on rough grazing, with small farms and low incomes.
Too much rain causes waterlogged soils to be poached, which shortens the grazing season, so animals are housed and fed silage for longer at greater cost.
The exceptionally wet summer of 2012 and the cold late spring of 2013 caused a fodder crisis in spring 2013.
Too little rain stops grass growing, and in summer 2018 an anticyclonic spell from late May to mid-July almost halted grass growth.
In India the south-west monsoon reaches Kerala around 1 June and brings about three-quarters of the annual rainfall, so a late or weak monsoon cuts harvests, farm incomes and GDP.
Egypt is almost rainless, so its farming depends on irrigation from the Nile, and the Aswan High Dam of 1970 permits year-round irrigation and more than one crop a year.
Aspect 2 – Drought and desertification:
Drought is a long period with much less precipitation than normal, and desertification is land degradation in dry areas caused by climatic variations and human activities.
Droughts are caused by blocking anticyclones, by the failure or shift of the ITCZ or monsoon, and by El Niño.
The economic effects of drought include crop failure, loss of livestock, higher food prices and loss of farm income.
In poor countries drought brings famine and migration to cities and abroad.
Drought in the Horn of Africa in 2011 followed two failed rainy seasons, and the UN declared famine in parts of southern Somalia in July 2011.
A later UN-backed study estimated about 260,000 deaths in Somalia, about half of them children under five.
In the Millennium Drought in south-eastern Australia, from the late 1990s to 2009, farming in the Murray–Darling Basin was hit and farm output fell.
A rich economy such as Australia absorbs drought through insurance, government aid, water trading and desalination plants.
In the Sahel the droughts of the 1970s and 1980s caused failed harvests and livestock deaths, pastoralists lost their livelihoods and the region became dependent on food imports.
Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s, which hit fishing and farming around it.
In Niger farmer-managed natural regeneration has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, and the Great Green Wall was launched by the African Union in 2007.
Aspect 3 – Tourism:
Sunshine, warmth, rainfall and snow cover are resources for tourism and decide the length of the tourist season.
Hot, dry, sunny summers have made the Mediterranean one of the world's most visited regions.
Spain received about 85 million international tourists in 2023, making it the world's second most visited country after France.
Mass package tourism grew from the 1960s with cheap jet flights.
Tourists arrive in the driest season, so water demand from hotels, pools and golf courses peaks when supply is lowest.
The Canary Islands have mild dry winters and the cooling Canary Current, which gives them a year-round tourist season that attracts northern Europeans.
Alpine ski tourism depends on reliable snow cover, and climate change is raising the snow line and shortening seasons, so resorts invest in snow-making.
Ireland's cool, cloudy, wet and unreliable summers limit beach tourism and push Irish holidaymakers abroad.
Ireland instead markets its scenery and outdoor activities, and Fáilte Ireland launched the Wild Atlantic Way, a touring route of about 2,500 km, in 2014.
Hurricane Irma, a Category 5 hurricane in September 2017, damaged or destroyed almost every building on Barbuda in the Caribbean.
In July 2023 wildfires on the Greek island of Rhodes forced thousands of tourists to be evacuated from resorts.
