PART "16" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Soil processes shape the depth, colour, structure, acidity and fertility of soil, as podzolisation, calcification and soil erosion show.
Aspect 1 – Podzolisation:
Podzolisation is an intense form of leaching that forms the podzol, a strongly acidic and infertile soil.
It occurs in cool, wet climates where precipitation is well above evapotranspiration, on acid parent material such as granite or sandstone.
Coniferous forest or heather supplies acid mor humus, which releases organic acids into percolating water.
These acids combine with iron and aluminium and carry them, along with bases and humus, down the profile.
This leaves a bleached, ash-grey, sandy A/E horizon of quartz grains.
Iron, aluminium and humus are deposited in the B horizon, which turns dark reddish-brown.
A thin, hard iron pan may form in the B horizon, which blocks drainage and root growth and can let peat form on top.
The result is a soil with sharply defined horizons and a pH of about 4 to 4.5, found on the granite of the Wicklow Mountains and under Sitka spruce plantations.
Because of these characteristics podzols are used only for rough grazing and forestry, and they can be improved by deep ploughing to break the iron pan and by heavy liming.
Aspect 2 – Calcification:
Calcification is the process in dry continental climates by which calcium carbonate is moved a short distance and deposited in the B horizon, forming the fertile chernozem.
It occurs where evapotranspiration roughly equals or exceeds precipitation, with low rainfall of about 250 to 500 mm a year.
Chernozems lie under the natural grassland of the steppes of Ukraine and southern Russia and the Prairies of North America.
Rain penetrates only part of the way down the soil, so leaching is weak and bases are not lost.
Calcium carbonate is dissolved and deposited in the B horizon as white nodules or a lime-rich layer.
In dry periods capillary action draws water and calcium back up towards the surface.
Deep grass roots take up calcium and return it to the surface in litter, and the dying roots supply large amounts of organic matter.
The result is a neutral to slightly alkaline soil with a black, humus-rich A horizon commonly about 1 m deep and a crumb structure.
These characteristics make chernozems some of the world's main wheat and maize lands.
Aspect 3 – Soil erosion:
Soil erosion is the removal of soil, especially topsoil, faster than it forms, and it changes soil characteristics by thinning the profile.
Natural erosion is slow and balanced by soil formation, but accelerated erosion follows the removal of vegetation.
Erosion by water takes the forms of rain-splash, sheet wash, and rills and gullies.
Erosion by wind, called deflation, removes dry, loose, bare soil.
Erosion removes the humus-rich A horizon and its nutrients, which lowers fertility and exposes infertile subsoil.
It also worsens soil structure and reduces its water retention.
In the 1930s the Great Plains of the USA, where grassland had been ploughed for wheat, were hit by drought, and winds blew away the bare soil in the Dust Bowl and forced many farming families to leave.
The eroded material is redeposited elsewhere as alluvium or loess.
Contour ploughing, terracing and shelter belts of trees reduce erosion by trapping water and cutting wind speed, and the US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935.

QUESTION geography-pp-2025-higher-q117 (LC Geography Higher 2025 — Part Two — Q17, 80 marks)

PART "17" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The four components of soil, mineral matter, organic matter, water and air, together control the texture, structure, fertility and horizons of a developing soil.
Aspect 1 – Mineral matter:
Mineral matter is particles of weathered rock, from stones down to clay, and it forms the skeleton of the soil and decides its texture.
Parent rock controls the result, as limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils while granite gives sandy, acidic soils such as those of the Wicklow Mountains.
As it weathers, mineral matter releases nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium.
Clay particles are the chemically active part, and together with humus they form the clay-humus complex that holds nutrient ions against leaching.
Most Irish soils formed on transported parent material such as glacial till, laid down by the last glaciation about 10,000 years ago.
Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay, and sandy soils are free-draining but easily leached while clay soils hold water but become waterlogged.
A loam of roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay combines good drainage with good water and nutrient retention, which makes it the best texture for farming.
Aspect 2 – Organic matter:
Organic matter is living organisms plus dead plant and animal remains, and it makes up about 5% of a good loam by volume.
Decomposers break litter down into humus, a dark, jelly-like substance that is the end product of decomposition.
Humus darkens the soil, binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients and is the main store of nitrogen in the soil.
Earthworms mix and aerate the soil and drag litter down, as Charles Darwin showed in his last book in 1881.
Vegetation decides the humus type, as deciduous woodland and grassland give mild mull humus and brown earths, while coniferous forest and heath give acidic mor humus and podzols.
Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules of legumes such as clover add nitrogen to the soil.
In waterlogged, anaerobic conditions decomposition is slow, so organic matter builds up as peat, which covers about a fifth of Ireland.
Aspect 3 – Water:
Water enters the soil as precipitation and fills about a quarter of a good loam by volume, in the pore spaces.
Dissolved nutrients in the soil solution are absorbed by plant roots.
Gravitational water drains down through large pores after rain and carries nutrients away in the process of leaching.
Capillary water is held in small pores by surface tension and is the water that is available to plants.
Where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, as in Ireland, water moves down the profile and leaches the soil, giving brown earths and podzols.
Where evaporation exceeds precipitation, capillary action raises water to the surface and leaves salts and lime there, as in deserts and grasslands.
Too much water fills all the pores and causes waterlogging and gleying, which produces grey, mottled, cold gley soils such as those of the drumlin belt of Cavan, Monaghan and Leitrim.
Aspect 4 – Air:
Air fills the pores not occupied by water, about a quarter of the volume of a good loam, and when water rises in the pores the air falls.
Soil air supplies oxygen for root respiration and for the aerobic organisms that decompose litter.
Soil air also supplies nitrogen for nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
Soil air is richer in carbon dioxide than the atmosphere, which forms weak carbonic acid in soil water and aids chemical weathering.
Poorly aerated, anaerobic soils decompose slowly, so organic matter builds up as peat and iron is reduced, giving grey colours.
Good crumb structure built by humus opens pores for both air and water.
Compaction by heavy machinery and trampling by livestock on wet ground, called poaching in Ireland, destroys the soil peds and reduces the air in the soil.

PART "18" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Human activity has altered the world's biomes, as the felling of tropical rainforests, intensive agricultural practices and industrial development show.
Aspect 1 – Felling of tropical rainforests:
The felling of tropical rainforests destroys the biome of the Amazon Basin, the largest rainforest in the world, which covers about 5.5 million km² with about 60% of it in Brazil.
Close to a fifth of the original Amazon forest has been cleared.
Cattle ranching is the largest cause of clearance, and soya farming spreading north from Mato Grosso, much of it exported as animal feed, is another.
Roads such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway, begun in the early 1970s, and mining at Carajás opened the forest to settlers and clearance.
Clearance destroys habitat and species, many of them found only in the region.
The nutrient cycle is broken, because most nutrients are held in the vegetation, so cleared latosols lose their fertility within a few years.
Exposed soil erodes under heavy rain and can bake into a hard laterite crust, and rivers silt up.
Less evapotranspiration reduces rainfall downwind, and scientists warn that losing about 20 to 25% of the forest could push parts of the Amazon towards degraded savanna.
Burning and decay release the carbon stored in the forest as carbon dioxide, which adds to global warming.
Aspect 2 – Intensive agricultural practices:
Intensive farming uses high inputs of capital, machinery, fertiliser, pesticides and water to get maximum output per hectare, and it creates monocultures and larger fields.
In Ireland the end of EU milk quotas on 1 April 2015 led to the dairy herd expanding.
Hedgerows were removed and fields enlarged, and wetlands and callows were drained.
Species-rich meadows were reseeded with ryegrass, and mechanised silage cutting destroys the nests of ground-nesting birds such as the corncrake.
Heavy use of fertiliser and slurry raises nitrate levels in rivers and causes eutrophication of lakes.
Wild bees and farmland birds have declined.
In the Aral Sea region, Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers from the 1960s to irrigate cotton and rice in the desert, and the sea, once the world's fourth-largest lake, shrank to a fraction of its former size.
The salinity of the Aral Sea rose and its fishing industry collapsed, leaving ports such as Moynaq far from the shore.
Dust storms now carry salt and pesticide residues from the exposed seabed, and salinisation and waterlogging damaged the irrigated soils.
Aspect 3 – Industrial development:
Industrial development alters biomes through pollution and land use, as burning coal and oil releases gases that harm forests and lakes far from their source.
Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from power stations, industry and vehicles form acids that are carried downwind across national borders.
The Black Triangle, where Germany, Poland and the Czech Republic meet, had lignite-burning power stations, and large areas of spruce forest in the Ore Mountains died in the 1970s and 1980s in a process known as Waldsterben.
Acid rain acidified thousands of lakes in southern Norway and Sweden from the 1960s to the 1980s, and fish died out in many of them, largely because of the emissions of other countries.
Acid rain causes needle loss and dieback in coniferous forests and leaches nutrients from the soil.
Aluminium released into the lake water by acid rain kills fish.
The Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution of 1979 and flue-gas desulphurisation helped to cut sulphur dioxide emissions in Europe by well over half.
The Athabasca oil sands near Fort McMurray in Canada are mined by open-pit methods that strip away boreal forest and peatland and leave large tailings ponds of polluted water.
Oil spills in the Niger Delta, Nigeria, have polluted creeks, mangrove forest and farmland, and a UNEP study of Ogoniland in 2011 found that a clean-up could take 25 to 30 years.
