PART 8C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Environmental impact 1 – Global warming:
Burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas releases carbon dioxide, the main greenhouse gas.
Extra carbon dioxide traps heat in the atmosphere, which enhances the greenhouse effect and causes global warming.
Fossil fuels supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy, so the amount of carbon dioxide released is enormous.
China has been the world's largest carbon dioxide emitter since the mid-2000s, although the USA emits far more per person.
Global warming melts glaciers and ice sheets and raises sea levels, which threatens low-lying coasts.
Environmental impact 2 – Acid rain:
Sulphur dioxide, mainly from coal and heavy oil, and nitrogen oxides, mainly from vehicles and power stations, dissolve in water vapour to form sulphuric and nitric acids.
These fall as acid rain or as dry deposition of acidic particles and gases.
Acid rain is a transboundary problem, as emissions from Britain and Germany were carried by the prevailing south-westerly winds to Norway and Sweden.
Thousands of Scandinavian lakes were acidified, and aluminium released from the soil killed the fish.
Forests were damaged, as the Black Forest in Germany was reported dying in the 1980s and the Black Triangle of Germany, Poland and Czechia suffered from lignite-burning power stations.
Limestone and marble buildings and statues dissolve in acid rain.
Responses include the 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution, scrubbers fitted at Moneypoint power station in County Clare, and catalytic converters, and European sulphur dioxide emissions have fallen by well over three-quarters since 1990.
Environmental impact 3 – Smog:
Burning coal and turf in homes releases smoke and fine particles.
In calm, cold winter weather an anticyclone creates a temperature inversion that traps the smoke near the ground and forms smog.
Smog causes bronchitis, asthma and heart and lung disease.
London's Great Smog of December 1952 caused about 4,000 deaths at the time and led to the Clean Air Act of 1956.
The ban on smoky coal in Dublin from 1990 cut black smoke by about 70% and was linked to about 350 fewer deaths a year.
Other impacts:
Oil spills during transport pollute the sea and coasts.
Coal mining and peat cutting damage the landscape.
Power stations release warm cooling water into rivers, which causes thermal pollution.
