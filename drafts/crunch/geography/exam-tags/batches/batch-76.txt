QUESTION geography-pp-2022-higher-q123 (LC Geography Higher 2022 — Part Two — Q23, 80 marks)

PART 23 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Mid-latitude depressions, anticyclones and land and sea breezes each form through differences in air pressure and heating, and each has a distinct impact on weather patterns in Ireland and elsewhere.
Aspect 1 – Mid-latitude depressions:
A depression is a low-pressure system about 1,000 to 3,000 km across with closed isobars, anticlockwise winds in the northern hemisphere and fronts, and it is the main source of Ireland's rain and wind.
At the polar front cold polar maritime air from the north meets warm moist tropical maritime air from the south-west.
A wave develops on the front, often where the jet stream above is diverging and removing air aloft.
Warm air pushes into the cold air and rises over it, so surface pressure falls and air is drawn in and turned anticlockwise by the Coriolis force.
A warm front forms ahead of the warm air and a cold front forms behind it, with the warm sector between them.
The cold front moves faster and catches up with the warm front, lifting the warm sector off the ground in an occlusion, and the depression then fills and dies after a few days to about a week.
At the warm front the cloud thickens and lowers to nimbostratus, and a long period of steady rain follows.
At the cold front cumulonimbus clouds bring short heavy showers, hail and gusty squalls, the temperature falls sharply and the wind veers north-westerly.
Because depressions cross Ireland regularly the weather is changeable with rain in every month, and the west is wetter than the east because depressions arrive there first.
Strong winds and storms are most common in autumn and winter, as with ex-hurricane Ophelia on 16 October 2017.
Aspect 2 – Anticyclones:
An anticyclone is an area of high pressure with widely spaced isobars, light winds blowing clockwise outwards in the northern hemisphere and no fronts.
Air converging high in the atmosphere sinks in a process called subsidence.
The sinking air is compressed and warms, so its relative humidity falls and clouds evaporate.
Dynamic highs such as the Azores High form from sinking air in the general circulation and often extend a ridge over Ireland in summer.
Thermal highs such as the Siberian High form when the ground cools intensely in winter.
In summer anticyclones bring clear skies, strong sunshine, high temperatures and light winds, and prolonged highs cause heatwaves and drought.
In winter clear nights cause hard frost and freezing fog in valleys.
Anticyclonic gloom occurs when a layer of stratocumulus is trapped under a temperature inversion and keeps the sky grey, and smog forms where pollution is trapped.
A blocking anticyclone stays in place, blocks the westerly flow and diverts depressions north or south, giving a long dry spell.
In late February and early March 2018 a block of high pressure over Scandinavia drew bitterly cold polar continental air over Ireland in the Beast from the East, and Storm Emma then brought heavy snow and the first nationwide Red snow warning.
Aspect 3 – Land and sea breezes:
Land and sea breezes are local winds caused by the different rates at which land and sea heat and cool.
By day the land heats faster than the sea, so air over the land warms, expands and rises, creating lower pressure over the land.
The cooler sea has relatively higher pressure, so cool air flows onshore as a sea breeze.
The air that rose returns seaward at height and sinks over the sea, completing a circulation cell.
Sea breezes are strongest on hot, calm, sunny summer afternoons, often under an anticyclone.
After sunset the land loses heat faster than the sea, so air over the land cools and becomes denser, creating higher pressure over the land.
Air then flows offshore from the land to the sea as a land breeze, which is weaker than the sea breeze because the night-time temperature difference is smaller.
Sea breezes lower coastal temperatures in the afternoon and raise humidity.
A sea-breeze front can trigger a line of cumulus clouds or showers inland.
Sea breezes can carry sea fog onshore and trap urban pollution, while land breezes help clear coastal fog.

QUESTION geography-pp-2022-higher-q124 (LC Geography Higher 2022 — Part Two — Q24, 80 marks)

PART 24 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: This discussion shows how climate characteristics of temperature, rainfall and wind change across space with latitude, relief, distance from the sea and ocean currents, and over time from ice ages to present-day global warming.
Aspect 1 – Change over space with latitude and relief:
Temperature falls and seasonal contrasts grow from the equator to the poles because the sun's rays strike the surface at a lower angle in higher latitudes.
The equatorial climate of the Amazon and Congo basins has monthly mean temperatures of about 26 to 28 °C and an annual range of only 2 to 3 °C, because the sun is always high in the sky.
Rainfall there is over 2,000 mm a year, and Manaus in Brazil receives about 2,300 mm, falling in every month.
The rain is convectional, because intense heating and very moist air build towering clouds that produce heavy afternoon thunderstorms.
The trade winds converge at the ITCZ, which lifts and reinforces the moist air over the equator.
Further from the equator the Mediterranean climate has hot dry summers, with July means of about 25 °C or more in southern Italy, and mild wet winters.
This change happens because the pressure belts shift with the sun, so in summer the subtropical high brings sinking, warming air and drought, while in winter the westerlies and their depressions bring rain.
Climate also changes with altitude, because temperature falls with height, so high mountains have a vertical sequence of climates and snow lies on high ground even near the equator.
Across a mountain range, the windward side gets relief rain and the leeward side lies in a rain shadow, so the east of Ireland receives about 750 mm a year while the west receives 1,000 to 1,250 mm and some mountains over 2,000 mm.
Aspect 2 – Change over space with the sea and ocean currents:
Distance from the sea changes the annual range of temperature, because land heats and cools quickly while the sea heats and cools slowly.
Ireland has a cool temperate oceanic climate with a mean January temperature of about 4 to 7 °C, a mean July temperature of about 14 to 16 °C and an annual range of only about 10 °C.
Moscow, deep inside the Eurasian continent, has a January mean of about −7 °C and a July mean of about 19 °C, a much larger range.
Verkhoyansk in Siberia has an annual range of over 60 °C, because it lies furthest from the moderating influence of the oceans.
Precipitation also changes with distance inland, because the westerlies lose most of their moisture crossing the land, so Moscow receives only about 600 to 700 mm a year, mostly from summer convectional thunderstorms.
In Ireland most rain is frontal, because depressions form on the polar front and are steered across the Atlantic by the jet stream, so the weather is changeable.
Warm and cold ocean currents change the climate of coasts at the same latitude, because the North Atlantic Drift warms the winter air and seas of western Europe so that frost and snow are infrequent on low ground in Ireland.
Cold currents such as the Humboldt, Benguela and Canary cool and dry the west coasts of continents, which helps to create the Atacama, Namib and Sahara deserts.
In winter the cold, dry, clear Siberian High sits over the continental interior, and snow lies there for months, in sharp contrast to the mild, wet winters of oceanic Ireland.
Aspect 3 – Change over time:
Climate changes over timescales from millions of years to a few years, and the causes differ with the timescale.
The Quaternary glaciation began about 2.6 million years ago, and since then the earth has swung between long glacial periods and shorter, warm interglacials, with a cycle of about 100,000 years over the last 800,000 years.
At the Last Glacial Maximum about 20,000 years ago, ice sheets covered much of North America and northern Europe, including almost all of Ireland, and sea level was about 120 m lower than today.
Milankovitch cycles caused these swings, because changes in the shape of the earth's orbit (about 100,000 years), the tilt of its axis (about 41,000 years) and its wobble (about 19,000 to 26,000 years) alter the distribution of sunlight by latitude and season.
Feedbacks amplified the small orbital changes, because more ice raised the albedo and colder oceans absorbed more carbon dioxide, so CO2 fell to about 180 ppm at glacial maxima compared with about 280 ppm in interglacials.
The Medieval Warm Period, from about AD 950 to 1250, was a warm phase in the North Atlantic and Europe, and Norse settlers led by Erik the Red settled Greenland from about AD 985.
The Little Ice Age, from the 14th to the mid-19th century, was coldest in the 17th century, and the River Thames in London froze so that frost fairs were held on the ice, the last in 1814.
Volcanic eruptions cool the climate for short periods, because sulphate aerosols reflect sunlight, so after Mount Pinatubo erupted in June 1991 global average temperature fell by about 0.5 °C for about a year.
Recent warming is caused by humans, because burning fossil fuels, deforestation and farming raise greenhouse gases that absorb outgoing long-wave radiation, so CO2 has risen from about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm.
Global average surface temperature has risen by about 1.1 to 1.2 °C since the late 1800s, 2024 was the warmest year on record, and Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13% per decade since 1979.
Ireland's average temperature has risen by about 0.9 °C since 1900, which shows that climate is still changing today.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.0] Prevailing south-westerly winds bring maritime air (air that has crossed the sea, so it is mild and moist) off the Atlantic.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.10.3] Depressions form frequently on the polar front (where warm tropical and cold polar air meet) and are steered by the jet stream (fast high-altitude winds), so they bring bursts of rain across the region.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.11.b] The Mediterranean climate lies on the west sides of continents between about 30° and 40° (some places to about 45°). It has hot summers and mild winters.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.11.1] Summers are hot: lowland July means are about 25 °C or more, for example in southern Italy.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.b] Winter rain and summer drought both come from the seasonal shift of the pressure belts. The summer subtropical high (a belt of high pressure with sinking air) is the key to the explanation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.2] In summer the subtropical high (the Azores High in the Mediterranean, the North Pacific High in California) moves poleward over the region, bringing sinking, warming air, clear skies and no rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.3] In winter the pressure belts shift towards the equator, so the westerlies and their depressions bring rain; the warm Mediterranean Sea also spawns its own winter depressions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.12.4] On some of these coasts cold currents (the California, Humboldt, Benguela and Canary currents) keep summers cooler and drier.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.b] Precipitation is lower than on oceanic coasts and peaks in summer, the opposite of Ireland. The reasons are the land itself, the distance from moisture and the pressure systems.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.3] Distance from the oceans: the westerlies have lost most of their moisture crossing Europe, and in North America the Rockies block Pacific air from reaching the interior.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.15.4] The winter Siberian High, a cold, dry, clear thermal anticyclone (high pressure caused by intense cooling of the land), sits over the continent; North America has an equivalent.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.b] Climate is not the same everywhere because the controls on it vary from place to place. Each kind of change across space has its own cause.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.0] From equator to pole (latitude): temperature falls and seasonal contrasts grow with latitude.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.1] From coast to interior: continentality increases the annual range; Ireland's annual range is about 10 °C, while Verkhoyansk's is over 60 °C.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.2] From west to east coasts: cold currents cool and dry west coasts, while warm currents such as the North Atlantic Drift warm them.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.3] With altitude: temperature falls with height, so high mountains have a vertical sequence of climates, and snow lies on high ground even near the equator.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.18.4] Across a mountain range: the windward side gets relief rain, while the leeward side lies in a rain shadow, as the Deccan does in India.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.b] Climate changes over very different timescales, and each timescale has its own natural causes. Human activity has added a further cause in recent times.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.0] Timescales: long-term change over millions of years (plate movement, mountain building); glacial cycles over tens to hundreds of thousands of years; centuries (the Little Ice Age); years (El Niño and volcanic eruptions); and recent human-caused warming.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.1] Milankovitch orbital cycles (regular changes in the earth's orbit and tilt, described under Ice Ages causes below).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.3] Volcanic eruptions: sulphate aerosols (tiny airborne particles) reflect sunlight and cool the earth for a year or two; after Mount Pinatubo, Philippines, erupted in June 1991, global average temperature fell by about 0.5 °C for about a year (1992).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.4] Plate tectonics: moving continents and opening or closing seaways change ocean currents, so heat is redistributed around the world.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.5] Changes in ocean circulation, because currents carry heat around the globe.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.19.6] Feedbacks (effects that amplify a change): the ice–albedo feedback (albedo is how much sunlight a surface reflects), and CO2 and methane released or absorbed by oceans and permafrost (permanently frozen ground).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.20.b] Since the Industrial Revolution people have become a cause of climate change.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.20.0] The enhanced greenhouse effect (extra heat trapped by added greenhouse gases) comes from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and agriculture; land-use change and aerosols also alter the climate. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.3] Pollen preserved in peat bogs and lake muds; Irish bogs record changes in vegetation, and therefore climate, since the Ice Age.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.21.5] Historical records such as harvest dates, frost fairs and ships' logs; instrument records since the 19th century.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.b] The first of the three climate-change settings is the Ice Ages. The present era has swung repeatedly between very cold and warmer conditions.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.0] The Quaternary glaciation began about 2.6 million years ago; since then the earth has swung between long glacial periods and shorter, warm interglacials.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.1] Over the last 800,000 years the cycle has been about 100,000 years long; we now live in an interglacial.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.2] At the Last Glacial Maximum (the coldest point of the last glacial period), about 20,000 years ago, ice sheets covered much of North America and northern Europe, including Scandinavia, Britain and Ireland.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.3] Global sea level was about 120 m lower than today, because water was locked in ice, exposing land bridges between islands and continents (for example between Britain and continental Europe).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.22.4] Global average temperature was several degrees colder, so the climate belts were pushed towards the equator.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.23.0] Ireland had two main cold stages, the Munsterian and the Midlandian. The Munsterian ice covered almost all of Ireland; the Midlandian ice had gone by about 10,000 years ago.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.b] Small changes in the earth's orbit start an ice age, and feedbacks make the change much bigger. The orbital changes alter the distribution of sunlight, not the total amount.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.0] Milankovitch cycles, named after Milutin Milanković, a Serbian scientist who calculated them in the early 20th century, change the distribution of sunlight by latitude and season more than its total amount.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.1] Eccentricity (the shape of the orbit) varies over about 100,000 years; obliquity (the tilt, varying between about 22° and 24.5°) over about 41,000 years; precession (the wobble of the axis) over roughly 19,000–26,000 years.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.3] Feedbacks amplify the small orbital change: more ice raises albedo (reflects more sunlight), and colder oceans absorb more CO2, lowering greenhouse gases.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.4] Ice cores show CO2 near 180 ppm (parts per million) at glacial maxima and about 280 ppm in interglacials.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.5] Changes in the Atlantic circulation, which means a change in how heat is carried to the north.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5.24.6] Land near the North Pole allows ice sheets to build, because snow can pile up on land but not on open sea.
