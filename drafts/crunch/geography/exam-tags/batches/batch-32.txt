QUESTION geography-pp-2018-higher-q120 (LC Geography Higher 2018 — Part Two — Q20, 80 marks)

PART "20" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Multiculturalism is the coexistence of several distinct cultural groups within one society, and this answer discusses its causes, its benefits and its challenges in the modern world.
Aspect 1 – Causes of multicultural societies:
Multiculturalism is the presence of several distinct cultural, ethnic or religious groups within one society, each keeping its own identity.
Migration is the main cause, as people move to other countries for work, safety or education and settle there.
Economic migrants move to where jobs are, as workers from the new EU states did when they came to Ireland after the 2004 enlargement.
After 1945 Italian guest workers came to Switzerland and Belgium to fill labour shortages, and a 1946 agreement sent Italians to work in Belgian coal mines.
Belgium signed labour agreements with Morocco and Turkey in 1964, which brought new communities to Belgian cities.
Refugees fleeing war and persecution add to cultural diversity, as in the 2015 movement of displaced people along the Balkan route into Europe.
Free movement within the EU lets citizens live and work in other member states, and Switzerland has applied free movement of persons with the EU since 2002.
Colonial links led people from former colonies to settle in the countries that once ruled them, such as Britain and France.
Globalisation, cheap travel and the internet make movement easier and help migrants keep in touch with their home cultures.
Aspect 2 – Cultural enrichment and economic benefits:
Migrants bring new foods, festivals, religious celebrations and sports, which become part of everyday life in their new country.
Polish shops and Polish food are now common in Irish towns, so new foods sit beside traditional dishes.
Census 2022 recorded Polish as the most common home language in Ireland after English and Irish, which shows growing language diversity.
About 20% of residents in Ireland were born outside the country in Census 2022, so new communities are now part of Irish society.
Multicultural festivals blend traditions, as the Notting Hill Carnival in London shows through its Caribbean music, costume and food.
Fusion food combines traditions, as the döner kebab became popular in Germany through Turkish immigrants.
Migrants fill labour shortages and pay taxes, and foreign nationals make up about 27% of the resident population of Switzerland (2023).
Cities such as Brussels, which hosts the main EU institutions and NATO headquarters, become international and cosmopolitan centres.
Contact between cultures builds understanding and tolerance between groups.
Aspect 3 – Challenges and government responses:
Different groups can end up living in separate neighbourhoods, which limits contact between them and slows integration.
Migrants may face racism and discrimination when looking for jobs and housing.
Language barriers slow the integration of newcomers in schools and workplaces.
Some people fear that the majority culture will be diluted, and the Swiss initiative against mass immigration passed with about 50.3% in February 2014.
The Schwarzenbach Initiative of 1970 sought to cap the foreign population of Switzerland, although it was rejected by about 54%.
Canada follows a policy of multiculturalism that recognises several cultures within one state.
France follows assimilation, which expects minorities to adopt the dominant culture, and its principle of laïcité shapes the debate on religious dress in public life.
Violent extremism, such as the Islamic State bombings in Brussels in March 2016 that killed 32 people, has raised tension and security concerns around integration.
Success depends on policies that give migrants equal access to education, work and political life while respecting their cultures.

QUESTION geography-pp-2018-higher-q121 (LC Geography Higher 2018 — Part Two — Q21, 80 marks)

PART "21" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Boundaries, both physical and political, shape identity by uniting the people inside them, dividing cultural groups and creating minorities.
Aspect 1 – Physical boundaries:
Physical boundaries follow natural features such as mountains, rivers, lakes or seas.
The Pyrenees form much of the border between France and Spain, as fixed by the Treaty of the Pyrenees in 1659.
The Rhine forms part of the border between France and Germany, and the Rio Grande forms part of the border between the USA and Mexico.
Physical boundaries often fit cultural divides, so the people on each side develop a distinct national identity.
They can also cut through a cultural group, as the Basques live on both sides of the Pyrenees and share an identity across the border.
The sea around the island of Ireland makes it a clear physical unit, which supports a shared sense of island identity.
About 60% of Switzerland lies in the Alps, and the mountains isolated valleys, which helped preserve local dialects and Romansh.
Barriers limit contact between peoples, so isolated groups keep their own customs and traditions.
The Himalayas separate India from China and help give each side a distinct culture.
Aspect 2 – Political boundaries and partition:
A political boundary is a line drawn by governments, not by nature, which defines the territory and citizens of a state.
The Government of Ireland Act 1920 created two administrations on the island, and Northern Ireland was established in 1921.
The border is about 499 km long and follows old county boundaries rather than physical features.
It cuts through communities, farms and the Erne and Foyle catchments.
Partition created two states, two majorities and two minorities, with most unionists feeling British and most nationalists feeling Irish.
Identity is expressed through symbols, as Orange Order parades on 12 July, GAA clubs and painted murals and kerbstones mark out unionist and nationalist areas.
The Troubles from the late 1960s to 1998 killed over 3,500 people, which shows how deeply identity can divide.
Under the Good Friday Agreement of 1998 people in Northern Ireland may be Irish, British or both, which recognises layered identity.
The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020 after Northern Ireland voted about 56% Remain in 2016, and the Windsor Framework of 2023 keeps the land border free of checks.
Aspect 3 – Imposed, hardened and softened boundaries:
Superimposed boundaries are imposed by outside powers regardless of the cultures they divide.
The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 set the rules for the European division of Africa, and many African borders are straight lines that ignore ethnic territories.
These borders split some groups and joined rival ones, which caused later conflict and secession.
The Treaty of Lausanne in 1923 drew the borders of modern Turkey with no Kurdish state, so the Kurds are divided between Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria.
Turkey banned Kurdish in public life until the early 1990s, yet the Kurds keep their identity through their language and Newroz on 21 March.
Boundaries can harden into barriers, as the Berlin Wall stood from 1961 to 1989 and the Cyprus Green Line has divided Nicosia since 1974.
Boundaries can also soften, as the Schengen Agreement of 1985 removed passport checks between most EU states.
Open borders encourage a European identity to grow alongside national identities.
Changing boundaries create new identities and new minorities, as the break-up of Yugoslavia produced new nation states in the 1990s.

PART "22" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Mid-latitude depressions, anticyclones and land and sea breezes each form in a different way, and each has a clear impact on weather patterns.
Aspect 1 – Mid-latitude depressions:
A depression is a low-pressure system about 1,000 to 3,000 km across, with closed isobars and winds blowing anticlockwise inwards in the northern hemisphere.
It forms at the polar front, where cold polar maritime air from the north meets warm, moist tropical maritime air from the south-west.
A wave or kink develops on the front, often where the jet stream above removes air aloft.
Warm air rises over the cold air, surface pressure falls and air is drawn in and turned anticlockwise by the Coriolis force.
A warm front forms ahead of the warm air and a cold front forms behind it, with the warm sector between them.
The cold front moves faster and catches up, lifting the warm sector to form an occluded front, and the depression then fills and dies after a few days to a week.
The warm front brings thickening cloud and a long spell of steady rain, while the cold front brings short heavy showers, gusts and a sharp fall in temperature.
Because depressions cross Ireland regularly, the weather is changeable with rain in every month, and the west is wetter than the east.
The strongest storms come in autumn and winter, as with ex-hurricane Ophelia on 16 October 2017 and Storm Éowyn on 24 January 2025, and flooding follows when depressions arrive in quick succession.
Aspect 2 – Anticyclones:
An anticyclone is an area of high pressure with widely spaced closed isobars, light winds blowing clockwise outwards in the northern hemisphere and no fronts.
It is large and slow-moving and can last for days or weeks.
Air converging high in the atmosphere sinks in a process called subsidence, and as it is compressed it warms, so its relative humidity falls and cloud evaporates.
Dynamic highs form from sinking air in the general circulation, as the Azores High does when it extends a ridge over Ireland in summer.
Thermal highs form when the ground cools intensely in winter, as the Siberian High does over Central Asia.
In summer an anticyclone brings clear skies, strong sunshine, high daytime temperatures and light winds, and a prolonged spell causes heatwaves and drought.
In winter clear nights bring hard frost and freezing fog in valleys, or anticyclonic gloom when cloud is trapped under a temperature inversion.
A blocking anticyclone diverts depressions north or south, so one region has a long dry spell while others get prolonged rain.
In late February and early March 2018 a block over Scandinavia drew polar continental air from Russia over Ireland in the Beast from the East, and Storm Emma then brought heavy snow on 1 and 2 March.
Aspect 3 – Land and sea breezes:
Land and sea breezes are local coastal winds caused by the uneven heating of land and sea.
By day the land heats faster than the sea under sunshine.
The air over the land warms, expands and rises, which creates lower pressure over the land while the cooler sea has relatively higher pressure.
Cool air flows onshore as a sea breeze, and the air that rose returns seaward at height and sinks over the sea to complete a circulation cell.
After sunset the land loses heat faster than the sea, so the air over the land cools and becomes denser, which creates higher pressure over the land.
Air then flows offshore as a land breeze, which is weaker than the sea breeze because the night-time temperature difference is smaller.
Sea breezes lower coastal temperatures in the afternoon and raise humidity.
A sea-breeze front can trigger a line of cumulus cloud or showers inland, and sea breezes can carry sea fog onshore.
Land breezes help clear coastal fog, and sea breezes are strongest on hot, calm summer afternoons, often under an anticyclone.
