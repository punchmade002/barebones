PART 6B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named region – the Paris Basin, France:
The Paris Basin is a large, low sedimentary lowland in northern France, centred on Paris.
It is the main arable region of France, with cereals in the Beauce and vines in Champagne.
Factor 1 – Relief:
The basin is a saucer of sedimentary rock and the land is mostly below about 300 m, so the relief is low and gently rolling.
Gentle slopes allow large fields to be worked with machinery, and many cereal farms in the Beauce exceed 100 hectares.
The flat plateau of the Beauce, south-west of Paris around Chartres, is the granary of France and grows wheat, barley and maize.
The level plateaux of Brie and Picardy also suit large mechanised farms, which grow sugar beet and oilseed rape.
On flat plateaux the deep, fine limon soil stays in place instead of washing downhill, so it remains fertile and easy to work.
On the eastern side, resistant rock layers form escarpments, and vines grow on their slopes around Reims and Épernay.
The sloping ground and thin chalk soils drain freely, which suits vines, and only sparkling wine from this protected area may be called Champagne.
The dry chalk plain east of Reims is flat and was once called Champagne pouilleuse, but fertiliser and mechanised farming turned it into productive cereal land.
The gentle gradients of the Seine and its tributaries make them navigable, so barges can carry grain and other produce towards Rouen and Paris.
Between the escarpments lie low, flat clay vales, and the wetter low-lying land in the west suits grass, so Normandy has dairy farming and produces Camembert cheese.
Factor 2 – Markets:
Île-de-France has about 12 million people, about 18% of France's population, so there is a very large market for food close to the farms.
Market gardening of vegetables and flowers is practised on the edge of Paris because fresh produce reaches the market quickly.
Rungis International Market, south of Paris, opened in 1969 and is one of the world's largest wholesale fresh-food markets, linking farms to the city's shops and restaurants.
The road and rail network is radial, centred on Paris, so produce travels quickly from the farms to the city.
Rouen is France's main grain-exporting port, so it links the basin's cereal surplus to overseas markets.
Le Havre, at the mouth of the Seine, is France's largest container port and gives farm produce a further outlet to export markets.
Brie, east of Paris, is a dairy area that supplies its cheese to the city's large market.
Champagne is sold under a protected AOC name as a branded product, which adds value to the grapes grown on the slopes.
Farm co-operatives and agribusiness firms buy, process and market the produce, so farmers have a ready outlet for their crops.

PART 6C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named region – India:
India is a continental region in south Asia in which language and religion have strongly shaped development.
Process 1 – Language:
India has 22 languages recognised in the Eighth Schedule, and Census 2011 found 121 languages each spoken by 10,000 or more people.
Indo-Aryan languages such as Hindi, Bengali and Gujarati are spoken in the north, and Dravidian languages such as Tamil and Telugu in the south.
The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew state boundaries along language lines, so most states are language regions.
Andhra State, created in 1953 for Telugu speakers, was the first state formed for a language.
Hindi, the mother tongue of about 44% of Indians, is the official language of the Union, and the Hindi belt covers states such as Uttar Pradesh and Bihar.
Resistance to Hindi being imposed, especially in Tamil Nadu, led to the anti-Hindi agitation of 1965 and created tension between north and south.
The Official Languages Act of 1963 kept English in official use, so it acts as a link language between states.
English is an economic advantage that helped India develop IT and business-process outsourcing industries.
Process 2 – Religion:
Census 2011 recorded Hindus at about 80% of the population, Muslims at about 14%, Christians at about 2.3% and Sikhs at about 1.7%.
Religions are regionally concentrated, with Sikhs in Punjab, Christians in Nagaland, Mizoram and Meghalaya, and Muslims in Jammu and Kashmir.
Partition in 1947 divided British India into India and Muslim-majority Pakistan, displaced more than 10 million people and killed hundreds of thousands.
India is constitutionally a secular state, but communal tension has persisted, as in the riots after the demolition of the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya in 1992.
The caste system linked to Hinduism still affects rural life, and the Constitution abolished untouchability in 1950 and reserved places for Scheduled Castes.
Religion also shapes the economy, because pilgrimage tourism at Varanasi and the Kumbh Mela, and spending at Diwali, support local incomes.
Sacred cattle support dairying, and vegetarian diets increase demand for pulses and milk.

PART 7B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named MNC – Intel:
Intel Corporation is a multinational company with its headquarters in Santa Clara, California, and fabrication plants in Leixlip in Ireland, Hillsboro in Oregon, Chandler in Arizona and Kiryat Gat in Israel.
Heading 1 – Product life cycle:
The product life cycle shapes where Intel makes each generation of chip and how long its Leixlip plant stays competitive.
Raymond Vernon's product life cycle theory says a product passes through introduction, growth, maturity and decline, and its production location changes at each stage.
Intel develops each new chip process at its research and development fab at Hillsboro, Oregon, close to headquarters and its most skilled engineers.
Under its Copy Exactly policy the equipment and procedures are then copied exactly into other fabs such as Leixlip.
Fab 34 at Leixlip opened in 2023 and makes the Intel 4 and Intel 3 generations using extreme ultraviolet lithography, so Ireland won the growth stage of a new generation.
Each chip generation has a life of only a few years, so older fabs are upgraded or closed as their products reach maturity and decline.
Intel missed the start of the smartphone and artificial intelligence chip cycles, which helped rivals such as Nvidia and cut Intel's profits.
Apple began replacing Intel chips in its Mac computers with its own M1 chip in November 2020, which pushed one of Intel's mature products towards decline.
Heading 2 – Corporate strategies and branch plants:
Intel's corporate strategies decide whether a branch plant like Leixlip expands or is cut back, because the decisions are made at headquarters in California.
Intel invested over €30 billion at Leixlip by 2023, making it Ireland's largest private-sector investment, as part of a strategy of producing inside the EU market.
Ireland's corporation tax of 12.5%, IDA grants and its educated English-speaking workforce helped attract Intel's investment in 1989.
Government support shapes the strategy, as the US CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 provided about $50 billion for chip making and the EU Chips Act followed in 2023.
In 2024 Intel sold 49% of the Fab 34 joint venture to the American investment firm Apollo Global Management for about $11 billion to raise money for its other projects.
Intel announced about 15,000 global job cuts in August 2024 when profits fell, with further cuts in 2025, as part of a cost-cutting strategy.
Intel cancelled a planned fab at Magdeburg in Germany, which had been announced in 2022, while it opened Fab 34 in Ireland, showing that headquarters moves investment between its plants.
Branch plants compete with each other inside the company for investment, so Leixlip's jobs are vulnerable if Ireland's tax advantage narrows or its costs rise.

PART 7C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Importance for Ireland's environment:
Renewable energy is crucial for Ireland's environment because it cuts the emissions from burning fossil fuels that cause global warming.
Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, which traps heat and enhances the greenhouse effect, whereas wind, hydro and solar power produce little or no carbon dioxide.
Ireland must meet its 2030 emissions targets, and replacing peat, coal and gas in electricity generation helps to meet them.
Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from coal and heavy oil cause acid rain, and renewable sources release little or none of these pollutants.
Peat cutting damages raised bogs, which store large amounts of carbon, so replacing peat power with wind and biomass, as at Edenderry, Co. Offaly, protects them.
Renewables are inexhaustible, so they avoid the landscape damage caused by coal mining and peat cutting.
Imported oil and gas carry the risk of oil spills during transport, which renewable energy avoids.
Importance for Ireland's economy:
Renewable energy is crucial for Ireland's economy because it reduces the huge cost of imported fuel and makes the energy supply more secure.
Ireland imported about three-quarters of its energy in the early 2020s at a cost of billions of euro a year, and home-produced wind power reduces this import bill.
Using home-produced energy shelters the economy from sudden rises in world oil and gas prices.
Renewable energy creates jobs, often in rural areas, in building and maintaining wind farms, and landowners earn income from hosting turbines.
Missing EU renewable and emissions targets exposes Ireland to the cost of buying compliance, so meeting the targets avoids this expense.
Green electricity attracts multinational companies that want renewable power, which supports foreign direct investment and jobs.
Interconnectors such as the East-West Interconnector to Wales, opened in 2012, improve energy security by linking Ireland to other electricity markets.
Example – Wind energy in Ireland:
Wind is Ireland's main renewable source and supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s.
Examples include Galway Wind Park in Connemara, opened in 2017, and Arklow Bank Wind Park off Co. Wicklow, Ireland's first offshore wind farm, opened in 2004.
