QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q122 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q22, 80 marks)

PART "22" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Evaporation from the oceans, the movement of moist air and condensation over sea and land produce distinctive weather and climate regimes, such as the Indian monsoon, the Irish maritime climate and the fog deserts of cold-current coasts.
Aspect 1 – The Indian monsoon regime:
Roughly 85 to 90% of global evaporation comes from the oceans, and wind carries this moisture over the land.
In summer intense heating of the land creates low pressure over north-west India and the ITCZ moves north.
The south-east trade winds cross the equator and are deflected by the Coriolis force into south-west winds, which gather moisture over the sea.
The wet south-west monsoon lasts from June to September, normally reaching Kerala about 1 June and covering all of India by mid-July.
Moist air forced up the Western Ghats gives heavy relief rain, and Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in the Khasi Hills are among the wettest places on earth.
The Deccan lies in a rain shadow, because the descending air warms and the clouds evaporate.
In winter the land cools, high pressure builds over the interior of Asia and dry north-east winds blow off the land, which gives the dry season.
Late or weak monsoons bring drought and very heavy ones bring floods, and El Niño years often bring a weaker monsoon.
Kharif crops such as rice are sown with the monsoon rains.
Aspect 2 – The Irish maritime regime:
Ireland's moisture comes from the Atlantic, carried by prevailing south-westerly winds that bring maritime air.
The North Atlantic Drift warms the winter air and seas, and because the sea heats and cools slowly Ireland's annual temperature range is only about 10 °C.
Warm sea adds moisture and energy to the Atlantic depressions that form on the polar front.
Water evaporated over warm oceans carries latent heat, which is released where the vapour condenses in a depression over Ireland.
Most Irish rain is frontal, and rain falls in all months with a maximum in autumn and winter.
Rainfall is about 750 mm around Dublin, about 1,000 to 1,250 mm across much of the west and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
Relief uplift on the western mountains and the rain shadow in the east explain why the west is wetter than the east.
There are about 150 wet days a year on the east and south-east coasts and up to about 225 in parts of the west.
The weather is changeable as depressions and ridges of high pressure alternate, and ex-hurricane Ophelia, which had been a major hurricane south of the Azores, reached Ireland on 16 October 2017.
Aspect 3 – Fog deserts of cold-current coasts:
Cold currents such as the Canary, Benguela, Humboldt and California currents chill the air above them.
The cooled lower air is stable, in a temperature inversion, so it cannot rise to form rain clouds.
Warm, moist air moving over the cold sea cools to its dew point, so advection fog forms instead of rain.
The Namib Desert receives much of its moisture as fog from the cold Benguela Current.
The Atacama Desert in Chile, beside the Humboldt Current, is the driest non-polar desert on earth.
Sinking, warming air in the subtropical high-pressure belt at about 30° also prevents cloud from forming.
On coasts such as Peru, winds push surface water away from the shore and cold, nutrient-rich water upwells, which chills the air and gives fog and aridity.
The Welwitschia plant of the Namib absorbs moisture from fog, and the Namib fog-basking beetle collects fog droplets on its back.
The Grand Banks off Newfoundland show the same process in reverse, as warm moist air from over the Gulf Stream crosses the cold Labrador Current and makes the area one of the foggiest on earth.

QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q123 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q23, 80 marks)

PART "23" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The equatorial climate of the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia is hot, wet and uniform all year, and its characteristics are explained by the high sun, the ITCZ and an abundant supply of moisture.
Aspect 1 – Temperature:
The equatorial climate lies close to the equator, mainly in the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia, including Indonesia and Malaysia.
Monthly mean temperatures are high all year, at about 26 to 28 °C.
The annual range is tiny, at about 2 to 3 °C.
The sun is always high in the sky and is overhead at the equator at the equinoxes in March and September, so there is little change in heat input through the year.
The equator has almost the same length of day and night all year, so there are no temperate-style seasons.
The daily range is larger than the annual range, with afternoon highs of about 30 to 32 °C and night lows of about 22 to 24 °C.
Cloud cover stops temperatures rising much above the low 30s °C.
Clouds also keep nights warm by trapping long-wave radiation.
The constant heat gives a climate with the same character in every month.
Aspect 2 – Rainfall:
Rainfall is over 2,000 mm a year and falls in every month, and Manaus in Brazil receives about 2,300 mm.
There are often two wetter peaks after the equinoxes, when the overhead sun and the ITCZ pass over.
The rain is convectional, as intense heating warms the ground and the air above it, which expands, rises and cools adiabatically.
When the rising air reaches its dew point, cumulus and then cumulonimbus clouds form and give heavy afternoon thunderstorms.
Very moist air from warm seas and from forest transpiration supplies the water vapour.
A large share of the Amazon's rain is water recycled from the forest by evapotranspiration.
The trade winds converge at the ITCZ, which lifts and reinforces the moist air.
Latent heat released as the vapour condenses slows the cooling of the rising air and helps it to keep rising into towering clouds.
Some areas have a short drier season when the ITCZ shifts.
Aspect 3 – Pressure, winds and humidity:
Pressure is permanently low in the equatorial trough, the ITCZ or doldrums.
In the Hadley cell air rises at the equator, flows poleward at height, sinks at about 30° and returns as the trade winds.
Because the trade winds converge and air rises, winds are light and variable.
Relative humidity is often over 80%, and the air is humid at night too.
Warm seas and dense forest give high evaporation and transpiration, which keeps the air close to saturation.
High humidity makes the heat oppressive, because sweat evaporates slowly.
The daily cycle begins with a clear, calm, humid morning.
Cumulus builds by late morning, and cumulonimbus brings torrential rain with thunder in the afternoon.
The sky clears in the evening, and the same cycle then repeats the next day.

QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q124 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q24, 80 marks)

PART "24" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Solar energy is transformed into heat and motion at the earth's surface and is then redistributed from the tropics towards the poles by the circulation of the atmosphere and the oceans.
Aspect 1 – Solar energy and the heat budget:
Solar energy arrives as short-wave radiation, while the much cooler earth gives off long-wave heat radiation.
Of 100 units of incoming radiation, about 30 are reflected back to space, about 20 are absorbed by the atmosphere and clouds, and about 50 are absorbed by the land and ocean surface.
Absorbed radiation is transformed into sensible heat, and energy used to evaporate water is stored as latent heat in water vapour.
Heat differences create pressure differences, which become the kinetic energy of wind and, through wind drag, of waves and currents.
At the equator the sun's rays strike nearly vertically and are concentrated, while towards the poles the same beam is spread over a larger area and passes through more atmosphere.
Fresh snow reflects about 80 to 90% of radiation, so polar regions reflect most of the little energy they receive.
Between the equator and about 35 to 40° north and south more energy is received than is radiated away, which is the surplus.
Poleward of about 35 to 40° more energy is lost than is received, which is the deficit.
Without a poleward transfer the tropics would keep heating and the poles would keep cooling, so the atmosphere and oceans move heat from the surplus to the deficit belt.
Aspect 2 – Atmospheric circulation:
In the Hadley cell, air rises at the equator, flows poleward at height, sinks at about 30° to form the subtropical highs and deserts, and returns to the equator as the trade winds.
In the Ferrel cell, between 30 and 60°, surface westerlies carry warm air poleward to meet cold polar air at the polar front.
In the polar cell, polar air sinks at the poles and flows out as the polar easterlies.
Air moves from high to low pressure and is deflected to the right in the northern hemisphere by the Coriolis force.
Most mid-latitude heat transfer is done by depressions and anticyclones forming along the polar front and by the meandering jet stream.
Depressions form where cold polar maritime air meets warm tropical maritime air, and they move from west to east, steered by the jet stream, bringing Ireland's rain and wind.
Water evaporated over warm tropical oceans carries latent heat poleward, and the heat is released where the vapour condenses in a depression over Ireland.
Tropical cyclones draw heat and moisture from tropical oceans and carry them poleward, and ex-hurricane Ophelia reached Ireland on 16 October 2017.
The pressure belts and the ITCZ move north in the northern summer, which brings the Indian monsoon its summer rain.
Aspect 3 – Ocean circulation:
Ocean currents are driven mainly by wind drag, as the trade winds push the equatorial currents westward and the westerlies push the mid-latitude currents eastward.
The continents deflect the currents into gyres, which turn clockwise in the northern hemisphere.
Warm currents carry heat poleward along the western sides of ocean basins, such as the Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian and Agulhas currents.
Cold currents return cooled water towards the equator along the eastern sides of ocean basins, such as the Canary, Benguela, Humboldt and California currents.
In the North Atlantic gyre the North Equatorial Current flows west, the Gulf Stream flows north along the American coast, and the North Atlantic Drift carries warm water north-east to Ireland, Britain and Norway.
The Drift gives Ireland mild winters and keeps Hammerfest and Murmansk ice-free, while the coast of Labrador, at a similar latitude to Ireland, has sea ice because of the cold Labrador Current.
In the thermohaline circulation cold, salty, dense water sinks in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica and spreads through the deep ocean, while warm surface water flows in to replace it, and one circuit takes about 1,000 years.
The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation pulls the Gulf Stream's warm water north, and the IPCC judged in 2021 that it is very likely to weaken over the 21st century, which could cool north-west Europe.
In an El Niño, which recurs every 2 to 7 years, the trade winds weaken and warm water flows back east towards South America, which shifts heat and rainfall across the Pacific.
