QUESTION geography-pp-2022-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2022 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART 14 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Social and political decisions by governments and international bodies shape where economic and political refugees can go and whether human rights are protected, as Irish and EU migration policy, the Syrian crisis and the treatment of minorities show.
Aspect 1 – Economic refugees and labour policy:
An economic migrant moves mainly to work or to improve living standards, so government decisions on labour markets and permits decide where they go.
On 1 May 2004 Ireland, the UK and Sweden were the only existing EU members to give citizens of the new member states immediate access to their labour markets.
This political decision drew large numbers of Poles to Ireland, and Polish nationals were the largest non-Irish group in Census 2022 at about 94,000.
Non-EU workers need an employment permit issued by the Department of Enterprise, so Irish policy is skills-based.
The Critical Skills Employment Permit covers occupations in short supply such as ICT, engineering and medicine, and it can lead to family reunification.
A labour-market test checks that no suitable Irish or EU worker is available, which protects local jobs but limits low-skilled migrants.
The Common Travel Area lets Irish and British citizens live and work freely between Ireland and the UK, and it was kept after Brexit.
Free movement of workers under the Treaty of Rome in 1957 lets EU citizens live and work in any member state, and the Schengen Agreement removed internal border checks.
The EU Blue Card directive of 2009 admits highly skilled non-EU workers because labour shortages and ageing populations create demand for workers.
Housing pressure and public opposition push governments to restrict entry, so Irish policy is open to EU citizens and skilled workers but restrictive for low-skilled non-EU workers.
Aspect 2 – Political refugees and asylum policy:
A political refugee has a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion, and political decisions decide whether they are protected.
The war in Syria from 2011 forced millions to flee, and about 1.3 million asylum applications were made in the EU in 2015.
Over 1 million people crossed the Mediterranean to Europe in 2015, most reaching Lesbos and other Greek islands from Turkey.
In August 2015 Chancellor Angela Merkel said 'Wir schaffen das', and Germany received the largest number of asylum seekers in the EU in 2015 to 2016.
In September 2015 the EU agreed to relocate 160,000 asylum seekers from Italy and Greece.
Hungary, Poland and the Czech Republic refused to take part, and in 2020 the EU Court of Justice ruled that they had broken EU law.
Hungary under Viktor Orbán built a fence on its border with Serbia in 2015 to keep migrants out.
The Dublin system makes the state of first entry responsible for an asylum claim, which places most pressure on frontier states such as Italy and Greece.
The EU–Turkey statement of March 2016 required irregular migrants reaching the Greek islands to be returned to Turkey, and arrivals in Greece fell sharply.
The Irish Refugee Protection Programme of September 2015 committed Ireland to taking up to 4,000 people, including refugees from Syria.
In Ireland asylum applicants live in direct provision centres with meals and a small weekly payment, and the McMahon Report of 2015 criticised the system.
From 4 March 2022 the EU Temporary Protection Directive gave people fleeing the invasion of Ukraine an immediate right to live, work and use services, and Ireland received over 100,000 Ukrainians by 2024.
Aspect 3 – Human rights issues:
Human rights are the basic rights of every person, and the decisions of states and courts can either abuse or protect them.
The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted on 10 December 1948 as a common standard for all states.
Human rights abuses by a state against its own people are one of the main reasons that people seek asylum.
In Xinjiang in China an estimated one million or more Uyghurs and other Muslims have been held in detention or re-education camps since 2017.
A UN human rights office report of August 2022 found serious human rights violations that may amount to crimes against humanity.
Tibetans see Han Chinese migration, controls on monasteries and Mandarin-medium schooling as threats to their culture.
In D.H. and Others v Czech Republic in 2007 the European Court of Human Rights ruled that placing Roma children in special schools was unlawful discrimination.
Irish Travellers were formally recognised as an ethnic minority on 1 March 2017.
The All Ireland Traveller Health Study of 2010 found that Traveller men lived about 15 years less than men in the general population.
The Equal Status Acts of 2000 outlaw discrimination in services in Ireland.
A Supreme Court ruling of 2017 found an absolute ban on work for asylum applicants unconstitutional, so applicants gained a right to work after a waiting period from 2018.

QUESTION geography-pp-2022-higher-q115 (LC Geography Higher 2022 — Part Two — Q15, 80 marks)

PART 15 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sustainable development meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, and it works as a model for the future by balancing environmental, economic and social needs, as resource use, fair trade and social justice show.
Aspect 1 – Environmental sustainability in resource use:
The environmental pillar requires renewable resources such as forests and energy to be used no faster than they regenerate.
Costa Rica cleared forests for cattle pasture in the 1970s and 1980s, and by the late 1980s forest covered only about a fifth of the country.
Since 1997 the Payment for Environmental Services programme, run by the fund FONAFIFO, has paid landowners to protect and replant forest.
The programme is paid for largely by a tax on fuel.
Forest cover in Costa Rica has since risen to over half of the country.
Since 2015 almost all of Costa Rica's electricity has come from renewable sources, mainly hydroelectric, geothermal and wind.
Costa Rica won the first Earthshot Prize in 2021 for its forest recovery.
In Ireland Bord na Móna moved from burning peat to renewable energy under its Brown to Green strategy, and peat harvesting ended in January 2021.
Cutaway bogs such as Lough Boora Discovery Park in Co. Offaly are being rehabilitated, and Bellacorick in Co. Mayo has wind farms on cutaway bog.
The Climate Act 2021 requires Ireland to cut emissions by 51% by 2030 and reach net zero by 2050.
The model is hard to apply because Irish agriculture produces over a third of greenhouse gas emissions and only about half of Irish waters are in good ecological status.
Aspect 2 – Fair trade as a model of sustainable trade:
Fair trade is a trading partnership that seeks greater equity in international trade so that producers in developing countries can develop sustainably.
Commodity prices are low and unstable, and small farmers receive a tiny share of the final price.
The Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production.
The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative and spent on projects that the members choose democratically.
Kuapa Kokoo is a Fairtrade cocoa farmers' co-operative in Ghana that was founded in 1993.
Divine Chocolate was set up in Britain in 1998 and is part-owned by Kuapa Kokoo, so the farmers share in the profits of the brand and not just the price of the beans.
Premium spending in Ghana has included wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
Kuapa Kokoo encourages women to take leadership roles in its village societies.
Fairtrade environmental standards restrict pesticides and protect water and forests.
Close to 2 million farmers and workers belong to Fairtrade-certified producer organisations in about 70 countries.
Fair trade is only a tiny share of world trade and does not change trade rules such as tariff escalation, which limits its impact.
Aspect 3 – Social justice and self-reliance:
Development is not sustainable if it excludes or harms groups of people, and minority groups often lack the political power to protect their land and rights.
Minority groups often lose their land to dams, mines, plantations and wind farms built for development.
The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples of 13 September 2007 includes the right to free, prior and informed consent.
The Fosen wind farm in central Norway was built on Sámi reindeer grazing land.
On 11 October 2021 Norway's Supreme Court ruled that the Fosen turbines violated the herders' right to enjoy their culture.
The Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River in Brazil was completed in 2019, opposed by the Kayapó and displaced thousands of people.
In 2023 Brazil declared a public health emergency in Yanomami territory after illegal gold mining caused malnutrition and disease.
Irish Travellers were recognised as an ethnic minority on 1 March 2017, and the 2022 Census counted about 33,000.
The All Ireland Traveller Health Study of 2010 found that Traveller men lived about 15 years less than men in the general population.
The Green Belt Movement was founded in Kenya in 1977 by Wangari Maathai, and women's groups have planted tens of millions of trees for fuel, soil and income.
In Niger farmer-managed natural regeneration has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, showing self-reliance that restores land.

PART 16 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Soil characteristics such as texture, structure, drainage, pH, organic content and parent material decide which processes operate in a soil, so they control how the soil develops, as Irish brown earths, gleys, podzols and rendzinas show.
Aspect 1 – Texture, structure and drainage:
Texture is the proportions of sand, silt and clay and structure is the way particles group into peds, so together they control drainage and decide which process dominates.
Clay soils have high porosity but low permeability because their pores are tiny, so they drain slowly.
Sandy soils are free-draining, so water moves quickly down through them and encourages leaching.
Where drainage is poor, as on impermeable boulder clay, the pores fill with water and air is excluded, so conditions become anaerobic.
Bacteria in these conditions reduce iron from its red ferric form to its grey or blue-grey ferrous form, which begins gleying.
Orange mottles form where air enters along roots or cracks and re-oxidises the iron.
Gleys occur in the drumlin belt of Co. Cavan, Co. Monaghan and Co. Leitrim, in the wet hollows between drumlins.
Gley soils are wet, sticky and cold, so they are slow to warm and slow to decompose organic matter.
Crumb structure in humus-rich topsoil creates many pores, so well-structured loams drain and aerate well and support rapid humification.
Field drains and arterial drainage improve gleys by lowering the water table.
Aspect 2 – Acidity and organic matter:
The pH of a soil and its organic content control which organisms live in it and how fast litter is changed into humus.
Soils become acidic where heavy rain leaches out bases and where acid litter such as conifer needles or heather builds up.
In slightly acidic to neutral soils earthworms thrive and mull humus forms, because the worms mix and break down the litter.
Mull humus under deciduous woodland and grassland produces brown earths, Ireland's main lowland soil, as in the Golden Vale of Co. Tipperary and Co. Limerick.
Brown earths have a pH of roughly 5.5 to 6.5 and a dark brown loamy A horizon with crumb structure and no bleached E horizon.
In acid conditions there are few earthworms, so litter decomposes poorly and forms acid mor humus.
Mor humus releases organic acids into percolating water, which carry iron, aluminium and humus down the profile in podzolisation.
This leaves a bleached ash-grey E horizon above a reddish-brown B horizon, and a thin iron pan may form.
Podzols on granite in the Wicklow Mountains are strongly acidic at about pH 4 to 4.5 and are infertile.
Humus content also affects colour, structure, water retention and fertility, and it is high in chernozems but low in desert soils.
Aspect 3 – Parent material and mineral content:
Parent material supplies the mineral matter of a soil and sets its texture, base content and starting pH, so it influences the soil that develops.
Residual soils form from rock weathered in place, while transported soils form on material carried by ice, water or wind.
Limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils.
On the limestone of the Burren thin, dark, lime-rich rendzinas form in the grikes and hollows.
Granite and sandstone give sandy, acidic, free-draining soils, so leaching and podzolisation are faster on them.
Most Irish soils formed on glacial till laid down before the last glaciation ended about 10,000 years ago, so they are young.
Sand and gravel deposited by glacial meltwater give coarse, free-draining soils that dry out easily.
Shale and clay parent materials give heavy, slowly draining soils that are prone to gleying.
Base-rich parent material gives fertile soil because the clay-humus complex holds nutrient ions such as calcium, magnesium and potassium so that they are not washed away.
On the steppes the lime-rich loess parent material helps form the chernozem, which has a very thick black A horizon with lime nodules in the B horizon.
