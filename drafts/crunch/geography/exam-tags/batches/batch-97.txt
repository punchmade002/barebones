QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q119 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q19, 80 marks)

PART 19 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Conflict arises when state boundaries and governments cut across or deny the identity of cultural groups, as the cases of Northern Ireland, the Kurds and Catalonia show.
Aspect 1 – Northern Ireland and partition:
The Government of Ireland Act 1920 and the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921 partitioned the island, creating Northern Ireland from six of Ulster's nine counties.
The border is about 499 km long and follows old county boundaries rather than physical features, so it cuts through communities and farms.
Partition created two states, two majorities and two minorities, and Northern Ireland had a unionist majority with a large nationalist minority.
Discrimination against nationalists fuelled the civil rights movement from 1968.
The Troubles, from the late 1960s to 1998, killed over 3,500 people.
The Good Friday Agreement was signed on 10 April 1998, and the referendums of 22 May 1998 returned about 71% Yes in Northern Ireland and about 94% Yes in the Republic.
The Agreement created a power-sharing Assembly and Executive, a North/South Ministerial Council and the consent principle, and it did not move the border.
Articles 2 and 3 of the Irish Constitution were changed from a claim to the whole island to an aspiration to unity by consent, in force from December 1999.
Brexit challenged the settlement, as Northern Ireland voted about 56% Remain in 2016, and the Windsor Framework of 2023 keeps the land border free of checks.
Aspect 2 – The Kurds, a nation without a state:
The Kurds number 30 million or more and are the largest ethnic group without their own state, living in Kurdistan, which is divided between Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria.
The Treaty of Sèvres of 1920 provided for a possible Kurdish state, but the Treaty of Lausanne of 1923 drew the borders of modern Turkey with no Kurdish state.
Turkey long denied a separate Kurdish identity and banned Kurdish in public life until 1991 and in broadcasting until the early 2000s.
The PKK, founded in 1978, began an armed campaign in 1984, and the conflict cost about 40,000 lives.
On 16 March 1988 Saddam Hussein's forces attacked the Kurdish town of Halabja in Iraq with chemical weapons, killing about 5,000 people.
After the 1991 Gulf War a no-fly zone protected Iraqi Kurds, and Iraq's 2005 constitution recognised the Kurdistan Region as autonomous, with its own government in Erbil.
On 25 September 2017 about 93% voted for independence in a referendum that Iraq's government rejected, and Iraqi forces then retook Kirkuk.
In Syria, Kurdish-led forces fought the Islamic State group, including in the defence of Kobani in 2014–15.
The Kurdish language and Newroz, the Kurdish New Year on 21 March, remain major expressions of Kurdish identity.
Aspect 3 – Catalonia and the Basque Country in Spain:
Basques and Catalans have their own languages, autonomy statutes and strong regional identities within Spain.
Basque and Catalan were suppressed under Franco from 1939 to 1975, when both languages were banned from public life and schools.
Spain's 1978 constitution created 17 autonomous communities, and Catalonia's autonomy statute dates from 1979 and was revised in 2006.
In 2010 Spain's Constitutional Court struck down parts of the 2006 statute, including its description of Catalonia as a nation, which fuelled the independence movement.
On 1 October 2017 a referendum that Spain's courts had declared illegal went ahead despite police attempts to stop the voting, and about 90% of those voting backed independence on a turnout of about 43%.
Spain imposed direct rule using Article 155 of its constitution, and the regional president Carles Puigdemont fled to Belgium.
Nine Catalan leaders were jailed in 2019 and pardoned in 2021, and an amnesty law was passed in 2024.
In the Basque Country, ETA was founded in 1959 and killed over 800 people in its campaign for an independent Basque state, before declaring an end to violence in 2011 and dissolving in 2018.
Spain responded with autonomy, and the Basque Autonomous Community, created by the Statute of Gernika in 1979, and Navarre collect their own taxes.

PART 20 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Identity is shaped by a variety of cultural factors, including language, religion and shared traditions, and the European region of Switzerland shows how these combine in a national identity above local differences.
Aspect 1 – Language:
The main languages of Switzerland are German (about 62%), French (about 23%), Italian (about 8%) and Romansh (about 0.5%).
There are 17 German-speaking cantons, 4 French-speaking cantons including Geneva and Vaud, the Italian-speaking canton of Ticino, 3 bilingual cantons and trilingual Graubünden.
Under the territoriality principle each canton or commune has its own official language or languages, used in its schools and administration.
Romansh became a national language by referendum in 1938 and a partly official language in 1996, and the written standard Rumantsch Grischun was created in 1982.
The Röstigraben is the cultural divide between German- and French-speaking Switzerland, often visible in referendum results, and it runs roughly along the River Sarine in Fribourg.
The public broadcaster SRG SSR runs separate services in German (SRF), French (RTS), Italian (RSI) and Romansh (RTR).
Children learn a second national language at school, which builds links between the language groups.
No language group wants to join its neighbouring country, so Switzerland is a Willensnation, a nation formed by the will of its people rather than by a common language.
The mountains isolated valleys, which helped to preserve local dialects and Romansh.
Aspect 2 – Religion:
Ulrich Zwingli led the Reformation in Zurich from 1519, and John Calvin led it in Geneva from the 1530s and 1540s.
Zurich, Bern, Basel, Geneva, Vaud and Neuchâtel were historically Protestant, while the central cantons, Fribourg, Valais, Ticino and Jura were historically Catholic.
The Sonderbund War of November 1847 was a short civil war in which a league of Catholic, conservative cantons was defeated.
The federal constitution of 1848 then created the modern federal state.
Religion cuts across language, as both German- and French-speaking Switzerland contain Protestant and Catholic cantons, which reduces conflict between language groups.
In 2022 about a third of the population was Roman Catholic, about a fifth Reformed, about a third had no religion and about 6% were Muslim.
The canton of Jura was created on 1 January 1979 after the mainly Catholic, French-speaking northern districts voted to leave the mainly Protestant, German-speaking canton of Bern.
On 29 November 2009 a popular initiative banning the building of new minarets passed with about 57.5%.
In March 2021 a ban on face coverings in public passed with about 51% and came into force on 1 January 2025.
Aspect 3 – Traditions, music and festivals:
Yodelling and the alphorn are traditional Alpine music.
Schwingen, Swiss wrestling in sawdust rings, is held at Alpine festivals.
The national day on 1 August commemorates the Federal Charter of 1291 and is marked with bonfires and fireworks.
Basel Fasnacht begins at 4 a.m. on the Monday after Ash Wednesday with the Morgestraich, and it has been on UNESCO's intangible heritage list since 2017.
Sechseläuten in Zurich in April features a parade of the guilds and the burning of the Böögg, a snowman figure, to mark the end of winter.
The Fête des Vignerons in Vevey is a winegrowers' festival held only about four or five times a century, most recently in 2019.
The Montreux Jazz Festival has been held since 1967, the Locarno Film Festival since 1946 and Art Basel since 1970, which gives Switzerland an international cultural identity.
Fondue, raclette, rösti, chocolate, Gruyère and Emmental are typical Swiss foods.
The Red Cross was founded in Geneva in 1863 by Henri Dunant, and Geneva hosts the UN's European office, which expresses a neutral, international identity.

PART 21 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Language is an important cultural indicator because it carries a group's history and marks who belongs to it, and it shows how identity is expressed, challenged and defended.
Aspect 1 – Language, identity and nationalism:
Language carries a group's history, literature, songs, place names and ways of thinking.
It marks who belongs to a group, and because census questions on language are easy to map it is used to define cultural regions.
Many nation states were built around a single language.
The Gaelic League, founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill, led a language revival that fed the Irish independence movement.
Suppressing a language has been used to weaken a group, as Basque and Catalan were banned from public life and schools under Franco from 1939 to 1975.
The Welsh Not was a wooden board hung round the neck of children caught speaking Welsh in some 19th-century schools.
Place names preserve a language long after it has been replaced, as Irish elements such as cill and dún survive in Kilkenny (Cill Chainnigh) and Dundalk (Dún Dealgan).
A shared national language can unite a state, but language borders can divide it, as Belgium's does.
Languages cross state borders, as Basque is spoken in both Spain and France and French is spoken in France, Belgium, Switzerland and Quebec.
Aspect 2 – Language, power and the spread of English:
Languages spread through migration, conquest and empire, as Latin spread with the Roman Empire.
Colonial languages became official languages of former colonies, such as English in India and Nigeria, French in Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire, Spanish across most of Latin America and Portuguese in Brazil.
Arabic spread with Islam, which shows that religion and trade also carry languages.
The language of the powerful brings jobs and status, so speakers of minority languages shift to it.
English has about 1.5 billion speakers when second-language speakers are counted, and it is Europe's main second language.
Television, film, music, streaming and the internet are dominated by English, and about half of all websites are in English.
English loanwords enter other languages, such as the French le weekend.
France responded with the Académie française, founded in 1635, and the Toubon Law of 1994, which requires French in advertising, product labels and public signage.
Quebec's Charter of the French Language, Bill 101 of 1977, made French the sole official language of Quebec.
Aspect 3 – Decline and survival of minority languages:
About 40% of the world's languages are endangered.
Minority languages decline because of suppression, the need for a dominant language for jobs and education, emigration, urbanisation and mass media, and because parents stop passing the language on to their children.
In Ireland the 1851 census recorded about 1.5 million Irish speakers (about 23%), and in Census 2022 about 1.87 million (about 40%) said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside education.
A 2007 study found that Irish is viable as a community language in the Gaeltacht only where at least 67% speak it daily.
Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution makes Irish the first official language, the Official Languages Act 2003 gives the public the right to deal with public bodies in Irish, and Údarás na Gaeltachta was set up in 1980.
Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972 and TG4 launched on 31 October 1996, which gives Irish a place in the media.
The Welsh Language Society was founded in 1962, S4C launched on 1 November 1982, and Welsh is compulsory in schools up to age 16, yet only 17.8% of people aged 3 and over could speak Welsh in the 2021 census.
The Statute of Gernika of 1979 made Basque co-official with Spanish in the Basque Autonomous Community, and Basque-medium ikastolak have helped use of Basque to rise among the young.
In Brittany, where state support is weak and the French Constitution states that the language of the Republic is French, Breton has declined sharply and most of its speakers are elderly.
