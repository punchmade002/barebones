PART 17 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Brown earth is Ireland's main lowland soil, and its composition and characteristics reflect the mild, wet climate and deciduous woodland under which it formed.
Aspect 1 – Composition of brown earth:
Brown earth is made of four components, which are mineral matter, organic matter, water and air.
A well-structured loamy soil such as brown earth has about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water and 25% air by volume.
The mineral matter is particles of weathered rock from stones down to clay, and the parent material is usually glacial till.
Mineral matter forms the skeleton of the soil and releases nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium as it weathers.
Organic matter is living organisms plus dead plant and animal remains, with decomposers breaking litter down into humus.
The humus is mild, crumbly mull, well mixed into the soil by earthworms.
Humus darkens the soil, binds particles into crumbs and holds water and nutrients.
Water held in the pores carries dissolved nutrients as the soil solution, from which roots absorb them.
Air fills the pores not occupied by water and supplies oxygen for root respiration and for the organisms that decompose litter.
Brown earth formed under deciduous woodland of oak, ash, elm and hazel after the Ice Age, so the soil is only about 10,000 years old or less.
Aspect 2 – Profile and location of brown earth:
The soil profile is a vertical section from the surface to bedrock, divided into layers called horizons.
The O horizon is a thin layer of mull humus on the surface.
The A horizon is dark brown and loamy, with a crumb structure, and is roughly 20 to 30 cm deep.
The A horizon is rich in humus, so it is the most fertile layer.
The B horizon is lighter brown, with some iron and clay washed in by mild leaching.
The boundary between the A and B horizons is gradual because earthworms mix the soil.
There is no bleached E horizon, because leaching is mild and not intense as in a podzol.
The C horizon is weathered glacial till or rock, with bedrock below.
Brown earth is the most widespread soil of the Irish lowlands, especially in the south, east and Midlands.
It is also found under temperate deciduous forest in Britain, France, Germany and the eastern USA.
Aspect 3 – Characteristics of brown earth:
Brown earth has a loamy texture, a balanced mix of sand, silt and clay that gives good drainage with good water and nutrient retention.
Its structure is crumb, with small rounded peds that open pores for air and water.
Its brown colour comes from humus plus iron compounds.
It is slightly acidic, at roughly pH 5.5 to 6.5, which suits most crops and earthworms.
It is fertile because the clay-humus complex holds nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium so that they are not washed away.
Its organic content is moderate, higher than in desert soils and lower than in chernozems or peat.
It drains freely enough to avoid waterlogging and is well aerated, so decomposition is aerobic.
Mild leaching under rainfall of roughly 750 to 1,000 mm in the east carries some iron and clay down to the B horizon.
Brown earth supports the fertile grassland and tillage of the Golden Vale in Tipperary and Limerick and of the south-east.
Lime is applied where the pH falls below the Teagasc targets of about 6.3 for grassland and 6.5 for tillage.

QUESTION geography-pp-2018-higher-q118 (LC Geography Higher 2018 — Part Two — Q18, 80 marks)

PART 18 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The tropical rainforest of the Amazon Basin has a hot, wet climate and infertile soils, and together these control the dense, layered and specially adapted vegetation of the biome.
Aspect 1 – Climate of the tropical rainforest:
The tropical rainforest lies mainly between about 10 degrees north and south of the equator, as in the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia.
Mean monthly temperatures are about 26 to 28 °C all year, with an annual range of only 2 to 3 °C.
The range is small because the sun stays high and day length barely changes.
Annual rainfall is over 2,000 mm, and Manaus in the central Amazon receives about 2,300 mm.
Rain falls throughout the year, and relative humidity is often over 80%.
Intense morning heating makes moist air rise, and it cools and builds cumulonimbus clouds.
Heavy afternoon convectional thunderstorms follow almost daily.
The ITCZ, the low-pressure belt of rising air along the equator, drives this rainfall.
A large share of the Amazon's rain is water recycled from the forest by evapotranspiration.
The daily range of temperature is greater than the annual range, and there are no seasons in the temperate sense.
Aspect 2 – Soils of the tropical rainforest:
The soils are latosols, which are deep, often many metres, and red or yellow in colour.
They form by laterisation, in which heat and heavy rain leach out silica and bases and leave iron and aluminium oxides.
They are clay-rich and acidic, with very little humus.
Litter decomposes within weeks in the heat and humidity, so little humus builds up.
Shallow roots and mycorrhizal fungi take up nutrients almost at once and pass them to the trees.
Most nutrients are therefore held in the living vegetation rather than the soil, so the nutrient cycle is rapid and closed.
The soils are infertile despite the lush forest above them.
When the forest is cleared, the soil loses fertility within a few years and erodes under heavy rain.
Cleared soil that dries out can bake into a hard laterite crust.
Shifting cultivation has traditionally used the soil for a few years before moving on.
Aspect 3 – Impact on the vegetation:
Constant heat and heavy rain give year-round growth, so the forest is dense, evergreen and broadleaved.
The vegetation forms layers, with emergents such as kapok and the Brazil nut tree at 45 to 60 m, a canopy at 25 to 40 m, an understorey up to 15 m and a shrub layer below.
Only about 2% of the sunlight reaching the canopy arrives on the forest floor, so understorey plants have large leaves to capture light.
Shallow roots catch nutrients from the litter layer, and buttress roots support tall trees such as kapok.
Drip tips and waxy leaves shed the heavy rain.
Trees shed and replace leaves all year, and a fallen tree opens a light gap into which young trees grow rapidly.
Lianas climb trees to reach the light, and epiphytes such as orchids and bromeliads grow on branches and take moisture from the air and rain.
Pitcher plants in the nutrient-poor soils of South-East Asia trap and digest insects.
Hundreds of tree species can grow in a single hectare of Amazon rainforest, compared with a handful in an Irish wood.
Rainforests cover only about 6% of the earth's land surface but are widely estimated to hold over half of the world's land plant and animal species.

PART 19 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Religion is an important cultural indicator because it shapes beliefs, identity, landscape, daily life and politics, as the distribution of world religions, the Irish experience and religious conflict show.
Aspect 1 – Distribution of world religions:
Christians are the largest group at about 2.3 billion, or about 29% of the world in 2020, followed by Muslims at about 2 billion, or about 26%.
Hindus are about 15% of the world, and the religiously unaffiliated, who have no religion, are about 24%.
Christianity is divided between Roman Catholic in southern Europe, Ireland, Poland and Latin America, Protestant in northern Europe, the UK and the USA, and Orthodox in eastern Europe, Russia, Greece and Serbia.
Islam is strong in the Middle East, North Africa, Pakistan and Bangladesh, and Indonesia has the world's largest Muslim population.
Hinduism is found mainly in India and Nepal, where about four in five Indians are Hindu, and Buddhism is found in Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Tibet, Japan and Vietnam.
Religions spread from their hearths through empire, missionaries, trade, colonialism and migration, as Christianity spread through the Roman Empire and Irish monks such as St Columbanus carried it into continental Europe.
Holy cities such as Jerusalem, Mecca, Varanasi and Amritsar mark where each religion began or is most sacred, and the Hajj to Mecca is a duty for every Muslim able to make it.
Aspect 2 – Religion in the landscape and daily life:
Religion shows in the landscape through churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, holy wells, wayside shrines and cemeteries.
It shapes diet, such as halal and kosher food and Hindu vegetarianism.
It shapes dress, such as the headscarf, the turban and the kippah.
It shapes the calendar through festivals such as Christmas, Easter, Eid, Diwali and Yom Kippur.
Religion is closely linked with language and nationality, such as Arabic with the Qur'an and Islam, Hebrew with Judaism and Catholicism with Irish and Polish identity.
It affects law and education, since Saudi Arabia's law is based on Sharia and about nine in ten Irish primary schools are under Catholic patronage.
Census questions on religion make it easy to map, and in Ireland Census 2022 showed about 69% Roman Catholic, down from about 78% in Census 2016.
Aspect 3 – Religion and the state in Ireland:
The Irish Constitution of 1937 began In the Name of the Most Holy Trinity, and Article 44 originally recognised the special position of the Roman Catholic Church.
Article 44 also guaranteed freedom of conscience and practice and recognised other churches, including the Church of Ireland, and the Jewish congregations.
The Church influenced social policy, and the free Mother and Child health plan of 1950–51 was dropped after opposition from the Catholic hierarchy, which led the Minister for Health Noel Browne to resign.
Other states differ, since Iran has been an Islamic Republic under a Supreme Leader since 1979, while France separated church and state in 1905 under the principle of laïcité.
Change followed in Ireland, as the Fifth Amendment of 1972 removed the special position clause, divorce was approved narrowly in 1995 and marriage equality passed in 2015 with about 62% voting Yes.
The Ryan Report and the Murphy Report of 2009 on abuse in institutions and the Dublin archdiocese damaged the standing of the Church.
Secularisation is shown by about 14% of people having no religion in Census 2022, while Orthodox Christians and Muslims are growing groups through immigration.
Aspect 4 – Religion and conflict:
Religion often marks group identity and overlaps with national identity and political power, so it can feed conflict.
In Northern Ireland the Plantation of Ulster from 1609 settled English and Scottish Protestants on confiscated land, and partition created Northern Ireland in 1921 with a Protestant and unionist majority.
Catholics faced discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting, and the civil rights movement began in 1968.
The Troubles, from the late 1960s to 1998, killed over 3,500 people, including 13 civil-rights marchers shot dead on Bloody Sunday in Derry on 30 January 1972.
The Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998 set up a power-sharing Assembly, though peace walls still separate some Catholic and Protestant areas of Belfast.
The partition of India in August 1947 created Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan, displaced around 15 million people and killed hundreds of thousands.
Jerusalem contains the Western Wall, the Al-Aqsa Mosque and Dome of the Rock, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, so competing religious claims feed the Israeli–Palestinian conflict.
