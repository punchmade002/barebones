QUESTION geography-pp-2023-higher-q122 (LC Geography Higher 2023 — Part Two — Q22, 80 marks)

PART "22" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Solar energy is transformed into other forms of energy at the earth's surface and in the air, and the atmosphere and oceans then redistribute it from the tropics towards the poles.

Aspect 1 – Receipt and transformation of solar energy:
Solar energy arrives as short-wave radiation, and about 1,361 W/m² reaches the top of the atmosphere, which is called the solar constant.
Of 100 units of incoming radiation about 30 are reflected to space by clouds, the surface and scattering, so the earth's albedo is about 30%.
About 20 units are absorbed by the atmosphere and clouds and about 50 units are absorbed by the land and ocean surface.
Absorbed radiation becomes heat, called sensible heat, which warms the air by conduction and convection.
Energy used to evaporate water from the oceans is stored as latent heat in water vapour and is released when the vapour condenses, which powers storms.
Heat differences create pressure differences, which become the kinetic energy of wind and, through wind drag, of waves and currents.
The earth gives off long-wave radiation, and greenhouse gases absorb and re-radiate part of it, so the average surface temperature is about 15 °C instead of about −18 °C.
At the equator the sun's rays strike almost vertically and are concentrated on a small area, while at the poles low-angle rays spread over a larger area and pass through more atmosphere.
So between the equator and about 35–40° north and south more energy is received than lost, and poleward of that more is lost than received.
Without a poleward transfer the tropics would keep heating and the poles would keep cooling, so energy must be moved.

Aspect 2 – Redistribution by the atmosphere:
The atmosphere carries most of the heat moved poleward at mid and high latitudes, through its circulation cells and weather systems.
In the Hadley cell, between 0° and 30°, air rises at the equator, forming the ITCZ, and releases latent heat as convectional rain.
At height this air flows poleward and sinks at about 30°, forming the subtropical high pressure and deserts, and it returns to the equator as the trade winds.
In the Ferrel cell, between 30° and 60°, surface westerlies carry warm air poleward to meet cold polar air at the polar front, at about 60°.
In the polar cell polar air sinks at the poles and flows out as polar easterlies.
The Coriolis force deflects these winds to the right in the northern hemisphere, which gives the south-westerly westerlies that carry warm air to Ireland.
Most mid-latitude heat transfer is done by depressions forming along the polar front, where warm tropical maritime air from the south-west meets cold polar maritime air, and by the meandering jet stream.
Water evaporated over warm tropical oceans carries latent heat poleward, and the heat is released where the vapour condenses, for example in a depression over Ireland.
Tropical cyclones draw large quantities of heat and moisture from warm tropical oceans and carry them poleward.
Ex-hurricane Ophelia formed in the east Atlantic near the Azores, became a hurricane and reached Ireland on 16 October 2017.

Aspect 3 – Redistribution by the oceans:
The oceans carry a large share of the heat moved poleward in the tropics, mainly through surface currents and the deep thermohaline circulation.
Wind drag drives the surface currents, with the trade winds pushing equatorial currents westward and the westerlies pushing mid-latitude currents eastward, and the continents turn them into gyres that are clockwise in the northern hemisphere.
Warm currents carry heat poleward along the western sides of ocean basins, such as the Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian and Agulhas currents.
The Gulf Stream starts in the Gulf of Mexico and the Straits of Florida and carries more water than all the world's rivers combined.
The westerlies carry it across the Atlantic as the North Atlantic Drift towards Ireland, Britain and Norway.
Norway's coast stays ice-free in winter, even at Hammerfest north of the Arctic Circle, because of the warm water of the North Atlantic Drift.
Cold currents return cooled water towards the equator along the eastern sides of ocean basins, such as the Canary, Benguela, Humboldt and California currents.
The coast of Labrador, at about the same latitude as Ireland, has long freezing winters and sea ice because the cold Labrador Current flows south past it.
In the thermohaline circulation cold, salty, dense water sinks in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica and spreads through the deep ocean, and warm surface water flows in to replace it, so heat is carried slowly round the globe.
Melting Greenland ice makes North Atlantic water fresher and less dense, and the IPCC judged in 2021 that this Atlantic circulation is very likely to weaken during the 21st century, which could cool north-west Europe.

QUESTION geography-pp-2023-higher-q123 (LC Geography Higher 2023 — Part Two — Q23, 80 marks)

PART "23" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Atmospheric and oceanic phenomena are measured by standard instruments and networks, and these measurements are essential for forecasting, safety, the economy and climate science.

Aspect 1 – Measuring temperature and pressure:
Temperature and pressure are measured with standard thermometers and barometers.
A liquid-in-glass thermometer uses mercury or alcohol, which expands and rises up a narrow tube as it warms.
Six's maximum-minimum thermometer uses small metal indexes that are pushed to the highest and lowest points reached, and it is read once a day and reset with a magnet.
Thermometers are kept in a Stevenson screen about 1.25 m above the ground, which is white, louvred and has its door facing away from the sun, so readings are true shade air temperatures.
A mercury barometer works because the weight of the atmosphere supports a column of mercury in a glass tube, and the column rises as pressure rises.
An aneroid barometer has a sealed, partly emptied metal capsule that is squeezed as pressure rises and expands as it falls, and levers move a needle on a dial, so it is portable.
A barograph links an aneroid barometer to a pen that draws a continuous record on a rotating drum, which shows the trend of rising or falling pressure that matters most in forecasting.
Pressure is measured in hPa, with an average of about 1013 hPa, and readings are corrected to mean sea level so that stations at different heights can be compared and isobars drawn.

Aspect 2 – Measuring wind and humidity:
Wind and humidity are measured with an anemometer, a wind vane and a wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer.
A cup anemometer has three or four cups on arms that spin faster as wind speed rises, and the turns are counted electrically and converted to speed.
The standard height for measuring surface wind is 10 m above open ground, away from buildings and trees.
A wind vane has a large tail that is pushed downwind, so its pointer faces the direction the wind blows from.
Wind is always named from the direction it comes from, and windsocks show wind direction and strength at airports.
In a wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer the wet bulb is wrapped in wet muslin, and evaporation cools it so it reads lower than the dry bulb.
The drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the bigger the difference between the two readings, called the wet bulb depression, and if both read the same the air is saturated.
Relative humidity, given as a percentage, is read from tables using the dry bulb reading and the difference between the two, and a hair hygrometer uses human hair that lengthens in humid air.

Aspect 3 – Measuring the oceans and upper air:
Oceans and the upper atmosphere are measured by buoys, floats, balloons and satellites.
Moored weather buoys record air pressure, air and sea temperature, wind, wave height and humidity, and the Irish Marine Data Buoy Observation Network, run by the Marine Institute with Met Éireann, moors such buoys off the Irish coast.
Argo floats drift at depth, rise periodically to the surface and measure temperature and salinity down to about 2,000 m before transmitting by satellite, and about 4,000 floats now drift in the world's oceans.
Research ships lower CTD instruments, which measure conductivity, temperature and depth, to sample salinity and temperature at depth.
Tide gauges and satellite altimeters measure sea level.
Weather balloons carry radiosondes that measure temperature, humidity and pressure and radio them back, and tracking the balloon gives the upper winds.
Balloons are launched daily from stations including Valentia Observatory, Co. Kerry, which has one of the longest weather records in Ireland.
Geostationary and polar-orbiting weather satellites measure cloud, sea-surface temperature, water vapour and ice, and rainfall radar detects precipitation from the echo returned by raindrops.

Aspect 4 – Importance of the measurements:
These measurements matter because forecasts, warnings, businesses and climate science all depend on them.
Observations are the starting point for computer forecast models, so more and better observations give better forecasts.
Storm, rain, snow and heat warnings, including Met Éireann's colour codes, save lives and property.
Aviation needs wind, visibility, icing and pressure for altimeter settings, and shipping and fishing need sea area forecasts and wave heights from buoys.
Farmers time silage and hay making, spraying, sowing and harvesting by the forecast, and frost warnings protect crops.
Wind measurements are used to site wind farms and predict their output, and temperature forecasts predict electricity and gas demand.
Long, consistent records, such as the carbon dioxide record measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958, show trends such as global warming and sea-level rise and are the evidence used by the IPCC.
Buoy and Argo data on sea temperature and salinity track El Niño, ocean heat content and the Atlantic circulation, and they improve seasonal forecasts.

[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.17.1] Other sources include ships, buoys, aircraft, weather balloons, radar and satellites.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.17.2] Met Éireann (the Irish national weather service) collects the Irish data. There is more on this in Weather maps and weather data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.b] Temperature is measured by several instruments, each suited to a different job, and a screen keeps the readings honest.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.0] A liquid-in-glass thermometer uses mercury or alcohol, which expands and rises up a narrow tube as it warms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.1] A maximum-minimum thermometer, Six's thermometer, uses small metal indexes (markers) that are pushed up to the highest and lowest points reached and stay there until reset with a magnet; it is read once a day and then reset. James Six invented it in 1782.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.2] Electronic thermometers in automatic stations use sensors whose electrical resistance changes with temperature; a thermograph gives a continuous trace.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.3] Thermometers sit in a Stevenson screen (a ventilated white box) about 1.25 m above the ground. It is white to reflect sunlight, louvred (slatted) to let air circulate, and mounted on legs above grass.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.4] The Stevenson screen's door faces away from direct sun (north in Ireland), so readings are true shade air temperatures.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.18.5] A grass minimum thermometer, laid just above short grass, measures ground frost.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.b] Air pressure is the weight of the air above, and two types of barometer measure it in different ways.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.0] A mercury barometer: the weight of the atmosphere supports a column of mercury in a glass tube, and the column rises as pressure rises. Evangelista Torricelli invented it in 1643.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.1] An aneroid barometer (aneroid means without liquid) uses a sealed, partly emptied metal capsule that is squeezed in as pressure rises and expands as it falls; levers move a needle on a dial. It is portable, so it is used in homes and on ships.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.2] A barograph links an aneroid to a pen that draws a continuous record on a rotating drum; the trend of rising or falling pressure matters most in forecasting.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.3] Electronic pressure sensors are used in automatic stations.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.19.4] Readings are corrected to mean sea level so that stations at different heights can be compared and isobars (lines joining places of equal pressure) drawn. Pressure is measured in hPa (hectopascals, equal to mb), with an average of about 1013 hPa.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.b] Wind speed is measured by an anemometer, standing at a standard height so that readings can be compared.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.0] A cup anemometer has three or four cups on arms that spin faster as wind speed rises; the turns are counted electrically and converted to speed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.2] The WMO standard height for surface wind is 10 m above open ground, away from buildings and trees, which would shelter the instrument and lower its readings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.20.3] Speed is given in km/h, knots or m/s, and gusts (short bursts of stronger wind) are recorded separately from mean speed. Without instruments, speed is estimated using the Beaufort scale.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.21.b] Wind direction is the way the wind blows from, and it is shown by a vane or a windsock.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.21.0] A wind vane has a large tail that is pushed downwind, so its pointer faces the direction the wind blows from; direction is read against compass points or degrees.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.21.1] Windsocks show wind direction and strength at airports.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.21.2] Wind is always named from the direction it comes from, so a westerly wind blows from the west.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.b] Humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air. The wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer measures it by using evaporative cooling.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.0] A wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer (psychrometer) has two thermometers side by side. The wet bulb is wrapped in muslin (thin cloth) kept wet from a water reservoir; evaporation from the muslin cools it, so it reads lower than the dry bulb.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.1] The drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the bigger the difference, called the wet bulb depression. If both thermometers read the same, the air is saturated (holding all the vapour it can).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.2] Relative humidity, given as a percentage, is read from tables using the dry bulb reading and the difference between the two readings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.3] A hair hygrometer uses human hair, which lengthens in humid air and shortens in dry air, to move a pointer.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.22.4] Automatic stations use electronic (capacitance) sensors.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.23.0] Rain gauge: a funnel of standard size, set in open ground with its rim raised above the surface to avoid splash, collects rain into a measuring cylinder that is read in mm each day; tipping-bucket gauges record automatically.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.24.b] Weather also has to be measured high above the ground, where ground instruments cannot reach.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.24.0] Weather balloons carry radiosondes, small instrument packs that measure temperature, humidity and pressure and radio them back; tracking the balloon gives the upper winds.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.24.1] Balloons are launched twice a day, at fixed times, from stations worldwide, including Valentia Observatory, Co. Kerry, which has one of the longest weather records in Ireland, dating from the 19th century.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.24.2] Aircraft also report temperature and wind automatically.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.25.b] Remote sensing means measuring from a distance, without touching the thing measured.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.25.0] Geostationary (fixed over one spot) and polar-orbiting (passing over the poles) weather satellites measure cloud, sea-surface temperature, water vapour and ice. There is more on this in Satellite imagery.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.25.1] Rainfall radar detects precipitation from the echo returned by raindrops.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.b] Ocean measurements come from instruments floating on, drifting in and orbiting above the sea. Together they supply the data on the ocean side of the linked system.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.0] Moored weather buoys (anchored floating stations) record air pressure, air and sea temperature, wind, wave height and humidity, and transmit the readings, hourly. The Irish Marine Data Buoy Observation Network, run by the Marine Institute with Met Éireann, moors such buoys off the Irish coast.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.1] Drifting buoys follow ocean currents, and voluntary observing ships report weather at sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.2] Argo floats drift at depth, rise to the surface every 10 days or so, and measure temperature and salinity down to about 2,000 m before transmitting by satellite. The Argo programme began in 2000, and about 4,000 floats now drift in the world's oceans.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.3] Tide gauges and satellite altimeters (instruments that measure height from space) measure sea level; satellites also measure sea-surface temperature and sea ice.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.26.4] Research ships lower CTD instruments (conductivity, temperature, depth) to sample salinity and temperature at depth.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.27.0] Instruments must be correctly sited: in an open place, at the standard height, shaded for thermometers and away from buildings and heated surfaces, which would distort the readings.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.27.1] Instruments must be regularly calibrated (checked against a standard), read at the same times each day, and long records kept at the same site, so the record stays consistent.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.b] Measurements are collected because they are used. Each use below is a separate reason, from forecasting and safety to farming, energy and climate science.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.0] Forecasting: observations are the starting point (initial conditions) for computer forecast models, and more and better observations give better forecasts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.1] Warnings: storm, rain, snow and heat warnings, including Met Éireann's colour codes, save lives and property. There is more on this in Weather maps and weather data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.2] Storm surge (sea level raised by a storm) and flood warnings are issued, and tropical cyclones are tracked; a falling barograph trace warns of an approaching depression (a low-pressure weather system).
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.3] Aviation needs wind, visibility, icing and pressure for altimeter settings (so a plane's height reading is correct); shipping and fishing need sea area forecasts and wave heights from buoys; offshore work depends on the same data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.4] Farming: the timing of silage and hay making, spraying, sowing and harvesting depends on forecasts; frost warnings protect crops; soil temperature and moisture show when grass starts to grow; drought monitoring is also part of this use. There is more on drought in Climate and economic development.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.5] Energy and the economy: wind measurements are used to site wind farms and predict their output; temperature forecasts predict electricity and gas demand; construction, retail, insurance, sport and tourism planning all use weather data.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.6] Climate records: long, consistent records, such as the Mauna Loa carbon dioxide record and Valentia's long weather record, show trends including global warming, sea-level rise and ocean warming; they are the evidence used by the IPCC and by governments.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1.28.8] Ocean-atmosphere link: buoy and Argo data on sea temperature and salinity track El Niño, ocean heat content and the Atlantic circulation, and they improve seasonal forecasts.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.0.b] Water is constantly moving between the oceans, the air and the land. This continuous circulation is the hydrological (water) cycle, and it is the starting point for understanding how the oceans and atmosphere exchange water.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.3.b] The oceans are both the main source and the main destination of the water that moves through the air. This shows how much of the cycle happens over the sea.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.0] Humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air. Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapour in a volume of air, in g/m³.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.1] Relative humidity is the amount of water vapour present as a percentage of the most the air could hold at that temperature. Saturated air (air holding all it can) has a relative humidity of 100%.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.6.4] Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated as it cools; further cooling causes condensation. There is more on measuring humidity in The atmosphere and oceans as linked systems.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.1] Higher temperature increases evaporation because it gives more energy; strong sunshine and low air pressure also increase it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.2] Drier air, with low relative humidity, increases evaporation because it can take up more vapour.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.8.3] Wind increases evaporation by removing moist air and replacing it with drier air.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.0] Rising air expands because pressure falls with height, and it cools without losing heat to its surroundings; sinking air is compressed and warms.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.12.3] The height at which condensation begins is the condensation level, or cloud base.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.15.b] Clouds form wherever air is lifted and cooled below its dew point. The ways air is forced upward give five causes of cloud.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.20.2] Heights are approximate for mid-latitudes.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.21.b] Clouds appear in a predictable order as a front approaches, so they can be used to forecast weather.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.26.3] Sleet: melting snow, or rain and snow mixed.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.0] 1. The ground is heated strongly, warming the air above it.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.27.1] 2. The warm air expands, rises and cools adiabatically.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.b] Frontal rain falls where warm air meets cold air in a depression (a low-pressure weather system). It is Ireland's main source of rain.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.29.2] Frontal rain is Ireland's main source of rain. There is more on depressions in Atmospheric and oceanic circulation.
[geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3.33.2] Mediterranean: a wet winter and a dry summer.
