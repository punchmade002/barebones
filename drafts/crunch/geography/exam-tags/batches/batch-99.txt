PART "1B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Rock 1 – Limestone:
Limestone is a sedimentary rock made of calcium carbonate.
Associated landscape – Karst of the Burren, Co. Clare:
The landscape associated with limestone is karst, shaped mainly by solution, and the Burren in Co. Clare is Ireland's best-known example.
Rock 2 – Granite:
Granite is a hard, crystalline igneous rock.
Associated landscape – Wicklow Mountains granite upland:
The landscape associated with granite is the rounded granite upland of the Wicklow Mountains, formed from the Leinster batholith.
Examination – Limestone karst:
Limestone is permeable because water moves down through its joints and bedding planes, so the Burren has few surface streams.
Carbonation occurs when rainwater containing carbon dioxide forms weak carbonic acid, which reacts with calcium carbonate to form soluble calcium bicarbonate.
Glacial ice scraped the soil from the Burren, exposing bare jointed limestone to the rain and forming limestone pavement.
Carbonation widens the joints into deep grikes, which leave flat-topped clints standing between them.
Streams flowing off impermeable rock sink into swallow holes where they reach the limestone, as on the slopes of Cuilcagh Mountain in Co. Fermanagh.
Water dissolves caves along bedding planes and joints underground, as at Aillwee Cave in the Burren.
Dripping water deposits calcite, forming stalactites that hang from the cave roof and stalagmites that grow up from the floor, as at Doolin Cave, Co. Clare, which holds a stalactite about 7.3 m long.
Examination – Granite uplands:
Granite is hard and resistant to erosion, so the Leinster batholith, the largest granite mass in Ireland, stands high as the Wicklow Mountains.
Granite is impermeable, so rain runs off the surface and the upland has many streams and boggy ground.
Impermeable granite and high rainfall give thin, acidic soils and blanket bog, for example around the Sally Gap in Co. Wicklow.
Hydrolysis turns the feldspar in granite into clay, so the rock crumbles and the uplands are worn into rounded, dome-shaped summits.
Deep chemical weathering along closely jointed granite, followed by stripping of the rotted rock, left widely jointed blocks standing as tors, with clitter on the slopes below, as on Dartmoor in England.
Ice later cut deep valleys into the granite of the Wicklow Mountains.

PART "1C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Isostasy explained:
Isostasy is the state of balance between the crust and the denser mantle beneath it, on which the crust floats.
During the last glaciation thick ice sheets pressed the Irish crust down into the mantle in isostatic depression.
The north of Ireland had thicker ice, so it was depressed more and has undergone more isostatic rebound than the south.
When the ice melted the load was removed and the crust slowly rose, but rebound lags far behind melting.
Feature 1 – Raised beaches in the north:
Greater rebound in the north lifted former beaches, cliffs and rock platforms above present high tide, forming an emergent coast.
A raised beach at Malin Head, Inishowen, Co. Donegal, is a named Irish example of this emergent coast.
An old cliff line at Malin Head marks the former sea cliffs, which now stand inland of the sea.
A wave-cut notch is cut into the old cliff at the former sea level.
A flat wave-cut platform is a level rock shelf cut by waves at the former sea level and now stranded above the tide.
Beach shingle lies on the raised beach, well above the present tide reach.
Sea caves and sea stacks also stand above the tide reach on the raised coast at Malin Head.
Feature 2 – Drowned coasts in the south-west:
The south-west had thinner ice and less rebound, so the sea stood higher relative to the land and flooded the coast, giving a submergent coast.
Rias are drowned river valleys with a V-shaped cross-section and a winding outline.
Bantry Bay, the Kenmare River and Dingle Bay are named Irish rias in Co. Cork and Co. Kerry.
Killary Harbour, on the Galway and Mayo border, is a fjord, which is a drowned glacial trough that is deep and U-shaped.
Drowned drumlins form where the lowland between drumlins floods, so the crests stand as islands in the sea.
Clew Bay, Co. Mayo, and Strangford Lough, Co. Down, are named Irish examples of drowned drumlins.
Feature 3 – Rejuvenation of rivers:
A fall in base level, caused by the land rising relative to the sea, gives a river renewed erosive power, which is called rejuvenation.
The river erodes downwards again to the new, lower base level, and the renewed erosion works upstream from the mouth.
A knickpoint is a sudden steepening in the long profile of the river that can form a waterfall.
Incised meanders form when the river cuts down into its old meanders, leaving them entrenched in steep-sided valleys, as on the River Nore in Co. Kilkenny.
River terraces are left when the old valley floor stands as a step above the new, lowered channel.

PART "2B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option (i) – Sand spit at Inch, Co. Kerry:
A sand spit is a ridge of sand built out from the land and attached at one end only, and the example studied is Inch spit in Dingle Bay, Co. Kerry.
Role of deposition:
Deposition is the laying down of material by waves when they lose energy.
Waves lose energy where the coast changes direction, such as at a bay, so material is deposited there.
Deposition also occurs in sheltered bays, in shallow water and where refraction has reduced wave energy.
A large supply of sediment is needed, from rivers, cliff erosion or glacial deposits offshore.
Formation:
Waves approach the beach at an angle set by the prevailing wind, and swash carries sand up the beach at that angle.
Backwash drags the sand straight back down the slope under gravity, so material moves along the coast in a zigzag by longshore drift.
Longshore drift carries sand past a point where the coast changes direction, such as the entrance to a bay.
The sand is deposited in calmer, deeper water, building a ridge out from the land.
The ridge grows longer as more sand is deposited but stays attached to the land at one end only.
Wave refraction or a change in wind direction curves the tip, forming a recurved end.
Water behind the spit is sheltered, so it fills with silt and forms salt marsh or mudflat.
Examples:
Inch and Rossbeigh are two spits growing out from opposite sides of Dingle Bay, Co. Kerry.
Spurn Head, at the mouth of the Humber estuary in England, is another spit, built by drift of eroded material southward from the Holderness coast.
A plan view shows the drift direction, the ridge attached to the land, the recurved tip and the salt marsh behind.
If a spit grows right across the mouth of a bay and joins two headlands it becomes a sand bar, which traps a lagoon behind it.

PART "2C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Physical weathering – Freeze-thaw action:
Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process that breaks rock without changing its minerals.
It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 degrees Celsius often, together with water and jointed rock.
Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
At night the water freezes and expands by about 9 percent, which widens the crack.
By day the ice thaws and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular fragments.
It is most effective on mountain summits in upland Ireland, such as Carrauntoohil, Co. Kerry.
The fragments collect as scree slopes at the foot of mountain cliffs.
It also leaves jagged peaks and blockfields.
Chemical weathering – Carbonation:
Carbonation is a chemical weathering process that dissolves limestone.
Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate.
Calcium bicarbonate is soluble, so it is carried away in solution.
It works along joints and bedding planes, widening them into grikes and leaving flat-topped clints between them.
It is the process behind karst landscapes such as the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, because warmth speeds up chemical reactions.

[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.b] Rock at the surface is attacked by weather, water, plants and animals. Weathering is the first step in wearing landscapes down, and it must be kept separate from erosion.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.0] Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place (in situ) at or near the earth's surface, by weather, water, plants and animals. Erosion is the wearing away and removal of material by a moving agent such as a river, the sea, ice or wind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.2] Regolith is the layer of loose, weathered rock fragments lying on top of solid bedrock (the unbroken rock beneath).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.3] Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals (the chemical substances the rock is made of).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.4] Chemical weathering changes or dissolves the minerals in the rock, so the rock itself is altered, not just broken.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.5] Biological weathering by plants and animals works through both physical and chemical means.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.b] Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process. It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock (rock with natural cracks called joints).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.1] 2. At night the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.2] 3. By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.3] 4. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular (sharp-edged) fragments.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.4] It is most effective on mountains in upland Ireland, on high mountains and in periglacial areas (cold areas where the ground is frozen for much of the year). Freeze-thaw is active on Irish mountain summits such as Carrauntoohil, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.5] Result: scree (talus) slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields (areas covered in broken angular rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.1] The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day, and cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.4] Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.b] Rock can also break because the forces on it change, or because growing crystals push it apart. Neither process changes the minerals, so both are physical.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.0] Pressure release (also called unloading or sheeting): when the overlying rock is eroded, the weight pressing on buried rock such as granite is removed, so it expands upward and cracks into sheets parallel to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.2] Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores (tiny holes) and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.b] Plants and animals break rock down in two ways. Their physical action is a form of physical weathering, and the acids they produce cause chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.b] Carbonation is a chemical weathering process. It dissolves limestone, and it works along joints and bedding planes (the flat surfaces between layers of rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.0] Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.1] The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble, so it is carried away in solution (dissolved in water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.2] Word equation: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.2] Oxidation: oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron in the rock to form iron oxide (rust), which gives red, yellow or brown colours and weakens the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.1] Warm, wet climates favour fast chemical weathering because, by Van 't Hoff's rule, the rate of chemical reactions roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.2] Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, and hot deserts favour exfoliation and salt crystallisation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.3] Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.0] Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults (breaks in the rock where blocks have moved) let water in, so heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.0] Temperature acts in two ways: as a trigger, through the expansion and contraction that cause stress, and as a control on the rate of chemical reactions.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.3] Method: name the process and its type, state the temperature condition it needs, explain the stress or rate change it produces, and link this to the result, such as scree from freeze-thaw.

[geo-sea-s1.0.7] Backwash is the water draining back down the beach under gravity, so it can pull material back down with it, which is why the swash and backwash balance matters.
[geo-sea-s1.1.1] Constructive waves are typical of calm weather; their swash is stronger than their backwash, so they carry material up the beach and build it up.
[geo-sea-s1.1.3] Destructive waves are typical of storms; their backwash is stronger than their swash, so they drag material out to sea and erode the beach.
[geo-sea-s1.2.0] Refraction: waves approaching an indented coast slow down first in shallow water off headlands (points of land jutting into the sea), so the wave fronts bend.
[geo-sea-s1.2.1] Energy converges on headlands, causing more erosion there, and spreads out in bays (curved inlets of the sea), where it is weaker and deposition (the dropping of material) is favoured.
[geo-sea-s1.2.2] Refraction turns wave fronts until they are roughly parallel to the shore.

[geo-sea-s1.10.1] Longshore drift: waves approach the beach at an angle set by the prevailing wind; swash carries material up the beach at that angle, and backwash drags it straight back down the slope under gravity.

[geo-sea-s1.10.3] Longshore drift is the transport process behind spits, bars, lagoons and tombolos.
[geo-sea-s1.10.4] Example: on the Holderness coast, longshore drift moves eroded material southward to Spurn Head.

[geo-sea-s1.11.0] Waves deposit material when they lose energy: in constructive wave conditions, in sheltered bays, in shallow water, or after refraction has reduced their energy.
[geo-sea-s1.11.1] Deposition also happens where the coast changes direction, such as at a river mouth or bay.
[geo-sea-s1.11.2] A large supply of sediment is needed, from rivers, cliff erosion or glacial deposits offshore.
