PART 3A(iii) (4 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Wave refraction: The bending of waves as they approach the coast.
Waves slow down in shallow water near a headland and bend so that their energy is focused on the headland.

PART 3B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Interaction chosen – Mining:
Humans interact with the rock cycle through mining, which removes metal ores, coal and salt from the crust.
Mining acts as an exogenic process, because removing rock is the human equivalent of erosion and dumping waste is the human equivalent of deposition.
Extraction removes rock far faster than natural weathering and erosion do.
Example – Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath:
Tara Mines near Navan is an underground zinc and lead mine in production since 1977 and is the largest zinc mine in Europe.
The ore lies in Carboniferous limestone, where minerals were concentrated by hot fluids moving through the rock.
Exploration uses geological surveys and test drilling before a mine is developed.
Underground mining reaches the deep ore through shafts and tunnels.
The ore is crushed and ground and the mineral is separated from the waste rock.
Negative impacts:
Waste rock is stored in spoil heaps and fine waste in tailings ponds, which take up land and create new surface deposits.
Subsidence and landscape scars occur above and around underground workings.
At Avoca, Co. Wicklow, centuries of copper mining left acid mine drainage that pollutes the Avoca River with heavy metals.
Dust, noise and heavy traffic affect people living near a mine.
At Aberfan in south Wales in 1966 a colliery spoil tip collapsed after heavy rain and killed 144 people, 116 of them children.
Positive impacts and management:
Mining provides jobs, exports and local spending.
Environmental licensing and monitoring control how waste is handled and the quality of water leaving the site.
Tailings ponds are lined to stop seepage and capped when they are closed, and the Lisheen mine in Co. Tipperary was rehabilitated after it closed in 2015.

PART 3C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Landform – Fold mountains:
Folding influences landform development because compression squeezes rock layers into folds, which build fold mountains.
Formation of fold mountains:
Sediments are deposited in a sea lying between two converging plates.
The plates converge and the sediments are compressed by horizontal pressure.
The layers buckle into anticlines, which are upfolds, and synclines, which are downfolds.
Continued compression produces overfolds, recumbent folds and nappes, which thicken the crust and uplift it into long, high ranges.
Young fold mountains such as the Alps, formed by the collision of the African and Eurasian plates, are high and steep.
Old fold mountains such as those of Ireland have been worn down by weathering and erosion into lower, rounded mountains.
Examples – Munster ridge and valley landscape:
Armorican folding produced the east to west ridges and valleys of Munster.
Differential erosion leaves resistant Old Red Sandstone on the anticlines as ridges, such as Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains and the Caha Mountains.
Weaker Carboniferous limestone survives in the synclines as valleys, such as the Blackwater and Lee valleys.
The coastal ends of these valleys were drowned by the rising sea, forming rias such as Bantry Bay, Dingle Bay and the Kenmare River.
Caledonian folding produced the north-east to south-west trend of the mountains of Donegal, Mayo and Wicklow.
Second landform – Dome and escarpments in the Weald:
Doming pushes rock layers upward into a circular or oval upfold.
In the Weald of south-east England the layers were pushed up into a dome, and erosion removed the chalk from the top.
The chalk remains as two escarpments, the North Downs and the South Downs, with clay vales inside them and sandstone forming the High Weald in the centre.
Drainage and diagram points:
Rivers follow the weak rock along the synclines, and where a river cuts across a ridge it makes a sharp right-angled bend, as the Blackwater does when it turns south at Cappoquin, Co. Waterford.
A cross-section shows the anticline as an arch of sandstone forming a ridge, with the syncline of limestone forming a valley between the ridges.

PART 4B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Region – India, a sub-continental region:
India is a sub-continental region in South Asia, bounded by the Himalayas in the north, the Arabian Sea to the west and the Bay of Bengal to the east.
Factor 1 – The monsoon climate:
India has a tropical monsoon climate, and the south-west monsoon from June to September brings about three-quarters of the annual rainfall.
This rain lets kharif crops such as rice, cotton and maize be sown in June and July and harvested in autumn.
Warm tropical temperatures permit two or three crops a year where water is available.
Rice is grown by intensive wet-rice farming in the wet east, the south and the deltas, and India is the world's second-largest rice producer.
Rabi crops such as wheat are sown in October and November and depend on irrigation and on winter rain from western disturbances.
A late or weak monsoon causes drought, which cuts yields and causes farmer distress.
