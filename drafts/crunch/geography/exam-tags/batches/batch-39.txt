PART 9C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Developed economy – Ireland:
Ireland is a developed economy whose level of development has changed greatly since independence.
Change in the 1950s:
Ireland pursued protectionism and suffered economic stagnation in the 1950s.
This drove the highest emigration since the 1880s, with net emigration of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961.
The population fell to about 2.8 million in 1961, the lowest ever recorded.
T. K. Whitaker's Economic Development and the First Programme for Economic Expansion in 1958 shifted Ireland towards an outward-looking economy.
Change from the 1960s to the 1980s:
The IDA, set up in 1949, was given a central role in attracting foreign investment from 1969.
Free secondary education was introduced in 1967, which raised the skills of the workforce.
Ireland joined the EEC on 1 January 1973, gaining access to the single market and to guaranteed farm prices under the CAP.
The 1980s brought recession, high unemployment and net emigration of over 200,000 between 1981 and 1991, including many graduates.
Change during the Celtic Tiger, 1990s to 2007:
Multinationals such as Intel, at Leixlip from 1989, were attracted by skilled English-speaking workers, EU market access and low corporation tax, which was 12.5% from 2003.
Structural Funds, with Ireland an Objective 1 region from 1989 to 1999, paid for roads and other infrastructure that supported growth.
Ireland had net immigration from 1996 as returning Irish and foreign workers arrived, and the population reached about 4.2 million by 2006.
Ireland changed from a country of emigration to a country of immigration.
Change after the 2008 crash:
The property crash and banking crisis from 2008 led to the EU-IMF bailout in November 2010.
Unemployment rose to about 15% in 2012 and emigration peaked at about 89,000 in the year to April 2013.
The economy recovered, with unemployment falling below 5% by 2022 as multinationals and services expanded.
Ireland became a net contributor to the EU budget in 2014, because of its economic growth.
Ireland today:
Ireland has the second-highest GDP per head in the EU after Luxembourg, but GDP overstates prosperity because multinationals send profits abroad.
GNI* is only a little over half of GDP in the early 2020s, which is why the CSO introduced this measure from 2017.
Ireland ranked in the top ten of the Human Development Report 2023/24, showing very high development in health, education and income.

PART 10B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Central place theory:
Walter Christaller, a German geographer, published central place theory in 1933, based on a study of southern Germany.
A central place is a settlement that provides goods and services to the people in its surrounding area.
Threshold is the minimum number of customers needed to keep a service in business.
Range is the maximum distance people will travel to obtain a service.
A service locates where its threshold falls within its range.
Assumptions of the theory:
The theory assumes a flat, uniform plain with an evenly spread population of equal purchasing power.
It assumes equal ease of transport in all directions and that consumers buy at the nearest centre.
Predictions of the theory:
Market areas are hexagonal, because circles overlap or leave gaps while hexagons fit together without gaps.
A hierarchy forms, with many small, closely spaced low-order centres and fewer, more widely spaced high-order centres.
Low-order goods such as bread and newspapers have a low threshold and range, so they are found in every village.
High-order goods such as jewellers, department stores and hospitals have a high threshold and range, so they are found only in large centres.
Each higher-order centre also supplies all the lower-order goods.
In nesting, the marketing principle gives a pattern of three, the transport principle four and the administrative principle seven.
Named example – Noordoostpolder, Netherlands:
The Noordoostpolder was drained in 1942, and Emmeloord was planned as its central town.
Ten villages, including Marknesse, Ens, Kraggenburg and Bant, were placed around Emmeloord.
The villages were spaced so that farmers could reach one by bicycle, giving a planned two-level hierarchy.
Second example – Irish Midlands market towns:
In parts of the Irish Midlands, market towns serving farming areas are regularly spaced.
Limitations of the theory:
Relief, rivers and coasts are not uniform, and population is unevenly spread.
Car ownership, motorways, out-of-town shopping centres and online shopping change ranges and thresholds.
Dublin's primacy, history and government decisions break the regular hierarchy.

MODEL ANSWER: Impact 1 – Emigration and rural population decline:
The State's population fell at almost every census from 1841 to 1961 and reached about 2.8 million in 1961, and rural areas bore the brunt of this decline.
In the 1950s net emigration was about 400,000, mostly to Britain, because of economic stagnation and the decline of small farms and farm labouring in the countryside.
Rural areas, as donor regions, lost young workers, so their population declined and the age structure became unbalanced.
In western counties such as Mayo, many rural parishes were left depopulated by emigration.
An ageing rural population meant closed national schools, fewer marriages and lower birth rates.
Village services such as the post office, Garda station, pub, shop and school closed.
Emigration also separated families in rural communities.
Impact 2 – Brain drain and later emigration:
Net emigration of over 200,000 between 1981 and 1991 included many graduates, so rural areas lost the skills they had paid to educate.
After the 2008 crash emigration peaked at about 89,000 in the year to April 2013, and many of those leaving were young people from rural parishes in Mayo and Donegal heading for Australia, the UK, Canada and the USA.
The loss of young adults left farms without successors and local GAA clubs short of players.
Impact 3 – Remittances, return and immigration:
Emigrant remittances supported many rural families in the west.
Returning emigrants brought skills, capital and experience back to rural areas in the 1970s and 1990s.
Emigration reduced pressure on jobs and welfare in rural areas with few employers.
Immigration since the mid-1990s has brought migrant workers to rural meat plants, mushroom farms and dairy farms that struggled to find local labour.
Young immigrant workers pay taxes and offset the ageing of rural populations.
Immigrant children help to keep small rural schools open.
Immigrant families have helped to keep rural parishes and local sports clubs active.
The diaspora supports rural tourism and investment, as many emigrants return to visit family and ancestral places in the west.
