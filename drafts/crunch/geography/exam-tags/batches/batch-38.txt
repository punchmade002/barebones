PART 8B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Example 1 – Dell computer manufacturing, Limerick:
Dell opened in Raheen, Limerick, in 1990, attracted by access to the EU single market, an English-speaking workforce and low corporation tax.
In January 2009 Dell announced that manufacturing would move to Łódź in Poland, with about 1,900 jobs lost in Limerick.
Poland joined the EU on 1 May 2004, so a plant there had tariff-free access to the same market at lower cost.
Dell kept sales and service functions in Ireland, showing that only part of the activity moved.
Example 2 – Apple, Hollyhill, Cork:
Apple opened at Hollyhill in 1980 and first made computers there.
As Apple's manufacturing moved to contract manufacturers in Asia, the Cork site shifted mainly to operations, support and services.
Cork is now Apple's European headquarters with about 6,000 staff, showing how the function of a location changes over time.
Why modern activities are mobile:
Containerisation and air freight have lowered transport costs, so goods can move cheaply over long distances.
Instant communications through the internet and fibre let firms manage plants at a distance.
Standardised production can be done by low-skilled workers, so work can move to wherever labour is cheapest.
The removal of trade barriers through the EU single market and the World Trade Organization makes it easier to sell across borders from any location.
Multinationals can compare countries and move capital quickly.
High-technology products such as microchips are footloose, as Intel chose Leixlip in Co. Kildare for its plant, opened in 1989, from many possible sites.
Consequences for host countries:
Investment brings rapid job creation, but jobs can leave just as quickly.
Branch plants are vulnerable because decisions are made at a distant headquarters.
Countries compete for investment by offering tax and grants.
Rationalisation, automation, outsourcing or the end of a product's life cycle can all lead to closure.
Host countries can reduce the risk by investing in education and research, such as Science Foundation Ireland set up in 2000, so that jobs are harder to move.

PART 8C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Renewable energy source – Wind energy:
Wind turns turbine blades that drive a generator, with onshore farms such as Galway Wind Park in Connemara and offshore farms such as Arklow Bank off Co. Wicklow.
Economic advantages:
Wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s, so it is a main home-grown source of power.
Once the turbines are built the fuel is free, so running costs are low.
Ireland imported about three-quarters of its energy in the early 2020s, at a cost of billions of euro a year, and wind reduces this import bill.
Less dependence on imports improves energy security and shields Ireland from fossil fuel price shocks.
Wind farms create jobs in construction, maintenance and manufacture, often in rural areas.
Farmers and landowners earn rental income from turbines on their land, which supports rural communities.
Meeting EU and national 2030 renewable targets avoids the cost of buying compliance.
Green electricity attracts multinational companies that want renewable power.
Environmental advantages:
Wind produces no carbon dioxide when generating electricity, so it reduces the enhanced greenhouse effect and global warming.
Unlike coal and oil, it releases no sulphur dioxide or nitrogen oxides, so it does not add to acid rain.
Wind is inexhaustible, so it does not deplete finite fossil fuel resources.
Replacing coal and peat in power generation avoids the landscape damage of mining and peat cutting.
Wind farms need no cooling water, so unlike thermal power stations they cause no thermal pollution of rivers.
Land around onshore turbines can still be used for grazing, so the footprint on farmland is small.
Offshore sites such as Arklow Bank use the sea and take up no farmland.
Denmark gets about half of its electricity from wind, showing that large cuts in emissions are possible.

[geo-mapwork-s1.3.1] Longitude is the angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian (0°), which passes through Greenwich, London, up to 180°. Lines of longitude (meridians) run north–south and meet at the poles.
