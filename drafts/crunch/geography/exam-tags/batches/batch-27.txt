PART 9B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: HDI as a measure:
The Human Development Index is a composite measure published by the United Nations Development Programme since 1990 that gives each country a value between 0 and 1.
It combines life expectancy at birth, years of schooling and GNI per head at purchasing power parity.
Life expectancy measures a long and healthy life, and it is about 84 years in Japan but below 60 in countries such as Chad and Lesotho.
Schooling is measured by mean years for adults aged 25 and over and expected years for children starting school, so it shows access to knowledge.
Income is measured by GNI per head in PPP dollars, so it shows the standard of living in terms of what money actually buys.
Countries are grouped as very high (0.800 and above), high, medium or low (below 0.550), which makes comparison simple.
Norway ranked first in the 2020 report with 0.957 and Niger was last with 0.394, which shows the wide gap between rich and poor countries.
It is broader than income alone, so Cuba ranks higher on HDI than on income because of strong public health care and education.
It is a national average that hides inequality between rich and poor, men and women, and regions, and it leaves out political freedom and environmental sustainability.
GNP as a measure:
Gross National Product is the value of the output owned by a country's residents and firms in a year, calculated as GDP plus income earned abroad minus profits and interest sent out of the country.
GNP is divided by the population to give GNP per head, so countries of different size can be compared, because a large total such as India's can hide a low figure per head.
It is simple, gives one figure for almost every country and lets change over time be tracked.
It is linked to a government's ability to fund health, education and infrastructure.
It is an average that hides inequality, and it ignores the informal economy, subsistence farming and unpaid work such as caring and housework.
It counts harmful spending such as pollution clean-up as if it added to well-being, and it ignores environmental damage.
It says nothing directly about health, education or freedom, which is why it is a limited measure of development.
Comparing the two:
Ireland's GNP is well below its GDP because multinationals send their profits abroad, so GDP overstates Irish prosperity.
Equatorial Guinea ranks far higher on income than on HDI because of its oil wealth, while Cuba shows the opposite pattern.
Using both measures together, with other indicators, gives the fullest picture of development.

MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Dublin:
Dublin is the urban area studied, and both housing and transport problems are examined for it.
Problem 1 – Housing:
Housing is a problem because demand from population growth and migration has outrun supply, producing high rents and homelessness.
Part V of the Planning and Development Act 2000 requires part of new private housing developments to be social and affordable housing, and the share was restored to 20% in 2021.
Rent Pressure Zones were introduced in December 2016, first in Dublin and Cork, to limit rent increases.
Housing for All in 2021 set a target of about 33,000 new homes a year to 2030.
The National Planning Framework of 2018 promotes compact growth, with 40% of new homes to be built within the footprint of existing settlements on brownfield and infill land.
Ballymun's seven 15-storey towers, built in 1966 to 1969, were demolished between 2004 and 2015 and replaced by low-rise homes, with residents involved in the regeneration.
This was effective in improving housing quality, but the recession stalled the new town centre and unemployment stayed high.
Overall the strategies have been only partly effective, because completions remain below the level of need and over 15,000 people were in emergency accommodation in 2025.
Problem 2 – Transport:
Traffic congestion arises because radial roads converge on the centre, there are few crossings of the Liffey and sprawl has made commuters car dependent.
The Luas opened in 2004 and Luas Cross City linked the Red and Green lines through the city centre in December 2017, which has been effective.
The DART, opened in 1984, gives a fast rail alternative along the coast.
The Port Tunnel, opened in 2006, took port traffic out of the city centre, and a 2007 restriction kept most five-axle lorries out, which succeeded in its aim.
The M50 orbital motorway, built in stages from 1990, takes traffic around the city instead of through it.
Dublin Bikes was launched in 2009 and the Leap card was introduced in 2011, while BusConnects is redesigning the bus network.
Metro North was shelved in 2011 and its successor MetroLink has been repeatedly delayed with no date or cost set, which shows a weakness of planning.
Congestion persists because land use remains dispersed and people still need cars to reach jobs and shops, so the strategies have been only partly effective.

PART 10C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Trend in migration:
Europe received about 1.3 million asylum applications in 2015 and a similar number in 2016, driven by the war in Syria from 2011 and conflict in Afghanistan, Iraq and Eritrea.
Over 1 million people crossed the Mediterranean in 2015, most reaching Greek islands such as Lesbos from Turkey, while the Central Mediterranean route ran from Libya to Lampedusa.
Impact 1 – Relocation and the Dublin system:
The surge exposed the Dublin system, which makes the state of first entry responsible for a claim, as unfair to frontier states such as Italy and Greece, and it broke down in 2015.
In September 2015 the EU agreed to relocate 160,000 asylum seekers from Italy and Greece.
Hungary, Poland and the Czech Republic refused to take part, and in 2020 the EU Court of Justice ruled that they had broken EU law, which showed the tension between solidarity and national sovereignty.
Germany under Angela Merkel received the largest number of asylum seekers in 2015 to 2016.
Impact 2 – Tighter border control:
Hungary under Viktor Orbán built a fence on its border with Serbia in 2015.
Some states reintroduced internal border checks, which weakened the free movement of the Schengen area.
Frontex, the EU border and coast guard agency based in Warsaw, was strengthened.
Impact 3 – Externalisation of controls:
The EU–Turkey statement of March 2016 required irregular migrants reaching the Greek islands to be returned to Turkey, with €6 billion promised for refugees there, and arrivals in Greece fell sharply.
Deals with Libya and other North African states aim to control departures before migrants reach EU borders.
Impact 4 – Temporary protection and the 2024 Pact:
The Temporary Protection Directive was activated for the first time on 4 March 2022, giving people fleeing Ukraine the immediate right to live and work without an asylum claim.
The Pact on Migration and Asylum, adopted in May 2024, introduces faster border procedures and mandatory solidarity, under which each state takes in relocated asylum seekers or pays into a fund.
Impact 5 – Irish policy:
Ireland chose to opt in to the Pact in 2024.
Ireland's policy is open to EU citizens and skilled workers but restrictive for low-skilled non-EU workers, and its direct provision system for asylum seekers has been widely criticised.
Labour shortages pull policy one way while housing pressure and public opposition pull it the other, so policy remains under tension.
