QUESTION geography-pp-2026-higher-q122 (LC Geography Higher 2026 — Part Two — Q22, 80 marks)

PART 22 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Solar energy is transformed into heat, latent heat and kinetic energy, and is then moved from the tropical surplus to the polar deficit by the circulation of the atmosphere and the oceans.
Aspect 1 – Solar energy and the heat budget:
Solar energy arrives as short-wave radiation, while the cooler earth gives off long-wave radiation.
Of 100 units of incoming radiation about 30 are reflected to space, giving the earth an albedo of about 30%.
About 20 units are absorbed by the atmosphere and clouds, and about 50 units by the land and ocean surface.
Radiant energy absorbed by the surface becomes sensible heat.
Energy used to evaporate water is stored as latent heat in water vapour, which later powers storms.
Heat differences create pressure differences, which become the kinetic energy of wind and, through wind drag, of waves and currents.
Between the equator and about 35 to 40 degrees north and south more energy is received than is lost, which is a surplus.
Poleward of about 35 to 40 degrees more energy is lost than is received, which is a deficit.
Without a poleward transfer the tropics would keep heating and the poles would keep cooling, so energy must be moved.
Aspect 2 – Atmospheric circulation:
In the Hadley cell air rises at the equator, creating low pressure and the ITCZ, where it releases latent heat as convectional rain.
At height this air flows poleward and sinks at about 30 degrees to form the subtropical high pressure and the deserts.
It returns to the equator as the trade winds, completing the cell.
In the Ferrel cell surface westerlies carry warm air poleward to meet cold polar air at the polar front at about 60 degrees.
Depressions forming along the polar front carry heat poleward in the mid-latitudes.
The polar front jet stream, a fast band of westerly wind at 9 to 12 km, steers depressions towards Ireland.
Water evaporated over warm tropical oceans carries latent heat poleward and releases it where the vapour condenses, as in a depression over Ireland.
Tropical cyclones form over seas of at least 26 to 27 °C and carry heat and moisture from the tropical oceans poleward.
Ex-hurricane Ophelia was a major hurricane south of the Azores and reached Ireland as a post-tropical storm on 16 October 2017.
Aspect 3 – Oceanic circulation:
Winds drag the surface water, with the trade winds pushing the equatorial currents west and the westerlies pushing the mid-latitude currents east.
The Coriolis effect and the continents turn the currents into gyres, which turn clockwise in the northern hemisphere.
The Gulf Stream flows from the Gulf of Mexico and the Straits of Florida north-east along the US coast and carries more water than all the world's rivers combined.
The westerlies carry it across the Atlantic as the North Atlantic Drift, which brings warmth to Ireland, Britain and Norway.
This gives Ireland mild winters and keeps the Norwegian coast at Hammerfest ice-free in winter.
The cold Labrador Current flows south, so the coast of Labrador, at about the same latitude as Ireland, has long freezing winters and sea ice.
Cold currents such as the Canary, Benguela and Humboldt return cooled water towards the equator along the eastern sides of ocean basins.
The thermohaline circulation is driven by cold, salty, dense water that sinks in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica.
The deep water spreads slowly through the world's oceans while warm surface water flows in to replace it, so heat is carried round the globe in a circuit of about 1,000 years.

QUESTION geography-pp-2026-higher-q123 (LC Geography Higher 2026 — Part Two — Q23, 80 marks)

PART 23 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Water exchanged between the oceans and the atmosphere falls as precipitation in different forms and in a distribution pattern that varies with latitude, relief and ocean currents.
Aspect 1 – Water exchange between ocean and atmosphere:
The hydrological cycle is a closed system driven by solar energy and gravity, in which water moves between stores.
The oceans hold about 97% of the earth's water, while the atmosphere holds only about 0.001%.
Roughly 85 to 90% of global evaporation comes from the oceans.
Roughly three-quarters of global precipitation falls on the oceans.
The surplus evaporated over the oceans is carried over the land by wind and returns to the sea through rivers and groundwater.
Evaporation increases with high temperature, drier air, wind and a large supply of water.
Evaporation is greatest over the subtropical oceans under clear skies and steady trade winds, and along warm currents such as the Gulf Stream.
Water stays in the atmosphere for only about 9 days on average, so atmospheric water is recycled quickly.
Rising air cools adiabatically to its dew point and condenses around nuclei such as dust and sea salt to form cloud.
Aspect 2 – Types of precipitation:
Convectional rainfall forms when strongly heated ground warms the air, which rises, cools to dew point and forms cumulonimbus, giving heavy afternoon showers.
It is typical of the equatorial belt every day, where Manaus in the Amazon receives about 2,300 mm a year.
Relief rainfall forms when moist air from the sea is forced to rise over a mountain barrier, cools and condenses.
This gives heavy rain on the windward slope, as on the mountains of Ireland's west coast.
On the leeward side the air descends and warms, so clouds evaporate and a rain shadow results.
In India the Western Ghats force the Arabian Sea branch of the monsoon to rise, giving heavy rain on the Konkan and Malabar coasts, with the Deccan in rain shadow.
Frontal rainfall forms in a depression when warm, moist tropical air is forced to rise over cold polar air at a front.
At the warm front the ascent is gentle and rain is long and steady, while at the cold front the ascent is sharp and showers are short and heavy.
Frontal rain is Ireland's main source of rain.
Aspect 3 – Distribution of precipitation:
The equatorial belt has over 2,000 mm a year, because of low pressure, rising air, convergence at the ITCZ and high evaporation.
The subtropical high-pressure belts at about 20 to 30 degrees have very low rainfall, because sinking, warming, stable air and clear skies produce the hot deserts.
The Atacama in Chile is the driest non-polar desert on earth.
In the mid-latitudes at about 40 to 60 degrees depressions in the westerlies bring moderate to heavy rain, which is wettest on west coasts.
Polar regions have very low precipitation because cold air holds little vapour, and Antarctica is the driest continent.
Warm currents add moisture and instability, while cold currents chill the air and make it stable, so the Namib coast beside the Benguela Current has fog rather than rain.
Continental interiors are dry because they lie far from the moisture of the oceans.
The pressure belts move with the overhead sun, which gives India a wet summer monsoon and a dry winter.
Ireland receives about 750 mm around Dublin, about 1,000 to 1,250 mm across much of the west and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.

MODEL ANSWER: The cool temperate oceanic climate, as experienced in Ireland, is mild, wet and changeable because of Ireland's position on the Atlantic edge of Europe in the path of the westerlies.
Aspect 1 – Temperature and its causes:
This climate lies on west coasts in the path of the westerlies, including Ireland, Britain, north-west France and coastal Norway.
Winters are mild and summers are cool, with Ireland's mean January temperature about 4 to 7 °C and its mean July about 14 to 16 °C.
The annual range is small, at about 10 °C.
The sea heats and cools slowly, so it moderates the seasons.
The North Atlantic Drift, the extension of the Gulf Stream, warms winter air and seas.
Prevailing south-westerly winds bring maritime air off the Atlantic.
Ireland's mid-latitude position means the sun is at a moderate angle, so summers are cool.
Frost and snow are infrequent on low ground.
Eastwards the oceanic influence weakens, and the climate grades into the continental climate of central and eastern Europe.
Aspect 2 – Precipitation:
Rain falls in all months, with a maximum in autumn and winter.
Annual rainfall is about 750 mm in the driest east around Dublin, about 1,000 to 1,250 mm across much of the west and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
There are about 150 wet days a year on the east and south-east coasts and up to about 225 in parts of the west.
Most rain is frontal, from depressions forming on the polar front.
Frontal rain forms when warm, moist tropical air is forced to rise over cold polar air.
Relief rain falls on west-facing mountains as moist Atlantic air is forced to rise.
Amounts fall from west to east, because Atlantic depressions reach the west first.
The east lies in the rain shadow of the western mountains.
Occasional convectional showers fall on summer afternoons, especially inland.
Aspect 3 – Weather, air masses and pressure systems:
Depressions form frequently on the polar front and are steered by the jet stream, so the weather is changeable.
Depressions and ridges of high pressure alternate across Ireland.
The Icelandic Low and the Azores High dominate Ireland's weather.
In the positive phase of the North Atlantic Oscillation the westerlies are strong and winters are mild, wet and stormy.
Polar maritime air, cool, moist and showery, is Ireland's most frequent air mass.
Tropical maritime air from near the Azores is warm, moist and mild, with cloud and drizzle.
Occasional anticyclones bring dry spells, most often in summer.
Polar continental air from Russia and Scandinavia in winter is very cold and dry and brings snow to east coasts.
Winds blow mainly from the south-west and are strongest in winter.
