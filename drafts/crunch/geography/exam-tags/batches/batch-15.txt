PART 8B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Multinational company – Intel:
Intel is an American multinational founded in 1968 with its headquarters in Santa Clara, California.
It operates wafer fabrication plants at Leixlip in Ireland, Hillsboro in Oregon, Chandler in Arizona and Kiryat Gat in Israel, and assembly and test plants in Malaysia, Vietnam and China.
Impact 1 – Falling trade barriers expand trade:
Globalisation has grown world trade much faster than world output, because the World Trade Organisation, set up in 1995, and the EU single market have cut tariffs and other barriers.
Intel benefits because it can sell microprocessors to computer makers such as Lenovo in China and HP and Dell in the USA from plants in Ireland and elsewhere.
Ireland's EU membership gives the Leixlip plant tariff-free access to the single market.
Chips are small, light and very valuable, so cheap air freight lets them move across continents.
Impact 2 – Global supply chains in components:
Globalisation spreads production into stages, so components and semi-finished goods cross borders several times before the final product is sold.
Intel buys lithography machines from ASML in Veldhoven in the Netherlands, the only maker of extreme ultraviolet machines, and silicon wafers from specialist makers.
Processed wafers from Leixlip are flown to assembly and test plants in Penang and Kulim in Malaysia, Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam and Chengdu in China.
There the wafers are cut into chips, packaged and tested, and the chips then go to computer makers' assembly plants, mostly in Asia.
Multinationals trade components between their own plants in different countries, which adds to the volume of world trade.
Impact 3 – New international division of labour:
Intel keeps headquarters and research and development in the core, at Santa Clara and Hillsboro, while fabs and assembly are placed where costs and skills suit.
Leixlip makes processed wafers only, and cutting, packaging and testing are done in Malaysia and Vietnam.
Fab 34 at Leixlip, opened in 2023, was the first fab in Europe to make chips in high volume using extreme ultraviolet lithography.
Impact 4 – Vulnerability and competition:
Dependence on Asian supply chains makes trade fragile, as COVID-19 disruption caused the global chip shortage of 2021, which halted car and electronics production worldwide.
Competition from TSMC in Taiwan and Samsung in South Korea, and the loss of Apple as a customer in 2020, show how global competition reshapes trade flows.
For Ireland, Intel's exports add to export earnings and corporation tax, but global job cuts announced in 2024 show that the plant depends on decisions made in California.

PART 8C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sustainable development defined:
Sustainable development meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, as set out in the Brundtland Report of 1987.
It balances environmental protection, economic growth and social fairness, so economic activity continues while its impact on the environment is kept within limits.
Principles include using renewable resources no faster than they regenerate and applying the polluter pays principle.
Sustainable approach 1 – Environmental impact assessment:
Large projects such as motorways, wind farms, mines, quarries and power stations must complete an environmental impact assessment before planning permission is decided.
The developer prepares a report on likely effects on water, air, wildlife, landscape and heritage, with measures to reduce them.
The public and bodies such as the National Parks and Wildlife Service can make submissions, and the planning authority may grant permission with conditions.
This prevents damage before it happens, which is cheaper than paying for clean-up afterwards.
At Derrybrien, Co. Galway, a peat slide occurred in October 2003 during wind-farm construction, and in 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland because no proper assessment had been carried out.
Sustainable approach 2 – Fishing quotas:
Overfishing is an environmental impact of primary activity, caused by sonar, factory ships and huge nets that take fish faster than stocks can breed.
At the Grand Banks off Newfoundland the cod stock collapsed, a ban was declared in 1992 and about 30,000 people lost their jobs.
The EU Common Fisheries Policy, begun in 1983, sets catch limits for each stock and divides them into national quotas.
Minimum mesh sizes, closed areas and seasons and the 2013 landing obligation, which ends discards, help fleets to fish at maximum sustainable yield.
The Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority and the Naval Service monitor Irish waters, so mackerel stocks landed at Killybegs can support the fleet in the long term.
Sustainable approach 3 – Licensing and mining controls:
The Environmental Protection Agency licenses large industries such as pharmaceutical plants and mines, and each licence sets emission limits for air, water, waste and noise.
Copper mining at Avoca, Co. Wicklow, left acid mine drainage that still pollutes the Avoca River long after the mines closed.
By contrast Tara Mines at Navan operates under an EPA licence, stores tailings in a lined facility and monitors the water.
The Lisheen mine in Co. Tipperary closed in 2015 and the site has been rehabilitated, and a new mine needs a closure and restoration plan before it opens.
