PART 6B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named region – India:
India is a subcontinental region in South Asia, bounded by the Himalayas to the north, the Arabian Sea to the west and the Bay of Bengal to the east.
A sketch map would show the Chota Nagpur Plateau and Jamshedpur in the north-east of the peninsula, Mumbai on the west coast, Pune inland from it on the Deccan and Bengaluru on the southern Deccan.
Factor 1 – Raw materials:
Heavy industries locate near the raw materials they use, because these are costly to move over long distances.
The Chota Nagpur Plateau holds most of India's coal and iron ore, with coal mined in the Jharia and Damodar valley coalfields in Jharkhand.
Iron and steel is the first example of secondary activity, and the Tata Iron and Steel Company, founded in 1907, built India's first large steel works at Jamshedpur on these resources.
Limestone and manganese from the same plateau are also used in steel making.
This clustering made the Chota Nagpur and Damodar valley region around Jamshedpur a major industrial region.
Cotton grown on the black soils of Maharashtra and Gujarat supplied the cotton textile mills of Mumbai and Ahmedabad.
Cotton textiles is the second example of secondary activity, and Ahmedabad became known as the Manchester of India.
Jute grown in the Ganges delta supplied the jute mills along the Hooghly near Kolkata.
Factor 2 – Government policy:
Government policy has changed both the scale and the location of Indian industry.
After 1947 industrialisation was state-led under the Five-Year Plans, and the state built steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur.
Imports were protected from foreign competition by high tariffs, so home industry was sheltered while it developed.
The Licence Raj required permits to start or expand a factory, which made industry inefficient and slowed growth.
A balance-of-payments crisis in 1991 led to reforms that removed most licences, cut tariffs and opened India to foreign investment.
After 1991 manufacturing and export markets grew rapidly.
Special Economic Zones, created by the Act of 2005, offer incentives that steer firms to chosen sites.
Make in India was launched in 2014 and production-linked incentives from 2020 subsidise electronics, cars and pharmaceuticals, for example the Foxconn assembly of iPhones at Sriperumbudur near Chennai.

PART 6C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named regions – Dublin core and western periphery:
The Greater Dublin Area, made up of Dublin, Kildare, Meath and Wicklow, is the core region of Ireland.
The West of Ireland, including Counties Mayo and Leitrim in the Northern and Western region, is the peripheral region.
Difference 1 – Economic activity:
The core has a large tertiary sector, including finance, company headquarters and high-tech industry, such as Google's European headquarters in the Dublin docklands.
The West depends more on primary activity such as small farms, fishing and forestry, and has few large employers.
Most foreign direct investment goes to the Dublin core, whereas factories in the West are few, small and often grant-aided.
Incomes are high and unemployment is low in the core, but incomes are lower and unemployment or underemployment is higher in the West.
Cumulative causation explains the gap, because each advantage in Dublin attracts more firms and jobs, so the gap tends to widen.
Difference 2 – Population and migration:
The Greater Dublin Area has about 2.1 million people, about 40% of the State's population in Census 2022.
Population density in Co. Dublin is about 1,600 people per square kilometre, compared with about 25 in Mayo.
Young, educated workers move into the core, which gives it a young population.
Young adults leave the West for jobs and college, which leaves an ageing population and falling numbers in rural areas.
As a result the West has small, scattered settlements and fewer services, while Dublin has dense settlement.
Difference 3 – Infrastructure and access:
The core has dense, well-developed infrastructure, including motorways, Dublin Airport and Dublin Port.
The West is distant from the main markets and ports, so access to markets and services is poor.
The peripheral West has difficult physical conditions such as mountains, high rainfall and poor soils.
Dublin is accessible and has a large market and labour force, which attracts firms and keeps the core growing.
Dublin has universities and the seat of government, while the West has relied on EU funds and State agencies to invest in roads and broadband.

PART 7C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Developing economy – India:
India, a developing economy in South Asia, was ruled by Britain, first through the East India Company and from 1858 directly through the Raj, until independence on 15 August 1947.
Challenge 1 – Deindustrialisation:
Under British rule India changed from a major manufacturer to a supplier of raw materials.
India's large handloom textile industry, including Bengal's fine cottons and Dhaka muslin, was destroyed by cheap machine-made cloth from the Lancashire mills.
Raw cotton was exported to feed those mills, so India lost jobs and the income from making goods.
After independence India had to rebuild industry through state-led planning, with steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur, and growth stayed slow under the Licence Raj.
Challenge 2 – Cash crops, taxation and famine:
Food crops were replaced by cash crops such as cotton, jute, indigo and opium, and by tea on plantations in Assam, all grown for the colonial economy.
The Permanent Settlement of Bengal in 1793 collected land revenue through zamindars, who passed the tax burden on to the peasants.
Heavy land taxes left the peasantry in debt and poverty.
Famines followed, including the Great Famine of 1876 to 1878 and the Bengal famine of 1943, which killed about 3 million people while grain was still being exported.
The drain of wealth, as revenue, profits and salaries were sent to Britain, left India with less capital to invest.
India later had to overcome food insecurity through the Green Revolution, and food-grain output rose from about 51 million tonnes in 1950 to 1951 to about 330 million tonnes in 2022 to 2023.
Challenge 3 – Colonial infrastructure and cities:
Railways, the first running from Bombay to Thane in 1853, were built mainly to carry raw materials to the ports of Bombay and Calcutta and to move troops.
Development was concentrated on the coast around port cities such as Bombay and Calcutta, so the interior was less developed.
Gaps in infrastructure and pollution remain serious problems today.
Challenge 4 – Political and social legacies:
At independence literacy was only about 18% and life expectancy about 32 years, because education had reached only a small elite.
Partition on 15 August 1947 displaced more than 10 million people and hundreds of thousands were killed in the violence.
The Kashmir region has been disputed with Pakistan since 1947.
A positive legacy is English, which later helped the IT industry in Bengaluru, along with the inherited courts, civil service and parliamentary system.
