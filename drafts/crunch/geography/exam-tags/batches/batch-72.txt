MODEL ANSWER: Developed-world example – Dublin:
Dublin is a developed-world city that faces both of the issues below.
Issue 1 – Housing supply and affordability:
Demand for homes in developed-world cities rises with population growth, smaller households and migration.
In successful cities such as Dublin, the supply of homes has not kept pace with this demand.
High rents and house prices put homes beyond the reach of many households.
Homelessness is a visible result, with over 15,000 people in emergency accommodation in 2025.
Housing for All, launched in 2021, aims for about 33,000 new homes a year on average to 2030.
Rent Pressure Zones, introduced from 2016, limit rent increases in the areas where they apply.
Higher-density apartments near transport use land and transport more efficiently.
Public and cost-rental housing provide homes at below-market rents.
Issue 2 – Transport and sustainable mobility:
Car dependence produces congestion and vehicle emissions in growing cities.
Dublin plans MetroLink and BusConnects to move more people by public transport.
The Greater Dublin Area Transport Strategy 2022–2042 is the long-term transport plan for the region.
Copenhagen is a cycling city, where cycling is the most common way of commuting to work or study.
London's Ultra Low Emission Zone began in 2019 and was extended London-wide in August 2023.
Paris is promoting the 15-minute city, where everyday needs can be met within 15 minutes on foot or by bike.
Vauban in Freiburg, Germany, is a low-car eco-district built from the late 1990s on a former military barracks.

North of Northing 21 settlement is dense, with the large town of Clonmel and many farmhouses and villages.
Variation 2 – Sparse settlement south of Northing 21:
South of Northing 21 settlement is sparse, with almost no buildings on the high ground.
Map evidence:
Clonmel's built-up area around S 203 225 is the largest settlement on the map.
Many black dots show dispersed farmhouses around Rathronan at S 203 256.

Examination – Reasons for dense settlement in the north:
The land north of Northing 21 is low and gently sloping, so houses can be built almost anywhere.
Fertile, well-drained soils in the Suir valley support farming, so farmers live on their own holdings and farmhouses are dispersed.
Irish farms are family-owned, and each family lives on its own farm.
Clonmel grew at a crossing of the River Suir, where roads meet, so it is a route focus that attracts settlement.
Roads radiate north from Clonmel through places such as Rathduff at S 189 262.
Villages such as Doon at S 216 257 and Newchapel at S 162 266 are clustered where roads meet and services gather.
Houses are strung in lines along the roads leading out of Clonmel, because roadside sites are cheaper and close to services.
Cars make commuting from the countryside to Clonmel practical, so people build homes near the town.
Good transport on the N24 and its bypass north of the town attracts more housing and services.
Examination – Reasons for sparse settlement in the south:
South of Northing 21 the land rises steeply to Long Hill at 404 metres and Lachtnafrankee at 520 metres.
Contour lines are close together, showing steep slopes that are unsuitable for building and farming.
Upland soils are thin, poor and exposed, so only rough grazing is possible.
Forestry plantations and woodland take up land that could hold houses.
Settlement is confined to the lower ground and a few roads, such as near Barravakeen at S 246 198 and Glendaw at S 254 205.
It is costly to provide roads, water and electricity to scattered houses on steep ground.

PART 13 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Empowering people means giving them control over resources such as land, credit and income, and where ownership of land is widely shared, economic growth is turned into human development in health, education and choice, as the land systems of Ireland, Latin America, Africa, East Asia and Kerala show.
Aspect 1 – Land ownership and empowerment in Ireland:
In rural societies land is the main source of food, income and security, so whoever owns the land decides who benefits from growth.
In nineteenth-century Ireland a small, mainly Protestant landlord class, many of them absentee, owned most of the land, and tenants-at-will had no security and could be evicted.
Landlords charged rack-rents and holdings were subdivided into tiny plots, so tenants had little incentive to invest and lived in poverty.
Dependence on the potato left families exposed, and the Great Famine of 1845–49 killed about 1 million people and caused about 1 million to emigrate.
The Land League, founded in 1879 by Michael Davitt and Charles Stewart Parnell, demanded fair rent, fixity of tenure and free sale.
Gladstone's Land Act of 1881 granted these demands and set up the Land Commission.
The Ashbourne Act of 1885 and the Wyndham Land Act of 1903 let tenants buy their holdings with government loans repaid by annuities.
By the early twentieth century most farmers owned their land, which gave them security, income and political power, and so empowered them.
The Congested Districts Board, founded in 1891, improved farms, fishing and cottage industries in poor western areas, yet many farms stayed small and the west stayed poor, which shows that ownership alone is not enough.
Aspect 2 – Unequal land ownership in the developing world:
Land in Latin America is concentrated in latifundia, which are large estates owned by a few people, while most farmers work tiny minifundia.
Brazil has one of the most unequal patterns of land ownership in the world, so most rural families have too little land to escape poverty.
The Landless Workers' Movement, founded in 1984, empowers landless families by organising them to claim land.
South Africa's Natives Land Act of 1913 limited Black South Africans to owning land in reserves of about 7% of the country, later about 13%.
This denied the majority access to credit and farm income, so unequal ownership held back their human development.
Post-apartheid land reform since 1994 has transferred land slowly, so the inequality persists.
Kenya's colonial White Highlands reserved the most fertile highland for European settlers.
Zimbabwe's land seizures from 2000 cut farm output, and hyperinflation peaked in 2008, which shows that badly managed reform can damage development.
Women make up about 43% of the agricultural labour force in developing countries but fewer than one in five agricultural landholders worldwide.

Aspect 3 – Land reform that links growth to development:
South Korea's land reform began in 1950 and gave tenant farmers ownership of the land they worked.
Taiwan's land to the tiller programme from 1953 did the same.
Both reforms raised rural incomes before industrialisation, which gave families money to spend on food, schooling and health.
Kerala abolished landlord tenancy through land reform implemented from 1970.
Kerala has literacy of about 94% against about 62% in Bihar in Census 2011, which is a high level of human development.
Kerala's total fertility rate is about 1.8 against about 3.0 in Bihar, which shows how education and empowerment go together.
Co-operatives such as Amul, founded in 1946 at Anand in Gujarat, give small farmers ownership and a share of the benefits, and Operation Flood made India the world's largest milk producer.
Where land is held by an elite, growth is captured by a small group, but where it is widely shared, growth reaches ordinary families and becomes human development.
Land grabbing, where foreign companies buy or lease large areas of farmland in poor countries, takes land that local people depend on for food and income.
