QUESTION geography-pp-2025-higher-q122 (LC Geography Higher 2025 — Part Two — Q22, 80 marks)

PART "22" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Mid-latitude depressions, anticyclones and land and sea breezes each form in a different way and each has a distinct impact on weather patterns.
Aspect 1 – Mid-latitude depressions:
A depression is a low-pressure system about 1,000 to 3,000 km across, with closed isobars, winds blowing anticlockwise inwards in the northern hemisphere, and warm and cold fronts.
It forms at the polar front, where cold polar maritime air from the north meets warm, moist tropical maritime air from the south-west.
A small wave develops on the front, often where the jet stream above is diverging and removing air aloft.
Warm air pushes over the cold air and rises, so surface pressure falls and air is drawn in and turned anticlockwise by the Coriolis force.
A warm front forms ahead of the warm air and a cold front behind it, with the warm sector between them, and the system deepens as latent heat is released.
The cold front moves faster and catches up with the warm front, lifting the warm sector off the ground to form an occluded front, and the depression then fills and dies.
Ahead of the warm front the cloud thickens to nimbostratus and a long period of steady rain falls.
When the cold front passes, cumulonimbus clouds bring short, heavy showers, the temperature falls sharply and the wind veers to westerly or north-westerly.
Because depressions cross Ireland regularly, Irish weather is changeable with rain in every month, and the west is wetter than the east.
Storm Éowyn on 24 January 2025 is an example of a strong depression, as Met Éireann issued Red wind warnings.
Aspect 2 – Anticyclones:
An anticyclone is an area of high pressure with widely spaced, closed isobars, light winds blowing clockwise outwards in the northern hemisphere, and no fronts.
It forms when air converging high in the atmosphere sinks, a process called subsidence.
As the air sinks it is compressed and warms, so its relative humidity falls and cloud evaporates.
Dynamic highs form from sinking air in the general circulation, and the Azores High often extends a ridge over Ireland in summer.
Thermal highs form when the ground cools intensely in winter, as with the Siberian High over Central Asia.
In summer an anticyclone brings clear skies, strong sunshine, high daytime temperatures and light winds, and a prolonged high causes heatwaves and drought.
In winter clear nights let the ground lose heat rapidly, causing hard frost and radiation fog in valleys.
A temperature inversion often forms in the sinking air, trapping moisture and pollution beneath it, which gives anticyclonic gloom or smog.
A blocking anticyclone stays in place and diverts depressions north or south, giving a long spell of dry weather.
In late February 2018 a block of high pressure over Scandinavia drew bitterly cold polar continental air from Russia over Ireland in the Beast from the East, and Storm Emma then brought heavy snow on 1 and 2 March.
Aspect 3 – Land and sea breezes:
By day the land heats faster than the sea under sunshine, so air over the land warms, expands and rises, creating lower pressure over the land.
The cooler sea has relatively higher pressure, so cool air flows onshore from the sea as a sea breeze.
The air that rose over the land returns seaward at height and sinks over the sea, completing a circulation cell.
After sunset the land loses heat faster than the sea, so air over the land cools and becomes denser, creating higher pressure over the land and lower pressure over the warmer sea.
Air then flows offshore from the land to the sea as a land breeze, which is weaker than the sea breeze because the night-time temperature difference is smaller.
Sea breezes lower coastal temperatures in the afternoon and raise humidity.
Sea breezes are strongest on hot, calm, sunny summer afternoons, often under an anticyclone, and in the tropics they may blow most days.
A sea-breeze front can trigger a line of cumulus cloud or showers inland, and sea breezes can carry sea fog onshore.
Land breezes help to clear coastal fog.
Sailing fishermen traditionally went out at dawn with the land breeze and came back in the afternoon with the sea breeze.

QUESTION geography-pp-2025-higher-q123 (LC Geography Higher 2025 — Part Two — Q23, 80 marks)

PART "23" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Atmosphere-ocean phenomena such as temperature, pressure, wind, humidity and ocean conditions are measured with a range of instruments, and these measurements are important for forecasting, safety, the economy and climate science.
Aspect 1 – Measuring temperature and pressure:
Air temperature is measured with a liquid-in-glass thermometer, in which mercury or alcohol expands and rises up a narrow tube as it warms.
A Six's maximum-minimum thermometer uses small metal indexes that are pushed to the highest and lowest points reached, and it is reset with a magnet after the daily reading.
Thermometers sit in a Stevenson screen about 1.25 m above the ground, which is white, louvred and faces away from direct sun, so the readings are true shade air temperatures.
A mercury barometer measures pressure because the weight of the atmosphere supports a column of mercury in a glass tube, and the column rises as pressure rises.
An aneroid barometer has a sealed, partly emptied metal capsule that is squeezed in as pressure rises, and levers move a needle on a dial.
A barograph links an aneroid to a pen that draws a continuous record on a rotating drum, so the trend of rising or falling pressure can be seen.
Pressure readings are corrected to mean sea level and given in hPa, with an average of about 1013 hPa, so that stations at different heights can be compared.
Aspect 2 – Measuring wind and humidity:
A cup anemometer has three or four cups on arms that spin faster as wind speed rises, and the turns are counted electrically and converted to speed.
The standard height for measuring surface wind is 10 m above open ground, away from buildings and trees.
A wind vane has a large tail pushed downwind, so its pointer faces the direction the wind blows from, and wind is always named from the direction it comes from.
Without instruments, wind speed is estimated using the Beaufort scale.
A wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer measures humidity, as the wet bulb wrapped in wet muslin is cooled by evaporation and reads lower than the dry bulb.
The drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the bigger the difference between the two readings, and relative humidity is read from tables.
A hair hygrometer uses human hair, which lengthens in humid air and shortens in dry air, to move a pointer.
Aspect 3 – Measuring the oceans and upper air:
Moored weather buoys record air pressure, air and sea temperature, wind, wave height and humidity and transmit the readings, as the Irish Marine Data Buoy Observation Network does off the Irish coast.
Argo floats drift at depth, rise periodically to the surface and measure temperature and salinity down to about 2,000 m before transmitting by satellite.
The Argo programme began in 2000, and about 4,000 floats now drift in the world's oceans.
Tide gauges and satellite altimeters measure sea level, and satellites also measure sea-surface temperature and sea ice.
Weather balloons carry radiosondes that measure temperature, humidity and pressure in the upper air and radio them back, and tracking the balloon gives the upper winds.
Balloons are launched on a fixed daily schedule, including from Valentia Observatory in Co. Kerry, which has one of the longest weather records in Ireland.
Geostationary and polar-orbiting weather satellites measure cloud, water vapour and sea-surface temperature, and rainfall radar detects precipitation from the echo returned by raindrops.
Aspect 4 – Importance of the measurements:
Observations are the starting point for computer forecast models, so more and better measurements give better forecasts.
Measurements support storm, rain, snow and heat warnings, including Met Éireann's colour codes, which save lives and property.
A falling barograph trace warns of an approaching depression.
Aviation needs wind, visibility, icing and pressure data, and shipping and fishing need sea area forecasts and wave heights from buoys.
Farmers time silage making, spraying, sowing and harvesting by forecasts, and frost warnings protect crops.
Wind measurements are used to site wind farms and predict their output, and temperature forecasts predict electricity and gas demand.
Long, consistent records such as the Mauna Loa carbon dioxide record, kept since 1958, show trends such as global warming, sea-level rise and ocean warming, and they are the evidence used by the IPCC.
