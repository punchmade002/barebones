PART 16 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Human activity can damage soil through overcropping, overgrazing and poor land management, but it can also protect and improve soil through conservation and careful management.
Aspect 1 – Negative impact of overcropping:
Overcropping is growing crops on the same land too intensively, without fallow, rotation or enough organic matter returned.
Nutrients are removed faster than they are replaced, and the humus content falls.
The crumb structure breaks down, so the soil becomes dusty or compacted and holds less water.
Monoculture takes the same nutrients each year and encourages pests, so yields fall.
In the 1930s the natural grassland of the Great Plains of the USA, including Oklahoma and Kansas, was ploughed up for wheat.
Severe drought then left bare, dry soil that strong winds blew away in huge dust storms, and many farming families were forced to leave in the Dust Bowl.
The US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935, and shelter belts of trees, contour ploughing and crop rotation were promoted.
Aspect 2 – Negative impact of overgrazing:
Overgrazing is keeping more animals on land than its vegetation can support, so the vegetation dies and the soil is exposed to rain-splash and wind.
Trampling compacts the soil, and on wet Irish ground this poaching reduces infiltration and increases runoff.
From the 1980s EU headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in the west of Ireland, such as in Connemara.
Overgrazing there stripped heather and grass, eroded blanket peat and sent silt into rivers, harming salmon and trout spawning grounds.
In the Sahel, the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara, livestock were concentrated around wells, fallow periods shortened and fuelwood was cut, while droughts in the 1970s and 1980s left bare soil to be eroded.
This desertification brought loss of topsoil and humus, falling yields, food shortages and migration to cities and abroad.
Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s through drought and the use of its water for irrigation.
Aspect 3 – Positive impact of soil conservation:
Contour ploughing across a slope makes furrows trap water instead of channelling it downhill.
Terracing cuts steep slopes into level steps, as in the rice terraces of Banaue on Luzon in the Philippines.
Shelter belts and windbreaks of trees and hedges cut wind speed and reduce wind erosion.
Crop rotation, fallow, cover crops and green manure such as clover restore nutrients and humus.
In Niger, farmer-managed natural regeneration, where farmers protect and prune tree shoots that regrow from old stumps, has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s.
In Yatenga in Burkina Faso, farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo revived zaï pits, which catch rainwater and concentrate manure around the seed.
Trees shade the soil, cut wind speed and add leaf litter, and species such as Faidherbia albida also add nitrogen.
Aspect 4 – Positive impact of improving soils:
Liming raises the pH of acid soils, and field drainage and arterial drainage improve gleys.
The Arterial Drainage Act of 1945 began state drainage programmes to deepen and straighten Irish rivers to drain wet farmland.
On the bare limestone of the Aran Islands, islanders built soil by layering sand and seaweed, and later manure, on the rock inside stone-walled fields.
In the Burren, the BurrenLIFE project of 2005 to 2010 and the later Burren Programme pay farmers by results, scoring each field for habitat quality.
Iceland's Soil Conservation Service, founded in 1907, fences off eroding land to keep sheep out and sows lyme grass to stabilise drifting sand.
Farmer-led reclamation projects called Farmers Heal the Land began in Iceland in 1990, and sheep numbers have fallen since their peak in the late 1970s.
Iceland shows both sides, because Norse settlers from about AD 874 cut and burned birch woodland and kept sheep that prevented regrowth, yet conservation has reclaimed land for over a century.

PART 17 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Brown earth is Ireland's main lowland soil, and its composition and characteristics are explained by the mild, wet climate, the glacial till and the deciduous woodland under which it formed.
Aspect 1 – Location and formation of brown earth:
Brown earth is the most widespread soil of the Irish lowlands, especially in the south, east and Midlands.
The fertile grassland and tillage soils of the Golden Vale in Tipperary and Limerick and of the south-east are brown earths.
It formed under Ireland's mild, wet, cool temperate oceanic climate, with rainfall of roughly 750 to 1,000 mm in the east.
The deciduous woodland of oak, ash, elm and hazel that covered most of Ireland after the Ice Age supplied the litter.
The parent material is usually glacial till laid down during the last glaciation, which ended about 10,000 years ago, so the soil is young.
Mild leaching carries some iron and clay down from the A horizon to the B horizon.
Earthworms mix the mull humus into the soil, so no bleached layer forms.
Aspect 2 – Profile of brown earth:
The O horizon is a thin layer of mull humus.
The A horizon is dark brown and loamy, with a crumb structure, and is roughly 20 to 30 cm deep.
The B horizon is lighter brown, with some iron and clay washed in from above.
The boundary between the A and B horizons is gradual, because earthworms mix the soil.
The C horizon is weathered till or rock.
There is no bleached E horizon, because leaching is only mild, unlike in a podzol.
The brown colour comes from humus together with iron compounds.
Aspect 3 – Composition of brown earth:
A good loam such as brown earth has about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water and 25% air by volume.
Mineral matter is particles of weathered rock, which form the soil's skeleton and decide its texture.
The mineral matter releases nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium as it weathers.
Organic matter is living organisms plus dead remains, which decomposers break down into humus.
Mull humus is mild, crumbly and well decomposed, and it binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients and releases nutrients slowly.
Earthworms mix and aerate the soil, and their casts improve its structure.
Water and air share the pore spaces, so the soil drains well and is aerated yet holds enough moisture for plants.
Aspect 4 – Characteristics and uses of brown earth:
The texture is loamy, which combines good drainage with good water and nutrient retention.
The structure is crumb, the best structure for farming, because it opens pores for both air and water.
The soil is slightly acidic, at roughly pH 5.5 to 6.5.
It is fertile because the clay-humus complex holds nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium so that they are not washed away.
It is used for grassland and tillage in the Golden Vale and the south-east.
Farmers spread lime where the pH falls below the Teagasc targets of about 6.3 for grassland and 6.5 for tillage.
Grey-brown podzolics, slightly more leached relatives with clay washed into the B horizon, occur on the limestone-rich till of the east Midlands in Meath and Kildare.

PART 18 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The tropical rainforest, found in the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia, has a hot, wet equatorial climate that shapes its soils and its vegetation.
Aspect 1 – Climate of the tropical rainforest:
Mean monthly temperatures are about 26 to 28 °C all year.
The annual range is only about 2 to 3 °C, because the sun stays high and day length barely changes.
Annual rainfall is over 2,000 mm, and Manaus in the central Amazon receives about 2,300 mm a year.
Rain falls in every month, with no dry season.
Intense morning heating makes moist air rise, cool and build up cumulonimbus clouds.
Heavy afternoon thunderstorms follow, almost daily.
The low-pressure belt of rising air along the equator, the ITCZ, drives this convectional rainfall.
Relative humidity is often over 80%.
A large share of the Amazon's rain is water recycled from the forest by evapotranspiration.
Aspect 2 – Impact of the climate on soils:
The heat and heavy rain cause intense chemical weathering to great depth, so latosols are many metres deep.
Heavy rainfall leaches silica and bases out of the soil in a process called laterisation.
Insoluble iron and aluminium oxides are left behind, which gives the soil its deep red or yellow colour.
The soil is acidic, clay-rich and low in nutrients.
Litter decomposes within weeks in the heat and humidity, so little humus builds up.
Shallow roots and mycorrhizal fungi take up nutrients almost at once and pass them to the trees.
Most nutrients are held in the living vegetation, so the nutrient cycle is rapid and closed.
When the forest is cleared the soil loses fertility within a few years and erodes under heavy rain.
Where cleared soil dries out, the iron-rich layer can harden into a brick-like laterite crust.
Aspect 3 – Impact of the climate on vegetation:
The constant warmth and moisture support a dense, evergreen, broadleaved forest, with leaves shed and replaced all year.
Hundreds of tree species grow in a single hectare of Amazon rainforest, compared with a handful in an Irish wood.
Emergents such as kapok and the Brazil nut tree rise above the canopy at about 45 to 60 m.
The canopy at about 25 to 40 m is a dense, continuous layer that intercepts most sunlight and rain.
Only about 2% of the sunlight reaching the canopy arrives at the dark, humid forest floor.
Large leaves in the understorey capture the little light that reaches it.
Drip tips and waxy leaves shed the heavy rain.
Because the nutrients are held near the surface, trees have shallow roots, and buttress roots support tall trees such as kapok.
Lianas climb trees to reach the light, and epiphytes such as orchids and bromeliads grow on branches and take moisture from the air and rain.
