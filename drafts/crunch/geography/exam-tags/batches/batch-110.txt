PART "3B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Flood control measure – levees on the Mississippi:
Levees and embankments confine the flow to the channel, so the river can no longer spill onto its floodplain.
Impact 1 – Less deposition on the floodplain:
Natural floods deposit fertile alluvium on the floodplain, but levees stop this.
The floodplain receives no new silt, so its natural renewal ends.
Levees stop flood silt reaching the delta, which contributes to the loss of Louisiana's coastal wetlands.
Impact 2 – A raised river bed:
With the water confined, the river deposits its load in the channel, so the bed rises.
The banks must then be raised again, so the cycle continues.
Water held in a narrower channel flows faster, so it erodes the bed and banks more.
Impact 3 – Flood risk downstream:
Floodwater is passed downstream faster, so flood peaks downstream are higher and arrive sooner.
If levees fail, the floods are sudden and severe.
Example – Lower Mississippi, USA:
The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 led to the federal levee and floodway system run by the US Army Corps of Engineers.
The 1993 flood breached many levees along the upper Mississippi and Missouri.
Hurricane Katrina in August 2005 caused levee and floodwall failures in New Orleans, and about 80 per cent of the city flooded.
Cut-offs made by the Corps of Engineers shortened the river, which steepened its gradient and sped up the flow.
Natural levees of coarse material dropped beside the channel in floods have been artificially raised.
Evaluation:
Levees protect homes and farmland, but the problems are moved downstream.
Soft engineering such as washlands and floodplain zoning lets natural flooding and deposition continue.
Room for the River in the Netherlands moves dykes back so that the river can spread out safely.

PART "3C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Role of temperature:
Temperature acts as a trigger, because the expansion and contraction of rock cause stress within it.
Physical weathering depends on changes in temperature, not just on warmth.
Process 1 – Freeze-thaw action:
Freeze-thaw needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock.
Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
At night the water freezes and expands by about 9 per cent, widening the crack.
By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular fragments.
It is most effective on mountains and in periglacial areas, such as the summit of Carrauntoohil in County Kerry.
The result is scree slopes at the foot of cliffs and jagged peaks.
Process 2 – Exfoliation:
Exfoliation, or onion weathering, occurs in hot deserts such as the Sahara, where the daily temperature range is large.
The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day and cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
Stress builds up, and curved sheets peel off like the layers of an onion.
This leaves rounded boulders.
Process 3 – Granular disintegration:
Dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen.
The grains fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
Temperature and climate:
Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, and hot deserts favour exfoliation.
Shady slopes stay frozen longer, so freeze-thaw is stronger on them.

The West region is Counties Galway, Mayo and Roscommon, and it is peripheral, far from Dublin and from European markets.

The main farming in the West is cattle rearing, with suckler herds, and sheep grazing on the hill commonage of Connemara and the Mayo mountains.

Impact on cultural groups:
Boundaries drawn across cultural groups create minorities, displacement and conflict.

The Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998 introduced power-sharing between the two communities.
