PART "18" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: This answer takes the tropical rainforest biome of the Amazon Basin, accounts for its hot, wet equatorial climate and explains how that climate shapes its soils and vegetation.
Aspect 1 – The climate of the Amazon:
The Amazon Basin lies on the equator in South America and covers about 5.5 million km², with roughly 60% of it in Brazil.
Mean monthly temperatures are about 26 to 28 °C all year.
The annual range is only about 2 to 3 °C, because the sun stays high and day length barely changes.
The daily range is greater than the annual range, so there are no seasons in the temperate sense.
Rainfall exceeds 2,000 mm a year and falls in every month, and Manaus receives about 2,300 mm.
The rain is convectional, because intense morning heating makes warm, moist air rise.
The rising air cools and builds cumulonimbus clouds, which give heavy thunderstorms almost every afternoon.
The ITCZ, a low-pressure belt where the trade winds converge, lifts and reinforces the moist air.
Relative humidity is often over 80%, and a large share of the rain is water recycled by evapotranspiration from the forest.
Aspect 2 – Impact of climate on soils:
The soils are latosols, which are deep, red or yellow, acidic and infertile with little humus.
Heat and constant moisture make chemical weathering rapid, so the soils are very deep.
Heavy rainfall leaches silica and other minerals from the soil in a process called laterisation.
Iron and aluminium oxides are left behind, which gives the soil its red colour.
Heat and humidity make litter decompose within weeks, so little humus builds up.
Most nutrients are held in the living vegetation rather than the soil, so the nutrient cycle is rapid and closed.
Shallow roots and mycorrhizal fungi take up nutrients almost at once and pass them to the trees.
The soils are therefore infertile despite the lush forest above them.
If the forest is cleared the nutrient store is removed, and heavy rain washes away soil left bare.
Aspect 3 – Impact of climate on vegetation:
The hot, wet climate with no dry or cold season supports a dense, evergreen, broadleaved forest.
Leaves are shed and replaced all year, so the forest is always green.
Emergent trees such as the kapok and the Brazil nut rise to 45 to 60 m above the canopy.
The canopy at about 25 to 40 m intercepts most of the sunlight and rain.
Only about 2% of the sunlight reaches the dark forest floor, so the shrub layer is sparse.
Buttress roots support the tall trees, which have shallow roots in the nutrient-rich litter layer.
Drip tips and waxy leaves shed the heavy rain quickly.
Lianas climb trees to reach the light, and epiphytes such as orchids and bromeliads grow on branches and take moisture from the air and rain.
Hundreds of tree species grow in a single hectare, but each species is represented by few individuals.

MODEL ANSWER: Minority languages can be helped to survive through official status in law, education and the media, as the examples of Irish, Welsh and Basque show.
Aspect 1 – Official status in law:
A minority language is spoken by a minority of a state's population, and official status in law gives it protection and prestige.
Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution makes Irish the national language and the first official language of Ireland.
The Official Languages Act 2003 gave the public the right to deal with public bodies in Irish.
An Coimisinéir Teanga, set up in 2004, enforces the Act.
The 2021 amendment sets a target that 20% of new public-service recruits be competent in Irish by 2030.
Irish became an official EU language in 2007, with full working-language status from 1 January 2022.
The Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave Welsh official status and led to a Welsh Language Commissioner from 2012.
The Cymraeg 2050 strategy of 2017 sets a target of 1 million Welsh speakers by 2050.
The Gaeltacht Act 2012 created 26 Language Planning Areas, each with its own language plan.
Aspect 2 – Education through the language:
Schools can pass a language on to young people, so education is a key strategy for survival.
Irish is a compulsory subject through primary and post-primary school.
Gaelscoileanna are Irish-medium schools outside the Gaeltacht, and their umbrella body was founded in 1973.
Gaeltacht summer colleges bring learners to live with Irish-speaking families.
Welsh is compulsory in all schools in Wales up to age 16, and Welsh-medium schools are found across Wales.
Urdd Gobaith Cymru, founded in 1922, and the National Eisteddfod promote Welsh among the young.
The Basque Autonomous Community was created by the Statute of Gernika in 1979, and its schools use models A, B or D.
Model D teaches all subjects through Basque with Spanish as a subject, and it is now chosen for most pupils.
Ikastolak are Basque-medium schools that began as a clandestine movement under Franco.
Aspect 3 – Media and broadcasting:
Minority-language speakers consume most media in the majority language, so broadcasting in their own language is needed.
Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972.
TG4 launched on 31 October 1996 and is based in Baile na hAbhann, Conamara.
TG4 broadcasts Irish-language drama, sport and music programmes.
S4C, the Welsh-language television channel, launched on 1 November 1982.

BBC Alba, the Scottish Gaelic television service, launched in 2008.
EITB, the Basque public broadcaster, began television in 1982.
Social media and online content also let small languages reach young people.
