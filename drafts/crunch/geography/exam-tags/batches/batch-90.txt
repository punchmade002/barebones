PART 6B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named region – India, South Asia:
India is a subcontinental region in South Asia, bounded by the Himalayas to the north, the Arabian Sea to the west and the Bay of Bengal to the east, with over 1.4 billion people.
Population distribution is very uneven, and a national density of 382 people per km² in Census 2011 hides huge differences between areas.
Factor 1 – Relief and soils:
The Northern Plains of the Ganges are flat and low and are built of fertile alluvium, so they are the population heartland of India.
Flat land makes farming, building and transport easy, and Bihar on the plain is the densest state at about 1,100 people per km².
The Himalayas are sparsely populated because of cold temperatures, steep slopes and thin soils.
The Deccan Plateau behind the Western Ghats lies in their rain shadow and is much drier, so it carries fewer people than the plains.
Factor 2 – Climate and water:
The south-west monsoon, from June to September, brings most of the year's rain and supports dense farming in wet areas such as Kerala and West Bengal.
The Thar Desert in Rajasthan is the driest region and is sparsely populated because of aridity.
Perennial Himalayan rivers such as the Ganges supply water for irrigation and settlement along the plain.
The forested hills of the north-east are sparsely settled, and Arunachal Pradesh is the least dense state at about 17 people per km².
Factor 3 – Human factors:
A long history of farming and settlement on the Ganges plain has built up very high densities, and Uttar Pradesh has about 200 million people.
The deltas and coastal plains, such as Kerala and West Bengal, are dense because of fertile land and water.
Large cities such as Mumbai, Delhi and Kolkata attract migrants seeking jobs, so density is high around them.
Greater Mumbai had about 12.4 million people in Census 2011.
The Chota Nagpur Plateau is rich in minerals and supports mining and industry, which draw workers to the area.
Transport routes such as Indian Railways and the Golden Quadrilateral link places and attract settlement along them.
About 31% of Indians lived in towns in Census 2011, up from about 17% in 1951, as people move from rural areas to cities.
Government policy and investment in industry and services in the big cities also direct population into them.

PART 6C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Core region – Île-de-France, Paris Basin:
Île-de-France, the region around Paris at the heart of the Paris Basin, is a core region of France with about 12 million people.
It produces about 30% of the GDP of France.
In primary activity, the Beauce south-west of Paris is the granary of France, with large, mechanised cereal farms that often exceed 100 hectares.
Market gardening on the edge of Paris supplies fresh vegetables and flowers to a very large urban market.
Rungis International Market, opened in 1969, is one of the world's largest wholesale fresh-food markets and links farms to the city.
In secondary activity, manufacturing concentrated around Paris because it is the largest market and labour force in France.
Renault has a car plant at Flins and Stellantis has one at Poissy, both west of Paris.
Paris-Saclay is a research and high-technology cluster that combines universities, research institutes and company laboratories.
In tertiary activity, Paris is the centre of government, finance, head offices and international organisations such as the OECD and UNESCO.
La Défense, west of the city, is one of Europe's largest purpose-built business districts.
Tourism is also strong, with the Eiffel Tower, the Louvre and Disneyland Paris, which opened in 1992.
Peripheral region – Mezzogiorno, southern Italy:
The Mezzogiorno, southern Italy and its islands, is a peripheral region that holds about one-third of the population of Italy but produces only about 22% of its GDP.
In primary activity, Puglia is Italy's leading olive-oil region and durum wheat is grown on the Tavoliere di Puglia around Foggia.
Farming is limited by drought and soil erosion, many farms depend on CAP support, and disease has killed millions of olive trees in the Salento.
In secondary activity, industry is weak because of distance from markets, poor infrastructure and a small local market.
The state built large growth poles such as the Taranto steelworks in the 1960s and petrochemical plants at Brindisi.
These plants created few jobs and few local spin-off firms, and they are known as cathedrals in the desert.
Fiat has car plants at Pomigliano d'Arco near Naples and at Melfi in Basilicata, but the Termini Imerese plant in Sicily closed in 2011.
In tertiary activity, tourism at sites such as Pompeii and the Amalfi Coast is important but seasonal, and public-sector and informal employment are large sources of jobs.
Activity in the core is dominated by high-value services and research, while the periphery relies more on farming, tourism and public-sector jobs.
