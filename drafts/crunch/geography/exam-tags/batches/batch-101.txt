PART 5B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – tourism in Kerry:
Tourism is the tertiary activity examined in Co. Kerry in the south-west of Ireland, centred on Killarney, the Ring of Kerry and the Dingle Peninsula.
Tourism is a service activity because it earns income by providing accommodation, food, transport and guiding for visitors rather than by making goods.
Kerry is a mass-tourism region, with Killarney as its main visitor centre.
Factor 1 – Natural and cultural attractions:
Kerry has outstanding scenery, including the Lakes of Killarney, Torc Waterfall and the Macgillycuddy's Reeks, which draws visitors to the region.
Killarney National Park, created in 1932 from the Muckross estate, was Ireland's first national park and protects the lakes, woodland and Muckross House as attractions.
The Ring of Kerry, a circular route of about 180 km around the Iveragh Peninsula, offers coastal and mountain views that support coach tours, car hire and roadside services.
Skellig Michael, an early Christian monastery that has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1996 and appeared in Star Wars films, adds international appeal.
Because the attractions are spread across the county, visitors stay longer, so demand grows for hotels, restaurants, guides and boat trips and tourism becomes a major source of jobs.
Factor 2 – Accessibility and transport:
Accessibility has strongly influenced tourism development because visitors must be able to reach Kerry easily.
The railway reached Killarney in 1853, which opened the town to visitors from Cork and Dublin and began its tourism in the 19th century.
Queen Victoria's visit to Killarney in 1861 publicised the area and increased visitor numbers.
Kerry Airport at Farranfore, together with the international airports at Cork and Shannon, brings overseas visitors within reach of the main resorts.
The Wild Atlantic Way touring route, launched in 2014, steers visitors along Kerry's coast, which increases spending in its small towns and villages.
Sketch map in words:
A labelled sketch map of Kerry would show the Dingle Peninsula and the Iveragh Peninsula reaching into the Atlantic, with the Ring of Kerry route drawn around Iveragh.
It would name Killarney, Tralee, Dingle and Kenmare as the main tourist towns, and mark Killarney National Park, Carrauntoohil and Kerry Airport at Farranfore.

PART 5C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Climate option – cool temperate oceanic region:
Climate defines a region when a large area has similar temperature, precipitation and seasonal pattern, and the cool temperate oceanic region of Ireland and North West Europe is the example examined.
Climate is a physical criterion, so areas that share the same climate form one region that differs from the climates around it.
Characteristics of the climate:
Winters are mild, with mean January temperatures of about 4 to 7°C.
Summers are cool, with mean July temperatures of about 14 to 16°C.
The annual temperature range is small, only about 10°C, and snow is rare on low ground.
Rain falls in every month, so there is no dry season, and the weather is changeable.
Causes of the climate:
The North Atlantic Drift, an extension of the Gulf Stream, keeps winters mild and coasts free of ice.
Prevailing south-westerly winds carry mild, moist air from the Atlantic across Ireland.
Frequent Atlantic depressions and their fronts bring rain throughout the year.
Variation and boundaries:
Within Ireland the west is wetter than the east, with about 1,000 to 1,250 mm of rain a year across much of the west against about 750 mm around Dublin.
Western mountains cause relief rainfall, with over 2,000 mm a year on many of them, while the east lies in a rain shadow.
The same climate covers Britain, Brittany, Belgium, the Netherlands and Denmark, because all face the Atlantic and lie in the path of westerly depressions.
Moving east, winters become colder, summers warmer and rainfall lower, so the boundary of the region is a transition zone rather than a sharp line.
Second example – Mediterranean climate region:
The warm temperate oceanic or Mediterranean region has hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, which makes it clearly different from the Irish region.
Sketch map in words:
A labelled map of western Europe would shade Ireland, Britain, Brittany and the Low Countries as cool temperate oceanic, with the North Atlantic Drift drawn as an arrow flowing north-east towards the coast.
It would also mark the south-westerly winds, the wetter west and drier east of Ireland, and a dashed transition zone leading eastwards into continental Europe.

[geo-region-concept-s1.0.b] Geographers divide the earth into regions so that places can be studied as groups instead of one by one. A region is an area of the earth's surface that shares one or more characteristics, called criteria, which make it different from the areas around it.

[geo-region-concept-s1.0.1] Administrative criteria: the boundaries set by government, such as county lines.
[geo-region-concept-s1.0.5] These five types of region are the five subsections of this chapter, and this subsection deals with physical regions.

[geo-region-concept-s1.1.4] Administrative regions have exact boundaries, fixed by law, so you can say precisely which side of the line a place lies on.
[geo-region-concept-s1.2.b] Climate is the usual pattern of weather in a place over many years. A climatic region is a large area with similar temperature, precipitation (rain, snow and other moisture falling from the sky) and seasonal pattern, usually with similar natural vegetation and soils.

PART 6B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named region and example – Paris Basin car industry:
The Paris Basin in northern France, centred on Île-de-France, is a core European region where manufacturing developed around Paris.
The car industry is the secondary activity examined, with Renault at Flins and PSA, now Stellantis, at Poissy, both west of Paris.
Manufacturing also developed along the Lower Seine between Rouen and Le Havre, based on ports, oil refining and petrochemicals.
Factor 1 – Market and labour:
The Paris region is the largest market in France, so manufacturers locate close to their customers.
It has the largest labour force in France, which supplies workers to factories such as Flins and Poissy.
The head offices and finance of large firms are based in Paris, so factories have capital and management close at hand.
Universities and research institutes provide skills and ideas, and the research cluster at Paris-Saclay supports high-technology firms.
These advantages reinforce each other, because a large market attracts firms, firms attract migrant workers and the growing population enlarges the market.
Factor 2 – Transport and infrastructure:
Paris is the hub of the French road, rail, river and air networks, so firms can move inputs in and products out from one location.
The Seine and the Lower Seine carry bulk goods by water between Paris, Rouen and Le Havre.
Le Havre is France's largest container port, so imported components and exported goods pass through it.
Since 2021 the ports of Le Havre, Rouen and Paris have formed one body, HAROPA, which links the factories of the basin to world markets.
Good transport lowers the cost of getting inputs in and products out, which reinforces the location advantage of the region.

It would label the car plants at Flins and Poissy west of Paris and the Lower Seine industrial area, with the Armorican Massif to the west and the Vosges to the east marking the rim of the basin.

PART 6C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named urban area – São Paulo, Brazil:
São Paulo, in south-east Brazil on a plateau inland from the port of Santos, is the largest city in South America.
It has about 11.5 million people in the city and about 21 million in its metropolitan region (Census 2022).
Factor 1 – Site, coffee wealth and transport:
The city was founded by Jesuit missionaries in 1554 on a plateau above the Tietê river, where the climate is milder than on the coast.
Coffee grown in São Paulo state in the 19th century brought great wealth to the city.
The coffee was exported through the port of Santos, and the Santos–Jundiaí railway, opened in 1867, carried it from the interior across the plateau.
Santos is now Latin America's busiest container port, so São Paulo's trade and industry remain well connected to world markets.
Factor 2 – Industrial growth:
From the 1950s São Paulo became Brazil's industrial heartland.
Car plants, including Volkswagen, opened in the ABC region of Santo André, São Bernardo do Campo and São Caetano do Sul.
These factories created large numbers of jobs that drew workers from rural areas.
Banks, services and company headquarters followed the factories, making the city the economic centre of the country.
The concentration of jobs and investment attracted still more migrants and further growth.
Factor 3 – Migration and immigration:
Immigration from Europe, especially Italy, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries supplied labour for coffee farming and early industry.
Japanese immigration began in 1908, and São Paulo now has the largest Japanese community outside Japan, centred on the Liberdade district.
Drought and poverty in the North-East pushed migrants called nordestinos to the city to work in its industries.
Rural-urban migration from across Brazil continued for decades because wages in the city were higher than in the countryside.
Problems caused by rapid growth:
Rapid growth produced favelas such as Paraisópolis, which borders the wealthy district of Morumbi.
Heliópolis, the city's largest favela, grew on unused land as migrants arrived faster than housing could be built.
Traffic congestion is severe because of the huge number of vehicles on the roads.
Air pollution is a serious health problem in the city.
The Tietê river floods in heavy summer rain.
