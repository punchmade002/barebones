PART 11B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact 1 – Technology cuts death rates:
Medical technology such as vaccines, antibiotics and clean water cut death rates far faster than birth rates fell.
This caused the population explosion of the 20th century, which is stage 2 of the demographic transition model.
Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928, and antibiotics cut deaths from infection.
The World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated in 1980, which removed a major killer of children and lowered death rates.
Named example – India's Green Revolution:
From the mid-1960s high-yield wheat bred by Norman Borlaug was introduced in India under M. S. Swaminathan, with fertiliser and irrigation.
Higher yields in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh ended the famines that had checked population growth, such as the Bengal famine of 1943.
Food-grain output rose from about 51 million tonnes in 1950 to 1951 to about 330 million tonnes in 2022 to 2023.
This raised the carrying capacity, so more people could be fed and India became self-sufficient in food.
Better food supply let India's population rise from about 360 million in 1951 to over 1.4 billion today.
Fewer famine deaths and better child nutrition lowered death rates and so increased population growth.
Impact 2 – Technology lowers birth rates:
Contraceptive technology lowered birth rates once it became available.
In Ireland contraception was legalised by the Health (Family Planning) Act 1979, and the total fertility rate fell from about 4 in the early 1970s to about 1.5 in 2023.
India's total fertility rate fell from about 6 in the 1950s to about 2.0 in 2019 to 2021.
Lower birth rates slow population growth and move a country towards stages 3 and 4 of the demographic transition model.
Lack of technology – the Sahel:
Subsistence farming without modern technology keeps carrying capacity low in the Sahel.
Desertification and drought there cause famine and an overpopulated environment.
Without irrigation, fertiliser or high-yield seeds, food output cannot keep pace with population growth of about 3% a year, so famine raises death rates.

PART 11C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: City named – Dublin:
Dublin shows the zones of a modern European city, with an inner city in transition and an outer city growing along the M50.
Zone 1 – Central business district:
Land values are highest in the CBD because accessibility is greatest, so tall buildings maximise the use of expensive sites.
Grafton Street south of the Liffey and Henry Street north of it are pedestrianised shopping streets with department and chain stores.
Offices, banks and company head offices cluster together in the centre, and few people live there, so it is quiet at night.
Zone 2 – Zone in transition:
The inner-city docklands and north inner city contain older industrial and port land where decay and renewal exist side by side.
Regeneration of the Docklands has brought a return to inner-city apartment living.
Zone 3 – Inner residential zone:
Older terraced housing lies between the centre and the outer suburbs.
The Georgian squares, Merrion Square and Fitzwilliam Square, are now largely offices.
Zone 4 – Suburbs and new towns:
Suburbs have lower-density, mainly semi-detached housing that was built later and further from the centre.
The western new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown were planned in the Wright Report of 1967.
Suburban shopping centres such as The Square, Tallaght and Dundrum Town Centre lie beside the M50 for car access, free parking and cheaper land.
Zone 5 – Industrial and transport land:
Industry moved out of the inner city to the edge because land is cheaper and road access is better, as when Jacob's moved from Bishop Street to Tallaght in the 1970s.
Business and industrial parks such as Grange Castle, Citywest and Sandyford lie near the M50 and the N7.
Dublin Port and Dublin Airport are major transport and industrial land uses.
Zone 6 – Open space:
Phoenix Park, about 700 hectares and established in 1662, is one of Europe's largest enclosed city parks.
The Dublin Mountains and Dublin Bay are further open spaces at the edge of the city.

Mumbai in India grew from seven islands joined by reclamation into India's financial capital, with a Greater Mumbai urban area of about 18.4 million people in Census 2011.
Migrants from states such as Bihar and Uttar Pradesh arrive seeking jobs, so growth is rapid and creates problems.
Problem 1 – Housing and slums:
Land and housing costs are very high on the narrow peninsula, which limits the space available for housing.
Poor migrants cannot afford formal housing, so they build slums.
Dharavi is one of Asia's largest slums, with many small home-based industries such as recycling, leather and pottery.
Water supply and sanitation cannot keep pace with the dense population, which spreads disease.
The Slum Rehabilitation Authority set up in 1995 rehouses slum dwellers, but its flats are criticised for poor design and for breaking up Dharavi's industries.
Problem 2 – Transport and congestion:
The suburban railway carries several million passengers a day and its trains are overcrowded.
Road congestion adds to the pressure on the transport network.
Mumbai Metro Line 1 opened in 2014 and the Atal Setu sea bridge to Navi Mumbai opened in January 2024 to ease the pressure.
Problem 3 – Flooding:
On 26 July 2005 about 944 mm of rain fell in one day at Santacruz and flooded the city.
Dense building on reclaimed land and in slums makes the city vulnerable to flooding.
Problem 4 – Economic decline:
The Great Bombay textile strike of 1982 led to the closure of most cotton mills, causing job losses.
Mill land was later redeveloped, so the city's industrial base changed.
Navi Mumbai, a planned twin city begun in the 1970s, was designed to take population and industry from the crowded core.
