QUESTION geography-pp-2019-higher-q119 (LC Geography Higher 2019 — Part Two — Q19, 80 marks)

PART "19" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Identity in Switzerland is layered across canton, language, religion and nation, and Switzerland is a Willensnation, a nation formed by the will of its people rather than by one language or ethnicity.
Aspect 1 – Nationality and shared history:
The Federal Charter of 1 August 1291 was an alliance of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden, and 1 August is the national day, marked with bonfires and fireworks.
The Congress of Vienna in 1815 recognised Switzerland's permanent neutrality and fixed its borders.
The federal constitution of 1848, which followed the Sonderbund civil war of November 1847, created the modern federal state, which now has 26 cantons.
The cantons have wide powers over schools, police, taxes and churches, so diversity is managed locally.
Direct democracy gives citizens referendums several times a year, and a popular initiative needs 100,000 signatures.
The Federal Council has seven members from the main parties who govern collectively, and the language regions are represented by custom.
No language group wants to join its neighbouring country, because French, Italian and German speakers each identify with Switzerland.
Switzerland is not in the EU, as voters rejected the European Economic Area on 6 December 1992 by about 50.3%, which marks a boundary of identity between Switzerland and the EU.
Geneva, where Henri Dunant founded the Red Cross in 1863 and where the UN has its European office, gives Switzerland an international identity.
Aspect 2 – Language:
The main languages are German (about 62%), French (about 23%), Italian (about 8%) and Romansh (about 0.5%).
There are 17 German-speaking cantons, 4 French-speaking cantons, 1 Italian-speaking canton (Ticino), 3 bilingual cantons and 1 trilingual canton, Graubünden.
Under the territoriality principle each canton or commune has its own official language or languages, used in its schools and administration.
German-speaking Swiss speak Swiss German dialects in daily life but write Standard German.
Romansh became a national language by referendum in 1938 and a partly official language in 1996, and the written standard Rumantsch Grischun was created in 1982.
The Alps isolated valleys, which helped preserve local dialects and Romansh.
The Röstigraben is the cultural divide between German- and French-speaking Switzerland, often visible in referendum results, and it runs roughly along the River Sarine.
The public broadcaster SRG SSR runs separate services in German, French, Italian and Romansh.
The canton of Jura was created on 1 January 1979 when its French-speaking, mainly Catholic districts voted to leave German-speaking, mainly Protestant Bern, so language and religion together shaped a new boundary.
Aspect 3 – Religion:
Ulrich Zwingli led the Reformation in Zurich from 1519, and John Calvin led it in Geneva from the 1530s and 1540s, so Geneva became known as the Protestant Rome.
Zurich, Bern, Basel, Geneva, Vaud and Neuchâtel were historically Protestant.
The central cantons, Fribourg, Valais, Ticino and Jura were historically Catholic.
The Sonderbund War of November 1847 was a short civil war in which a league of Catholic, conservative cantons was defeated.
Religion cuts across language, because both German- and French-speaking Switzerland contain Protestant and Catholic cantons, which reduces conflict between the language groups.
In 2022 about a third of people were Roman Catholic, about a fifth were Reformed and about a third had no religion.
Muslims are about 6% of the population, and most have roots in former Yugoslavia or Turkey.
A popular initiative banning the building of new minarets passed with about 57.5% on 29 November 2009.
A ban on face coverings in public passed in March 2021 with about 51% and came into force on 1 January 2025.

QUESTION geography-pp-2019-higher-q120 (LC Geography Higher 2019 — Part Two — Q20, 80 marks)

PART "20" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Conflict arises when a state's boundaries or political structures ignore, dominate or suppress a cultural group, as shown by Northern Ireland, Spain and the Kurds.
Aspect 1 – Northern Ireland:
The Government of Ireland Act 1920 led to partition, and Northern Ireland was established in 1921 with six counties and a Protestant, unionist majority.
The state was created around a unionist majority with a large nationalist minority.
Catholics faced discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting, including gerrymandering in Derry.
The civil rights movement began in 1968, British troops were deployed in 1969 and internment without trial was introduced in 1971.
On Bloody Sunday, 30 January 1972, British paratroopers shot dead 13 civil rights marchers in Derry.
The Troubles, from the late 1960s to 1998, killed over 3,500 people.
In the 1981 hunger strikes ten republican prisoners, including Bobby Sands, died.
The Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998 redesigned the political structure with a power-sharing Assembly and Executive, North-South bodies and the consent principle.
Peace walls separate Catholic and Protestant areas of Belfast, and the first was built in 1969 and some still stand.
Aspect 2 – Basques and Catalans in Spain:
Basques and Catalans have their own languages, autonomy statutes and strong regional identities within Spain.
Basque and Catalan were banned from public life and schools under Franco from 1939 to 1975.
Spain's 1978 constitution created 17 autonomous communities.
The Basque Autonomous Community was created by the Statute of Gernika in 1979 and collects its own taxes.
ETA, founded in 1959, killed over 800 people in its campaign for an independent Basque state.
ETA declared a definitive end to violence in 2011 and dissolved in 2018.
In 2010 Spain's Constitutional Court struck down parts of Catalonia's 2006 autonomy statute, including its description of Catalonia as a nation, which fuelled the independence movement.
On 1 October 2017 an independence referendum was declared illegal by Spain's courts and police tried to stop the voting, and about 90% of those voting backed independence on a turnout of about 43%.
Spain imposed direct rule using Article 155 of its constitution, and nine Catalan leaders were jailed in 2019 and pardoned in 2021.
Aspect 3 – The Kurds:
The Kurds, commonly estimated at 30 million or more, are the largest ethnic group without their own state.
Kurdistan is divided between Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria, and the largest number of Kurds live in south-east Turkey.
The Treaty of Sèvres in 1920 provided for a possible Kurdish state, but the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923 drew the borders of modern Turkey with no Kurdish state.
Turkey long denied a separate Kurdish identity and banned Kurdish in public life until 1991 and in broadcasting until the early 2000s.
The PKK, founded in 1978, began an armed campaign in 1984, and the conflict cost about 40,000 lives.
On 16 March 1988 Saddam Hussein's forces attacked the Kurdish town of Halabja with chemical weapons, killing about 5,000 people.
Iraq's 2005 constitution recognised the Kurdistan Region as autonomous, with its own government and parliament in Erbil.
About 93% voted for independence in a referendum on 25 September 2017, which Iraq's government rejected, and Iraqi forces then retook Kirkuk.
In Syria Kurdish-led forces fought the Islamic State group, including the defence of Kobani in 2014 and 2015.

PART "21" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Migration changes the racial and ethnic make-up of host societies, and its impact on racial patterns shows in new diversity, in segregated settlement and in racism and the policies that respond to it, as seen in Ireland, France, Germany and Britain.
Aspect 1 – Increased diversity in Ireland:
Ireland moved from a mostly white, Catholic society to a diverse one from the 1990s.
In Census 2022 about one in five people, just over 1 million, were born outside Ireland.
About 630,000 people were non-Irish nationals, which is about 12% of the population.
The largest non-Irish groups are Polish, UK, Indian, Romanian, Lithuanian and Brazilian.
Roman Catholics fell to about 69% in 2022 from about 78% in 2016, while those with no religion rose to about 14%.
Census 2022 counted about 82,000 Muslims, about 100,000 Orthodox Christians and about 33,000 Hindus.
The Islamic Cultural Centre of Ireland in Clonskeagh, Dublin, is one of the new places of worship.
Positive outcomes include cultural enrichment through food, music, sport and festivals, and mixed marriages.
Educate Together, founded in 1984, runs multi-denominational schools open to all faiths and none, and the Education (Admission to Schools) Act 2018 removed the baptism barrier.
Aspect 2 – Segregated settlement in Europe:
Segregation is the clustering of migrants in particular areas, either by choice for support, cheap housing and chain migration or because discrimination forces them there.
Post-war immigrant labour came to France from Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia and Portugal, with large-scale Algerian migration after the Algerian War ended in 1962.
France has the largest Muslim population in western Europe.
Many migrants settled in banlieues, which are segregated high-rise estates on the edge of cities with high unemployment.
In October and November 2005 riots began in the Paris banlieue of Clichy-sous-Bois after two teenagers died fleeing police, and they spread across France until a state of emergency was declared.
Germany signed a recruitment agreement with Turkey in 1961, and recruitment of guest workers stopped in 1973, but many stayed and brought their families.
Germany long denied them citizenship, and segregated districts formed, such as Kreuzberg in Berlin.
About 3 million people of Turkish origin now live in Germany.
The Empire Windrush arrived at Tilbury in June 1948 with passengers from the Caribbean, who then faced racism in housing and work in Britain.
Aspect 3 – Racism, tension and government response:
Competition for jobs, housing and services, and visible difference such as skin colour, make minorities targets of prejudice.
Racism is prejudice or discrimination based on race or ethnicity, and xenophobia is fear or hostility towards foreigners.
The Notting Hill riots took place in London in 1958.
The Race Relations Act 1965 was the first UK law against racial discrimination.
The Windrush scandal of 2018 saw long-settled Caribbean-born residents wrongly detained, denied services or deported because they lacked documents.
The Dublin riot of 23 November 2023 followed a knife attack on children outside a school on Parnell Square, and buses, a Luas tram and shops were burned and looted.
The National Front was founded in France in 1972 and renamed the National Rally in 2018, and Germany's anti-immigration AfD, founded in 2013, grew after the 2015 refugee arrivals.
The Employment Equality Act 1998 and the Equal Status Act 2000 prohibit discrimination in Ireland on nine grounds, including race and religion.
France follows an assimilation model that treats all citizens alike, whereas Germany reformed its citizenship law in 2000 and again in 2024.
