QUESTION geography-pp-2020-higher-q119 (LC Geography Higher 2020 — Part Two — Q19, 80 marks)

PART "19" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Minority languages such as Irish, Welsh and Basque survive when the state, schools, media and communities act together to support them.
Aspect 1 – Official status and legislation:
Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution makes Irish the national language and the first official language.
The Official Languages Act 2003 gives the public the right to deal with public bodies in Irish, and the Language Commissioner set up in 2004 enforces it.
Irish became an official EU language in 2007, with full working-language status from 1 January 2022.
In Wales the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave Welsh official status and led to a Welsh Language Commissioner from 2012.
The Welsh Language Act 1993 gave Welsh and English equal treatment in the public sector.
The Statute of Gernika of 1979 made Basque co-official with Spanish in the Basque Autonomous Community.
By contrast, France signed the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 1999 but has not ratified it, and Breton has declined sharply.
Aspect 2 – Education through the minority language:
Irish is a compulsory subject through primary and post-primary school.
Gaelscoileanna are Irish-medium schools outside the Gaeltacht, and their umbrella body was founded in 1973.
Gaeltacht summer colleges bring learners to live with Irish-speaking families.
Welsh is compulsory in all schools in Wales up to age 16, and Welsh-medium schools are found across Wales.
Basque ikastolak began as a clandestine movement under Franco, and school model D, taught all through Basque, is now chosen for most pupils.
Diwan immersion schools for Breton, the first of which opened in 1977, are run as a voluntary association rather than by the state.
Use of Basque is rising among the young, which shows that schooling can reverse decline.
Aspect 3 – Media and cultural events:
Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972.
TG4 launched on 31 October 1996 and is based in Baile na hAbhann in Conamara.
S4C, the Welsh-language television channel, launched on 1 November 1982 after a campaign for it.
EITB, the Basque public broadcaster, began television in 1982.
Social media and online content let small languages reach young people.
Seachtain na Gaeilge is an annual Irish-language festival around St Patrick's Day, and the National Eisteddfod of Wales is held entirely in Welsh.
Youth movements such as Urdd Gobaith Cymru, founded in 1922, promote Welsh among the young.
Aspect 4 – Community, jobs and planning:
Údarás na Gaeltachta, set up in 1980, grant-aids firms in the Gaeltacht such as those on the Gaoth Dobhair industrial estate, so that jobs keep Irish speakers at home.
The Gaeltacht Act 2012 created 26 Gaeltacht Language Planning Areas, each with its own language plan.
The 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language 2010–2030 aims to raise the number of daily speakers to 250,000.
The Comprehensive Linguistic Study of 2007 found that Irish is viable as a community language only where at least 67% speak it daily, which shows why use in the home and community matters.
In Wales the Cymraeg 2050 strategy of 2017 sets a target of 1 million Welsh speakers by 2050.
Second homes push up house prices in Welsh-speaking areas, so from April 2023 Welsh councils may charge up to a 300% council-tax premium on second homes.
Possible future measures, such as language conditions for housing in the Gaeltacht or more online content in Irish, could further support survival.

PART "20" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Identity in Wales is shaped by a variety of cultural factors working together, namely its history and boundary, its language, its religion and its music, arts and festivals.
Named region – Wales:
Wales is a Celtic region on the western side of Great Britain with about 3.1 million people according to Census 2021.
Aspect 1 – History and boundaries:
Offa's Dyke is an eighth-century earthwork along much of the Wales–England border.
Edward I conquered Wales in 1282–83, and Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the last native Prince of Wales, was killed in 1282.
Edward I built castles including Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech and Beaumaris to hold the conquered land.
The Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 annexed Wales to England, and the border has remained unchanged since.
In the devolution referendum of 18 September 1997 about 50.3% voted Yes.
The National Assembly for Wales opened in 1999 and was renamed the Senedd in 2020, giving Wales its own parliament.
The boundary is unchanged but Wales's political powers have grown, which strengthens its distinct identity.
Aspect 2 – Language:
Census 2021 recorded about 538,000 people, or 17.8%, who could speak Welsh.
Welsh speaking is highest in Gwynedd, at about 64%, and on Anglesey, where it is over half, in the north-west heartland known as Y Fro Gymraeg.
The industrial south-east is mainly English-speaking, because the 19th-century coal and iron industries drew English and Irish workers into south Wales.
The 1847 government reports on education in Wales, known as the Treachery of the Blue Books, blamed the Welsh language for the people's backwardness.
Cymdeithas yr Iaith Gymraeg was founded in 1962 and used civil disobedience, such as painting over English-only road signs.
S4C, the Welsh-language television channel, launched in 1982 after a campaign by language activists.
The Cymraeg 2050 strategy of 2017 sets a target of 1 million Welsh speakers by 2050.
Aspect 3 – Religion and the chapels:
The 1851 religious census showed that most worshippers in Wales attended Nonconformist chapels, such as Methodist, Baptist and Independent, rather than the Anglican church.
Chapels were centres of Welsh-language education.
Chapels were also centres of singing and community life.
Religion therefore reinforced the Welsh language and Welsh identity.
The Church in Wales was disestablished in 1920.
Chapel-going has declined since, so religion is now a weaker marker of identity.
Newer immigrant communities live in Cardiff, so the cultural mix of Wales is now broader than the chapel tradition.
Aspect 4 – Music, art and festivals:
The National Eisteddfod of Wales is a week-long festival of poetry, music and literature held entirely in Welsh, alternating between north and south Wales.
The Gorsedd of Bards holds ceremonies at the Eisteddfod, including the chairing of the bard.
The Urdd Gobaith Cymru, the Welsh youth movement of 1922, holds its own Eisteddfod, one of Europe's largest youth festivals.
Male voice choirs grew in the mining valleys.
The harp is a traditional Welsh instrument.
The Welsh Rugby Union was founded in 1881, making rugby a national sport that expresses Welsh identity.
St David's Day on 1 March is the national day, and the red dragon, the daffodil and the leek are national symbols.

PART "21" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Most states contain more than one cultural group because of conquest, boundaries and migration, and states respond by sharing power or by suppressing differences, sometimes leading to conflict.
Aspect 1 – Why states contain several cultural groups:
A nation state is a state whose people largely form one nation, but most states are not pure nation states.
Historical conquest and union brought different peoples into one state, as the UK is made up of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
Boundaries drawn without regard to culture placed groups in the wrong state.
Many African borders are straight lines drawn after the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 that ignore ethnic territories, splitting some groups and joining rival ones.
The Treaty of Lausanne of 1923 drew the borders of modern Turkey with no Kurdish state, so the Kurds are divided between Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria.
Partition created two states in Ireland, so Northern Ireland was built around a unionist majority with a large nationalist minority.
Migration adds new cultural groups, as West Germany signed a recruitment agreement with Turkey on 30 October 1961 and many guest workers settled with their families.
Census 2022 recorded that about 20% of residents in Ireland were born outside Ireland, creating new immigrant communities.
Irish Travellers, about 33,000 people, were recognised as an ethnic minority on 1 March 2017.
Aspect 2 – How states accommodate cultural groups:
Federalism and autonomy give regions control of their own affairs, as in Spain's autonomous communities.
Spain's 1978 constitution created 17 autonomous communities, including the Basque Country and Catalonia, which have their own languages.
The Basque Autonomous Community, under the Statute of Gernika of 1979, and Navarre collect their own taxes.
Basque and Catalan were suppressed under Franco from 1939 to 1975, so autonomy reversed earlier suppression.
Devolution in 1999 created the Scottish Parliament, the National Assembly for Wales and the Northern Ireland Assembly.
The Scottish independence referendum of 18 September 2014 voted about 55% No.
Switzerland has 26 cantons with wide powers over schools, police and taxes and four national languages, German, French, Italian and Romansh.
Canada adopted an official multiculturalism policy in 1971, recognising several cultures within one state.
Quebec's Charter of the French Language of 1977 made French the sole official language of Quebec.
Aspect 3 – Conflict between states and cultural groups:
Conflict arises where a dominant group controls the state, where a group's language and religion are suppressed, or where two groups claim the same territory.
Discrimination against nationalists in Northern Ireland fuelled the civil rights movement from 1968, and the Troubles killed over 3,500 people between the late 1960s and 1998.
The Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998 set up a power-sharing Assembly and Executive for the two cultural groups.
The 2021 census recorded Catholics at about 46% and Protestants and other Christians at about 43% in Northern Ireland, so neither group is overwhelmingly dominant.
ETA, founded in 1959, killed over 800 people in its campaign for an independent Basque state before it dissolved in 2018.
In Catalonia an independence referendum on 1 October 2017 was declared illegal by Spain's courts, and Spain imposed direct rule under Article 155 of its constitution.
Turkey denied a separate Kurdish identity and banned the Kurdish language in public life until the early 1990s.
The PKK began an armed campaign in 1984 that cost about 40,000 lives.
Since 2017 an estimated one million or more Uyghurs and other Muslims have been held in detention camps in Xinjiang in China.
