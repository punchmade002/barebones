PART "2B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option (i) – Process of erosion – hydraulic action:
Hydraulic action is the force of moving water that dislodges loose material and forces air into cracks in the bed, widening them until rock breaks away.
Landform named – waterfall:
The landform is a waterfall in the upper course of a river, with its plunge pool and gorge.
Example – Powerscourt Waterfall:
Powerscourt Waterfall on the River Dargle, Co. Wicklow, is Ireland's highest waterfall at about 121 m.
Niagara Falls on the USA-Canada border is another example, with a resistant cap of dolomite over weaker shale.
Role of erosion in formation:
A waterfall forms where a band of resistant rock lies over less resistant rock across the river's course.
The river erodes the soft rock below faster than the hard rock above, by hydraulic action and abrasion.
Abrasion occurs as the river's load of stones scrapes and grinds the soft rock at the base.
The soft rock is undercut, leaving an overhang of unsupported hard rock.
Falling water and its load swirl at the base and scour a deep plunge pool by hydraulic action and abrasion.
Pebbles swirled in the plunge pool drill it deeper, which undercuts the overhang further.
The unsupported overhang eventually collapses into the plunge pool.
The waterfall therefore retreats upstream as this process repeats.
Repeated retreat leaves a steep-sided gorge downstream of the falls.
At Niagara the retreat of the falls has cut the Niagara Gorge below them.
Where the rock is limestone, solution adds to erosion by dissolving the rock in slightly acidic water.
Steep gradient in the upper course gives the river high energy, so vertical erosion dominates there.
A cross-section shows a hard rock cap, soft rock beneath, the overhang, the plunge pool and the direction of retreat upstream.

PART "2C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Intrusive features – batholith:
Intrusive activity is magma cooling slowly underground, and the features become landforms only when the rock above is eroded away.
A batholith is a huge dome-shaped mass of magma that cooled slowly deep underground into granite.
The Leinster batholith intruded about 400 million years ago during the Caledonian mountain building period.
It is the largest granite mass in Ireland and runs from south Co. Dublin south-west through Wicklow and Carlow.
Erosion removed the overlying slate and exposed the granite as the rounded Wicklow Mountains, including Lugnaquilla at 925 m.
Heat from the magma altered the surrounding rock into a ring of mica schist called a metamorphic aureole.
The Mourne Mountains in Co. Down are granite intrusions about 56 million years old, with Slieve Donard as the highest peak.
Intrusive features – sill and dyke:
A sill is a horizontal sheet of magma injected between layers of rock, which forms cliffs and ledges when exposed.
Fair Head in Co. Antrim is a dolerite sill with columnar jointing.
A dyke is a vertical sheet of magma that cuts across layers of rock and stands up as a wall-like ridge if it is harder than the surrounding rock.
Extrusive features – lava plateau:
Extrusive activity is magma reaching the surface and cooling quickly as lava, building landforms on the surface.
Basic lava poured out of long fissures about 60 million years ago, as the North Atlantic began to open, and spread widely before cooling.
Repeated flows built up on top of one another to form the high, flat-topped Antrim plateau in Co. Antrim.
Along the Antrim coast, dark basalt lies on top of white chalk, which the basalt protected from erosion.
Extrusive features – columnar jointing:
A thick basalt flow cooled slowly and contracted, forming cracks at about 120° to each other and mainly six-sided columns.
Sea erosion exposed about 40,000 interlocking basalt columns on the north Antrim coast, forming the Giant's Causeway, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986.
Extrusive features – volcanic plug:
A volcanic plug forms when magma solidifies in a volcano's vent and the softer cone around it is eroded away.
Slemish in Co. Antrim is a resistant plug left standing as a steep-sided hill.

PART "3B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sedimentary rock named – limestone:
Limestone is an organic sedimentary rock made of the shells and skeletons of sea creatures.
Carboniferous limestone formed about 360 to 300 million years ago, when Ireland lay near the equator under a warm, shallow, clear sea.
Shells and skeletons of dead sea creatures built up on the sea floor in layers.
The weight of the overlying layers squeezed out water and air and packed the grains together, which is compaction.
Minerals such as calcite, carried by groundwater, bound the grains together, which is cementation.
Compaction and cementation together are called lithification and turn the loose sediment into solid rock.
Limestone floors the central lowland of Ireland, and it often contains fossils and bedding planes.
Sedimentary rock named – Old Red Sandstone:
Old Red Sandstone is a clastic sedimentary rock made of broken fragments of sand and pebbles.
It formed in the Devonian period in hot, semi-arid conditions, from sand and pebbles laid down by rivers.
Its red colour comes from iron oxide.
The sediment came from the weathering and erosion of older rock, and it built up in layers in a subsiding basin.
Compaction and cementation by silica, calcite and iron oxide then turned the sediment into sandstone.
Old Red Sandstone forms the ridges of Munster in the south of Ireland.
Further rock – coal:
Coal is an organic rock formed from plant remains that accumulated in swamps and were compressed over time.
Coal was mined at Arigna in Co. Roscommon and at Castlecomer in Co. Kilkenny.
General features:
Sedimentary rock is formed from sediment laid down in layers called strata, and bedding planes separate one layer from the next.

PART "3C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Prediction of earthquakes:
Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely, so scientists identify where they are likely and estimate how often they occur.
Mapping active faults and plate boundaries shows the areas most at risk, such as the Pacific Ring of Fire.
Studying the history and frequency of past earthquakes helps estimate how often they recur.
Seismic gap theory says a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time has the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next.
Scientists monitor foreshocks, which are smaller tremors before the main shock.
Ground deformation is measured using GPS, tiltmeters and lasers across faults, and radon gas release and changes in well-water levels are also monitored.
Haicheng, China, was evacuated after foreshocks in 1975, hours before a magnitude 7.3 earthquake, saving many lives.
Tangshan, China, in 1976 had no warning and about 240,000 people died, which shows the limits of prediction.
Early warning:
Early-warning systems detect the fast P waves, giving seconds of warning before the destructive surface waves arrive.
Those seconds are enough to stop trains, shut off gas supplies and let people take cover, and Japan has a national system.
Tsunami warning systems use seabed pressure sensors and buoys, and one was set up in the Indian Ocean after 2004, operational from 2006.
Reducing the effects – buildings:
Earthquake-resistant buildings have deep foundations anchored to bedrock and flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames that sway rather than crack.
Base isolation places rubber and steel bearings under a building so the ground moves beneath it.
Tall buildings such as Taipei 101 in Taiwan have a giant tuned mass damper that offsets sway.
Automatic shut-off valves on gas mains and flexible pipe joints reduce fires, which were a major cause of damage in San Francisco in 1906.
Strict building codes and retrofitting of older buildings followed the Kobe earthquake of 1995, where elevated expressway sections toppled and many old wooden houses collapsed.
Reducing the effects – planning:
Land-use planning avoids building on soft ground and fault lines, because soft sediment shakes more and can liquefy.
Emergency drills, education and supplies prepare people, and Japan holds national drills each 1 September.
Tsunami walls and evacuation routes protect coastal communities.
