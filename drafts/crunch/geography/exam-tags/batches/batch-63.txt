QUESTION geography-pp-2021-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2021 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART "14" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sustainable development, which meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, is a model for future human and economic development, as resource use, fair trade and self-reliance show.
Aspect 1 – Resource use:
Sustainable resource use means using renewable resources no faster than they regenerate, which is the sustainable yield, so that future generations still have them.
In the 1970s and 1980s Costa Rica cleared forests for cattle pasture, and by the late 1980s forest covered only about a fifth of the country.
Since 1997 its Payment for Environmental Services programme, run by FONAFIFO, has paid landowners to protect and replant forest.
The programme is paid for largely by a tax on fuel.
Forest cover has risen to over half of the country, and about a quarter of the land is in national parks and other protected areas.
Ecotourism is now one of Costa Rica's largest sources of foreign income, which shows that conservation and economic development can work together.
Almost all of Costa Rica's electricity comes from renewable sources, mainly hydroelectric, geothermal and wind.
In Ireland, Bord na Móna has moved from burning peat to renewable energy under its Brown to Green strategy, and peat harvesting ended in January 2021.
Cutaway bogs, such as Lough Boora Discovery Park in Co. Offaly, are being rehabilitated and rewetted, because peatland is valued as a store of carbon.
Aspect 2 – Fair trade:
Small farmers receive a tiny share of the final price of their crops because commodity prices are low and unstable and middlemen take a large share of the value.
Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grow over half of the world's cocoa, mostly exported as beans, so most of the value is added in rich countries.
Fair trade is a trading partnership based on dialogue, transparency and respect that seeks greater equity in international trade.
The Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production, which gives farmers a more stable income.
The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative and spent on projects that the members choose democratically.
Kuapa Kokoo, a Fairtrade cocoa farmers' co-operative in Ghana, was founded in 1993 and is part-owner of Divine Chocolate, so its farmers share in the profits of the brand and not just the price of the beans.
Premium spending in Ghana has included wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
Labour standards ban forced and child labour, and environmental standards restrict pesticides and protect water and forests.
Fair trade is only a tiny share of world trade and it does not change trade rules such as tariff escalation, so its impact on development depends on its scale.
Aspect 3 – Self-reliance:
Self-reliance means that communities and countries meet their own needs with their own resources, skills and decisions, which reduces dependence on aid, loans and foreign companies.
The Green Belt Movement was founded in Kenya in 1977 by Wangari Maathai, and women's groups have planted tens of millions of trees for fuel, soil and income.
Maathai won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004, the first African woman to do so.
Zaï pits and stone lines, used by Yacouba Sawadogo in Yatenga in Burkina Faso, trap water and soil and restore degraded farmland.
Farmer-managed natural regeneration in Niger has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s.
In Tanzania, Julius Nyerere's Arusha Declaration of 1967 set out socialism and self-reliance, known as Ujamaa, with collective farming in villages.
Literacy and school enrolment rose, and Kiswahili was promoted as a uniting national language.
Forced villagisation from 1973 to 1976 moved millions of people, and food and export output fell, so the economy went into crisis and Tanzania agreed an IMF programme in 1986.
Complete self-reliance cuts countries off from trade, investment and technology, so communities still need fair trade rules, infrastructure and some outside support.

QUESTION geography-pp-2021-higher-q115 (LC Geography Higher 2021 — Part Two — Q15, 80 marks)

PART "15" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Views of development and underdevelopment have changed over time as different explanations of poverty and progress have replaced one another.
Aspect 1 – Environmental determinism:
Environmental determinism held that the physical environment, especially climate, decides how far a society can develop.
Temperate climates were said to produce energetic, advanced peoples, while tropical climates were said to produce poor and backward societies.
It was developed by geographers such as Ellen Churchill Semple in 1911 and Ellsworth Huntington in 1915.
It was used to justify colonialism and racial hierarchies by presenting European rule as the natural order.
It ignores history, including colonialism, slavery and unfair trade rules.
Singapore is tropical yet one of the world's richest countries, and Botswana, landlocked and one of the poorest countries at independence in 1966, became upper-middle-income through diamonds and stable government.
Possibilism, associated with Paul Vidal de la Blache, replaced it by arguing that the environment offers possibilities and people choose which to use.
Aspect 2 – Modernisation theory:
In the 1950s and 1960s development meant GNP growth and industrialisation, and modernisation theory held that all societies follow one path from traditional to modern, as Western Europe and North America did.
Walt Rostow set out five stages in 1960, from traditional society through preconditions, take-off and drive to maturity to high mass consumption.
Take-off occurs when investment rises to over 10% of national income and a few leading industries grow fast.
Rostow dated Britain's take-off to the late 18th century.
South Korea, left among the world's poorest countries by the Korean War, industrialised through export-led growth from the 1960s, led by chaebol such as Samsung and Hyundai, and UNCTAD reclassified it as a developed country in 2021.
Ireland's growth is often read as following the stages, with the First Programme for Economic Expansion in 1958, EEC membership in 1973 and the Celtic Tiger from the mid-1990s.
Critics say the model is Eurocentric and linear, because countries can stall or go backwards, as Syria did after civil war began in 2011.
It also assumes that wealth will trickle down from growth to the poor, which often does not happen.
Aspect 3 – Dependency theory:
Dependency theory emerged in Latin America in the 1960s and 1970s and argued that poor countries are poor because of how they were drawn into the world economy and not because of backwardness.
Rich core countries develop by draining wealth from the poor periphery, and Andre Gunder Frank argued in 1966 that Latin America was underdeveloped by its links with the core.
In 1950 Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer argued that the terms of trade of primary-product exporters tend to decline.
Brazil's colonial economy moved through export cycles of brazilwood, sugar, gold, coffee and rubber, each tied to demand in the core.
Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grow over half of the world's cocoa, mostly exported as beans, and tariff escalation discourages them from processing it.
Multinationals send profits home and debt repayments drain income that could fund development.
Critics say dependency theory cannot explain the Asian Tigers and China, which developed through trade with the core, while countries that cut links with the world economy stayed poor.
Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems theory lets countries move between core, semi-periphery and periphery, as China did after its reforms began in 1978.
Aspect 4 – The North–South divide:
The Brandt Report of 1980 divided the rich, industrialised North from the poor South along the Brandt Line, which runs roughly along 30°N and dips south to include Australia and New Zealand.
The World Inequality Report 2022 found that the richest 10% of people own about 76% of global wealth and the poorest half about 2%.
The model is now outdated because many countries of the South, such as Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan and Hong Kong, are high-income or industrial.
China is the world's second-largest economy and India is among the five largest.
The BRIC term was coined in 2001 by Jim O'Neill, South Africa joined in 2011 to form BRICS, and the G20, set up in 1999, gives countries of the South a stronger voice.
In 2016 the World Bank announced that it would stop dividing countries into developing and developed in its main data tables.
Hans Rosling argued in Factfulness in 2018 that the division is out of date, and he replaced it with four income levels because most of the world's people now live in middle-income countries.

PART "16" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Different soil patterns develop around the world because climate, vegetation, parent material and drainage control the soil forming processes, so each major soil can be explained by the process that formed it.
Aspect 1 – Podzolisation and the podzol:
Podzolisation is an intense form of leaching that occurs in a cool, wet climate where precipitation is well above evapotranspiration.
Podzols lie in the boreal coniferous forest of northern Canada, Scandinavia and Siberia, and on Irish uplands such as the granite Wicklow Mountains.
Acid litter from coniferous forest or heather forms acid mor humus and releases organic acids into percolating water.
The acids combine with iron and aluminium, a process called chelation, and carry them down together with bases and humus.
This leaves a bleached, ash-grey, sandy E horizon of quartz.
Iron, aluminium and humus are deposited in the B horizon, which turns dark reddish-brown.
A thin, hard iron pan may form in the B horizon, blocking drainage and roots so that peat may form above it.
The podzol is strongly acidic, at about pH 4 to 4.5, and infertile, so it is used for rough grazing and forestry.
Aspect 2 – Laterisation and the latosol:
Laterisation occurs in hot, wet tropical climates, such as the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia, where temperatures are about 27 °C all year and rainfall exceeds 2,000 mm.
High temperatures and heavy rain cause intense chemical weathering to great depth, so latosols are many metres deep.
Silica and bases are leached out because silica is soluble in warm water under these conditions.
Insoluble iron and aluminium oxides, called sesquioxides, are left behind and concentrated, giving a deep red or yellow colour.
Litter decomposes so quickly that little humus accumulates, and nearly all the nutrients are held in the living vegetation and recycled quickly.
The profile has a thin O layer, a thin leached A horizon, a very deep red B horizon and deep weathered rock below, and the soil is clay-rich, acidic and low in nutrients.
When the forest is cleared the soil loses its fertility within a few years, erodes under heavy rain and can bake into a hard laterite crust.
Extreme laterisation of aluminium-rich rock forms bauxite, which is mined in tropical countries such as Guinea and Jamaica.
Aspect 3 – Calcification and the chernozem:
Calcification occurs in dry continental climates where evapotranspiration roughly equals or exceeds precipitation, as on the steppes of Ukraine and southern Russia, the Prairies of North America and the Pampas of Argentina.
The climate has hot summers, very cold winters and low rainfall of roughly 250 to 500 mm a year.
Rain penetrates only part of the way down, so leaching is weak and calcium carbonate is deposited a short distance down in the B horizon as white lime nodules.
In dry periods capillary action draws water and calcium back up, and deep grass roots return calcium to the surface in litter.
The dense, deep roots of the grassland die back each year and supply large amounts of organic matter, which forms a black, humus-rich A horizon about 1 m deep with a crumb structure.
Burrowing animals, such as susliks on the steppe and prairie dogs in North America, mix the soil and leave filled-in burrows called krotovinas.
The chernozem is neutral to slightly alkaline and very fertile, so it supports some of the world's main wheat and maize lands, and Ukraine has been called the breadbasket of Europe.
Aspect 4 – Gleying and the gley:
Gleying happens in waterlogged soils, such as the impermeable boulder clay in the wet hollows between drumlins in Cavan, Monaghan and Leitrim.
The pores fill with water and air is excluded, so conditions become anaerobic.
Bacteria reduce the iron from its red ferric form to its grey or blue-grey ferrous form, which gives the soil its grey colour.
Where air enters along roots or cracks the iron is re-oxidised and forms orange or rusty mottles.
The gley is wet, sticky and cold, and it is slow to warm and slow to decompose.
Gleys are intrazonal soils, because the local factor of drainage dominates them, unlike the podzol, latosol and chernozem, which are zonal soils set by climate and vegetation.
Wet ground is easily poached, so the grazing season is short and the land is used mainly for grass for cattle, and field drains and arterial drainage are used to improve it.
