PART "1B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Plutonic igneous rock – granite:
Granite is a plutonic igneous rock formed from magma that cools slowly deep in the crust.
Magma forms when rock melts, for example above subduction zones where a sinking plate melts in the hot mantle.
The magma is less dense than the surrounding rock, so it rises and collects in huge chambers in the crust.
It cools very slowly over millions of years, which gives time for large crystals to grow.
Quartz, feldspar and mica crystallise to give a speckled rock with large interlocking crystals.
The magma solidifies as intrusions such as batholiths, which are huge dome-shaped masses, and as dykes and sills.
The Leinster batholith, the granite core of the Wicklow Mountains, formed about 400 million years ago when the Iapetus Ocean closed during Caledonian mountain building.
Granite is seen at the surface only because the rock above it has since been eroded away.
Metamorphic rock – quartzite:
Quartzite is a metamorphic rock formed when sandstone is changed by great heat and pressure without melting.
This is regional metamorphism, which acts over huge areas during plate collision and mountain building.
The closing of the Iapetus Ocean in Caledonian mountain building produced the heat and pressure that altered the rocks of Donegal and Mayo.
Minerals in the sandstone recrystallise while the rock stays solid, so the rock becomes harder and denser.
Quartzite is pale, very hard and glassy, and it resists erosion, so it is left standing as mountains.
Errigal in Co. Donegal is quartzite, as are Croagh Patrick in Co. Mayo and the Twelve Bens in Connemara.
The heat of the Leinster granite also baked the surrounding rock in thermal metamorphism, forming an aureole of mica schist around it.

PART "1C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Description of constructive boundaries:
At constructive boundaries two plates move apart and new crust is formed in the gap.
Convection currents in the mantle carry the plates away from each other.
As the plates separate, pressure drops and the mantle partly melts.
Magma rises into the gap and cools to form new basaltic oceanic crust.
Repeated eruptions build up a mid-ocean ridge, a long underwater mountain chain.
The new crust is added on both sides of the ridge, so the ocean floor widens over time.
Features of constructive boundaries:
A rift valley runs along the crest of the ridge where the plates pull apart.
Frequent shallow earthquakes occur as the plates move and the crust fractures.
Volcanic activity includes fissure eruptions of runny basalt lava.
Transform faults cut across the ridge and offset it in sections.
Where the ridge rises above sea level it forms volcanic islands.
Named example – Mid-Atlantic Ridge:
The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is where the North American and Eurasian plates are moving apart.
Iceland is a volcanic island on the ridge, and the rift can be seen on land at Thingvellir.
Surtsey, a new volcanic island, formed off the south coast of Iceland in an eruption that began in 1963.
Second example – East African Rift:
The East African Rift Valley is where a continent is starting to split, as the crust stretches and a block drops between parallel faults.
Mount Kilimanjaro in Tanzania is a volcano associated with this rift.
The Red Sea, between Africa and the Arabian plate, shows the next stage, where the sea has flooded in and a new ocean has begun.

PART "2B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Answer (ii) – Mass movement process – Slumping:
Slumping is a fast mass movement in which saturated material slides downslope under gravity along a curved slip plane.
The moving mass rotates backward, leaving a crescent-shaped scarp at the top, a back-tilted block and a bulging toe at the foot.
Slumps are common on clay slopes and on clay cliffs undercut by the sea.
Named example – Antrim coast between Larne and Cushendall:
Slumping has repeatedly occurred along the Antrim coast between Larne and Cushendall, and Garron Point is a classic landslip site.
Slumps and mudflows have repeatedly blocked the Antrim Coast Road (A2), so engineers built retaining walls and drainage and fitted rock-catch fences and netting.
Factor 1 – Rock type and structure:
Hard, jointed basalt lies on chalk, which lies on soft Lias clay and other mudstones.
Rainwater soaks easily through the permeable jointed basalt and chalk until it reaches the clay.
Water collects at the junction with the clay, which becomes slippery and plastic and acts as the slip plane.
The rock above then slumps and rotates seaward in rotational slides.
Factor 2 – Water content:
Heavy or prolonged rain is the trigger for most fast movements.
Saturation fills the pores of the material, which adds weight to the slope.
Water also reduces friction between particles and lubricates the slip plane, so the material flows or slides.
A little water adds cohesion, but too much destroys it.
Factor 3 – Undercutting at the base of the slope:
Waves erode the foot of the coastal slope and remove the support that holds the material above.
The undercut slope becomes unstable and fails more easily after rain.
Factor 4 – Gradient and vegetation:
The steeper the slope, the greater the downslope pull of gravity.
Bare slopes have no roots to bind the soil or to absorb water, so they move more easily.

PART "2C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Physical weathering process – Freeze-thaw action:
Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is physical weathering that breaks rock without changing its minerals.
It needs jointed rock, water and temperatures that rise above and fall below 0 degrees Celsius often.
Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
At night the water freezes and expands by about 9 per cent, which widens the crack.
By day the ice thaws and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular fragments of rock.
It is most effective on exposed mountain summits, such as Carrauntoohil in Co. Kerry.
The result is scree slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks.
Chemical weathering process – Carbonation:
Carbonation is chemical weathering that dissolves limestone by changing its minerals.
Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate.
Calcium bicarbonate is soluble, so it is carried away in solution.
Carbonation works along the joints and bedding planes of the rock, which it widens.
Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering such as carbonation.
It operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
It is the process behind karst features such as limestone pavement.

[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.b] Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process. It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock (rock with natural cracks called joints).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.1] 2. At night the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.2] 3. By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.3] 4. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular (sharp-edged) fragments.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.4] It is most effective on mountains in upland Ireland, on high mountains and in periglacial areas (cold areas where the ground is frozen for much of the year). Freeze-thaw is active on Irish mountain summits such as Carrauntoohil, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.5] Result: scree (talus) slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields (areas covered in broken angular rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.0] Exfoliation, or onion weathering, is an insolation process in hot deserts such as the Sahara, where the daily temperature range (the gap between day and night temperature) is large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.1] The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day, and cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.4] Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.b] Rock can also break because the forces on it change, or because growing crystals push it apart. Neither process changes the minerals, so both are physical.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.0] Pressure release (also called unloading or sheeting): when the overlying rock is eroded, the weight pressing on buried rock such as granite is removed, so it expands upward and cracks into sheets parallel to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.2] Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores (tiny holes) and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.b] Plants and animals break rock down in two ways. Their physical action is a form of physical weathering, and the acids they produce cause chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.b] Carbonation is a chemical weathering process. It dissolves limestone, and it works along joints and bedding planes (the flat surfaces between layers of rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.0] Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.1] The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble, so it is carried away in solution (dissolved in water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.2] Word equation: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.2] Oxidation: oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron in the rock to form iron oxide (rust), which gives red, yellow or brown colours and weakens the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.1] Warm, wet climates favour fast chemical weathering because, by Van 't Hoff's rule, the rate of chemical reactions roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.2] Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, and hot deserts favour exfoliation and salt crystallisation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.3] Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.4] Rock type (its mineral composition): limestone is vulnerable to carbonation, granite to hydrolysis, and quartzite (a very hard rock) resists both.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.0] Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults (breaks in the rock where blocks have moved) let water in, so heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.3] Method: name the process and its type, state the temperature condition it needs, explain the stress or rate change it produces, and link this to the result, such as scree from freeze-thaw.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.0] Mass movement (also called mass wasting) is the downslope movement of weathered material, such as regolith (the loose broken rock and soil that covers solid rock), soil, rock, mud, peat or snow, under the influence of gravity.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.3] Processes are classed, so that they can be compared, by speed (slow or fast), by water content (dry or wet) and by type of movement (heave, slide, flow or fall).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.0] In space, it is most intense on steep, wet and unvegetated slopes, because steepness increases the pull downslope while water and bare ground reduce the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.b] The slowest mass movement, moving soil by a few millimetres to centimetres a year. It is too slow to watch, so you recognise it from its effects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.1] On drying or thawing, the particles drop vertically under gravity, so each cycle moves them a little further downslope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.2] Landslides often follow heavy rain, which lubricates the plane so the rock slips more easily, or an earthquake, which shakes it loose.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.b] A slump is a fast slide in which the moving mass turns as it goes, because the surface it slides on is curved rather than flat.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.0] Saturated material, often clay over or under permeable rock (rock that lets water through), slides along a curved (concave) slip plane and rotates backward.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.1] The slump leaves a crescent-shaped scar (scarp) at the top, a back-tilted block and a bulging toe at the foot.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.2] Slumps are common on clay cliffs undercut by the sea, because the sea removes the support at the base, for example on the Antrim coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.b] A bog burst is a fast mass movement of peat, common in the uplands of Ireland. Peat (partly rotted plant matter that builds up in waterlogged ground) on a slope fills with water and then slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.0] Blanket bog (a thick layer of peat covering a hillside) on a slope becomes saturated after prolonged or intense rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.2] A bog burst blocks rivers, kills fish, and destroys roads, bridges and land.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.0] Gradient (slope angle): the steeper the slope, the greater the downslope pull of gravity and the faster the movement. Gentle slopes favour creep; steep slopes favour falls and slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.1] Water content: some water adds cohesion, but saturation fills the pores (the tiny gaps between particles), adds weight, reduces friction and acts as a lubricant, so material flows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.2] Heavy or prolonged rain and snowmelt trigger most fast movements, because they saturate the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.3] Clay becomes slippery and plastic (easily moulded) when wet. Jointed or bedded rock dipping towards the slope slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.4] Permeable rock over impermeable clay or shale (rock that water cannot pass through) concentrates water at the junction, as on the Antrim coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.0] Vegetation: roots bind soil and plants absorb water, so bare slopes move more easily.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.1] Climate and weathering: freeze-thaw loosens rock, heavy seasonal rainfall adds water, and periglacial climates produce solifluction.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.2] Undercutting: erosion by the sea or a river at the base of a slope removes support, so the material above is left unsupported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.3] Loading: extra weight at the top of a slope, from rock, buildings or spoil (waste rock), increases the downslope force.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.5] Human activity also affects slope stability. There is more on this in Human interaction: mass movement.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.b] The Antrim coast is an Irish example of landslides and slumps. Its layers of rock, hard on top of soft, explain why the slopes fail.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.0] Location: the Antrim coast between Larne and Cushendall, along the Antrim Coast Road (A2).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.1] Geology: hard basalt lies on chalk, which lies on soft Lias clay with other mudstones.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.2] Rainwater soaks through the jointed basalt and chalk and saturates the clay, which becomes slippery and plastic.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.3] The rock above slumps and rotates seaward in rotational slides, and clay flows out as mudflows. These have repeatedly blocked the coast road.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.4] Garron Point is a classic landslip site on this coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.10.5] Responses, so that the road stays open: engineers have built retaining walls and drainage, and fitted rock-catch fences and netting to protect the road.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.b] Italy gives two international examples: mudflows after heavy rain at Sarno, and a landslide into a reservoir at Vajont.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.12.0] Sarno and neighbouring towns, Campania, southern Italy, 5-6 May 1998: after prolonged heavy rain, loose volcanic ash and soil on steep limestone slopes became saturated and slid off as fast mudflows into the towns below. About 160 people were killed.
[geo-sea-s1.0.3] Water particles move in circles as a wave passes, so it is the energy that travels, not the water; the water does not travel forward until the wave breaks.
[geo-sea-s1.0.7] Backwash is the water draining back down the beach under gravity, so it can pull material back down with it, which is why the swash and backwash balance matters.
[geo-sea-s1.1.3] Destructive waves are typical of storms; their backwash is stronger than their swash, so they drag material out to sea and erode the beach.
[geo-sea-s1.4.1] Abrasion (corrasion): waves hurl sand, shingle and boulders against the cliff, grinding and undercutting it. It is the most effective process at the cliff base.
[geo-sea-s1.4.3] Solution (corrosion): sea water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone.
[geo-sea-s1.5.0] Above the waves, weathering (the breakdown of rock in place, by freeze-thaw and salt crystallisation) and mass movement (rockfalls, and slumping on soft clay cliffs) wear the cliff back.
[geo-sea-s1.5.3] Rock structure matters: joints, faults (breaks in the rock where it has moved) and bedding (the layers in sedimentary rock) give waves lines of weakness to exploit.
[geo-sea-s1.7.1] 2. The rock above the notch becomes an overhang, which collapses because nothing supports it.
[geo-sea-s1.7.2] 3. Waves remove the debris, and the cliff retreats inland, remaining steep.
[geo-sea-s1.8.3] 4. The stack is undercut at its base and collapses into a low sea stump, which is covered at high tide.
[geo-sea-s1.10.b] Waves do not only erode; they also carry material along the shore. This movement supplies the sediment that builds depositional landforms.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.0] Compression (pushing together) produces folds and reverse or thrust faults. It occurs where plates converge, meaning move towards each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.3] Rock bends into folds when compressed slowly, especially when it is deep, hot and under pressure, because rock in those conditions can flex instead of snapping.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.0.4] Rock breaks, forming faults (fractures along which rock has moved), when stress is sudden or the rock is brittle, because brittle rock cannot bend without cracking.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.b] Folding is the first of the structures of deformation (changes to rock layers caused by stress). It starts with compression, and the folds that result have named parts you must be able to label.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.0] Compression is horizontal pressure that squeezes layers of rock into a shorter distance, so they buckle into folds. This is the short definition to give if asked.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.1] An anticline is an upfold: layers are pushed upwards into an arch. A helps you remember: A for apex.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.2] A syncline is a downfold: layers are pushed downwards into a trough (a U-shaped dip).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.3] The limbs are the sides of a fold; the axis is the line along its crest (the top of an anticline) or along the bottom of a trough.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.1.4] Diagram: label the anticline A and the syncline B.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.1] Asymmetrical fold: one limb is steeper than the other, because the compression is stronger from one side.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.2.2] Overfold: one limb is pushed over the other, because the compression is stronger still.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.3.3] 4. Continued pressure produces overfolds, recumbent folds and nappes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.4.2] Ireland's mountains are old fold mountains, formed by Caledonian folding (north-east to south-west trend: Donegal, Mayo, Wicklow) and Armorican folding (east to west trend: Munster).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.0] Ridges of Old Red Sandstone include Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains, the Knockmealdown Mountains and the Caha Mountains.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.7.1] Valleys of Carboniferous limestone include the Blackwater valley and the Lee valley.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.9.1] Recumbent folds and nappes are characteristic of the Alps, which fits the very strong compression there.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.b] Doming is a different kind of upward bending. Instead of a long ridge of folds, the rock is pushed up into a single rounded shape.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.11.0] Doming pushes rock layers upwards from below into a circular or oval upfold, by compression or by rising magma (molten rock from underground).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.12.0] Rock layers in Kent, Surrey and Sussex were pushed up into a dome, an elongated anticline (an upfold stretched in one direction).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.13.b] Where rock is too brittle to bend, it breaks instead. A break along which the rock moves is a fault, and the way the rock moves is called displacement.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.13.0] A fault is a fracture in the crust along which rocks have moved.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.b] A normal fault is the fault produced when the crust is pulled apart.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.14.2] Diagram: a block sliding down along a fault plane, marked as a normal fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.b] A reverse fault is the opposite of a normal fault. Compression squeezes the crust, so the movement along the fault is reversed.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.0] Compression pushes one block up the fault plane over the other, so the crust is shortened.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.1] A thrust fault is a low-angle reverse fault that can push rocks a long way forward, linked to nappes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.15.2] Diagram: a block pushed up and over the other along a fault plane, marked as a reverse fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.16.1] A transform fault is a tear fault at a plate boundary, so it is where plates slide past each other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.17.0] The shattered rock of the fault zone is eroded into a straight valley, because crushed rock wears away easily.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.b] Ireland has its own tear faults, in Donegal. They show how a fault zone becomes a straight valley.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.18.2] The Gweebarra Fault and the Leannan Fault are two of them; the Gweebarra River valley follows the Gweebarra Fault.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.0] A fault scarp is a steep slope or cliff where one block has been raised above the other.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.2] A graben, or rift valley, is a block that sinks between two parallel normal faults, forming a long, flat-floored valley with steep walls, often holding lakes.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.19.3] Diagram: a fault scarp as a steep cliff between two blocks at different heights.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.20.0] 1. Tension stretches the crust, producing normal faults on either side of a block.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.0] The Rhine rift valley, a graben, lies between the Vosges, France, and the Black Forest, Germany, which are horsts.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.1] The East African Rift Valley includes a graben in which Lake Tanganyika lies.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5.21.2] The Central Lowlands of Scotland are a rift valley between the Highland Boundary Fault and the Southern Uplands Fault.
