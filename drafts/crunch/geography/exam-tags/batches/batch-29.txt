PART "12B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Change 1 – Population size:
The State's population fell to about 2.8 million at Census 1961, the lowest ever recorded, because of heavy emigration in the 1950s.
Net emigration was about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961, mostly to Britain, because of economic stagnation, unemployment and Britain's demand for labour.
The population then rose to about 3.4 million by 1981 and to about 5.15 million in 2022, above 5 million for the first time since 1851.
Change 2 – Migration:
The 1970s were the first decade of net immigration since the Famine, because of returning emigrants and new factories after Ireland joined the EEC in 1973.
Recession in the 1980s brought net emigration of over 200,000 between 1981 and 1991, including many graduates in a brain drain.
The Celtic Tiger brought net immigration from 1996, and after EU enlargement in 2004 Ireland opened its labour market immediately, so about 420,000 non-Irish nationals lived here by 2006.
After the 2008 crash emigration peaked at about 89,000 in the year to April 2013, and net immigration returned in the mid-2010s.
By Census 2022 about 630,000 non-Irish nationals lived in Ireland, about 12% of the population, with Polish people the largest group at about 94,000.
Change 3 – Fertility:
Births peaked at about 74,000 in 1980, and the total fertility rate fell from about 4 in the early 1970s to below replacement level around 1990 and about 1.5 in 2023.
This fall was due to free secondary education from 1967, the removal of the marriage bar in 1973, the legalising of contraception in 1979 and more women in paid work.
Secularisation and the cost of housing and childcare also reduced family size.
Change 4 – Age structure and life expectancy:
Life expectancy rose to about 82 to 83 years because of better health services, vaccination, diet and clean water, and infant mortality is about 3 per 1,000.
In Census 2022 about 776,000 people were aged 65 and over, about 15% of the population and up about 22% since 2016.
The population pyramid has changed from a young, wide-based shape to an ageing one, and average age rose from 37.4 in 2016 to 38.8 in 2022.
Change 5 – Urbanisation and distribution:
Census 1971 was the first in which more people lived in towns than in rural areas, and by 2016 about 63% were urban.
The population has become concentrated in the east, with the Greater Dublin Area holding about 2.1 million people, about 40% of the State.

PART "12C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Dublin and the Greater Dublin Area:
Dublin's growth has spread into the commuter counties of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow, and together with Dublin they hold about 2.1 million people, about 40% of the State.
Impact 1 – Loss of farmland and habitat:
Urban expansion consumes farmland at the edge of the city as fields are rezoned for housing and commerce.
Sprawl also destroys wildlife habitats on the land that is built over.
Developers preferred cheaper greenfield sites and Celtic Tiger tax incentives encouraged building, which speeded up this loss.
Impact 2 – Commuter and dormitory towns:
Rural towns such as Navan, Ashbourne, Naas, Newbridge, Maynooth, Bray and Greystones have become commuter towns for Dublin.
Their residents sleep there and work in Dublin, so little activity remains in the towns during the day.
Market functions such as marts and shops serving farmers decline, and shops and housing shift to serve commuters.
Meath, Kildare and Wicklow have grown rapidly as a result.
Impact 3 – Commuting and traffic:
Long commutes cause congestion on the roads into Dublin, and car dependence is high because the commuter areas are poorly served by public transport.
Cars and motorways widen the city's hinterland, so rural areas further out are drawn into its commuter zone.
Impact 4 – Ribbon development and services:
Houses are built one plot deep along rural roads leading out of towns, because sites are cheaper and road frontage gives access to water, electricity and phone lines.
This causes traffic hazards from many entrances onto fast roads, and it is costly to service because school buses, post and waste collection must reach scattered houses.
Septic tanks in unsewered houses can pollute groundwater and private wells.
Impact 5 – Ghost estates and responses:
A Government survey in 2010 found almost 3,000 unfinished housing estates, known as ghost estates, after the 2008 property crash.
Commuters bring spending to rural areas and help keep local schools, shops and GAA clubs open.
The National Planning Framework aims to curb sprawl by placing 40% of new homes within the footprint of existing settlements.

MODEL ANSWER: Decisions about emissions, forests, refugees and workers made in one country have direct consequences for people and environments in other countries, which shows how interdependent the global economy is.
Aspect 1 – Global warming:
Greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels in industrialised and emerging economies build up in a shared atmosphere, so one country's emissions warm the whole planet.
Atmospheric carbon dioxide rose from about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s.
China has been the largest emitter since the mid-2000s, but the USA emits far more per person, and rich countries carry historical responsibility for much of the carbon already in the atmosphere.
Bangladesh, which emitted very little, faces floods, cyclones and sea-level rise in the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta.
Pakistan produces under 1% of global emissions, yet its floods of 2022 covered about a third of the country and affected about 33 million people.
Small island states such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives are threatened by sea-level rise caused by emissions elsewhere.
The Paris Agreement of 2015 and the Loss and Damage Fund agreed at COP27 in 2022 show countries trying to respond together to a problem none can solve alone.
Aspect 2 – Deforestation:
About 10 million hectares of forest a year were cleared in 2015–2020, mostly in the tropics.
Much of this loss is driven by demand in other regions for beef, soya, palm oil, timber and cocoa.
In the Amazon close to a fifth of the original forest has been cleared, with cattle ranching the largest cause and soya exported as animal feed to Europe and China.
Carbon released from cleared forest adds to global warming, so the effects reach every country.
Indonesia and Malaysia produce about 85% of the world's palm oil, and the burning of peat swamp forest there caused regional haze in Singapore and Malaysia in 1997 and 2015.
Côte d'Ivoire has lost most of its forest since independence in 1960, much of it to cocoa farms that supply the chocolate industry.
Responses by other countries include the Amazon Soy Moratorium of 2006, the Amazon Fund of 2008 with Norway as the largest donor, and the EU Deforestation Regulation adopted in 2023.
Aspect 3 – Political decisions and refugees:
In Syria, protests in March 2011 were met with force by President Assad's government, which led to civil war.
At the height of the war over half of Syria's pre-war population of about 22 million was displaced, including over 6 million refugees abroad.
Turkey hosted over 3 million Syrian refugees, and Lebanon has the most refugees per head of population of any country.
About 1.3 million asylum applications were made in the EU in 2015, when Germany decided to admit refugees while Hungary built a border fence with Serbia.
The EU–Turkey statement of March 2016 returned irregular migrants from the Greek islands to Turkey, and the EU was to provide €6 billion for refugees in Turkey.
Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 led the EU to activate its Temporary Protection Directive for the first time on 4 March 2022, and Ireland had received over 100,000 Ukrainians by 2024.
Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law left most Rohingya stateless, and a military campaign from August 2017 drove about 740,000 of them into Bangladesh.
Aspect 4 – Social decisions, migration and human rights:
After EU enlargement on 1 May 2004 Ireland, the UK and Sweden opened their labour markets at once, and workers from Eastern Europe moved west.
Migrant workers, mainly from India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Pakistan and the Philippines, make up most of the population and workforce of Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, where the kafala system ties a worker's legal status to an employer.
Qatar was criticised over conditions for workers building World Cup stadiums and introduced reforms in 2020, including a minimum wage and the right to change jobs.
Remittances to low- and middle-income countries were about $650 billion in 2023, which shows how migration decisions in rich countries support families in poorer ones.
Source regions lose doctors, nurses and young workers, while receiving regions fill jobs in health care, construction and farming.
The collapse of Rana Plaza near Dhaka on 24 April 2013 killed about 1,130 people in factories that supplied Western brands, and led to the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh.
In 1984 Mary Manning, a Dunnes Stores worker in Dublin, refused to handle South African grapefruit in protest against apartheid, and in 1987 the Irish government banned imports of South African fruit and vegetables.
