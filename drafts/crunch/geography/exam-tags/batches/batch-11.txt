PART "1B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Intrusive features – Batholith:
Intrusive activity occurs when magma rises into the crust and cools slowly underground instead of reaching the surface.
Slow cooling produces large crystals, so intrusive rock such as granite is coarse-grained.
A batholith is a huge dome-shaped mass of granite formed by magma cooling deep underground.
The Leinster batholith, the largest granite mass in Ireland, intruded about 400 million years ago during the Caledonian mountain-building period.
Heat from the magma altered the surrounding rock into a ring of mica schist, called a metamorphic aureole.
Over millions of years erosion stripped away the overlying slate and exposed the granite as the Wicklow Mountains.
Granite is resistant, so the exposed batholith forms rounded uplands, with Lugnaquilla at 925 m the highest peak in Wicklow.
Intrusive features – Sills and dykes:
A sill is a horizontal sheet of magma injected between rock layers, and a resistant exposed sill forms cliffs and ledges, as at Fair Head, Co. Antrim, a dolerite sill with columnar jointing.
A dyke is a vertical sheet of magma cutting across the layers, and a dyke harder than its surroundings stands up as a wall-like ridge when the softer rock is eroded.
The Mourne Mountains in Co. Down are granite intrusions of the same period as the Antrim basalts, with Slieve Donard as the highest peak.
Extrusive features – Antrim plateau:
Extrusive activity occurs when magma reaches the surface and cools quickly as lava, ash and other material.
About 60 million years ago, linked to the opening of the North Atlantic, runny basic lava poured from long fissures in the crust in north-east Ireland.
Each lava flow spread widely before cooling, and repeated flows built up layer upon layer into the high, flat-topped Antrim plateau with steep edges.
Along the Antrim coast the dark basalt lies on top of white chalk, which the basalt protected from erosion.
Extrusive features – Giant's Causeway:
Columnar jointing forms when a thick lava flow cools slowly and contracts, producing cracks at about 120 degrees to each other.
The cracks divide the lava into mainly six-sided columns at right angles to the cooling surface, which sea erosion has exposed on the north Antrim coast.
About 40,000 interlocking basalt columns form the Giant's Causeway, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986.
Extrusive features – Volcanic plug:
A volcanic plug forms when magma solidifies in the vent of a volcano and the softer cone around it is eroded away.
Slemish, Co. Antrim, is a resistant volcanic plug left standing as a steep-sided hill.

PART "1C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option chosen – Flood control measures:
Flood control measures change how rivers erode, transport and deposit, because they alter the river's velocity, discharge and sediment load.
Impact 1 – Levees and embankments:
Levees and embankments confine the river to its channel, so floodwater can no longer spread across the floodplain.
The floodplain therefore receives no fresh silt, so natural deposition on it stops and the land loses its fertility.
The confined flow is deeper and faster, so the river has more energy for erosion and transport within the channel.
Sediment is deposited on the bed between the levees, which raises the river bed and means the banks must be built higher again.
If a levee fails the water escapes suddenly, giving a more severe flood than a natural one.
The Mississippi levee system, built after the flood of 1927, stops flood silt reaching the delta, which contributes to the loss of Louisiana's coastal wetlands.
Impact 2 – Dams and reservoirs:
Dams and reservoirs store floodwater, so discharge downstream is regulated and natural flood peaks are evened out.
In the reservoir velocity falls to near zero, so the river deposits its load there and the reservoir slowly fills with sediment.
Water released below a dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks downstream.
In Cork in November 2009 the ESB released large volumes from Inniscarra dam on the River Lee because the reservoir was full, and the release added to the flooding of the city during very heavy rain.
Impact 3 – Dredging and flood walls:
Dredging and deepening a channel increases its capacity, but the water then flows faster and passes flood peaks downstream sooner, so flooding downstream can increase.
At Clonmel, Co. Tipperary, an OPW scheme on the River Suir uses demountable barriers that are put in place when floods are forecast, so the river is held in its channel and the town is protected.
Impact 4 – Soft engineering:
Soft measures such as floodplain zoning and washlands let the river keep flooding naturally, so floodplain deposition continues.
Room for the River in the Netherlands moves dykes back and digs side channels so the river can spread out safely, working with natural processes.

PART "2B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option chosen – Surface karst landscape:
Karst is a landscape formed on limestone, which is mainly calcium carbonate, by chemical weathering.
Process – Carbonation:
The main process is carbonation, in which rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
The acid reacts with calcium carbonate to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble and is carried away in solution.
Carbonation works along joints and bedding planes, so the pattern of cracks decides where the rock dissolves fastest.
Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, and the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, formed about 350 million years ago, is highly vulnerable to it.
Landform – Limestone pavement:
Limestone pavement is a bare, flat surface of limestone, exposed where ice stripped away the soil.
Carbonation widens the vertical joints into deep grooves called grikes.
The blocks of limestone left standing between the grikes are called clints.
A labelled cross-section shows the flat clints on top, the grikes cutting down between them and the horizontal bedding planes below.
Landform – Swallow holes and turloughs:
Where a stream flows onto limestone, solution enlarges a joint into a swallow hole that carries the water underground.
Surface streams therefore disappear, leaving a landscape with little surface drainage.
Turloughs are seasonal lakes on limestone, such as Rahasane turlough near Craughwell, Co. Galway, which flood in winter and drain away underground.
Named example – The Burren:
The Burren in Co. Clare is Ireland's best-known surface karst landscape, with extensive limestone pavement.
The Aran Islands are a continuation of the Burren limestone, and their thin, rocky soils show how little soil covers the bare rock.

PART "2C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option chosen – Volcanoes:
Volcanoes are not spread evenly across the globe but are concentrated in narrow belts along plate boundaries.
Global distribution:
The Pacific Ring of Fire around the edge of the Pacific plate contains about 75% of the world's active volcanoes.
Another belt follows the mid-ocean ridges such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, while very few volcanoes occur in the interior of plates, as in Ireland.
Constructive boundaries:
The tectonic cycle begins at constructive boundaries, where convection currents carry plates apart.
Pressure drops as the plates separate, so the mantle partly melts and magma rises into the gap.
The magma cools to form new basaltic oceanic crust, and where a ridge rises above sea level it forms volcanic islands such as Iceland.
Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and has runny fissure eruptions, such as the Laki eruption of 1783, and new islands such as Surtsey from 1963.
Destructive boundaries:
The cycle is completed at destructive boundaries, where dense oceanic crust is forced down into the mantle in a process called subduction.
The sinking plate melts in the hot mantle, and the magma rises to form a chain of volcanoes.
Where the Nazca plate subducts beneath the South American plate, the Peru-Chile Trench lies offshore and the Andes carry many volcanoes.
Where the Juan de Fuca plate subducts beneath the North American plate, the Cascade Range volcanoes form, including Mount St Helens.
Where two oceanic plates converge, the volcanoes form an island arc, as in the Aleutian Islands and the Mariana Islands.
These subduction zones surround the Pacific, which explains why the Ring of Fire forms a ring.
Where two continental plates collide, as in the Himalayas, the crust is too light to subduct, so there is little or no volcanic activity.
Hot spots and recycling:
A few volcanoes occur away from boundaries, such as the shield volcanoes of Hawaii, which sit over a hot spot in the Pacific plate.
Because crust is created at ridges and destroyed at subduction zones, the ocean floor is continually recycled, and volcanoes mark both of these zones.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.1] 2. Pressure drops as the plates separate, so the mantle partly melts, producing magma (molten rock).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.2] 3. Magma rises into the gap and cools to form new basaltic oceanic crust, basalt being a dark, runny volcanic rock.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.b] Earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains are concentrated in narrow belts along plate boundaries, with very few in plate interiors. This is how plate tectonics explains where they occur.

[geo-rivers-s2.0.b] A river can erode no lower than its base level, the lowest point it can cut down to. Sea level is the ultimate base level, and a river that has settled into balance with its base level has a smooth, predictable shape.
[geo-rivers-s2.0.1] A graded (equilibrium) profile is a smooth, concave long profile (the river's slope from source to mouth, steep at the top and flattening downstream) where the river's energy is just enough to carry its load (the material it transports), so erosion and deposition (dropping of load) are balanced.
[geo-rivers-s2.0.2] A change in base level upsets this balance, so the river adjusts its profile to the new level.
[geo-rivers-s2.1.b] Rejuvenation is renewed vertical erosion (cutting downwards) caused by a fall in base level relative to the land. It gives the river renewed energy and interrupts a cycle of erosion.
[geo-rivers-s2.1.1] Cause 2, isostatic uplift (the land itself moves): land depressed under the weight of an ice sheet rises, or rebounds, when the ice melts, raising it relative to sea level. There is more on this in Glaciation.
[geo-rivers-s2.1.2] Cause 3, tectonic uplift of the land by earth movements, which raises the land above its old base level so the river cuts down again.
[geo-rivers-s2.2.b] A fall in base level is felt first at the river's mouth (where it meets the sea), and the effect then travels up the valley. This leaves a river made of two parts.
[geo-rivers-s2.2.0] Rejuvenation begins at the mouth and works upstream by headward erosion (erosion that cuts back towards the source), because the extra energy is first available at the lowest point.
[geo-rivers-s2.3.b] A knickpoint is where the new, rejuvenated river meets the old one. It is the key feature of a rejuvenated long profile (the river's slope from source to mouth).
[geo-rivers-s2.3.1] 2. Where the new and old graded profiles meet, the long profile has a break of slope: this is the knickpoint.
[geo-rivers-s2.3.2] 3. A waterfall or rapids is often found at the knickpoint, because the river drops steeply there. The knickpoint is the break of slope itself, not the waterfall.
[geo-rivers-s2.3.3] 4. The knickpoint retreats upstream over time, and the new profile extends behind it.
[geo-rivers-s2.3.4] 5. Once the whole river has graded to the new base level, the knickpoint disappears.
[geo-rivers-s2.3.5] Diagram: long profile showing the old and new graded sections and the knickpoint at their junction.
[geo-rivers-s2.4.b] Renewed vertical erosion cuts existing meanders (bends in the river) down into the land, rather than forming them on a floodplain (the flat land beside a river). This leaves meanders set deep in a valley with steep sides.
[geo-rivers-s2.4.0] Ingrown meanders have an asymmetrical cross-section (a slice across the valley), with one gentle and one steep side, because downcutting is slower and there is some lateral (sideways) erosion.
[geo-rivers-s2.4.1] Entrenched meanders have a symmetrical cross-section with steep sides, forming a gorge (a deep, narrow, steep-sided valley), because downcutting is rapid.
[geo-rivers-s2.4.2] Example of entrenched meanders: the Goosenecks of the San Juan River, Utah, USA.
[geo-rivers-s2.4.3] Example of incised meanders: the River Wye, Wales-England border.
[geo-rivers-s2.5.b] When a river cuts down into its old floodplain, the old floodplain is left stranded above the river. River terraces are remnants of an old floodplain, left as flat steps above the new floodplain after the river has cut down.
[geo-rivers-s2.5.0] Paired terraces are at the same height on both sides of the valley; they form where the river downcuts rapidly, so it has little time to move sideways.
[geo-rivers-s2.5.1] Unpaired terraces are at different heights on each side; they form while the river downcuts and migrates (moves) sideways.
[geo-rivers-s2.5.2] Example: the river terraces of the River Thames in and around London, England.
[geo-rivers-s2.5.3] Diagram: cross-section of a valley showing paired terraces above the present floodplain.
[geo-rivers-s2.6.b] Uplift of the land gives a river renewed cutting power, and deep incision (cutting down) can produce a gorge, a deep, narrow valley with steep sides.
[geo-rivers-s2.6.0] A gorge can be cut by deep incision of a river into an uplifted landscape.
[geo-rivers-s2.7.b] Each episode of rejuvenation leaves a mark, so a diagram can be read like a record. Counting the marks tells you how many times the river has been rejuvenated.
[geo-rivers-s2.7.0] The number of rejuvenations equals the number of knickpoints on a long profile, because each fall in base level creates one.
[geo-rivers-s2.7.2] Rejuvenation features not shown in a diagram are knickpoints, waterfalls or rapids at knickpoints, incised meanders, river terraces and gorges.
[geo-rivers-s2.8.b] A rise in base level, from sea level rising or land sinking, reduces the river's energy. This is the opposite of rejuvenation, so it drowns rather than cuts.
[geo-rivers-s2.8.1] Coastal landforms caused by a change in base level are raised beaches (from a fall in base level) and rias (from a rise).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.b] Rock at the surface is attacked by weather, water, plants and animals. Weathering is the first step in wearing landscapes down, and it must be kept separate from erosion.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.0] Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place (in situ) at or near the earth's surface, by weather, water, plants and animals. Erosion is the wearing away and removal of material by a moving agent such as a river, the sea, ice or wind.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.2] Regolith is the layer of loose, weathered rock fragments lying on top of solid bedrock (the unbroken rock beneath).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.b] Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process. It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock (rock with natural cracks called joints).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.1] 2. At night the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.2] 3. By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.b] In hot deserts the rock surface swings between hot days and cold nights. Both processes here are physical weathering driven by that heating and cooling, called insolation (heating by the sun).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.2] Stress builds up, and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion, leaving rounded boulders.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.4] Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.0] Pressure release (also called unloading or sheeting): when the overlying rock is eroded, the weight pressing on buried rock such as granite is removed, so it expands upward and cracks into sheets parallel to the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.2] Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores (tiny holes) and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.b] Plants and animals break rock down in two ways. Their physical action is a form of physical weathering, and the acids they produce cause chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.b] Carbonation is a chemical weathering process. It dissolves limestone, and it works along joints and bedding planes (the flat surfaces between layers of rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.0] Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.1] The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble, so it is carried away in solution (dissolved in water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.2] Word equation: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.2] Oxidation: oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron in the rock to form iron oxide (rust), which gives red, yellow or brown colours and weakens the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.3] Hydration: some minerals absorb water and swell, setting up stress in the rock; anhydrite, for example, becomes gypsum.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.b] Weathering does not act at the same speed or in the same way everywhere. Climate decides which type of weathering dominates, and rock type decides how vulnerable the rock is.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.3] Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.4] Rock type (its mineral composition): limestone is vulnerable to carbonation, granite to hydrolysis, and quartzite (a very hard rock) resists both.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.0] Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults (breaks in the rock where blocks have moved) let water in, so heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.1] Vegetation and soil cover: roots and humic acids speed weathering, and soil holds moisture against the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
