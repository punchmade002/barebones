PART 11B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named Irish city – Dublin:
Dublin is the capital and largest city in Ireland, built around the mouth of the River Liffey.
The location of both industry and commerce in Dublin has changed greatly since the nineteenth century.
Industrial land-use – old inner-city location:
In the nineteenth century industry in Dublin located in the inner city beside the port, canals and railways, where raw materials and goods were easily moved.
Brewing, biscuit-making and distilling were typical of this old inner-city industry.
The Guinness brewery at St James's Gate is an exception, as it has stayed on its site since Arthur Guinness's lease of 1759.
Industrial land-use – move to the edge:
Over the twentieth century many inner-city factories closed or moved out because there was no space left to expand.
Industry relocated to suburban industrial estates and business parks near the M50 and N7, such as Grange Castle, Citywest and Park West.
Land on the edge is cheaper, and it offers better road access for lorries and less congestion.
Jacob's biscuit factory moved from Bishop Street in the inner city to Tallaght in the 1970s.
The vacated inner-city sites became brownfield land that was redeveloped, and the old Jacob's factory now houses the National Archives.
Commercial land-use – the traditional centre:
Traditionally commercial land-use was concentrated in the central business district on both sides of the Liffey, around Grafton Street, Henry Street and O'Connell Street.
This area had the highest land values, with department stores, offices and banks clustered together.
Commercial land-use – move to the suburbs:
From the 1970s commerce spread to the edge of the city, with suburban shopping centres built beside the M50 and other major roads.
The Square in Tallaght opened in 1990, Blanchardstown Centre in 1996, Liffey Valley in 1998 and Dundrum Town Centre in 2005.
These sites were chosen for car access, free parking and cheaper land.
Out-of-town office parks such as Sandyford and Park West were built on sites with good road access.
The city centre has suffered vacant units and lost trade as shoppers move to the edge, so pedestrianisation and regeneration of central streets have been used in response.
Commercial land-use has also moved into the former port land of the Docklands, where Google's European headquarters at Grand Canal Dock created 'Silicon Docks'.

PART 11C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Cause 1 – Resources not developed or degraded:
Overpopulation occurs when the population of an area is too large for its resources, technology and economy to support an acceptable standard of living.
In the Sahel, overgrazing, overcultivation, cutting of fuelwood and drought have caused desertification, so the land can support fewer people.
Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s because of irrigation demand and drought, removing a vital water resource.
Rainfall in the Sahel is only about 200 to 600 mm a year and very unreliable, so crops fail and famines occurred in the 1970s and 1980s.
In pre-Famine Ireland over-reliance on the potato and the subdivision of holdings into tiny plots left too many people dependent on one resource, which failed in the Great Famine.
Cause 2 – Society and culture:
In Niger, large families carry prestige and early marriage of girls is common.
Female literacy is low, so women have little education and few alternatives to early motherhood.
Children are valued as farm labour and as security in old age, so couples have many children.
Niger has a total fertility rate of about 6 children per woman and population growth of over 3% a year.
The median age in Niger is about 15, so a huge young population needs food, schools and jobs that the country cannot provide.
By contrast, Kerala in India has a fertility rate of about 1.8 because of high female literacy and good health care.
Cause 3 – Low income levels:
Low-income countries have high birth rates because children are economic assets, infant mortality is high and there are no pensions.
Poverty limits the ability of a country to develop its resources or to import food.
Savings go on feeding and educating more people rather than on investment, which creates a poverty trap.
Jobs cannot keep up with population growth, so unemployment and underemployment are widespread.
There is severe pressure on schools, hospitals, housing, water and sanitation, as in the Sahel.

MODEL ANSWER: Bronze Age (barrow): a barrow is a burial mound built over the grave of an important person.
It was made of earth and stones, and the dead person was often buried with pottery or a bronze tool.
It shows that people were farming and settled in the area about 4,000 years ago.
Early Christian (ringwork): a ringwork is a circular farmstead enclosed by an earth bank and ditch.
It protected the people, their house and their cattle from raiders and wild animals.
It shows that farming families lived here between about 500 and 1000 AD.
Norman (moated site): a moated site is a Norman farmstead or manor surrounded by a water-filled ditch.
The moat protected the lord's house and his tenants from attack.
It shows that the Normans settled and farmed the land here after their arrival in the 12th century.

PART 12C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Kolkata, India:
Kolkata lies on the low-lying delta land beside the Hooghly river and had an urban population of about 14.1 million at Census 2011.
It grew rapidly as a colonial port and capital, with migrants arriving from rural Bengal, Bihar and Odisha, and refugees after Partition in 1947.
Problem 1 – Slums and informal settlements:
Migrants with little money and no legal land settle in shanty towns, called bustees in Kolkata, built on marginal land such as river banks and beside railways.
Tenure is insecure, so residents can be evicted and have no legal right to the land they occupy.
Overcrowding is severe, and pavement dwellers in Kolkata have no shelter at all.
Housing cannot be built as fast as migration driven by rural poverty and by the pull of jobs and natural increase add people.
Problem 2 – Water, sanitation and health:
Without piped water or sewers, settlements depend on open drains and shared toilets.
Contaminated water and waste spread water-borne diseases such as cholera, typhoid and dysentery.
In Kolkata water supply and sewerage are poor, so many residents lack safe water and proper sanitation.
Monsoon rain floods the low-lying streets and slums, which worsens the risk to health.
Problem 3 – Traffic and pollution:
Road networks cannot cope with the growth in traffic, so congestion becomes permanent and journeys take longer.
Trains and buses are overcrowded at peak times.
Air pollution comes from traffic, industry and cooking fuels burned in crowded homes.
Untreated sewage and industrial waste are discharged into rivers, and rubbish builds up where it is not collected.
Problem 4 – Unemployment and social problems:
Jobs did not keep pace with the number of migrants, so many people depend on low-paid informal work such as street vending, waste picking and rickshaw pulling, which have no job security.
In Kolkata hand-pulled rickshaws still operate, and underemployment and child labour are common.
Weak local government struggles to provide services, and inequality and crime are visible where rich and poor districts sit side by side.
