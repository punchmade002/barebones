QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q116 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q16, 80 marks)

PART "16" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Soil develops over time through the interaction of its components, and mineral matter, organic matter and water each influence its character.
Factor 1 – Mineral matter:
Mineral matter, about 45% of a good loam by volume, is weathered rock particles that form the skeleton of the soil and decide its texture.
The parent rock decides the mineral content and starting pH, as limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils.
The limestone of the Burren in Co. Clare gives thin, dark, lime-rich rendzinas found in the grikes and hollows.
Granite, as in the Wicklow Mountains, gives sandy, acidic soils, and sandstone gives sandy, free-draining, acidic soils.
Texture is the mix of sand, silt and clay, and it controls drainage, as sandy soils are free-draining and easily leached while clay soils hold water but become waterlogged.
Clay particles are the chemically active part of the mineral matter, and with humus they form the clay-humus complex that holds nutrient ions such as calcium, magnesium and potassium against leaching.
As minerals weather they release nutrients, and the rate of weathering sets soil depth, so soils are deepest in the hot, wet tropics where chemical weathering is fastest.
Most Midland soils in Ireland formed on boulder clay left by the last glaciation about 10,000 years ago, so Irish soils are young and often differ from the rock beneath them.
Loess, a fine wind-blown silt, is the parent material of the chernozems of the steppe and prairie.
Factor 2 – Organic matter:
Organic matter, about 5% of a good loam, is living organisms plus dead plant and animal remains, which decomposers break down into humus.
Humus is a dark, sticky substance that binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients and releases nutrients slowly, and it is the main store of nitrogen in the soil.
Mull humus forms under deciduous woodland and grassland, is well mixed by earthworms and gives fertile brown earths such as those of the Golden Vale.
Earthworms mix and aerate the soil and drag litter down, as Charles Darwin showed in his book of 1881.
Mor humus is acidic and fibrous with few earthworms, and it forms under coniferous forest and heath, giving podzols such as those on the Wicklow Mountains.
Organic acids from mor humus carry iron and aluminium down the profile in podzolisation, leaving a bleached E horizon and an iron-enriched B horizon.
Deep grass roots supply large amounts of organic matter on the steppes of Ukraine and the Prairies of North America, which gives chernozems a black topsoil about 1 m deep.
In tropical rainforest litter decomposes so fast that little humus builds up, so latosols are low in humus and lose fertility within a few years once the forest is cleared.
In waterlogged, airless conditions decomposition is slow, so organic matter builds up as peat, as in the raised bogs of Co. Offaly.
Factor 3 – Water:
Water, about 25% of a good loam, is held in the pores as the soil solution, from which plant roots absorb dissolved nutrients.
Where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, as in Ireland, gravitational water moves down through the soil and leaches soluble bases, leaving the upper soil acidic and less fertile.
Mild leaching gives brown earths, while intense leaching in a cool, wet climate under acid vegetation gives podzols.
In podzolisation iron, aluminium and humus are washed out of the A/E horizon and deposited in the B horizon, where a hard iron pan may form.
Capillary water held in small pores is available to plants, and field capacity is the water a soil holds once gravitational water has drained away.
Waterlogging on impermeable boulder clay fills the pores, excludes air and causes gleying, as in the drumlin belt of Cavan, Monaghan and Leitrim.
In gleying bacteria reduce iron to its grey or blue-grey form, with orange mottles where air enters along roots, giving a cold, sticky gley soil.
Where evaporation exceeds precipitation, capillary action draws water up and calcium carbonate is deposited in the B horizon as nodules, giving the fertile chernozems of the steppe.
In hot, wet tropical climates heavy rain removes silica and bases and leaves iron and aluminium oxides, giving the deep red latosols of the Amazon Basin.

QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q117 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q17, 80 marks)

PART "17" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Human activity can degrade soil, which is a negative impact, or conserve and improve it, which is a positive impact, as the following four aspects show.
Aspect 1 – Overgrazing on Irish hill commonage:
Overgrazing is keeping more animals on land than its vegetation can support.
From the 1980s EU headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in Connemara and the mountains of Mayo, Donegal and Kerry.
Heather and grass were stripped, which exposed and eroded blanket peat and sent silt into rivers, harming salmon and trout spawning grounds.
Trampling compacts the soil, and on wet ground this poaching reduces infiltration and increases runoff.
Less vegetation means less litter and humus, so sheet, rill and gully erosion remove the topsoil.
Payments were changed from per head to per area, and Commonage Framework Plans set lower stocking rates from the early 2000s.
REPS, the Rural Environment Protection programme launched in 1994, paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways.
Aspect 2 – Desertification in the Sahel:
The Sahel is a semi-arid belt south of the Sahara stretching from Senegal and Mauritania to Sudan, with unreliable rainfall of roughly 200 to 600 mm a year.
Population growth led to more livestock concentrated around wells, shorter fallow periods, more cash crops such as groundnuts and cotton, and more fuelwood cutting.
Droughts in the 1970s and 1980s left bare soil that wind and heavy rain then eroded.
Topsoil and humus were lost and surface crusting meant rain ran off instead of soaking in, so yields fell.
Human effects included food shortages, loss of livestock and migration to cities and abroad.
In Burkina Faso, farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo revived zaï pits and stone lines, which catch rainwater and trap soil.
In Niger, farmer-managed natural regeneration of trees has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s.
Aspect 3 – The Burren in Co. Clare:
The Burren has thin, dark, lime-rich rendzinas on Carboniferous limestone, found mainly in the grikes and hollows.
Clearance and grazing by early farmers are thought to have eroded soil into the grikes and underground, which helped to expose the bare limestone pavement.
The flower-rich grassland on the thin soil depends on winter grazing by cattle on the uplands, known as winterage.
When farmers stopped winter grazing, hazel and blackthorn scrub spread over the grassland, so the threat there is undergrazing rather than overgrazing.
BurrenLIFE, an EU-funded project from 2005 to 2010, tested scrub removal, better feeding systems and grazing plans with local farmers.
The Burren Programme, running since 2016, pays farmers by results, with each field scored for habitat quality so that a higher score earns a higher payment.
Farmers decide how best to manage their land, and the model has been copied in other Irish agri-environment programmes.
Aspect 4 – Soil conservation and improvement:
Contour ploughing across a slope makes furrows trap water instead of channelling it downhill, and terracing cuts steep slopes into level steps, as in the rice terraces of Banaue in the Philippines.
Shelter belts of trees and hedges cut wind speed and reduce wind erosion, and strip cropping uses grass strips to trap soil.
Crop rotation, green manure such as clover and added manure restore nutrients and humus.
Liming raises the pH of acid soils, and field and arterial drainage improve gleys.
On the bare limestone of the Aran Islands in Co. Galway, islanders built soil by layering sand and seaweed on the rock inside stone-walled fields.
In Iceland, birch woodland was cleared and overgrazed after settlement around AD 874, and the light volcanic soils were blown and washed away.
The Soil Conservation Service of Iceland, founded in 1907, fences off eroding land, sows lyme grass to stabilise drifting sand and replants birch, which shows positive impact following centuries of negative impact.

QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q118 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q18, 80 marks)

PART "18" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Human activities such as rainforest felling, intensive agriculture and industrial development alter biomes by removing, replacing or degrading vegetation and by polluting soil, water and air.
Aspect 1 – The felling of tropical rain forests:
Cattle ranching is the largest cause of clearance in the Brazilian Amazon, and soya farming spread north from Mato Grosso, with much of the soya exported as animal feed to Europe and China.
Roads opened the forest, as the Trans-Amazonian Highway begun in the early 1970s brought settlers, and BR-364 into Rondônia led to a fishbone pattern of clearings.
Mining at Carajás in Pará, one of the world's largest iron-ore mines, and the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River, completed in 2019, also cleared and flooded forest.
Clearance peaked at about 27,800 km² in 2004, and close to a fifth of the original Amazon forest has been cleared.
Habitat and species are lost, many of them endemic, and fragmentation creates edge effects because the forest edge is drier, hotter and more exposed.
Cleared latosols lose their fertility within a few years, erode in heavy rain and can bake into a hard laterite crust, so pasture is abandoned.
Less evapotranspiration means less rainfall downwind, longer dry seasons and more frequent fires.
Burning and decay release the carbon stored in the forest as carbon dioxide.
Indigenous peoples lose land, and in 2023 Brazil declared a public health emergency in Yanomami territory after illegal gold mining caused malnutrition and disease.
Aspect 2 – Intensive agricultural practices:
Intensive farming uses high inputs of machinery, fertiliser, pesticides and water to get maximum output per hectare, and it creates monocultures, larger fields and drained wetlands.
After EU milk quotas ended on 1 April 2015 Irish dairy herds expanded, hedgerows were removed, fields enlarged, callows drained and species-rich meadows reseeded with ryegrass.
Mechanised silage cutting destroys the nests of ground-nesting birds such as the corncrake, now found mainly in north-west Donegal, west Mayo and the Shannon Callows.
Heavy use of fertiliser and slurry raises nitrate levels in rivers and causes eutrophication of lakes.
Wild bees and farmland birds have declined, and agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions.
Responses include the Nitrates Directive of 1991 and the agri-environment programmes REPS in 1994, GLAS in 2015 and ACRES in 2023.
The Aral Sea on the border of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan was the world's fourth-largest lake in 1960, until Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya from the 1960s to irrigate cotton and rice.
The sea shrank and its salinity rose, the fishing industry collapsed and ships were stranded at former ports such as Moynaq.
Dust storms carry salt and pesticide residues from the exposed seabed onto farmland and people, although the Kok-Aral Dam of 2005 has raised the level of the North Aral Sea and fishing has partly recovered there.
Aspect 3 – Industrial development:
Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from coal- and oil-burning power stations, industry and vehicles form acids that are carried downwind across national borders.
Acid rain acidified thousands of lakes in southern Norway and Sweden from the 1960s to the 1980s, and fish died out in many of them.
In the Black Triangle where Germany, Poland and the Czech Republic meet, lignite-burning power stations helped to kill large areas of spruce forest in the Ore Mountains in the 1970s and 1980s, a process called Waldsterben.
Responses include the Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution of 1979, flue-gas desulphurisation and the liming of lakes, and sulphur dioxide emissions in Europe have since fallen by well over half.
Bord na Móna, set up in 1946, harvested milled peat from the Midland raised bogs for power stations, although peatlands cover about a fifth of Ireland.
The peat stations at Shannonbridge and Lanesborough closed in December 2020 and harvesting ended in 2021, and cutaway bogs such as Lough Boora in Co. Offaly are being rehabilitated.
The Athabasca oil sands in Alberta are mined by open-pit methods that strip boreal forest and muskeg and leave large tailings ponds of polluted water.
In the Niger Delta repeated oil spills have polluted creeks, mangrove forest and farmland, and a UNEP study of Ogoniland in 2011 found that a clean-up could take 25 to 30 years.
The Chernobyl explosion on 26 April 1986 killed a nearby pine forest, the Red Forest, and left a 30 km exclusion zone, where wildlife such as wolves and elk has since become common.
