PART 7B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named multinational – Intel:
Intel Corporation is a multinational company with its headquarters in Santa Clara, California, and plants in several countries.
It makes microprocessors, the chips that act as the brain of computers and servers.
Impact 1 – Production spread across countries:
Globalisation has led Intel to split production into stages that are placed where each is cheapest or most efficient.
Research and development is kept in the home country, at Hillsboro in Oregon.
Wafers are made in fabrication plants at Leixlip in Ireland, Chandler in Arizona and Kiryat Gat in Israel.
Finished wafers are flown to assembly and test plants in Penang and Kulim in Malaysia, Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam and Chengdu in China.
This creates an international division of labour, in which the core keeps design and research and other regions do assembly.
Impact 2 – The volume of world trade grows:
Components cross borders several times before a chip is sold, which increases the volume of world trade.
Intel buys silicon wafers, chemicals and gases from specialist suppliers abroad and lithography machines from ASML in the Netherlands.
Chips are small, light and valuable, so they are moved by air freight, which speeds up trade in high-value goods.
Much of this trade takes place between Intel's own plants in different countries, so the company controls a large share of these trade flows.
Impact 3 – Markets become global:
Intel sells its processors worldwide to computer makers such as Lenovo in China and HP and Dell in the USA.
Falling trade barriers and EU membership make it cheaper to export from Leixlip to markets in North America, Europe and Asia.
Computer chips from plants such as Leixlip are one of Ireland's main goods exports.
Impact 4 – Risks and competition:
The global chip shortage of 2021, caused by COVID-19 disruption, halted car and electronics production worldwide.
Competition from TSMC in Taiwan and Samsung in South Korea forces Intel to keep investing, and Intel announced about 15,000 global job cuts in August 2024.

PART 7C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact 1 – Single market access and investment:
Ireland joined the EEC, now the EU, on 1 January 1973, which gave Irish firms access to a large market.
The single market began on 1 January 1993 and removed barriers to trade between member states.
Access to this market, a 12.5% corporation tax rate and an English-speaking workforce attracted foreign direct investment from multinationals such as Intel and Apple.
Since the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020, Ireland is the only euro-area country where English is the main language, which strengthens its appeal to multinationals.
Impact 2 – The Common Agricultural Policy:
The Common Agricultural Policy, launched in 1962, opened a guaranteed market for Irish beef and dairy after 1973.
Farm incomes rose sharply and farms modernised with machinery, fertiliser, silage and drainage in the golden age of the 1970s.
Milk quotas from 1984 to 2015 capped output and held back Irish dairying, but after their abolition milk output rose from about 5.5 billion litres in 2014 to about 8.3 billion litres in 2020.
Reforms such as the MacSharry reform of 1992 and the 2003 Fischler reform separated payments from production, but many beef and sheep farms still depend heavily on direct payments.
Intensive dairying has damaged water quality through nitrates and raised greenhouse gas emissions, which is a negative impact of the policy.
Impact 3 – Regional and social funds:
Ireland was a single Objective 1 region from 1989 to 1999 because its income per head was below 75% of the EU average.
Structural funds such as the European Regional Development Fund and the Cohesion Fund paid for motorways, bypasses, water treatment, ports and airports.
Better infrastructure improved access for peripheral regions and contributed to the Celtic Tiger.
The European Social Fund co-financed training and programmes such as Youthreach, which raised skills and helped attract investment.
In 2009 the European Globalisation Adjustment Fund approved about €14.8 million to retrain about 2,400 former Dell and supplier workers around Limerick.
Impact 4 – Costs and negative impacts:
Under the Common Fisheries Policy, Ireland had to share its rich waters with larger fleets from Spain, France and the Netherlands, and its quota share is small relative to the size of its waters.
After Brexit, Ireland lost about 26% of its mackerel quota.
Ireland has been a net contributor to the EU budget since 2014, after receiving over €40 billion more than it paid in between 1973 and about 2014.
The gap between the Dublin-centred east and the Northern and Western region has persisted despite EU regional funding.
