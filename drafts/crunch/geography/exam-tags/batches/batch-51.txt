The redevelopment was less effective for local people because early projects favoured offices and expensive apartments, and residents got few of the jobs created.

Seven 15-storey towers built in 1966 to 1969 suffered isolation, poor maintenance, unemployment and drugs.
Ballymun Regeneration Ltd was set up by Dublin Corporation in 1997.
The towers were demolished between 2004 and 2015 and replaced by new low-rise homes.
The regeneration was effective because housing quality improved and residents were involved in the process.

PART "12C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Influence – Income levels and population growth:
Income levels strongly influence population growth because birth rates fall as income rises.
Low-income countries tend to have high birth rates and fast growth, whereas high-income countries have low birth rates and slow growth.
Example – Niger:
Niger is a low-income, landlocked West African country, largely in the Sahel and Sahara.
Its population is about 27 million and is growing by over 3% a year.
Its total fertility rate is about 6 children per woman, among the highest in the world.
Niger is in stage 2 of the Demographic Transition Model, with a high birth rate, a falling death rate and rapid growth.
Its median age is about 15, and its population pyramid has a very wide base.
Factor 1 – Infant mortality:
In poor countries high infant mortality encourages large families because parents expect some children to die.
As incomes rise, health care improves and falling infant mortality removes the need for these extra births.
Factor 2 – Education of women:
Where incomes are low, fewer girls stay in education and women tend to have more children.
As incomes rise, educated women in paid work have later and fewer children.
Factor 3 – Cost and value of children:
In low-income rural areas children work and are the main security for parents in old age, so families are large.
As incomes rise, pensions replace children as old-age security.
Urbanisation also lowers the birth rate because children cost more and earn less in towns and cities.
Factor 4 – Contraception and health:
Higher incomes bring wider access to contraception, which lowers the birth rate.
Better sanitation, vaccination and medical care lower the death rate, which is why growth is rapid in stage 2 before the birth rate falls.
Contrasting example – Ireland:
Ireland's total fertility rate was about 4 in the early 1970s, fell below replacement level around 1990 and was about 1.5 in 2023.
Rising incomes, free secondary education introduced in 1967 and more women in paid employment contributed to this fall.
Contraception was legalised by the Health (Family Planning) Act 1979, which also cut family size.
Ireland is in stage 4 of the model, with about 55,000 births against about 35,000 deaths in 2023, a natural increase of about 20,000.
Life expectancy in Ireland is about 82 to 83 years, which shows how higher incomes lengthen life as well as shrinking families.
