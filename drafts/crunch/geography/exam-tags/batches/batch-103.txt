PART 9B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named developed economy – Ireland (financial services):
Ireland is the developed economy examined, and financial services, centred on the International Financial Services Centre in Dublin, are the activity studied, with political and human factors examined.
Factor 1 – Political factors:
Government decisions created the International Financial Services Centre in 1987, when the Haughey government set it up on the derelict Custom House Docks on the north bank of the Liffey.
Approved firms paid a 10% corporation tax rate, later replaced by the 12.5% national rate, which lowered costs and raised the profits of financial firms.
Very large multinational groups pay a 15% minimum rate from 2024 under the OECD agreement, so tax policy continues to shape location decisions.
Membership of the EU Single Market, which began in 1993, gives Irish-based firms access to the wider EU market, and adoption of the euro in 1999 removed currency risk in trade with the eurozone.
The Central Bank of Ireland regulates the sector, which rebuilds confidence in Irish finance after the 2008 banking crisis damaged it.
After the 2016 Brexit vote, Bank of America and Barclays moved their main EU operations to Dublin, because Ireland remained in the EU.
Government regeneration of the north docklands, with the Luas Red Line from 2004 and new housing nearby, improved the location for firms and workers.
Because of these political choices, Irish-domiciled investment funds held over €3 trillion in assets in the 2020s.
Factor 2 – Human factors:
Dublin has a large, educated, English-speaking workforce, which suits global financial firms.
Skilled graduates come from Trinity College Dublin, UCD, DCU, TU Dublin and Maynooth University.
Free second-level education, announced by Donogh O'Malley in 1966 and introduced in 1967, built a better-educated workforce over the following decades.
Firms recruit staff from across the EU for offices serving Europe, which widens the labour pool.
Agglomeration means firms gain from being close to each other, sharing skills and suppliers, so one financial firm attracts others to the IFSC.
Ireland's time zone lets firms trade with Asian markets in the morning and American markets in the afternoon.
Dublin Airport, with over 30 million passengers in 2023, and fibre connectivity link the region to the world.
Human enterprise created a second specialism, aircraft leasing, when Tony Ryan founded Guinness Peat Aviation at Shannon in 1975, and Ireland now manages about half of the world's leased commercial aircraft.

PART 9C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named source – wind energy in Ireland:
Wind energy, in which wind turns turbine blades that drive a generator, is the renewable source examined, with onshore farms such as Galway Wind Park in Connemara and offshore turbines at Arklow Bank off Co. Wicklow.
Economic advantages:
Wind is a free fuel once turbines are built, so a wind farm has no fuel purchase, extraction or transport costs.
Wind is Ireland's main renewable source and supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s.
Ireland imported about three-quarters of its energy in the early 2020s at a cost of billions of euro a year, so wind reduces the import bill and the exposure to price shocks.
Strong, frequent Atlantic winds give the western coasts and uplands a good wind resource, as at Galway Wind Park, completed in 2017.
Wind farms create jobs in construction, maintenance and servicing, often in rural areas.
Green electricity attracts multinational companies that want renewable power, which supports investment and jobs.
Wind helps Ireland meet its 2030 EU targets and avoid the cost of buying compliance.
Denmark, where wind supplies about half of electricity, has built a turbine industry around Vestas, which shows the export potential of wind.
Environmental advantages:
Wind produces little or no carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide or nitrogen oxides when generating electricity.
Replacing coal, peat and gas cuts Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions and helps meet its climate targets.
Less burning of fossil fuels reduces acid rain, which damages forests and kills fish in lakes.
Wind is inexhaustible, unlike fossil fuels, so it is a sustainable source of power for future generations.
Cutting the burning of smoky fuels reduces smog and improves air quality in towns and cities.
Wind farms can be built on cutaway bog, as at Bellacorick in Co. Mayo in 1992 and Oweninny, which re-uses land already stripped of peat.
Offshore wind, such as Arklow Bank Wind Park, which opened in 2004, places turbines at sea away from homes.
Wind generation needs no cooling water and produces no ash or radioactive waste.
