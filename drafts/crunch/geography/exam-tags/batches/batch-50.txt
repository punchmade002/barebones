PART 9B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named developed economy – Ireland:
Ireland is a developed economy in which services employ about three-quarters of workers.
Option chosen – financial services:
Financial services are a highly footloose, high-value tertiary activity.
They include banking, insurance, fund administration, asset management and aircraft leasing.
Named example – International Financial Services Centre (IFSC), Dublin:
The IFSC was set up in 1987 by the Charles Haughey government.
It was built on the derelict Custom House Docks on the north bank of the Liffey.
Origins and reasons for growth:
Approved firms paid a 10% corporation tax rate, which was later replaced by the 12.5% national rate.
Government incentives encouraged international banks and fund managers to locate there.
Ireland offered an educated, English-speaking workforce.
Membership of the EU gave firms access to the Single Market.
Ireland's time zone allows firms to trade with both Asian and American markets in one working day.
The project also aimed to regenerate a declining dock area.
Growth of the sector:
The IFSC became one of the world's largest centres for investment funds.
Irish-domiciled funds hold over €3 trillion in assets.
Aircraft leasing is a second specialism, and Ireland manages about half of the world's leased commercial aircraft.
Aircraft leasing grew from Shannon after Guinness Peat Aviation was founded there in 1975 by Tony Ryan.
Shannon Free Zone, set up in 1959, offered firms tax relief and duty-free import of components.
Effects of development:
The IFSC created many high-value jobs for graduates.
It regenerated the north docklands, which have been served by the Luas Red Line since 2004.
New housing was built near the redeveloped docklands.
Risks and problems:
The property bubble burst in 2007 and 2008, and the government guaranteed Irish bank liabilities on 30 September 2008.
The 2008 banking crisis damaged confidence in Irish finance, so the Central Bank of Ireland now regulates the sector.
The National Asset Management Agency was set up in 2009 to take over property loans from the banks.
From 2024 very large multinational groups pay 15% under the OECD agreement, which narrows Ireland's tax advantage.
Opportunities from Brexit:
After the 2016 Brexit vote, Bank of America and Barclays moved their main EU operations to Dublin.

PART 9C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact 1 – Global warming:
Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, the main greenhouse gas.
CO2 traps heat in the atmosphere, so the extra CO2 enhances the greenhouse effect and causes global warming.
Fossil fuels supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy, so the scale of these emissions is very large.
China produces and consumes about half the world's coal and has been the largest CO2 emitter since the mid-2000s.
Named fossil fuel – coal at Moneypoint:
Coal burned at Moneypoint power station in Co. Clare, which opened in 1985, releases CO2, SO2 and NOx.
Impact 2 – Acid rain:
Sulphur dioxide from coal and heavy oil and nitrogen oxides from vehicles and power stations dissolve in water vapour to form sulphuric and nitric acids.
Acid rain has a pH below 5.6 and falls as wet deposition in rain or as dry deposition of acidic particles.
It is a transboundary problem, as emissions from Britain and Germany were carried by south-westerly winds to Norway and Sweden.
Thousands of Scandinavian lakes were acidified and lost their fish, because aluminium released from the soil kills fish.
Lignite-burning power stations in the Black Triangle of East Germany, Poland and Czechia caused severe forest death in the 1970s and 1980s.
Acid rain also dissolves limestone and marble buildings and corrodes metal.
Flue-gas desulphurisation, catalytic converters and the 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution helped cut European SO2 emissions by well over three-quarters since 1990.
Impact 3 – Smog:
Burning coal and turf in homes releases smoke and fine particles.
In calm cold winter weather an anticyclone creates a temperature inversion that traps the smoke near the ground and forms smog.
Smog causes bronchitis, asthma, and heart and lung disease.
London's Great Smog of December 1952 caused about 4,000 deaths at the time.
Dublin banned the sale of smoky coal from 1 September 1990, and black smoke levels fell by about 70%.

PART 10C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Pattern 1 – Heavy emigration in the 1950s:
Emigration in the 1950s was the highest since the 1880s, with net emigration of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961.
It was caused by economic stagnation and protectionism, unemployment, and the decline of small farms and farm labouring as farming mechanised.
Most emigrants went to Britain, drawn by post-war demand for labour in building, factories, nursing and transport, in cities such as London, Birmingham and Manchester.
The Common Travel Area, low fares and chain migration made the move to Britain easy.
Pattern 2 – Net immigration in the 1970s:
The 1970s were the first decade of net immigration since the Famine, as returning emigrants and their children came back.
The First Programme for Economic Expansion of 1958 and the IDA's attraction of foreign factories from 1969 created jobs and cut emigration.
Ireland joined the EEC on 1 January 1973, and the State population rose from about 3.0 million in 1971 to about 3.4 million in 1981.
Pattern 3 – Emigration again in the 1980s:
Recession, high unemployment and high taxes caused net emigration of over 200,000 between 1981 and 1991.
Many emigrants were graduates, so the loss became known as the brain drain.
Destinations were Britain and the USA, where many Irish emigrants were undocumented until the Donnelly and Morrison visas.
Pattern 4 – Immigration during the Celtic Tiger:

Pattern 5 – Emigration after the 2008 crash:
The property crash, the banking crisis and the EU–IMF bailout of 2010 caused unemployment, and emigration peaked at about 89,000 in the year to April 2013.
Many leavers were young graduates, and the main destinations were Australia, the UK, Canada and the USA.
Pattern 6 – Renewed immigration since the mid-2010s:
Economic recovery, jobs in multinationals and services, and returning Irish emigrants brought net immigration back.
Over 100,000 people from Ukraine had come to Ireland by 2024 under EU temporary protection after Russia's invasion in February 2022.
