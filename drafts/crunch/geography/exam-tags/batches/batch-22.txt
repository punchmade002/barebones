PART 1B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact on the coastal landscape:
Isostasy is the state of balance between the crust, which floats on the denser asthenosphere, and the mantle beneath it.
During the Ice Age the weight of the ice sheets pressed the crust down, which is called isostatic depression, and the depression was greatest in the north of Ireland where the ice was thickest.
When the ice melted the load was removed and the crust slowly rose, which is called isostatic rebound.
Rebound lags far behind melting, so land can still be rising long after the ice has gone, as in Scandinavia, Hudson Bay and Scotland.
Impact 1 – Raised beaches:
Where the land rose faster than the sea the coast became emergent, so former shore features were lifted above present high tide.
A former beach, its cliffs and its rock platform now stand above the reach of the tide.
An old cliff line stands inland of the sea, with a wave-cut notch, sea caves and stacks above the present tide reach.
Beach shingle lies on the flat wave-cut platform, which was cut by waves at the former sea level.
Named example – Malin Head:
Raised beaches are found at Malin Head on the Inishowen peninsula in Co. Donegal.
Impact 2 – Rias in the south-west:
In the south-west the ice was thinner, so there was less depression and rebound, and the sea rose faster than the land.
Eustatic change raised sea level worldwide as meltwater returned to the oceans, and this flooded river valleys to create a submergent coast.
A ria is a drowned river valley with a V-shaped cross-section and a winding outline, as at Bantry Bay, the Kenmare River and Dingle Bay.
Impact 3 – Drowned drumlins and fjords:
Where the lowland between drumlins was flooded, the crests of the drumlins stand as islands, as in Clew Bay in Co. Mayo and Strangford Lough in Co. Down.
A fjord is a drowned glacial trough that is deep and U-shaped, as at Killary Harbour on the Galway and Mayo border.
The coastline therefore shows the combined result of isostatic and eustatic change, with emergent features in the north and submergent features in the south and west.

A labelled cross-section shows the present sea, the present beach, the old cliff line with its notch and caves, and the raised platform with shingle above high tide.
It also shows the former sea level, which is now above the present one.

PART 1C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Positive impact 1 – Fertile soils:
Weathered volcanic ash and lava break down to release minerals that form very fertile soils.
On the slopes of Mount Etna and Vesuvius these soils support vineyards and orchards.
Fertile soils allow intensive farming, which gives people a living and supports a dense population near the volcano.
Positive impact 2 – Geothermal energy:
Iceland lies on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge above a hot spot, so hot rock lies close to the surface.
Groundwater heated by the hot rock is brought up through wells as steam and hot water.
Geothermal water heats about nine in ten homes in Iceland, and Reykjavik has a geothermal district heating system.
Steam drives turbines at power stations such as Hellisheidi to generate electricity, which is renewable and low in carbon emissions.
Positive impact 3 – Tourism:
Volcanoes, geysers and hot springs attract large numbers of visitors, as at the geysers of Geysir and the Blue Lagoon in Iceland.
The Blue Lagoon is a pool of warm, mineral-rich waste water from the Svartsengi geothermal power station.
Tourism creates jobs in hotels, guiding and transport, and brings income to the local area.
Positive impact 4 – Minerals and building materials:
Volcanic areas provide minerals and building materials such as sulphur, pumice and basalt.
Positive impact 5 – New land:
Volcanic eruptions can build new land, as when the island of Surtsey emerged from the sea off southern Iceland in 1963.
Named volcano – Mount Etna, Sicily:
Mount Etna in Sicily is an active volcano whose fertile slopes are farmed and which attracts many tourists.

PART 2B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Role of temperature – Freeze-thaw action:
Temperature is central to physical weathering because rock and the water inside it change volume as temperatures rise and fall, and the repeated change breaks the rock without altering its minerals.
Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock.
By day water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
At night the temperature falls below freezing, so the water turns to ice and expands by about 9 per cent, which widens the crack.
When the temperature rises above 0 °C the ice thaws and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular fragments of rock.
It is most effective on exposed mountains, such as the summit of Carrauntoohil in Co. Kerry, where the temperature crosses freezing point regularly.
The result is scree slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields.
Shady slopes stay frozen longer, so freeze-thaw is stronger on them.
Role of temperature – Exfoliation:
Exfoliation, or onion weathering, depends on a large daily temperature range, as in hot deserts such as the Sahara.
The outer layer of rock heats and expands during the hot day.
At night it cools and contracts more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
Stress builds up between the layers, and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion.
Rounded boulders are left behind, and some moisture such as dew helps the process to work effectively.
Role of temperature – Granular disintegration:
In a rock such as granite, dark and light minerals heat and expand at different rates.
The differing expansion loosens individual grains, which fall away and leave gravelly sand.

Explanation of the ribbon lake – Glacial erosion:
During the Ice Age a valley glacier filled the valley beneath Kings Mountain and flowed down it.
The glacier eroded by plucking, as meltwater froze in joints in the bedrock and the moving ice tore out blocks of rock.
It also eroded by abrasion, as rock fragments frozen into the base of the ice scraped and polished the bedrock.
Together these processes deepened and widened the river valley into a broad, flat-floored trough with a U-shape.
The glacier cut off the interlocking spurs of the old river valley, leaving truncated spurs on the steep valley sides.
Erosion was greater in one section of the valley floor, where the rock was softer or more ice flowed together, so that section was overdeepened.
This left a hollow rock basin in the floor of the valley.
When the climate warmed about 10,000 years ago the ice melted and the glacier retreated.
Meltwater and rain filled the rock basin, forming a long, narrow lake.
On the map the lake is long and narrow, with steep slopes and closely spaced contours on the high ground to the north.
The fine rock flour ground by the ice and carried by meltwater gives such glacial lakes a milky colour.
Another example of a ribbon lake is Upper Lake in Glendalough, Co. Wicklow.

Mass movement process – Soil creep:
Soil creep is the slowest form of mass movement, in which soil moves downslope by a few millimetres to centimetres a year.
Mass movement is the downslope movement of weathered material under the influence of gravity, with no transporting agent such as a river or glacier.
Soil particles are lifted at right angles to the slope when the soil is wetted and swells, or when it freezes and heaves.
When the soil dries or thaws the particles drop vertically under gravity.
Each cycle therefore moves the particles a little further downslope, in a zigzag path.
It occurs on grass-covered slopes of all gradients, including the gentle ground near Cloonderry and the lower slopes beneath Kings Mountain.
Evidence of creep includes terracettes, which are small steps on grassy slopes.
Bent tree trunks, tilted fences, walls and poles, and cracked roads also show that the soil is moving.
Soil piles up behind walls and other obstacles on the slope.
Creep is faster on steeper slopes because the downslope pull of gravity is greater.
It is also helped by heavy rain, which wets the soil and makes it swell, and by freezing in winter.
Plant roots bind the soil and slow creep, so it is faster where vegetation is thin.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.0] A discontinuity is a sharp boundary between two layers of the earth, where the material changes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.1] The Moho (Mohorovičić discontinuity) is the boundary between the crust and the mantle, where earthquake waves speed up suddenly.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.2] The Moho is named after Andrija Mohorovičić, a Croatian seismologist (a scientist who studies earthquakes), who identified it in 1909 from earthquake records.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.2.3] The Gutenberg discontinuity is the boundary between the mantle and the core, at the base of the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.b] The mantle is the thickest layer. Its upper part contains the layers on which the crust rests and moves.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.0] The mantle extends to about 2,900 km depth and is made of dense rock rich in iron and magnesium.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.1] The mantle has an upper part and a lower part, and it is the upper part that holds the asthenosphere and the base of the lithosphere.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.2] Asthenosphere: the semi-molten (partly melted), plastic (able to flow slowly without breaking) layer in the upper mantle, beneath the lithosphere, on which the plates move. It is not liquid.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.3] Magma is molten rock in the mantle; it is called lava only once it erupts at the surface.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.4] Lithosphere: the rigid outer shell made of the crust plus the solid top of the upper mantle. It is about 100 km thick on average, thinner under the oceans and thicker under the continents.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.3.5] The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates (large slabs of rigid rock), which is why the rigid lithosphere can move as separate pieces over the plastic asthenosphere.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.4.b] The core is the centre of the earth. It is made of iron and nickel and is divided into a liquid outer part and a solid inner part.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.4.0] Exams ask for the two main metals of the core: iron and nickel.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.b] The inside of the earth is hot, and that heat keeps the mantle moving. This movement is what later drives changes in the crust.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.5.3] 2. Near the top of the mantle the rock spreads out sideways and cools.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.0] Arthur Holmes, a British geologist, suggested in the late 1920s that mantle convection (slow circulation of heated rock in the layer beneath the crust) could move continents.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.2] 1. Magma (molten rock) rises at mid-ocean ridges.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.3] 2. It cools to form new oceanic crust (the crust under the oceans) made of basalt, a dark volcanic rock.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.5] 4. Old crust is destroyed again at ocean trenches, so the ocean floor is constantly recycled.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.0] A plate is a rigid slab of lithosphere (the crust plus the solid upper part of the mantle) that floats and moves on the asthenosphere (the softer, slowly flowing layer beneath). A plate can carry continental crust, oceanic crust or both.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.1] The earth has seven major plates: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian and Antarctic. The Pacific plate is the largest.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.4] Convection currents in the mantle (rising and sinking flows of heated rock) drag the plates along, because the plates sit on top of the moving rock.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.7.0] Plate movement drives endogenic forces, which, for example, cause folding, faulting, volcanic activity and earthquakes and are concentrated mostly along plate boundaries.

[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.2] Regolith is the layer of loose, weathered rock fragments lying on top of solid bedrock (the unbroken rock beneath).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.3] Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals (the chemical substances the rock is made of).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.4] Chemical weathering changes or dissolves the minerals in the rock, so the rock itself is altered, not just broken.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.b] Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process. It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock (rock with natural cracks called joints).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.1] 2. At night the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.2] 3. By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.3] 4. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular (sharp-edged) fragments.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.4] It is most effective on mountains in upland Ireland, on high mountains and in periglacial areas (cold areas where the ground is frozen for much of the year). Freeze-thaw is active on Irish mountain summits such as Carrauntoohil, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.5] Result: scree (talus) slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields (areas covered in broken angular rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.0] Exfoliation, or onion weathering, is an insolation process in hot deserts such as the Sahara, where the daily temperature range (the gap between day and night temperature) is large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.1] The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day, and cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.2] Stress builds up, and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion, leaving rounded boulders.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.3] Many geographers stress that some moisture, such as dew, must be present for exfoliation to work effectively.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.4] Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.b] Rock can also break because the forces on it change, or because growing crystals push it apart. Neither process changes the minerals, so both are physical.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.1] The sheets peel off to leave domes, such as Half Dome, Yosemite, California, USA, a granite dome shaped by sheeting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.2] Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores (tiny holes) and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.0] Hydrolysis: water reacts with feldspar (a mineral in granite) and turns it into clay (kaolin, or china clay); the quartz grains (another mineral in granite) are left as sand, and the granite crumbles.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.0] Climate: temperature and rainfall decide which type of weathering dominates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.2] Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, and hot deserts favour exfoliation and salt crystallisation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.3] Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.0] Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults (breaks in the rock where blocks have moved) let water in, so heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.1] Vegetation and soil cover: roots and humic acids speed weathering, and soil holds moisture against the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.4] Aspect (the direction a slope faces): shady slopes stay frozen longer, so freeze-thaw is stronger on them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.b] Temperature links the processes above. It matters in two different ways, and the type of weathering that results depends on the temperature pattern of the place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.0] Temperature acts in two ways: as a trigger, through the expansion and contraction that cause stress, and as a control on the rate of chemical reactions.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.1] Freeze-thaw depends on temperature crossing the freezing point, and exfoliation and granular disintegration depend on a large daily range; both need changes in temperature, not just warmth.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.3] Method: name the process and its type, state the temperature condition it needs, explain the stress or rate change it produces, and link this to the result, such as scree from freeze-thaw.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.b] Slopes are never completely still. Mass movement is the way weathered material shifts downhill, and everything else in this subsection (the processes, the factors and the case studies) is a variation on it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.0] Mass movement (also called mass wasting) is the downslope movement of weathered material, such as regolith (the loose broken rock and soil that covers solid rock), soil, rock, mud, peat or snow, under the influence of gravity.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.1] No transporting agent such as a river, glacier or wind is needed, because gravity does the work. Weathering (the breakdown of rock where it lies) supplies the material that moves.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.2] Gravity pulls material downslope. Friction (grip between surfaces), cohesion (particles sticking together) and plant roots resist it. Movement happens when the downslope force exceeds the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.0] In space, it is most intense on steep, wet and unvegetated slopes, because steepness increases the pull downslope while water and bare ground reduce the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.1.1] In time, its frequency rises after heavy rain, snowmelt, earthquakes and seasonal freeze-thaw (the repeated freezing and thawing of water in rock cracks).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.b] The slowest mass movement, moving soil by a few millimetres to centimetres a year. It is too slow to watch, so you recognise it from its effects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.0] Soil particles are lifted at right angles to the slope by wetting (swelling) or by freezing (frost heave, the upward push of freezing ground).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.1] On drying or thawing, the particles drop vertically under gravity, so each cycle moves them a little further downslope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.2] Evidence: terracettes (small steps on grassy slopes), bent tree trunks, tilted fences, walls and poles, soil piled up behind walls, and cracked roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.3] Diagram: draw the zigzag path of a particle, lifted at right angles to the slope and dropped vertically.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.1] The slump leaves a crescent-shaped scar (scarp) at the top, a back-tilted block and a bulging toe at the foot.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.3] A slump moves on a curved plane. A landslide moves on a flat plane.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.0] Gradient (slope angle): the steeper the slope, the greater the downslope pull of gravity and the faster the movement. Gentle slopes favour creep; steep slopes favour falls and slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.2] Heavy or prolonged rain and snowmelt trigger most fast movements, because they saturate the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.0] Vegetation: roots bind soil and plants absorb water, so bare slopes move more easily.
