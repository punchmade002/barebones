QUESTION geography-pp-2019-higher-q122 (LC Geography Higher 2019 — Part Two — Q22, 80 marks)

PART "22" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Solar energy is received unevenly over the earth, and the atmosphere and oceans move heat from the tropical surplus to the polar deficit through winds, circulation cells, storms and currents.
Aspect 1 – The imbalance that drives redistribution:
Between the equator and about 35 to 40 degrees north and south more energy is received than is radiated away, which is the surplus.
Poleward of about 35 to 40 degrees more energy is lost than received, which is the deficit.
Without a poleward transfer the tropics would keep heating and the poles would keep cooling.
At the equator the sun's rays strike nearly vertically and are concentrated on a small area, but towards the poles the same beam strikes at a low angle and is spread over a larger area.
Low-angle rays also pass through more atmosphere, so more energy is absorbed, scattered and reflected before it reaches the ground.
Snow-covered polar regions reflect most of the little radiation they receive, as fresh snow reflects about 80 to 90%.
Of 100 units of incoming radiation about 30 are reflected to space, about 20 are absorbed by the atmosphere and about 50 are absorbed by the land and ocean surface.
Over a year the earth as a whole balances, with energy in equal to energy out, so its average temperature stays stable.
The atmosphere carries most of the heat at mid and high latitudes, and the oceans carry a large share in the tropics.
Aspect 2 – Atmospheric circulation:
In the Hadley cell intense heating at the equator makes air rise, creating low pressure and the ITCZ, and at height the air flows poleward, carrying heat with it.
The air sinks at about 30 degrees to form the subtropical high pressure and deserts, and it returns to the equator as the trade winds.
In the Ferrel cell between 30 and 60 degrees, surface westerlies carry warm air poleward to meet cold polar air at the polar front at about 60 degrees.
In the polar cell cold air sinks at the poles and flows out as polar easterlies.
Most mid-latitude heat transfer is done by depressions and anticyclones forming along the polar front and by the meandering jet stream.
Jet streams are narrow, fast bands of westerly wind at about 9 to 12 km, often blowing at over 150 km/h, and they steer depressions.
In a depression warm, moist tropical maritime air moves poleward as cold polar maritime air moves towards the equator.
Water evaporated over warm tropical oceans carries latent heat poleward, and the heat is released where the vapour condenses, for example in a depression over Ireland.
Tropical cyclones draw large quantities of heat and moisture from tropical oceans and carry them poleward, and ex-hurricane Ophelia reached Ireland on 16 October 2017.
Aspect 3 – Oceanic circulation:
Surface currents are driven mainly by wind drag, with the trade winds pushing the equatorial currents westward and the westerlies pushing the mid-latitude currents eastward.
Warm currents carry heat poleward along the western sides of ocean basins, such as the Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil Current and East Australian Current.
The Gulf Stream flows from the Gulf of Mexico and the Straits of Florida north-east along the US east coast, and it carries more water than all the world's rivers combined.
Off Europe it is called the North Atlantic Drift, which keeps Ireland and Britain mild.
Norway's coast stays ice-free in winter even north of the Arctic Circle at Hammerfest, and Murmansk in Russia is an ice-free Arctic port, because of the warm water of the North Atlantic Drift.
Cold currents return cooled water towards the equator along the eastern sides of ocean basins, such as the Canary, Benguela, Humboldt and California currents.
The coast of Labrador, at about the same latitude as Ireland, has long freezing winters and sea ice because the cold Labrador Current flows south past it.
The thermohaline circulation is driven by cold, salty, dense water that sinks in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica and spreads through the deep ocean.
Warm surface water flows in to replace the sinking water, so heat is carried slowly round the globe, and one circuit of the conveyor belt takes about 1,000 years.

QUESTION geography-pp-2019-higher-q123 (LC Geography Higher 2019 — Part Two — Q23, 80 marks)

PART "23" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Water is exchanged continuously between the oceans and the atmosphere, and the way it is evaporated, condensed and carried over the earth's surface controls how much precipitation falls and where.
Aspect 1 – Evaporation and the ocean–atmosphere exchange:
The hydrological cycle is a closed system driven by solar energy and gravity, in which water moves between stores.
Oceans hold about 97% of the earth's water.
Roughly 85 to 90% of global evaporation comes from the oceans, and roughly three-quarters of global precipitation falls on them.
The surplus evaporated over the oceans is carried over the land by wind and returns to the sea through rivers and groundwater.
Evaporation increases with higher temperature, strong sunshine, drier air and wind, and it needs a large supply of water.
Evaporation is greatest over the subtropical oceans under clear skies and steady trade winds and along warm currents such as the Gulf Stream, and it is least over cold polar seas.
Evaporation exceeds precipitation over the subtropical oceans, which helps explain their high salinity.
Warm air can hold about 7% more water vapour for each 1 °C rise in temperature, so warm tropical air carries more moisture.
Water stays in the atmosphere for only about 9 days on average, so the moisture that falls as rain is recycled quickly.
Aspect 2 – Condensation, cloud and types of precipitation:
Condensation is water vapour turning into liquid droplets when air cools to its dew point, and it needs condensation nuclei such as dust, sea salt or smoke.
Rising air expands and cools adiabatically, at about 10 °C per 1,000 m when unsaturated, and this is the main cause of cloud.
Once condensation begins, latent heat slows the cooling to about 5 to 7 °C per 1,000 m.
In convectional rainfall strong heating makes warm air rise and cool to dew point, and cumulonimbus forms and gives heavy afternoon showers, as in the equatorial belt almost every day.
In relief rainfall moist air from the sea is forced up a mountain barrier, so heavy rain falls on the windward slope and the leeward side lies in a rain shadow.
The Western Ghats force the Arabian Sea branch of the monsoon to rise, giving heavy rain on the Konkan and Malabar coasts while the Deccan lies in rain shadow.
In frontal rainfall warm, moist tropical air rises over cold polar air in a depression, and this is Ireland's main source of rain.
In mid-latitude clouds, including Ireland's, droplets grow by the Bergeron–Findeisen ice-crystal process, whereas in warm tropical clouds they grow by collision and coalescence.
Convergence rainfall occurs where the trade winds converge at the ITCZ and are forced to rise.
Aspect 3 – Distribution patterns of precipitation:
The equatorial belt receives over 2,000 mm a year because of low pressure, rising air, convergence at the ITCZ, convection and high evaporation from warm seas and forests, and Manaus in the Amazon receives about 2,300 mm.
The subtropical high pressure belts at about 20 to 30 degrees have very low rainfall because sinking, warming, stable air gives clear skies, and the Atacama in Chile is the driest non-polar desert on earth.
Cold currents chill the air and make it stable, so rain is rare though fog is common, as in the Namib, which receives much of its moisture as fog from the Benguela Current.
The mid-latitudes at about 40 to 60 degrees receive moderate to heavy rain from depressions in the westerlies, and it is heaviest on west coasts facing onshore winds.
Ireland's rainfall is about 750 mm in the driest parts of the east around Dublin and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains, because of south-westerly winds, Atlantic depressions reaching the west first, relief uplift and a rain shadow in the east.
Continental interiors are dry because they lie far from the moisture of the oceans.
Polar regions have very low precipitation because cold air holds little vapour, and Antarctica is the driest continent.
The pressure belts move north and south with the overhead sun and shift the rain belts with them, so the Sahel receives roughly 200 to 600 mm in one short summer wet season when the ITCZ moves north.
Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, where relief rain falls on monsoon air, are among the wettest places on earth.

MODEL ANSWER: The equatorial climate of the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia (Indonesia and Malaysia) is hot, wet and humid all year, which is accounted for by the position of the sun, the ITCZ and convection.
Aspect 1 – Temperature:
Monthly mean temperatures are about 26 to 28 °C all year round.
The annual range is tiny, about 2 to 3 °C.
The daily range is larger than the annual range, with afternoon highs of about 30 to 32 °C and night lows of about 22 to 24 °C.
The sun is always high in the sky and is overhead at the equator at the equinoxes in March and September, so there is little change in heat input through the year.
Day length barely changes, so there are no seasons in the temperate sense.
Nearly vertical rays concentrate the energy on a small area, which explains the high temperatures.
Cloud cover stops temperatures rising much above the low 30s °C.
Clouds trap long-wave radiation, so nights stay warm.
High humidity makes the heat oppressive because sweat evaporates slowly.
Aspect 2 – Precipitation:
Rainfall is over 2,000 mm a year and falls in every month, and Manaus in the central Amazon receives about 2,300 mm.
There are often two wetter peaks after the equinoxes, when the overhead sun and the ITCZ pass over.
The rain is convectional.
Intense heating warms the ground and the moist air above it, which rises.
The rising air cools to dew point and cumulonimbus clouds build up.
Heavy thunderstorms follow in the afternoon almost every day.
The air is very moist because of evaporation from warm seas and transpiration from the forest.
A large share of the Amazon's rain is water recycled from the forest by evapotranspiration.
The trade winds converge at the ITCZ, which lifts and reinforces the moist air.
Aspect 3 – Humidity, pressure and the daily weather cycle:
Relative humidity is often over 80%, and the air is humid at night too.
Pressure is permanently low in the equatorial trough, the ITCZ or doldrums, where the trade winds converge and air rises.
Winds are light and variable.
The daily cycle begins with a clear, calm, humid morning.
Cumulus clouds build up by late morning.
Cumulonimbus and torrential rain with thunder follow in the afternoon.
The sky clears in the evening, and the cycle repeats the next day.
The pattern is the same in every month, so there are no temperate-style seasons.
Some areas have a short drier season when the ITCZ shifts.
