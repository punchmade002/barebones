PART "9B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named developing economy – India:
India is a developing economy that was ruled by Britain until independence on 15 August 1947.
Impact 1 – Deindustrialisation:
Under British rule India's large handloom textile industry, including Bengal cottons and Dhaka muslin, was destroyed by cheap machine-made cloth from the Lancashire mills.
Raw cotton was exported from India to feed those mills, so India became a supplier of raw materials instead of a manufacturer.
Local industries that competed with British imports were destroyed, so many jobs were lost.
Angus Maddison estimated that India's share of world GDP fell from about a quarter around 1700 to about 4% by 1950.
Impact 2 – Cash crops and famine:
Cash crops such as cotton, jute, indigo, tea and opium replaced food crops.
The Permanent Settlement of 1793 fixed land revenue collected through zamindars, and heavy land taxes left peasants in debt and poverty.
Famines occurred under British rule, including the Great Famine of 1876 to 1878.
The Bengal famine of 1943 killed about 3 million people.
Grain was still exported while people starved.
Impact 3 – Drain of wealth and infrastructure:
Revenue, profits and salaries were sent from India to Britain, a drain of wealth described by Dadabhai Naoroji.
Railways, first run from Bombay to Thane in 1853, were built mainly to carry raw materials to the ports and to move troops.
Ports and colonial cities such as Bombay and Calcutta grew, while the interior was neglected.
Impact 4 – Political and social legacy:
At independence literacy was only about 18% and life expectancy was around 32 years.
Partition in 1947 displaced more than 10 million people.
The Kashmir region has been disputed with Pakistan since 1947.
Positive legacy – India inherited a civil service, courts and a parliamentary system from Britain.
English, the language of government and business, has since become an asset for India's IT industry.

PART "9C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Importance for Ireland's environment:
Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, the main greenhouse gas, which enhances the greenhouse effect and causes global warming.
Renewable energy produces little or no CO2, SO2 or NOx, so it reduces Ireland's emissions.
Cutting SO2 and NOx emissions reduces acid rain.
The sun, wind and water are inexhaustible, so renewables do not deplete natural resources.
Replacing peat and coal reduces the landscape damage caused by peat cutting and coal mining.
Importance for Ireland's economy:
Ireland imported about three-quarters of its energy in the early 2020s, at a cost of billions of euro a year.
Renewables reduce this import bill and Ireland's exposure to price shocks.
Once a wind farm is built the fuel is free.
Renewable projects create jobs, often in rural areas.
Green power attracts multinational companies that want renewable electricity.
Interconnectors such as the East-West Interconnector to Wales, opened in 2012, improve energy security.
Ireland must meet its 2030 targets for renewable electricity and emissions, and missing EU targets exposes it to the cost of buying compliance.
Example of renewable energy – wind:
Wind supplied about a third of Irish electricity in the early 2020s.
Strong, frequent Atlantic winds give Ireland an excellent wind resource.
Galway Wind Park in Connemara opened in 2017, and Arklow Bank Wind Park off Co. Wicklow, opened in 2004, was Ireland's first offshore wind farm.
Wind output varies with the weather, so backup or storage is needed.

PART "10B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Dublin:
Dublin is Ireland's capital and its largest urban settlement.
Issue 1 – Urban sprawl:
Urban sprawl is the uncontrolled outward spread of low-density housing over rural land at the edge of a city.
Dublin's sprawl has extended into the commuter counties of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow.
Population growth and high inner-city prices created demand for houses with gardens.
Car ownership and the M50 and other motorways made distant sites accessible.
Developers preferred cheaper greenfield sites that planners had zoned for development.
Long commutes and car dependence cause traffic congestion.
Farmland and habitats are lost as the city spreads.
Services such as roads, water and schools are costly to provide when spread thinly over a large area.
Commuter towns become dormitory towns where residents sleep but work elsewhere, leaving little local activity.
A 2010 survey found almost 3,000 unfinished ghost estates after the 2008 property crash.
Green belts and compact growth on brownfield land, set out in the National Planning Framework, aim to limit sprawl.
Issue 2 – Environmental issues:
Air pollution comes from smoke from coal fires and traffic emissions of nitrogen dioxide and particulates.

PART "10C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Overall change – from emigration to immigration:
Ireland changed from a country of heavy emigration in the 1950s to a country of net immigration in the 1970s and again from the mid-1990s.
Emigration returned in the 1980s and after 2008, so the pattern has reversed with the state of the economy.
In the 1950s – mass emigration to Britain:
Net emigration of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961 was the highest since the 1880s.
Economic stagnation, protectionism, unemployment and the mechanisation of farming pushed people out.
Britain's post-war demand for labour, low fares and the Common Travel Area pulled them to London, Birmingham, Manchester, Coventry and Liverpool.
The State's population fell to about 2.8 million in 1961, the lowest ever recorded.
In the 1960s and 1970s – return migration:
Whitaker's Programme for Economic Expansion of 1958 and the IDA's attraction of foreign firms created jobs and cut emigration.
The 1970s were the first decade of net immigration since the Famine, as emigrants returned to work in new factories.
In the 1980s – renewed emigration:
Recession, high unemployment and high taxes caused net emigration of over 200,000 between 1981 and 1991.
Many were graduates, the so-called brain drain, who went to Britain and the USA.
From 1996 to 2008 – Celtic Tiger immigration:
Net immigration began in 1996 as returning Irish, EU workers and non-EU workers on work permits arrived for jobs in the booming economy.
After EU enlargement on 1 May 2004, Ireland opened its labour market immediately to workers from Poland and the other new members.
Census 2006 recorded about 420,000 non-Irish nationals, about 10% of the population.
From 2008 to 2013 – recession emigration:
The property crash, banking crisis and unemployment caused emigration to peak at about 89,000 in the year to April 2013, with about half Irish nationals.
Young graduates went to Australia, the UK, Canada and the USA, and many recent immigrants also left.
Since the mid-2010s – renewed immigration:
Economic recovery and multinational job growth brought net immigration back, including returning Irish emigrants.
Over 100,000 people from Ukraine came under EU temporary protection by 2024.
Census 2022 counted about 630,000 non-Irish nationals, about 12% of the population, with Polish, UK and Indian nationals the largest groups.
