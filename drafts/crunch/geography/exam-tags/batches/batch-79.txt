PART "5B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – cattle rearing in the West:
The West Region of Galway, Mayo and Roscommon has cattle rearing as its main farming enterprise.
Suckler herds rear calves, and young store cattle are sold through local marts to farmers in the east to be fattened.
Sheep graze the hill commonage of Connemara and the Mayo mountains.
Factor 1 – Climate and soils:
High Atlantic rainfall, over 1,000 mm on lowlands and over 2,000 mm on some mountains, keeps the ground waterlogged.
Blanket bog and heavy gley soils cover much of Connemara and west Mayo, so most land is restricted to grazing.
Leached acidic podzols on the uplands are poor for crops.
Mild winters, warmed by the North Atlantic Drift, mean grass grows well, but the effective grazing season is short because of wet conditions.
Tillage is limited to the better-drained brown earths on the limestone of east Galway and Roscommon.
Exposure to Atlantic gales limits the land that can be farmed.
Factor 2 – Farm structure and economics:
Farms are small and often fragmented, a legacy of the rundale system and of population pressure before the Famine.
Part-time farming is common, with off-farm jobs supplementing low farm incomes.
Farm income depends heavily on EU subsidies.
CAP headage payments from the 1980s raised sheep numbers on the hill commonage, and overgrazing eroded the peat.
The Rural Environment Protection Scheme, from 1994, was a response to this damage.
Distance from markets and ports adds to the cost of moving livestock.
Many farmers are ageing with few young successors, so land is abandoned.
Sketch map of the region:
A sketch map would show Galway, Mayo and Roscommon, Galway city, the Twelve Bens and Mweelrea uplands in the west, and the limestone lowland in the east.
It would also show the Shannon on the eastern boundary and Ballinasloe, site of the October Fair.

PART "5C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Challenge 1 – Sovereignty and Brexit:
Member states pool sovereignty by accepting EU law, and some citizens and governments resist this loss of control over their own laws.
The United Kingdom voted to leave in 2016 and left the EU on 31 January 2020, showing that a member can reverse integration.
Brexit reduced the EU's population and budget and created trade problems for Ireland.
Future treaties and enlargement may need unanimity, so one government can block progress.
Challenge 2 – Economic union:
Economic union includes the single market and the euro, which was introduced in 1999 and as notes and coins in 2002.
Countries in the euro cannot set their own interest rates or devalue their currency to deal with a crisis.
The sovereign debt crisis after 2008 forced bailouts of Greece, Ireland and Portugal and divided richer northern from poorer southern members.
Gaps in income between richer and poorer regions remain, which cohesion funds try to narrow.
Future challenges include completing a banking union and agreeing common rules on debt.
Challenge 3 – Political union:
Political union would mean common foreign, defence and migration policies, but members have different interests and traditions.
The migration crisis of 2015 showed disagreement over sharing refugees between states.
Disputes over the rule of law in Hungary and Poland show that some governments reject common values.
Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 raised the need for a common defence policy and for decisions on Ukraine's candidate status.
Future of the EU and sketch map:
Further enlargement into the Balkans, Ukraine and Moldova would make decisions harder and cost more.
Climate targets and an ageing population add pressure on future budgets.
A sketch map would show the 27 member states, the euro area, Brussels as the main seat of the institutions and Ireland on the western edge.
It would also show the UK outside the EU and the candidate countries to the east.

PART "6B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Region and example – the Ruhr, Germany:
The Ruhr in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany, is a European region of heavy industry centred on Duisburg, Essen, Dortmund and Bochum.
A specific example is the ThyssenKrupp steelworks at Duisburg, which makes steel from iron ore and coal.
Heavy industry first developed here in the nineteenth century on the Ruhr coalfield.
Factor 1 – Transport:
Duisburg lies where the Ruhr meets the Rhine and has one of the largest inland ports in the world.
Bulky iron ore arrives by Rhine barge from Rotterdam in the Netherlands, which keeps the cost of moving heavy raw materials low.
Canals such as the Rhine-Herne Canal and the Dortmund-Ems Canal link the cities and ports.
Dense rail lines and autobahns such as the A40 carry steel to markets.
Good transport helped the steel industry by letting finished products reach markets in Germany and the rest of Europe.
Rail and road links help the region attract new industries as old ones decline.
Local coal was the basis of the industry, so transport links to bring in iron ore were essential.
Factor 2 – Labour:
Mining and steelmaking built up a large, skilled industrial workforce, many of whom came from other parts of Europe.
A dense population provides both workers and a large local market.
When coal mining ended, with the last mine at Prosper-Haniel closing in 2018, workers were retrained for new industries.
Universities such as Ruhr University Bochum, founded in 1962, and TU Dortmund, founded in 1968, supply graduates for modern firms.
Wages are higher than in Eastern Europe, which has led some firms to automate or relocate.
Sketch map of the region:
A sketch map would show the Rhine and Ruhr rivers, Duisburg, Essen, Dortmund and Bochum, and the Rhine-Herne Canal.
It would also show Rotterdam to the west, the main port for imported iron ore.

PART "6C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Region – the West of Ireland:
The West Region of Galway, Mayo and Roscommon had about 486,000 people in Census 2022, about 9% of the State.
Density is low, as Mayo has about 25 people per km² against a State average of about 73.
Galway city and county together had about 278,000 people, and the city is the region's only city and its main centre.
Physical factor 1 – Relief and soils:
The uplands of Connemara and west Mayo have steep slopes, thin soils and exposed rock, so few people live there.
Blanket bog and leached podzols in Erris and Connemara are waterlogged and poor for farming and building.
The limestone lowlands of east Galway and Roscommon have fertile brown earths and gentle relief, so densities are moderate.
Low land beside the Shannon is liable to flooding.
Physical factor 2 – Climate:
High rainfall and exposure to Atlantic gales on the west coast discourage settlement and limit farming.
Mild winters on the coast, warmed by the North Atlantic Drift, mean frost and snow are rare, so coastal settlement persists.
Human factor 1 – Employment and services in Galway city:
Galway city is a growth centre with jobs in medical devices, education, services and tourism, so it draws people in.
Multinationals in the medical devices cluster provide skilled jobs.
Towns such as Castlebar and Ballinasloe act as local centres with services.
Human factor 2 – History and migration:
The Great Famine of the late 1840s hit the West hard, followed by long emigration that left populations far below pre-Famine levels.
Young adults continue to move to Dublin and abroad, leaving an ageing population with many empty houses.
Recent growth comes from return migration, immigration and the expansion of Galway city.
Fishing and the Gaeltacht sustain coastal and island settlement, as on the Aran Islands.
Sketch map of the region:
A sketch map would show Galway city and the Corrib on the coast with the densest dots, Castlebar in Mayo, and Roscommon inland.
It would show sparse settlement on the Twelve Bens and Mweelrea uplands and the Erris bogs.
