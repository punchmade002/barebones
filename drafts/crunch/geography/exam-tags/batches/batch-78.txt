PART "3B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Earthquakes – Pacific Ring of Fire:
Earthquakes are not scattered randomly but occur in narrow belts, the largest of which is the Pacific Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean.
A second belt runs from the Mediterranean through Turkey, Iran and the Himalayas to Indonesia.
A third belt follows the mid-ocean ridges, such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
Reference to plate tectonics:
Plate tectonics states that the crust is broken into rigid plates that are moved by convection currents in the mantle.
Mapping earthquake epicentres showed that they line up along plate boundaries, which explains the global distribution.
Earthquakes occur when plates stick, stress builds up, and the rocks suddenly fracture and release energy.
Destructive boundary – Nazca and South American plates:
At a destructive boundary the denser oceanic Nazca plate is forced down beneath the lighter South American plate in a process called subduction.
Friction along the sinking plate causes earthquakes in the Benioff zone, shallow near the trench and deeper under the continent.
This explains the belt of earthquakes along the west coast of South America, where the Peru-Chile Trench lies offshore and the Andes rise on land.
The same process at the Japan subduction zone caused the Tōhoku earthquake of magnitude 9.0 on 11 March 2011.
Collision boundary – Himalayas:
Where the Indo-Australian plate collides with the Eurasian plate, neither plate can subduct, so the crust is crushed and folded and earthquakes are common.
The Nepal earthquake of 25 April 2015 had magnitude 7.8 and killed about 9,000 people.
Other boundaries:
At transform boundaries such as the San Andreas Fault in California, plates slide past each other and stress builds up to cause earthquakes.
At constructive boundaries such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge in Iceland, plates move apart and shallow earthquakes occur.
Cross-section of a destructive boundary:
A cross-section shows the oceanic plate bending down at an ocean trench and sinking into the mantle at an angle.
The focus is the point underground where the rock breaks, and the epicentre is the point on the surface directly above it.
Value of plate tectonics:
Knowing where plate boundaries lie lets countries in high-risk zones such as Japan enforce earthquake-resistant building codes.
It also explains why Ireland, in the middle of the Eurasian plate, has very few earthquakes.

PART "3C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Mining – Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath:
Tara Mines near Navan in Co. Meath is an underground zinc and lead mine, and it has been in production since 1977.
It is Europe's largest zinc mine and is owned by the Swedish company Boliden.
The ore lies in Carboniferous limestone, which is reached by shafts and tunnels.
Interaction – extraction of rock:
Mining is a human interaction with the rock cycle because people extract rock and minerals from the crust far faster than natural erosion removes them.
Humans therefore act as an exogenic agent, with the removal of rock acting like erosion.
The ore is crushed and ground and the zinc and lead minerals are separated from the waste rock.
Interaction – waste deposition:
The waste rock is dumped in spoil heaps and the fine waste, called tailings, is stored in tailings ponds, which is the human equivalent of deposition.
Tailings ponds take up land and must be lined to stop seepage and capped when closed.
Interaction – exposing fresh rock:
Extraction exposes fresh rock to weathering, which can release metals into water.
At Avoca in Co. Wicklow, centuries of copper mining left acid mine drainage that pollutes the Avoca River with heavy metals.
Negative impacts:
Underground workings can cause subsidence and landscape scars.
Dust, noise and heavy traffic affect people living near a mine.
At Aberfan in south Wales in 1966 a colliery spoil tip collapsed into the village and killed 144 people, 116 of them children.
Positive impacts and management:
Mines bring jobs, exports and local spending.
Environmental licensing and monitoring control waste handling and the quality of water leaving the site.
Lisheen zinc and lead mine in Co. Tipperary closed in 2015 and the site was rehabilitated.
Cross-section of a mine:
A cross-section shows the shaft and tunnels reaching the ore body in limestone, with the spoil heap and tailings pond on the surface.
It also shows ore removed from the crust and waste returned to the surface, which links mining to the rock cycle.

[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.0] Mass movement (also called mass wasting) is the downslope movement of weathered material, such as regolith (the loose broken rock and soil that covers solid rock), soil, rock, mud, peat or snow, under the influence of gravity.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.1] No transporting agent such as a river, glacier or wind is needed, because gravity does the work. Weathering (the breakdown of rock where it lies) supplies the material that moves.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.2] Gravity pulls material downslope. Friction (grip between surfaces), cohesion (particles sticking together) and plant roots resist it. Movement happens when the downslope force exceeds the resistance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.0.3] Processes are classed, so that they can be compared, by speed (slow or fast), by water content (dry or wet) and by type of movement (heave, slide, flow or fall).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.b] The slowest mass movement, moving soil by a few millimetres to centimetres a year. It is too slow to watch, so you recognise it from its effects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.0] Soil particles are lifted at right angles to the slope by wetting (swelling) or by freezing (frost heave, the upward push of freezing ground).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.1] On drying or thawing, the particles drop vertically under gravity, so each cycle moves them a little further downslope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.2] Evidence: terracettes (small steps on grassy slopes), bent tree trunks, tilted fences, walls and poles, soil piled up behind walls, and cracked roads.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.2.3] Diagram: draw the zigzag path of a particle, lifted at right angles to the slope and dropped vertically.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.b] Solifluction is a slow flow of waterlogged soil in cold regions near ice. It works because frozen ground below stops water draining away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.3.1] The saturated layer cannot drain, because the frozen ground beneath is impermeable, so it flows slowly downhill, forming lobes (tongue-shaped bulges) and terraces.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.b] Rockfall and landslide both move solid rock quickly. The difference is that rockfall is a free fall from a cliff, while a landslide is a slide along a plane.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.1] Landslide (rockslide): a mass of rock or debris slides suddenly along a flat plane, such as a bedding plane (a layer boundary in sedimentary rock) or joint (a crack) sloping in the same direction as the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.4.2] Landslides often follow heavy rain, which lubricates the plane so the rock slips more easily, or an earthquake, which shakes it loose.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.0] Saturated material, often clay over or under permeable rock (rock that lets water through), slides along a curved (concave) slip plane and rotates backward.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.5.3] A slump moves on a curved plane. A landslide moves on a flat plane.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.b] These three fast flows each carry a wet or loose mass downhill: mud, volcanic mud, or snow and ice. All are triggered by a sudden supply of water or by disturbance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.0] Mudflow: fine material (clay, silt, weathered rock or volcanic ash) saturated by heavy rain or snowmelt flows rapidly downhill like a liquid, often along valleys. It can bury settlements.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.1] Lahar: a volcanic mudflow of ash and water, from rain or from melted snow and ice on the volcano. It can travel very fast and far. The word is from Indonesia.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.6.2] Avalanche: a sudden, rapid slide of snow and ice down a steep mountain slope, triggered by heavy snowfall, rapid thaw or disturbance.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.b] A bog burst is a fast mass movement of peat, common in the uplands of Ireland. Peat (partly rotted plant matter that builds up in waterlogged ground) on a slope fills with water and then slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.7.1] The peat gains weight, and its base loses grip on the underlying mineral soil or rock. The peat bursts and flows downhill as a black slurry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.b] Whether a slope moves, and how fast, depends on a set of factors. The first three are the slope angle, the water in the ground and what the slope is made of.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.0] Gradient (slope angle): the steeper the slope, the greater the downslope pull of gravity and the faster the movement. Gentle slopes favour creep; steep slopes favour falls and slides.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.1] Water content: some water adds cohesion, but saturation fills the pores (the tiny gaps between particles), adds weight, reduces friction and acts as a lubricant, so material flows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.2] Heavy or prolonged rain and snowmelt trigger most fast movements, because they saturate the slope.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.8.4] Permeable rock over impermeable clay or shale (rock that water cannot pass through) concentrates water at the junction, as on the Antrim coast.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.b] The remaining factors either hold a slope together or remove what supports it. Some are natural, some come from the climate, and one is human.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.0] Vegetation: roots bind soil and plants absorb water, so bare slopes move more easily.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.1] Climate and weathering: freeze-thaw loosens rock, heavy seasonal rainfall adds water, and periglacial climates produce solifluction.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.2] Undercutting: erosion by the sea or a river at the base of a slope removes support, so the material above is left unsupported.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.3] Loading: extra weight at the top of a slope, from rock, buildings or spoil (waste rock), increases the downslope force.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s5.9.5] Human activity also affects slope stability. There is more on this in Human interaction: mass movement.

PART "4B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Tertiary activity – IT in Bengaluru, India:
India is a sub-continental region in South Asia, and its leading tertiary activity is information technology and business-process outsourcing.
Bengaluru, in the south, is called the Silicon Valley of India and is the leading IT centre.
Other IT centres are Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Gurugram and Noida.
Factor 1 – Skilled workforce:
India has a large English-speaking graduate workforce, which suits work for clients in the USA and Europe.
The Indian Institutes of Technology produce skilled engineers.
Bengaluru had an early lead in public-sector science and engineering, which gave it a pool of expertise.
Factor 2 – Low costs and time difference:
Wages are low compared with the USA and Europe, so firms cut costs by outsourcing to India.
The time difference with the USA means work can continue round the clock.
Factor 3 – Government policy:
The 1991 economic liberalisation opened the economy to foreign firms.
Software technology parks were established to support IT firms.
Specific examples of the activity:
Infosys, founded in 1981 in Pune, has its headquarters in Bengaluru.
Wipro is also based in Bengaluru.
Tata Consultancy Services, founded in 1968, is based in Mumbai.
Texas Instruments opened a design centre in Bengaluru in 1985, one of the first multinationals to do so, which attracted others.
Importance and sketch map:
Services are now the largest sector, producing over half of India's GDP.
A sketch map of India would show Bengaluru in the south, with Hyderabad and Chennai nearby, Pune and Mumbai on the west, and Gurugram and Noida near Delhi in the north.
India is among the world's five largest economies by GDP.

PART "4C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Urban area – Dublin:
Dublin is the capital and largest city of Ireland, with about 1.26 million people in the city and suburbs.
Its development has been influenced strongly by its transport links and its location.
Factor 1 – Transport:
Dublin grew at the lowest bridging point of the River Liffey, so roads converged there.
Radial motorways, the M1, M3, M4, M7 and M11, run out from the city and are joined by the M50 orbital motorway, which attracts firms and commuters.
Dublin Port is Ireland's busiest port, and ferries run to Holyhead in Wales, which supported trade, brewing and food processing.
The Dublin Port Tunnel, opened in 2006, takes freight directly onto the motorway network.
Dublin Airport is Ireland's busiest airport and handled over 30 million passengers in 2023, which draws multinationals and tourists.
DART, opened in 1984, and the Luas tram, opened in 2004, let the city spread into suburbs and commuter towns such as Bray and Maynooth.
Transport problems include congestion on the M50 and long commutes from Meath, Kildare and Wicklow.
Factor 2 – Location:
Dublin was founded as a Viking settlement in 841 on the east coast facing Britain and Europe, giving trade access across the Irish Sea.
Dublin Bay shelters the harbour at the mouth of the Liffey.
The surrounding lowlands of Meath and Kildare have fertile brown earths and gentle relief that made building and farming easy and supplied food to the city.
Its central position on routeways helped it become the capital, so government, administration and services concentrate there.
The Wicklow Mountains to the south limited growth in that direction, so the city sprawled north and west.
Planning shaped growth, as the Wright Report of 1967 proposed new towns at Tallaght, Clondalkin and Lucan.
Sketch map of Dublin:
A sketch map would show the Liffey entering Dublin Bay, the M50 ring, the radial motorways, the port and the airport to the north.
It would also show the Wicklow Mountains to the south and commuter towns such as Navan, Maynooth and Bray.
