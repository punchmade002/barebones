PART 8B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named developing economy – India:
India is a developing economy that was ruled by Britain, first through the East India Company and then directly by the Crown as the Raj from 1858, until independence on 15 August 1947.
Challenge 1 – Loss of industry and raw materials:
Under British rule India changed from a major manufacturer into a supplier of raw materials, which left it with a weak industrial base.
India's large handloom textile industry, including Bengal's cottons and Dhaka muslin, was destroyed by cheap machine-made cloth from the cotton mills of Lancashire.
Raw cotton was exported from India to feed those mills, so the profits of manufacturing went to Britain.
Cheap British cloth entered India with low duties while Indian cloth faced heavy duties in Britain, which weakened Indian producers.
Cash crops such as cotton, jute, indigo and tea replaced food crops, which made India vulnerable to hunger and to price changes.
Railways, which began with the line from Bombay to Thane in 1853, were built mainly to carry raw materials from the interior to the ports of Bombay and Calcutta rather than to link Indian regions.
Challenge 2 – Poverty, famine and the drain of wealth:
Colonial rule left India with deep rural poverty and repeated famine because of how land and revenue were organised.
The Permanent Settlement of Bengal in 1793 fixed land revenue and collected it through zamindars, who passed the tax burden on to peasants and left them in debt.
Famines occurred under British rule, including the Great Famine of 1876–78 and the Bengal famine of 1943, which killed an estimated 2 to 3 million people.
Grain was still exported during the Bengal famine while people starved.
Revenue, profits and salaries were sent from India to Britain, a drain of wealth described by Dadabhai Naoroji, so money was not invested in India's development.
Challenge 3 – Political and social legacies:
Colonial rule left India with weak human development and deep political divisions at independence.
Education reached only a small elite, so literacy in the 1951 census was only about 18% and life expectancy was around 32 years.
Partition on 15 August 1947 displaced more than 10 million people and hundreds of thousands were killed in the violence.
The Kashmir region has been disputed with Pakistan since 1947, which has led to continuing tension.
Inequality between regions continues, with literacy of about 94% in Kerala but only about 62% in Bihar in Census 2011.

PART 9B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact – Common Agricultural Policy and farm incomes:
EU policy, above all the Common Agricultural Policy, has transformed primary activity in Ireland by supporting farm incomes and modernising farming.
The CAP was launched in 1962 to raise farm productivity and give farmers a fair standard of living.
After Ireland joined in 1973, guaranteed intervention prices above world levels sharply raised farm prices and incomes.
A guaranteed market opened for Irish beef and dairy produce.
Farms modernised with machinery, fertiliser, silage, land drainage and farm buildings during the 1970s.
Milk quotas from 1984 to 2015 capped milk output and held back Irish dairying for 30 years.
After quotas were abolished on 1 April 2015, Irish milk output rose from about 5.5 billion litres in 2014 to about 8.3 billion litres in 2020, with expansion strongest in Munster.
The MacSharry reform of 1992 replaced price cuts with direct payments and the Fischler reform of 2003 decoupled payments from production.
Ireland's CAP Strategic Plan for 2023–2027 is worth about €9.8 billion, with about 45% for environmental and climate measures.
Impact – Costs and limits of the CAP:
Payments have historically favoured bigger farmers, and many beef and sheep farms depend heavily on direct payments.
Intensification, especially dairy expansion, has damaged water quality through nitrates, and the Nitrates Directive of 1991 limits the organic nitrogen spread per hectare.
The number of farms has fallen and farmers are ageing, with 33% of holders aged 65 or over and only 7% under 35 in the Census of Agriculture 2020.
Impact – Common Fisheries Policy:
The Common Fisheries Policy of 1983 created a common pond of EU waters, so Ireland had to share its rich waters with larger fleets from Spain, France and the Netherlands.
Total Allowable Catches are set each December and divided into national quotas, which gives Ireland a small share relative to the size of its waters.
The Hague Preferences of 1976 give Ireland a smaller cut when catches are reduced, in recognition of its coastal communities' dependence on fishing.
Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland's largest fishing port and specialises in mackerel, herring and blue whiting, supported by EU funding for boats, harbours and processing plants.
Under the EU–UK trade agreement from 1 January 2021 some EU quota was transferred to the UK, and Ireland lost about 26% of its mackerel quota.

PART 9C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Corrib gas field, Co. Mayo:
The Corrib gas field lies about 80 km off north-west Co. Mayo and was discovered in 1996.
Economic interests:
Gas first flowed in December 2015 and was processed at the Bellanaboy terminal.
For a time the field supplied over half of Ireland's gas.
A domestic gas supply improves energy security for a country that imports most of its energy.
The project earned revenue for the State and the companies involved.
Construction of the terminal and pipeline created jobs.
Environmental and local interests:
The original plan piped high-pressure, unprocessed gas onshore near homes at Rossport, which created a safety risk for residents.
The Shell to Sea campaign, from 2005, argued that the gas should be processed at sea rather than onshore.
Sruwaddacon Bay, a protected estuary and Special Area of Conservation, lay on the route of the pipeline.
Protests and outcome:
The Rossport Five were jailed in June 2005 for refusing to obey a court order not to obstruct pipeline work, and they were released after 94 days.
Protests were policed heavily for years and the dispute divided the local community.
The onshore route was changed to keep the pipeline further from houses, and a tunnel was bored under Sruwaddacon Bay.
Shell sold its share in the project in 2018.
Such conflicts are resolved through planning permission, environmental impact assessment, court cases and compromise.
Second example – Amazon rainforest, Brazil:
Cattle ranching is the main driver of clearing in the Amazon, and most cleared land becomes pasture.
The forest stores large amounts of carbon, holds great biodiversity and is home to indigenous peoples, so clearing it conflicts with global environmental interests.
The murder of the rubber tapper Chico Mendes in 1988 shows that the conflict over clearing is also a conflict between people.
