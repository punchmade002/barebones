PART "7B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Developing economy – India:
British rule in India began with the East India Company, passed to the Crown in 1858 and lasted until independence on 15 August 1947.
Impact 1 – Deindustrialisation and cash crops:
India's large handloom textile industry, including Bengal's fine cottons and Dhaka muslin, was destroyed by cheap machine-made cloth from the cotton mills of Lancashire.
Raw cotton was exported from India to Britain to feed those mills, so India became a supplier of raw materials and a market for British goods.
Economic historian Angus Maddison estimated that India's share of world GDP fell from about a quarter around 1700 to about 4% by 1950.
Cash crops replaced food crops, including cotton, jute, indigo, opium and tea from plantations in Assam.
Jute was processed in mills along the Hooghly near Calcutta, but most of the profit went to Britain.
This one-sided trade left India's economy dependent on exporting a few primary products.
Impact 2 – Land taxes, famine and the drain of wealth:
The Permanent Settlement of Bengal in 1793 fixed land revenue and collected it through zamindars, who passed the tax burden on to peasants.
Heavy land taxes left the peasantry in debt and poverty.
Famines occurred under British rule, including the Great Famine of 1876 to 1878 and the Bengal famine of 1943.
The Bengal famine killed about 3 million people, and grain was still exported while people starved.
Revenue, profits and salaries were sent from India to Britain, a drain of wealth set out by Dadabhai Naoroji in 1901.
Impact 3 – Infrastructure and legacies:
The first passenger train ran from Bombay to Thane on 16 April 1853, but the railways were built mainly to carry raw materials to the ports of Bombay and Calcutta and to move troops.
Ports and colonial cities such as Bombay and Calcutta grew, concentrating development on the coast.
British rule left English as the language of government and business, which is now an asset for India's IT industry, and a civil service, courts and a parliamentary system.
At independence literacy was only about 18% and life expectancy about 32 years, showing the lack of investment in people.
Partition on 15 August 1947 displaced more than 10 million people, and the Kashmir region has been disputed with Pakistan ever since.

PART "7C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: EU policy – Common Agricultural Policy (CAP):
The Common Agricultural Policy was launched in 1962 to raise farm productivity, give farmers a fair standard of living, stabilise markets and secure food supplies.
Influence 1 – Higher farm incomes and modernisation:
When Ireland joined on 1 January 1973, guaranteed prices above world levels raised Irish farm prices and incomes sharply.
Intervention buying opened a guaranteed market for Irish beef and dairy produce.
Farms were modernised with machinery, fertiliser, silage, land drainage and farm buildings, remembered as the golden age of the 1970s.
Ireland was a net recipient of EU money until about 2014, receiving over €40 billion more than it paid in, mostly through the CAP.
The MacSharry reform of 1992 replaced price cuts with direct payments, and the Single Farm Payment from 2005 decoupled payments from production.
These payments gave farmers stable incomes, and the Basic Payment Scheme replaced the Single Farm Payment from 2015.
Influence 2 – Limits and costs for Irish farming:
Milk quotas, introduced in 1984, capped each farm's output and held back Irish dairying for 30 years.
When quotas ended on 1 April 2015, Irish milk output rose from about 5.5 billion litres in 2014 to about 8.3 billion litres in 2020.
Intensification damaged water quality through nitrates and raised greenhouse gas emissions as the dairy herd grew.
The Nitrates Directive of 1991 limits the nitrogen spread per hectare, so EU rules directly shape Irish farming.
Payments have historically favoured bigger farmers, and many beef and sheep farms depend heavily on direct payments.
The number of farms has fallen and farmers are ageing, with the Census of Agriculture 2020 counting 135,037 farms and a third of holders aged 65 or over.
Influence 3 – Rural development and the environment:
LEADER, from 1991, funds local rural enterprise such as farm diversification, rural tourism and crafts.
The Rural Environment Protection Scheme ran from 1994 to 2009 and paid farmers for environmentally friendly farming.
Ireland's CAP Strategic Plan for 2023 to 2027 is worth about €9.8 billion, with about 45% for environmental and climate measures.
