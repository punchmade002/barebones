QUESTION geography-pp-2026-higher-q116 (LC Geography Higher 2026 — Part Two — Q16, 80 marks)

PART "16" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Soil characteristics such as texture and structure, organic matter and pH, and water and air content control the processes that form a soil, and so decide which soil type develops.

Aspect 1 – Texture and structure:
Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay in a soil, and it controls how fast water drains through the soil.
Sandy soils are free-draining, so water moves quickly downward and leaching of nutrients dominates their development.
Clay soils hold water and drain slowly, so they become waterlogged and cold, which leads to gleying.
Gleys develop on impermeable boulder clay in the drumlin belt of Cavan, Monaghan and Leitrim, where the hollows between drumlins stay wet.
Waterlogging excludes air, so iron is reduced to grey or blue-grey with orange mottles where air enters along roots.
A loam of roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay balances drainage with water retention, so it develops into fertile brown earths.
Structure is the grouping of particles into peds, and crumb structure in humus-rich topsoil opens pores for air and water.
Compaction by machinery and poaching by livestock destroy peds, so drainage worsens and waterlogging begins.
Clay particles and humus form the clay-humus complex, which holds nutrients that would otherwise be washed out of sandy soils.

Aspect 2 – Organic matter and pH:
Bacteria, fungi and earthworms decompose organic matter into humus, which is the main store of nitrogen in the soil.
Humus binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients, and darkens the topsoil.
Mull humus is mild and crumbly and forms under deciduous woodland and grassland, giving brown earths.
Brown earth, the main Irish lowland soil of areas such as the Golden Vale, has a dark brown loamy A horizon with crumb structure about 20 to 30 cm deep.
Mor humus is acidic, fibrous and poorly decomposed, and forms under conifer forest and heather.
Acid mor humus releases organic acids that carry iron and aluminium down the profile in podzolisation, leaving a bleached E horizon.
Podzols occur on the granite of the Wicklow Mountains, with a pH of about 4 to 4.5 and an iron pan in the B horizon.
Parent rock sets the starting pH, since limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils and granite gives sandy, acidic soils.
Earthworms prefer slightly acidic to neutral soils, so in acid soils fewer earthworms mix in the humus and decomposition is slow.

Aspect 3 – Water and air content:
Soil air fills the pores not occupied by water, so water and air share the pore space and one rises as the other falls.
Where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, as in Ireland, water moves downward and leaches bases such as calcium and potassium from the soil.
Mild leaching gives brown earths, while intense leaching gives podzols.
Where evaporation exceeds precipitation, as on the steppes of Ukraine, calcification produces the chernozem, with a thick black A horizon about 1 m deep.
Well-aerated soils support fast aerobic decomposition, so humification releases nutrients for plants.
Poorly aerated anaerobic soils decompose slowly, so organic matter builds up as peat.
Peat covers about a fifth of Ireland, with blanket bog in Mayo and Connemara and raised bog in Offaly.
In the hot, wet tropics, laterisation leaves deep red latosols rich in iron and aluminium oxides, with little humus because litter decays so fast.
Soil air is richer in carbon dioxide than the atmosphere, which forms weak carbonic acid in soil water and aids chemical weathering.

QUESTION geography-pp-2026-higher-q117 (LC Geography Higher 2026 — Part Two — Q17, 80 marks)

PART "17" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Human activity can damage soil through overcropping and overgrazing, yet it can also conserve and restore soil, as the Dust Bowl, the west of Ireland and the Sahel show.
Aspect 1 – Overcropping, the Dust Bowl:
Overcropping means growing crops too intensively on the same land without fallow, rotation or enough organic matter returned.
Nutrients are removed faster than they are replaced, so humus content falls and the crumb structure of the soil breaks down.
Monoculture takes the same nutrients each year and encourages pests.
The dusty, exhausted soil holds less water and is easily eroded by wind and rain, so yields fall.
In the 1930s the natural grassland of the Great Plains of the USA, including Oklahoma and Kansas, was ploughed up for wheat.
Severe drought then left the bare, dry soil exposed, and strong winds blew it away in huge dust storms.
Many farming families were forced to leave the land.
The US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935 to tackle the damage.
Shelter belts of trees, contour ploughing and crop rotation were promoted to protect the remaining soil.
Aspect 2 – Overgrazing, western Irish commonage:
Overgrazing means keeping more animals on land than its vegetation can support, which exceeds its carrying capacity.
From the 1980s EU headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in the west of Ireland.
The affected areas include Connemara in Co. Galway and the mountains of Mayo, Donegal and Kerry.
Heather and grass were eaten down and their roots died, leaving blanket peat exposed to rain-splash and wind.
Trampling compacted the soil, which reduced infiltration and increased runoff.
Sheet, rill and gully erosion removed topsoil and humus.
Silt washed into rivers harmed salmon and trout spawning grounds.
Payments were changed from per-head to per-area, and Commonage Framework Plans from the early 2000s set lower stocking rates.
REPS, introduced in 1994, paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways.
Aspect 3 – Positive impact, soil conservation in the Sahel:
Human activity can also conserve and improve soil, as farmers in the Sahel have shown after decades of desertification.
In Yatenga, northern Burkina Faso, farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo revived the traditional zaï technique.
Zaï are small pits dug in hard, crusted soil and filled with manure or compost.
The pits catch rainwater and concentrate nutrients around the seed.
Farmers also built stone lines along the contours to slow runoff and trap soil.
In Niger, farmer-managed natural regeneration means farmers protect and prune tree shoots that regrow from old stumps and roots.
This has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, especially in the Maradi and Zinder regions.
The trees shade the soil, cut wind speed and add leaf litter, and Faidherbia albida also adds nitrogen.
The Great Green Wall, launched by the African Union in 2007, aims to restore 100 million hectares of degraded land by 2030, although progress has been slow.
