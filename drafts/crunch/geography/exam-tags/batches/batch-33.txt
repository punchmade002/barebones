PART 23 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The cool temperate oceanic climate of north-west Europe, with Ireland as the main example, is distinctive, and it is examined here through its location, temperature and precipitation.
Aspect 1 – Location:
This climate lies on the west coasts of continents in the mid-latitudes, in the path of the westerly winds.
In Europe it includes Ireland, Britain, north-west France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark and coastal Norway.
Other west coasts at the same latitude in both hemispheres share it, such as British Columbia in Canada and southern Chile.
Prevailing south-westerly winds blow from the subtropical high towards the subpolar low and bring maritime air off the Atlantic.
The region lies near the polar front, where depressions form and are steered across the area by the jet stream.
The Icelandic Low and the Azores High dominate the weather of Ireland.
The North Atlantic Drift, the extension of the Gulf Stream, warms the coast.
Tropical maritime and polar maritime air masses are the most common.
Eastwards the oceanic influence weakens, and the climate grades into the continental climate of central and eastern Europe.
Aspect 2 – Temperature:
Winters are mild and summers are cool, so the annual range is small.
Ireland's mean January temperature is about 4 to 7 °C and its mean July temperature is about 14 to 16 °C.
The annual range is about 10 °C in Ireland, compared with over 60 °C at Verkhoyansk in the continental interior of Russia.
The sea heats and cools slowly, which moderates the seasons.
The North Atlantic Drift warms the winter air and seas.
South-westerly winds carry mild maritime air onto the land.
Frost and snow are infrequent on low ground.
Summers stay cool because the sun is lower in the sky at mid-latitudes than in the tropics.
Cold spells occur when a block of high pressure draws polar continental air from Russia, as in the Beast from the East in 2018.
Aspect 3 – Precipitation:
Rain falls in all months, with a maximum in autumn and winter.
There are about 150 wet days a year in the east and south-east of Ireland and up to about 225 in parts of the west.
Annual rainfall is about 750 mm around Dublin, about 1,000 to 1,250 mm across much of the west and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
Most rain is frontal, because depressions cross the region regularly along the polar front.
Relief rain falls on west-facing mountains, where moist air is forced to rise, cool and condense.
Amounts fall from west to east because depressions and moist Atlantic air arrive in the west first.
The warm, moist air that comes from the Atlantic carries a large amount of water vapour.
The weather is changeable as depressions and ridges of high pressure alternate.
Occasional anticyclones bring dry spells, most often in summer.

PART 24 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Climate is not fixed, and this answer discusses how it changes over time through natural causes and human activity, and how it differs across space.
Aspect 1 – Natural climate change over time:
The Quaternary glaciation began about 2.6 million years ago, and since then the earth has swung between long glacial periods and shorter warm interglacials.
At the Last Glacial Maximum about 20,000 years ago ice covered much of North America and northern Europe, including Ireland, and sea level was about 120 m lower.
Milankovitch cycles in the shape of the orbit, the tilt of the axis and the wobble of the axis, lasting about 100,000, 41,000 and 19,000 to 26,000 years, change the distribution of sunlight and trigger ice ages.
Feedbacks amplify the change, as more ice raises albedo and colder oceans absorb more carbon dioxide.
Volcanic eruptions cool the climate, as sulphate aerosols from Mount Pinatubo in 1991 lowered global temperature by about 0.5 °C for about a year.
The eruption of Tambora in April 1815 led to 1816 becoming the year without a summer, with harvest failures across Europe.
During the Medieval Warm Period, from about AD 950 to 1250, Norse settlers led by Erik the Red settled Greenland from about AD 985.
The Little Ice Age, from the 14th to the mid-19th century, was coldest in the 17th century, and the River Thames froze so that frost fairs were held on the ice, the last in 1814.
Past climates are known from ice cores, tree rings and pollen preserved in peat bogs.
Aspect 2 – Human-caused climate change over time:
The enhanced greenhouse effect comes from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and agriculture, which add gases that absorb more outgoing long-wave radiation.
Carbon dioxide was about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution and is over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s, as measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958.
Global average surface temperature has risen about 1.1 to 1.2 °C since the late 1800s, and 2024 was the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels.
Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13% per decade since 1979, and global sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018.
The ice–albedo feedback, in which melting ice exposes dark ocean that absorbs more heat, is why the Arctic is warming fastest.
Warmer air holds more water vapour, which brings heavier downpours and more severe droughts.
The European heatwave of summer 2003 caused an estimated 70,000 or so extra deaths.
Ireland's average temperature has risen about 0.9 °C since 1900, with wetter winters, more flooding and a summer drought in 2018.
The Paris Agreement of 2015 aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue 1.5 °C.
Aspect 3 – Climate change across space:
Temperature falls and seasonal contrasts grow from the equator to the poles, because the sun's rays strike at a lower angle at higher latitudes.
Continentality increases the annual range from coast to interior, as Ireland's is about 10 °C while Verkhoyansk's is over 60 °C.
Warm currents such as the North Atlantic Drift warm west coasts, while cold currents cool and dry west coasts elsewhere.
Temperature falls with altitude, so high mountains have a vertical sequence of climates, and snow lies on high ground even near the equator.
Across a mountain range the windward side gets relief rain while the leeward side lies in a rain shadow, as the Deccan does in India.
Pressure belts shape climate, as the Mediterranean has a dry summer because it lies under the subtropical high in summer.
In monsoon regions such as India the pressure belts shift with the sun, which brings a wet summer and a dry winter.
Climate boundaries are transition zones that shift, as the Sahel lies between desert and wetter land and moves with the ITCZ, with rainfall of roughly 200 to 600 mm.
Global warming is uneven across space, with a poleward shift of climate belts and an expansion of the subtropical dry zones.

PART 1B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option (i) – Fluvial landform – Delta:
A delta is a fan-shaped or finger-shaped landform built almost entirely by deposition where a river enters a sea or lake.
Role of deposition:
Deposition is the central process because a delta only forms when the river drops more sediment than the sea or lake can remove.
When the river enters the standing water its velocity falls sharply, so it loses the energy needed to carry its load.
The heaviest material is deposited first and the finest last, so the deposits are sorted by size.
In salt water, fine clay particles clump together by flocculation and sink faster, which speeds up deposition.
Conditions needed:
A large load of sediment from the upper and middle courses is needed to keep deposition going.
The water must be calm, with a small tidal range and weak currents, so that the deposits are not washed away.
Bed structure:
Coarser sediment settles on top in flat topset beds.
Deposition at the front builds steeply sloping foreset beds that push the delta out into the water.
The finest clay is carried furthest and settles in flat bottomset beds beyond the delta front.
Distributaries:
As deposits build up they block the main channel, so the river splits into smaller channels called distributaries.
Each distributary deposits its own load along its banks and at its mouth, extending the delta seaward.
Deposition between the distributaries creates new, fertile low-lying land.
Named example – Nile and Mississippi deltas:
The Nile delta in Egypt is an arcuate delta, with a fan shape formed where distributaries spread the deposits evenly.
The Mississippi delta in the USA is a bird's-foot delta, with long fingers of deposits built along its distributaries.
The Aswan High Dam traps silt in Lake Nasser, so less deposition reaches the Nile delta and its coast is now eroding.

PART 1C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact 1 – Caledonian folding:
Caledonian folding about 400 million years ago followed the closing of the Iapetus Ocean as two plates converged.
The collision compressed and folded the rock to build mountains running north-east to south-west.
These mountains include the uplands of Donegal, Mayo and Connemara and the Wicklow Mountains.
The Leinster granite batholith was intruded during this period and now forms the Wicklow Mountains.
Over hundreds of millions of years erosion has worn these old fold mountains down into low, rounded uplands.
Impact 2 – Armorican folding in Munster:
Armorican folding about 300 million years ago came from the collision of Africa with Europe.
It produced a ridge-and-valley landscape running east to west across Munster.
Ridges of Old Red Sandstone survive on the anticlines, such as Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains and the Caha Mountains.
Weaker Carboniferous limestone survives in the synclines as valleys, such as the Blackwater and Lee valleys.
The River Blackwater flows east along a limestone valley and turns sharply south at Cappoquin, giving trellised drainage.
The coastal ends of the valleys in Cork and Kerry were drowned to form rias such as Bantry Bay and the Kenmare River.
The Armorican folds were weaker north of the Munster ridges, so the Midlands remained a low limestone plain.
Impact 3 – Faulting in Donegal:
Shearing along the Donegal tear faults broke the rock along lines running north-east to south-west.
Shattered rock along the faults was eroded by rivers and glaciers into long, straight valleys.
The Gweebarra River valley follows the Gweebarra Fault.
Impact 4 – Volcanic activity in Antrim:
About 60 million years ago the Atlantic opened and magma rose through cracks in north-east Ireland.
The lava spread out and cooled to form the Antrim basalts.
The basalts still cover much of Co. Antrim and form a plateau.
Slow cooling of the lava produced the hexagonal columns of the Giant's Causeway.
