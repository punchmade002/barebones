QUESTION geography-pp-2023-higher-q116 (LC Geography Higher 2023 — Part Two — Q16, 80 marks)

PART 16 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Soil characteristics such as depth, texture, colour, pH and fertility are shaped by processes such as weathering, soil erosion and leaching operating in the immediate environment.
Aspect 1 – Weathering:
Weathering breaks parent material down into regolith, the loose mineral layer from which soil develops.
Physical and chemical weathering produce mineral particles of different sizes, which set the soil's texture.
As minerals weather they release nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium, which make the soil fertile.
Hydrolysis forms clay, the chemically active part of the soil that holds nutrients on the clay-humus complex.
Oxidation of iron compounds gives soils their red and brown colours.
The parent rock controls the result, since limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils such as the thin rendzinas of the Burren, while granite in the Wicklow Mountains gives sandy, acidic soils.
The rate of weathering sets soil depth, so soils are deep in the hot, wet tropics where chemical weathering is fastest, such as the latosols of the Amazon Basin, which are often many metres deep.
Soils are thin and immature in cold or dry regions where weathering is slow, as with the aridisols of the Sahara.
Most Irish soils are young, having formed on glacial deposits laid down about 10,000 years ago, so they often differ from the solid rock beneath them.
Aspect 2 – Soil erosion:
Soil erosion is the removal of soil, especially topsoil, faster than it forms.
Water erodes soil by rain-splash, sheet wash, and rills and gullies, while wind erodes dry, loose, bare soil by deflation.
Natural erosion is slow and balanced by soil formation, but accelerated erosion follows the removal of vegetation.
Erosion thins the profile and removes the humus-rich A horizon with its nutrients, so fertility falls.
It exposes infertile subsoil, which changes the texture and colour of the surface.
It also worsens soil structure and water retention.
Cleared latosols in the Amazon lose their fertility within a few years, erode under heavy rain and can bake into a hard laterite crust.
In the 1930s ploughed prairie soils in the USA were blown away in the Dust Bowl, when drought followed the ploughing of the grassland for wheat.
Eroded material is redeposited elsewhere as alluvium or loess, forming new soils, as with the loess of the Beauce in the Paris Basin.
Aspect 3 – Leaching:
Leaching happens where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, so gravitational water moves down through the soil.
The water dissolves soluble minerals and nutrient bases such as calcium, magnesium, potassium and sodium and carries them to lower horizons or into groundwater.
The upper soil therefore becomes more acidic and less fertile.
Material may be redeposited in the B horizon, so eluviation and illuviation give the soil distinct horizons.
Leaching is common in Ireland's wet climate, where mild leaching carries some iron and clay down and gives brown earths.
Brown earths such as those of the Golden Vale are slightly acidic at about pH 5.5 to 6.5, loamy and fertile.
Intense leaching on uplands such as the Wicklow Mountains produces podzols with a bleached, ash-grey E horizon and strong acidity of about pH 4 to 4.5.
Because leaching removes bases, Irish farmers spread lime to raise pH towards the Teagasc target of about 6.3 for grassland.
Where leaching is weak, as in the dry continental climate of the steppes of Ukraine, calcium stays in the profile and gives the neutral to slightly alkaline, very fertile chernozem.

QUESTION geography-pp-2023-higher-q117 (LC Geography Higher 2023 — Part Two — Q17, 80 marks)

PART 17 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Human activities such as felling forests, intensive farming and industrial development have altered biomes by replacing, degrading and polluting their natural vegetation, soils and water.
Aspect 1 – Felling of the Amazon rainforest:
Cattle ranching is the largest cause of clearance in the Brazilian Amazon, and soya farming has spread north from Mato Grosso, with much of the soya exported as animal feed to Europe and China.
Roads such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway, begun in the early 1970s, opened the forest to settlers, and BR-364 into Rondônia led to a fishbone pattern of clearings along its side roads.
Mining at Carajás, one of the world's largest iron-ore mines, and dams such as Belo Monte on the Xingu River, completed in 2019, have cleared or flooded forest and displaced people.
Clearance peaked at about 27,800 km² in 2004, fell to about 4,600 km² in 2012 and rose again to about 13,000 km² in 2021.
Close to a fifth of the original Amazon forest has been cleared.
Loss of habitat threatens many species that are found only in the region, and fragmentation creates edge effects because the forest edge is drier, hotter and more exposed.
The nutrient cycle is broken, so cleared latosols lose their fertility within a few years, erode under heavy rain and can harden into laterite.
Less evapotranspiration means less rainfall downwind, and burning and decay release the carbon stored in the forest as carbon dioxide.
Indigenous peoples such as the Yanomami lose land, and in 2023 Brazil declared a public health emergency in Yanomami territory after illegal gold mining caused malnutrition and disease.
Aspect 2 – Intensive agriculture:
Intensive farming uses high inputs of capital, machinery, fertiliser, pesticides and water to get maximum output per hectare, and it creates monocultures, larger fields and drained wetlands.
In Ireland hedgerows were removed, fields enlarged, wetlands and callows drained and species-rich meadows reseeded with ryegrass.
Dairy herds expanded after EU milk quotas ended on 1 April 2015.
Mechanised silage cutting destroys the nests of ground-nesting birds, so the corncrake has declined to a small population mainly in north-west Donegal, west Mayo and the Shannon Callows.
Fertiliser and slurry raise nitrate levels in rivers and cause eutrophication of lakes.
Agriculture is the largest source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, at over a third of the total according to the EPA.
The tallgrass prairie of North America has been almost entirely converted to farmland, and only a few per cent survives.
From the 1960s Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to irrigate cotton, so the Aral Sea shrank to a fraction of its size and split, its fishing collapsed and the port of Moynaq was left far from the shore.
Salt and pesticide dust storms now blow from the exposed seabed, and salinisation and waterlogging damaged the irrigated soils.
Aspect 3 – Industrial development:
Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from power stations, industry and vehicles form acid rain that is carried downwind across national borders.
Acid rain acidified thousands of lakes in southern Norway and Sweden from the 1960s to the 1980s and killed the fish in many of them, largely from the emissions of other countries.
In the Black Triangle, where Germany, Poland and the Czech Republic meet, lignite-burning power stations killed large areas of spruce forest in the Ore Mountains, a process known as Waldsterben.
Responses included the Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution of 1979 and flue-gas desulphurisation, and sulphur dioxide emissions in Europe have since fallen by well over half.
Bord na Móna, set up in 1946, harvested milled peat on a large scale from the Midland raised bogs for power stations, which removed peatland habitat.
Peat harvesting ended in 2021 and cutaway bogs are being rewetted, as at Lough Boora Discovery Park in Co. Offaly, which has been converted to wetlands, woodland and amenity land.
The Athabasca oil sands near Fort McMurray in Alberta, Canada, are mined by open-pit methods that strip away boreal forest and muskeg.
The mining produces large tailings ponds of polluted water and high greenhouse gas emissions per barrel.
Oil spills in the Niger Delta have polluted creeks, mangrove forest and farmland, and a UNEP study of Ogoniland in 2011 found that a clean-up could take 25 to 30 years.

PART 18 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The brown earth is Ireland's main lowland soil, a fertile loam formed under deciduous woodland in a mild, wet climate.
Aspect 1 – Location and formation:
Brown earth is the most widespread soil of the Irish lowlands, especially in the south, east and Midlands.
The fertile grassland and tillage soils of the Golden Vale in Tipperary and Limerick, and of the south-east, are brown earths.
It formed under Ireland's mild, wet, cool temperate oceanic climate, with rainfall of roughly 750 to 1,000 mm in the east.
The deciduous woodland of oak, ash, elm and hazel supplied leaf litter that decomposes into mild mull humus.
The parent material is usually glacial till laid down about 10,000 years ago, so the soil is young.
Mild leaching carries some iron and clay down from the A horizon to the B horizon.
Earthworms mix the mull humus through the soil, so the boundaries between the horizons are gradual.
Aspect 2 – Composition:
Like all soils it is made of mineral matter, organic matter, water and air, and a good loam has about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water and 25% air by volume.
Mineral matter from weathered till forms the skeleton of the soil and gives it a loamy texture, a balanced mix of sand, silt and clay.
The organic matter is mull humus, which is dark, crumbly and well decomposed, and it binds particles into crumbs and holds water and nutrients.
Living organisms such as earthworms, bacteria and fungi break down litter and aerate the soil.
Clay and humus form the clay-humus complex, whose negatively charged colloids hold nutrient ions such as calcium, magnesium and potassium so they are not washed away.
Water and air share the pore spaces, so the soil drains freely but still holds capillary water for plants.
Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules of clover, a legume common in Irish grassland, add nitrogen to the soil.
Aspect 3 – Profile and horizons:
The O horizon is a thin layer of mull humus on the surface.
The A horizon is dark brown, loamy and crumb-structured, and roughly 20 to 30 cm deep.
The B horizon is lighter brown, with some iron and clay washed in from above.
The boundary between the A and B horizons is gradual because earthworms mix the soil.
The C horizon is weathered till or rock.
There is no bleached E horizon, because leaching is only mild.
The brown colour comes from humus combined with iron compounds.
Aspect 4 – Characteristics and use:
Its loamy texture and crumb structure give good drainage and aeration and let roots penetrate easily.
It is slightly acidic, at roughly pH 5.5 to 6.5, which suits most crops and earthworms.
It is fertile because it is base-rich and has a moderate organic content.
Where pH falls below the Teagasc targets of about 6.3 for grassland and 6.5 for tillage, farmers spread lime to raise it.
Farmers add nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium to maintain fertility.
Brown earths are used for grassland and tillage in the Golden Vale and the south-east.
Grey-brown podzolics are slightly more leached relatives, with clay washed into the B horizon, and they are typical of the limestone-rich till of the east Midlands in Meath and Kildare.
