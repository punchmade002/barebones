PART "10B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Examples – Japan and Niger:
Japan, with low fertility and low mortality, and Niger, with very high fertility, show how changing rates shape population structure.
Impact of changing fertility rates:
A fall in fertility narrows the base of a population pyramid, while high fertility gives a very wide base.
Niger has a total fertility rate of about 6 children per woman, so its pyramid has a very wide base and a median age of about 15.
Niger's population grows by over 3% a year, and its large number of children creates high demand for schools and maternity care.
Japan's total fertility rate is about 1.2, far below the replacement level of 2.1, so its pyramid has a narrow base.
With fewer children there are fewer future workers, so Japan's population peaked at about 128 million around 2008 to 2010 and is now falling.
Fertility falls as female education and paid work, contraception and urbanisation lead to later marriage and smaller families.
A shrinking workforce brings labour shortages and school closures in Japan.
Responses to low fertility include immigration, pro-natalist incentives to raise birth rates, and later retirement.
Impact of changing mortality rates:
Falling mortality rates widen the middle and top of a pyramid and raise the share of older people.
In stage 2 of the Demographic Transition Model the death rate falls fastest, because of clean water, vaccination, better food and medical care, while birth rates stay high, so population grows rapidly as in Niger.
Japan has the world's oldest population, with about 29% aged 65 or over in 2023, because low death rates give long life expectancy.
Japan's pyramid is therefore top-heavy, with a narrow base and a wide top.
There are more women than men at the oldest ages because women live longer.
An ageing population raises pension and health costs, shrinks the tax base and raises the old-age dependency ratio.
Higher death rates from conflict, famine and diseases such as HIV/AIDS reduce the numbers in affected age groups and produce notches in a pyramid.
Falling infant mortality removes the need for insurance births, so over time fertility falls as well.

MODEL ANSWER: Developed region – Ireland, from the West to Dublin:
Ireland is a developed region where rural-to-urban migration has moved people from the West and North-West to Dublin and the other cities.
Impact on rural areas – decline in the West and North-West:
Migration to cities has caused population ageing and decline in rural areas of the West and North-West.
Young adults leave for jobs and college, so the rural population ages and dependency ratios rise.
Leitrim had about 155,000 people in 1841 and only about 35,000 in Census 2022, making it the least populous county.
With fewer young adults there are fewer births, which leads to the closure of schools.
Post offices, Garda stations, shops and pubs close and houses are left derelict.

There is cultural loss, including the decline of the Gaeltacht.

Impact on urban areas – growth of Dublin:
Migration has caused rapid growth in Dublin, which brings both opportunity and strain.
The Greater Dublin Area had about 2.1 million people in Census 2022, about 40% of the State's population.
A large labour pool attracts multinational companies and service firms.
Economies of scale support transport, universities and hospitals in the city.
There are housing shortages, high rents and prices, and homelessness.
Urban sprawl into Meath, Kildare and Wicklow has created commuter towns such as Navan and Naas, with long commutes and traffic congestion.

PART "11B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – São Paulo, Brazil:
São Paulo is a developing world city of about 11.5 million people, and about 21 million in its metropolitan region, according to Census 2022.
Traffic congestion:
Rapid growth in the number of people and vehicles overloads a road network that cannot cope, so congestion is permanent.
Car ownership has risen as the growing middle class can afford cars, while the road and metro network has lagged behind this growth.
The growth of the outer favelas has lengthened commutes, because many workers live on the edge of the city and make long journeys to jobs in the centre and industrial areas, which adds to peak-hour traffic.
Trains and buses are overcrowded at peak times, and the metro, whose first line opened in 1974, has not kept pace with growth.
Journeys take longer, which wastes time and reduces productivity.
Lorries carrying goods for the city's large industrial and commercial economy add to the traffic on the main roads.
Narrow streets in the old centre were not built for the volume of modern traffic, so they jam easily.
The city introduced the rodízio in 1997, which bans cars from central streets at rush hours according to the last digit of the number plate.
Urban sprawl:
Rural-to-urban migration, notably of nordestinos from the drought-prone North-East, and natural increase have fed the city's growth.
Migrants with little money and no legal land settle in favelas at the urban edge on marginal land such as steep slopes and floodplains.
The built-up area has spread far beyond the original city, so the metropolitan region has about 21 million people.
Industry has spread into the ABC region, where car plants such as Volkswagen's at São Bernardo do Campo were built.
Better-off families move out to gated condominiums such as Alphaville on the edge of the metropolitan region, which spreads the city further.
Sprawling informal settlements lack piped water and sewers, which spreads disease.
New housing on the edge of the city takes over farmland and green space as the city spreads outwards.
Building on floodplains and steep slopes at the edge removes vegetation, so heavy summer rain brings floods and landslides to the outer districts.
Further example – Kolkata, India:
In Kolkata, planned townships such as Salt Lake City and New Town have been built beyond the crowded core to take growth.

PART "11C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Reason 1 – Bridging point on the River Moy:
Foxford developed at a bridging point where routes cross the River Moy.
On the map the River Moy (An Mhuaidh) flows past the town centre at G 268 041, where the N26, R318 and N58 meet.
The river was a barrier, so travellers had to cross at this one place and traffic was funnelled to the crossing.
Traders and travellers stopping at the crossing encouraged inns, markets and shops to grow around it.
A bridge made the town accessible from both banks of the river, which widened its hinterland.
The River Moy also supplied water and fish to the early settlement.
Reason 2 – Route focus of roads:
Foxford developed as a route centre where several roads meet.
The N26 national primary road reaches the town at G 268 042 from the north-west and continues eastwards, the R318 joins it from Lough Cullin at G 255 042, and the N58 runs south towards Ballylahan Bridge at G 275 997.
Roads converging on the town gave it high accessibility, so people from the surrounding area could reach it easily.
High accessibility attracted shops, services and markets, so Foxford became a central place serving a wide hinterland.
Through traffic on the N26 brought extra customers, which supported services such as the post office at G 271 043 and the schools.
The N26 links Foxford to larger towns and carries visitors, which supports its tourism function.
Reason 3 – Low-lying farmland and water supply:
Foxford developed as a market centre in the middle of low-lying, fertile farmland with a good water supply.
The land around the town is flat and low, with spot heights of 21 metres at G 268 035 and 13 metres at G 274 032, and many scattered farmhouses lie around it, while Lough Cullin lies to the west and the hill of Carranarah at 197 metres rises to the south-east at G 286 029.
