QUESTION geography-pp-2020-higher-q113 (LC Geography Higher 2020 — Part Two — Q13, 80 marks)

PART 13 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Fair trade contributes to sustainable development by giving small producers in poor countries a fairer and more secure income, by empowering their communities and by protecting the environment.
Aspect 1 – Economic contribution through fairer income:
Commodity prices are low and unstable, and small farmers receive only a tiny share of the final price of the goods they grow.
Fair trade sets a Fairtrade Minimum Price, a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production, so farmers are protected when world prices collapse.
When the market price is above the minimum, producers receive the market price plus the premium, so the floor never lowers their income.
Long-term contracts and pre-finance give producers credit before the harvest, so they avoid dependence on moneylenders.
Higher and more stable income gives farmers money to invest in their farms and to plan for the future.
Kuapa Kokoo, a Fairtrade cocoa co-operative in Ghana founded in 1993, part-owns Divine Chocolate, which was set up in Britain in 1998.
The farmers therefore share in the profits of the brand as well as receiving the Fairtrade price, so they capture more of the value added.
The Oromia Coffee Farmers Co-operative Union in Ethiopia sells members' coffee directly to buyers, cutting out middlemen who take a large share of the value.
Child labour can be reduced when families earn a fairer income, because children are less needed to supplement earnings.
Aspect 2 – Social contribution through empowerment:
The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative, and members decide democratically how it is spent.
Kuapa Kokoo has spent premium money on wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
The premium of the Oromia union has paid for schools, clinics and water in coffee-growing communities.
Producers are organised in democratic co-operatives, which empowers them to take decisions together instead of depending on middlemen.
Women's participation in co-operatives gives women a voice and an income, and Kuapa Kokoo encourages women to take leadership roles in its village societies.
Fairtrade labour standards ban forced and child labour, protect the freedom to join unions and require safe conditions.
Producers learn processing and marketing skills, which helps them to move up the value chain.
Close to 2 million farmers and workers in about 70 countries belong to Fairtrade-certified producer organisations, so the benefits reach many communities.
The documentary Black Gold of 2006 told the story of the Oromia union and its leader Tadesse Meskela, raising awareness of coffee farmers' position.
Aspect 3 – Environmental sustainability and its limits:
Fairtrade environmental standards restrict pesticides, protect water and forests and ban GM crops.
Organic farming and reduced clearing of land help to protect soil and biodiversity on certified farms.
Côte d'Ivoire has lost most of its forest since 1960, much of it to cocoa farms, which shows the pressure on forests that these standards aim to reduce.
Fair trade raises consumer awareness in rich countries and pressures large firms to change how they buy.
Clonakilty in County Cork became Ireland's first Fairtrade town in 2003, and towns, schools and universities can apply for Fairtrade status.
Fairtrade Fortnight takes place each year in late February and March, and supermarkets stock Fairtrade ranges.
Fair trade has limits because it is only a tiny share of world trade and certification costs exclude the poorest farmers.
Benefits go mainly to co-operative members rather than hired labourers, and a 2014 study of Ethiopia and Uganda found that hired workers on some certified farms were paid less than those on comparable non-certified farms.
Fair trade does not change trade rules such as subsidies and tariff escalation, which discourage producers from processing their own crops, so its contribution depends on its scale and on wider changes.

QUESTION geography-pp-2020-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2020 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART 14 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Empowering people links economic growth with human development because growth only becomes better health, education and freedom when people control resources and have a voice, as national debt and aid programmes show.
Aspect 1 – National debt as a block to empowerment:
Economic growth does not automatically bring human development, because its benefits can be captured by an elite and debt repayments can drain public money away from people.
In the 1970s oil-price rises gave banks large deposits, which they lent to developing countries as petrodollars.
Interest rates soared about 1980 and commodity prices fell, making the loans far harder to repay, and Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts.
Debt creates a cycle of poverty, because repayments take up government revenue so less is spent on health, schools and water.
Poor health and education keep productivity and incomes low, so low tax revenue and export earnings force more borrowing and the debt grows.
Structural adjustment programmes tied to IMF and World Bank loans forced governments to cut public spending, introduce fees for schools and clinics and remove food subsidies, and the poorest people and women bore most of the cost.
UNCTAD found in 2023 that about 3.3 billion people live in countries that spend more on interest payments than on either health or education.
Aspect 2 – Debt relief as a route to empowerment:
Campaigns such as Jubilee 2000 pressed for the cancellation of poor countries' debts, and the HIPC Initiative of 1996 offered relief on condition that savings went to poverty reduction.
The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative of 2005 followed the G8 summit at Gleneagles, backed by Make Poverty History and Live 8.
More than 30 countries, most of them in Africa, received HIPC relief.
Uganda introduced Universal Primary Education in 1997 and in 1998 became the first country to receive HIPC relief, with savings going to schools, health and water.
Tanzania abolished primary school fees in 2001, so more children could attend school.
Zambia reached HIPC completion point in 2005 and in 2006 abolished user fees for health care in rural areas.
The enhanced HIPC framework of 1999 deepened the relief offered, so that more of the debt of the poorest countries was cancelled.
Aspect 3 – Aid programmes that empower people:
Aid can be emergency, long-term development, bilateral, multilateral or NGO aid, and long-term aid that builds skills does most to empower people.
Schools, clinics, wells and vaccination programmes funded by aid improve health and education, which are the core of human development.
Training and support for women's groups and small businesses build skills and incomes.
Irish Aid began its bilateral programme in 1974 and works with partner countries such as Uganda, Tanzania, Malawi and Ethiopia.
Ireland's aid is untied, so it can be spent where it helps people most.
The Irish NGO Bóthar, founded in Limerick in 1991, gives farm animals to poor families, who pass on the first female offspring to another family.
NGOs work close to communities and empower them through co-operatives, women's groups and microcredit.
Aspect 4 – Aid that fails to empower people:
The UN target of 1970 asks rich countries to give 0.7% of their gross national income as aid, but Ireland has not reached it.
Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, so money returns to donor firms.
UK aid for the Pergau Dam in Malaysia was linked to a British arms deal, and in 1994 the English High Court ruled that it was unlawful because it was not for sound development.
Corruption and elite capture can divert aid to powerful groups rather than the poor.
Dependency can develop when recipients rely on aid rather than building their own economies.
Haiti cut its rice tariff in the mid-1990s under pressure from lenders, cheap subsidised US rice flooded in, and many Haitian rice farmers were ruined.
Effective aid is untied, targeted at the poorest, locally owned and transparent and it supports self-help, which is what links growth to human development.

PART 15 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: In an interdependent global economy countries rely on each other, so global environmental problems and social and political decisions in one area have effects in other areas.
Aspect 1 – Global environmental issue of deforestation:
The Amazon is the world's largest tropical rainforest, with about 60% of it in Brazil, and close to a fifth of the original forest has been cleared.
Cattle ranching is the largest cause, and soya, much of it exported as animal feed to Europe and China, has spread north from Mato Grosso.
Loss is driven largely by demand in other regions for beef, soya, palm oil, timber, cocoa and minerals, so consumers elsewhere are part of the chain.
The carbon released by clearance adds to global warming, which affects every country.
Reduced rainfall recycling affects farming beyond the forest, and biodiversity and indigenous peoples are threatened.
Indonesia and Malaysia produce about 85% of the world's palm oil, and the burning of cleared peat swamp forest caused regional haze in 1997 and 2015 that affected Singapore and Malaysia.
Responses involve other countries, such as the Amazon Fund of 2008 with Norway as the largest donor and the EU Deforestation Regulation adopted in 2023.
Aspect 2 – Global environmental issue of global warming:
Greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels in industrialised and emerging economies, from deforestation and from agriculture cause global warming.
Atmospheric carbon dioxide has risen from about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s.
Countries that emitted least suffer most, and Bangladesh in the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta faces floods, cyclones and sea-level rise.
Small island states such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives are threatened by sea-level rise.
Pakistan's floods of 2022 affected about 33 million people and flooded about a third of the country, although Pakistan produces under 1% of global greenhouse gas emissions.
Drought in the Sahel, rising food prices when harvests fail and climate-driven migration spread the effects across regions.
The Paris Agreement of 2015 aims to keep warming well below 2 °C, and the Loss and Damage Fund agreed at COP27 in 2022 helps countries hit by climate damage.
Aspect 3 – Political decisions and refugees from Syria:
Protests in March 2011 were met with force by President Bashar al-Assad's government, and this political decision led to civil war.
At the height of the war over half of Syria's pre-war population of about 22 million were displaced, including over 6 million refugees abroad.
Turkey hosted over 3 million Syrian refugees, and Lebanon has the most refugees per head of population of any country.
About 1.3 million asylum applications were made in the EU in 2015, after Germany decided in August 2015 to admit refugees.
Hungary built a border fence with Serbia in 2015, a political decision that redirected the movement of refugees.
The EU–Turkey statement of March 2016 returned irregular migrants from the Greek islands to Turkey, and the EU was to provide €6 billion for refugees in Turkey.
The Rohingya were left stateless by Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law, and from August 2017 about 740,000 were driven into Bangladesh, so one state's decision placed a burden on another.
Aspect 4 – Social decisions and supply chain rights:
The collapse of Rana Plaza near Dhaka on 24 April 2013 killed about 1,130 garment workers whose factories supplied Western brands, including Primark.
The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh was signed in May 2013 in response.
Amnesty International documented child labour in the hand-dug cobalt mines of the DR Congo in 2016, which supply minerals used in electronics sold worldwide.
Migrant workers in Qatar were tied to employers under the kafala system while building World Cup stadiums, and Qatar introduced reforms in 2020 after criticism.
The US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, in force from June 2022, presumes that goods made wholly or partly in Xinjiang use forced labour and bans their import unless proven otherwise.
In July 1984 Mary Manning, a worker at Dunnes Stores in Dublin, refused to handle South African grapefruit in protest against apartheid.
In 1987 the Irish government banned imports of South African fruit and vegetables, so a decision in one country put pressure on the policies of another.
