QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q116 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q16, 80 marks)

PART "16" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Soil characteristics such as texture, structure, water content, pH and organic content control the processes that form a soil, and so they decide which type of soil develops.
Aspect 1 – Texture and structure:
Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay in a soil, and it controls how quickly water drains through it.
Sandy soils are free-draining, so water moves quickly downwards and carries nutrients away by leaching.
Where leaching is intense on acidic, sandy granite parent material, as in the Wicklow Mountains, podzolisation produces a podzol.
Clay soils hold water and drain slowly, so they become waterlogged and favour gleying.
Gleys have developed on the heavy, impermeable boulder clay of the drumlin belt in Cavan, Monaghan and Leitrim.
A loam, with roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay, combines good drainage with good water and nutrient retention, so it supports fertile brown earths such as those of the Golden Vale.
Structure is the way particles are grouped into peds, and the crumb structure of humus-rich topsoil opens pores for air and water, which helps a soil to develop.
A platy or compacted structure hinders drainage and root growth, so water lies on the surface and the soil may gley.
Compaction and poaching by livestock destroy peds, which reduces infiltration and increases runoff and the erosion of topsoil.
Aspect 2 – Water content and air:
Water enters the soil as precipitation, and where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, as in Ireland, gravitational water moves down and leaches the soil.
Mild leaching produces brown earths, while intense leaching produces podzols.
In podzolisation, organic acids carry iron and aluminium down from the A/E horizon, leaving a bleached ash-grey layer above a dark reddish-brown B horizon.
A thin iron pan may form in the B horizon, which blocks drainage and roots so that water lies above it and peat may form.
Where evaporation exceeds precipitation, capillary action raises water to the surface and leaves salts and lime there, as in hot deserts where salinisation occurs.
In the dry continental climate of the Ukrainian steppe, calcification moves calcium a short distance down and builds a thick black A horizon over lime nodules in the B horizon, forming a chernozem.
Air fills the pores not occupied by water, and in well-aerated soils aerobic organisms decompose litter quickly into humus.
In poorly aerated, anaerobic soils decomposition is slow, so organic matter builds up as peat.
Anaerobic conditions also reduce iron from its red ferric form to its grey or blue-grey ferrous form, so a gley is grey with orange mottles where air enters along roots.
Aspect 3 – pH and organic content:
pH measures acidity or alkalinity and decides which organisms can live in the soil.
Slightly acidic to neutral soils on limestone-rich till support earthworms, which mix litter into mull humus and build brown earths with a crumb structure.
Acid soils on granite or sandstone, or under conifers and heather, have few earthworms, so fibrous, poorly decomposed mor humus forms.
Mor humus releases organic acids into percolating water, and these drive podzolisation.
Heavy rain leaches out bases and makes soils more acidic, which slows humification.
Organic content darkens the soil, binds particles into crumbs, holds water and nutrients and releases nutrients slowly.
Dense, deep grass roots supply large amounts of organic matter, so chernozems develop a topsoil of about 1 m of black, humus-rich soil.
In the tropical rainforest litter decays so fast that latosols have very little humus, and nearly all the nutrients are held in the living vegetation.
The clay-humus complex holds nutrient ions such as calcium, magnesium and potassium, so base-rich soils support the vegetation that supplies the litter that feeds the soil.

QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q117 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q17, 80 marks)

PART "17" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The tropical rainforest biome of the Amazon Basin is characterised by its hot, wet climate, its infertile latosol soils and its layered, adapted flora.
Aspect 1 – Climate:
The tropical rainforest lies within about 10 degrees of the equator, in the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia.
Mean monthly temperatures are about 26 to 28 °C all year, and the annual range is only about 2 to 3 °C.
Temperatures stay constant because the sun is always high in the sky, overhead at the equator at the equinoxes, and day length barely changes.
Annual rainfall exceeds 2,000 mm and falls in every month, and Manaus in the central Amazon receives about 2,300 mm.
The rain is convectional, as intense morning heating makes moist air rise and cool, and cumulonimbus clouds then bring heavy thunderstorms almost every afternoon.
The ITCZ, a low-pressure belt of rising air where the trade winds converge, drives this uplift.
Relative humidity is often over 80%.
A large share of the Amazon's rain is water recycled from the forest by evapotranspiration.
The daily temperature range is greater than the annual range, and there are no seasons in the temperate sense.
Aspect 2 – Soils:
Rainforest soils are latosols, which are deep, red or yellow, acidic and infertile.
They form by laterisation, as the hot, wet climate causes intense chemical weathering to a depth of many metres.
Silica and bases are leached out, leaving insoluble iron and aluminium oxides that give the red or yellow colour.
Litter decomposes within weeks in the heat and humidity, so very little humus builds up.
Most nutrients are held in the living vegetation rather than the soil, so the nutrient cycle is rapid and closed.
Shallow roots and mycorrhizal fungi take up nutrients almost at once and pass them to the trees.
The profile has a thin litter layer, a thin leached A horizon, a very deep red B horizon of sesquioxides and deep weathered rock below.
When the forest is cleared the soil loses its fertility within a few years, erodes under heavy rain and can bake into a hard laterite crust.
Shifting cultivation, or slash and burn, traditionally used the soil for a few years before moving on.
Aspect 3 – Flora:
The vegetation is a dense, evergreen, broadleaved forest arranged in distinct layers.
Emergent trees such as the kapok and the Brazil nut tree rise above the canopy to about 45 to 60 m or more.
The canopy, at about 25 to 40 m, is a dense, continuous layer that intercepts most of the sunlight and rain.
Only about 2% of the sunlight reaching the canopy arrives at the dark forest floor.
Buttress roots support tall, shallow-rooted trees such as the kapok.
Drip tips and waxy leaves shed the heavy rain quickly.
Lianas are woody climbers that climb trees to reach the light, and epiphytes such as orchids and bromeliads grow on branches and take moisture from the air and rain.
Hundreds of tree species can grow in a single hectare of Amazon rainforest, compared with a handful in an Irish wood.
Strangler figs begin as epiphytes and eventually enclose their host tree.

QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q118 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q18, 80 marks)

PART "18" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Human activities change soil characteristics such as structure, humus content, fertility and water retention, with overgrazing and desertification degrading soils and conservation methods protecting and restoring them.
Aspect 1 – Overgrazing:
Overgrazing means keeping more animals on land than its vegetation can support, which exceeds its carrying capacity.
Vegetation is eaten down and roots die, so the soil is exposed to rain-splash and wind.
Less litter means less humus, so the organic content and crumb structure of the soil fall.
Trampling compacts the soil, which on wet Irish ground is called poaching, and this reduces infiltration and increases runoff.
Sheet, rill and gully erosion then remove the topsoil and its nutrients.
From the 1980s EU headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in Connemara and the mountains of Mayo.
This stripped the heather and grass and eroded blanket peat, and the silt washed into rivers harmed salmon and trout spawning grounds.
Bare peat surfaces erode very quickly once the vegetation is gone.
In Iceland, sheep grazing since the settlement of about AD 874 prevented birch regrowth, and the loose volcanic-ash andosols were then easily blown and washed away.
Aspect 2 – Desertification:
Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas, resulting from climatic variations and human activities.
The Sahel is a semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, stretching from Senegal to Sudan, with only about 200 to 600 mm of rain in one short wet season.
Population growth, overgrazing around wells, overcropping and fuelwood cutting left the soil bare.
Droughts in the 1970s and 1980s then left bare soil that was eroded by wind and by the heavy rain of the wet season.
Topsoil and humus are lost, so fertility falls.
Surface crusting forms, so rain runs off instead of soaking in and the soil's water retention is reduced.
Wind removes the finest particles and blows sand and dust away, so the remaining soil is coarser and poorer.
Where irrigation is poorly managed, as in Punjab and Haryana in India, salinisation leaves salt crusts at the surface.
In extreme cases the ground becomes bare and sterile.
Aspect 3 – Conservation methods:
Contour ploughing across a slope makes furrows trap water instead of channelling it downhill, which reduces runoff and soil loss.
Terracing cuts steep slopes into level steps, as in the rice terraces of Banaue in Luzon, Philippines, which have been farmed for centuries.
Strip cropping alternates strips of crops and grass, and the grass traps soil washed or blown from the cropped strip.
Shelter belts of trees and hedges cut wind speed and reduce wind erosion.
Crop rotation, fallow and green manure such as clover restore nutrients and humus.
Manure and compost build up humus and improve soil structure.
Liming raises the pH of acid soils, field drainage improves gleys, and deep ploughing breaks the iron pan of a podzol.
In Yatenga in Burkina Faso, farmers revived zaï planting pits, which catch rainwater and concentrate manure around the seed, and built stone lines along the contours to slow runoff.
In Niger, farmer-managed natural regeneration has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, as trees shade the soil, cut wind speed and add leaf litter.
