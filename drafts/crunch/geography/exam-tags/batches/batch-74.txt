PART 17 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The tropical rainforest biome of the Amazon Basin has a hot, wet equatorial climate, and that climate controls the formation of its deep, infertile soils and its dense, layered vegetation.
Aspect 1 – Climate of the Amazon rainforest:
The Amazon Basin is the largest tropical rainforest, covering about 5.5 million km² with roughly 60% of it in Brazil.
Mean monthly temperatures are about 26 to 28 °C all year, with an annual range of only about 2 to 3 °C.
The range is small because the sun stays high in the sky and day length barely changes near the equator.
The sun is overhead at the equator twice a year, at the equinoxes in March and September.
Annual rainfall is over 2,000 mm and falls in every month, and Manaus receives about 2,300 mm.
Intense morning heating warms the ground and the moist air above it, which rises.
The rising air cools and condenses into cumulonimbus clouds, which bring heavy afternoon thunderstorms almost daily.
The ITCZ, a low-pressure belt of rising air along the equator, drives this convectional rainfall.
Relative humidity is often over 80%, and a large share of the Amazon's rain is recycled by evapotranspiration from the forest.
Aspect 2 – Impact of the climate on soils:
The heat and heavy rain produce a deep, red, infertile soil called a latosol through the process of laterisation.
High temperatures and heavy rain cause intense chemical weathering to great depth, so latosols are often many metres deep.
Heavy rain leaches silica and bases out of the soil.
Insoluble iron and aluminium oxides are left behind, which gives the soil its deep red or yellow colour.
Litter decomposes within weeks in the heat and humidity, so very little humus builds up.
The soil is therefore acidic, clay-rich and low in nutrients.
Most nutrients are held in the living vegetation rather than in the soil, so the nutrient cycle is rapid and closed.
Shallow roots and mycorrhizal fungi take up nutrients from the litter almost at once and pass them to the trees.
When the forest is cleared the soil loses its fertility within a few years and erodes under heavy rain.
Exposed iron-rich soil can bake into a hard brick-like laterite crust.
Aspect 3 – Impact of the climate on vegetation:
Constant warmth and moisture produce a dense, evergreen, broadleaved forest with very high biodiversity arranged in layers.
Growth continues all year with no seasons, so the trees are evergreen and shed and replace their leaves all year.
Hundreds of tree species can grow in a single hectare of Amazon rainforest.
Emergent trees such as the kapok and the Brazil nut tree rise above the canopy to about 45 to 60 m.
The canopy at about 25 to 40 m is a dense continuous layer that intercepts most of the sunlight and rain.
Only about 2% of the sunlight reaching the canopy arrives at the forest floor, so the shrub layer is sparse and understorey plants have large leaves.
Drip tips and waxy leaves shed the heavy rain quickly.
The nutrient-poor soils mean roots are shallow, so tall trees such as the kapok have buttress roots for support.
Lianas climb the trees to reach the light, and epiphytes such as orchids and bromeliads grow on branches and take moisture from the air and rain.
Bromeliads hold pools of water in their leaf rosettes, which tree frogs use.

QUESTION geography-pp-2022-higher-q118 (LC Geography Higher 2022 — Part Two — Q18, 80 marks)

PART 18 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Desertification degrades soil through human pressure and drought, while conservation protects and restores soil, as the Sahel, the Dust Bowl and Irish and Icelandic examples show.
Aspect 1 – Causes of desertification in the Sahel:
Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas resulting from climatic variations and human activities.
The Sahel is the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara, running from Senegal and Mauritania through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad to Sudan, with rainfall of roughly 200 to 600 mm.
The rain falls in one short summer wet season when the ITCZ moves north, and it is highly unreliable from year to year.
Droughts in the 1970s and 1980s left bare soil that was eroded by wind and by the heavy rain of the wet season.
Population growth led to more cattle, goats and sheep concentrated around wells and boreholes, which caused overgrazing.
Shorter fallow periods and cash cropping led to overcropping that exhausted the soils.
Cash crops such as groundnuts in Senegal and cotton took the best land and pushed food crops and herders onto marginal land.
Cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood removes the cover that protects the soil.
Poorly managed irrigation causes salinisation of the soil.
Human pressure leaves the soil bare and drought and storms then remove it, so the two causes act together.
Aspect 2 – Impact of desertification on soil:
Desertification strips the soil of its fertility and structure, with serious effects on the people who depend on it.
Topsoil and humus are lost, so fertility falls.
Surface crusting makes rain run off instead of soaking in, so water retention is reduced.
Wind blows away fine particles and leaves sand and dust.
Salt crusts form where irrigation is poorly managed, and in extreme cases the ground becomes bare and sterile.
Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s through drought and the use of water for irrigation.
Falling yields cause food shortages, famine and loss of livestock, and people migrate to cities and abroad.
In the Dust Bowl of the 1930s the natural grassland of the Great Plains in Oklahoma and Kansas was ploughed up for wheat.
Severe drought then left bare dry soil that strong winds blew away in huge dust storms.
Many farming families were forced to leave the Great Plains.
Aspect 3 – Impact of conservation on soil:
Conservation methods protect soil from erosion, rebuild its fertility and restore vegetation cover.
In Yatenga in Burkina Faso farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo revived the traditional zaï technique.
Zaï are small pits dug in hard crusted soil and filled with manure or compost, which catch rainwater and concentrate nutrients around the seed.
Stone lines built along the contours slow runoff and trap soil.
In Niger farmer-managed natural regeneration has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s.
The regrown trees shade the soil, cut wind speed and add leaf litter, and Faidherbia albida also adds nitrogen.
The Great Green Wall, launched by the African Union in 2007, aims to restore 100 million hectares of degraded land by 2030, but progress has been slow.
After the Dust Bowl the US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935 and promoted shelter belts, contour ploughing and crop rotation.
On hill commonage in the west of Ireland, headage payments raised sheep numbers from the 1980s and overgrazing exposed and eroded blanket peat, sending silt into rivers.
Payments were changed from per head to per area, and the REPS farm payments of 1994 paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways.
In Iceland the Soil Conservation Service, founded in 1907, fences off eroding land to keep sheep out and sows lyme grass to stabilise drifting sand.

MODEL ANSWER: Religion is an important cultural indicator because it shapes beliefs, values and group identity, and its distribution, its influence on society and its role in conflict all show cultural patterns.
Aspect 1 – Distribution and spread of world religions:
Religion shows in the landscape, in diet, dress and festivals, and its distribution marks out cultural regions of the world.
In 2020 Christians were the largest religious group at about 2.3 billion, or about 29% of the world.
In 2020 Muslims numbered about 2 billion, or about 26% of the world, and Islam was the fastest-growing major religion from 2010 to 2020.
Christianity began with the life of Jesus in Roman Palestine and spread through the Roman Empire, which made it the official religion in the 4th century.
Islam spread with the Arab conquests from the 7th century across the Middle East and North Africa and into Spain within about a century.
Trade spread Islam to Indonesia, which now has the world's largest Muslim population.
Colonialism spread Christianity to the Americas, Africa and the Philippines.
Irish monks such as St Columbanus spread Christianity in continental Europe in the early medieval period.
Christianity is divided into Roman Catholic in southern Europe, Ireland and Poland, Protestant in northern Europe, and Orthodox in eastern Europe, Russia and Greece.
About four in five Indians are Hindu, and Varanasi on the Ganges is a major pilgrimage city.
Mecca in Saudi Arabia is Islam's holiest city, and the Hajj pilgrimage there is a duty for every Muslim able to make it.
Aspect 2 – Religion in Irish society:
The changing place of religion in Ireland acts as a marker of cultural change.
In Census 2022 about 69% of people in the Republic were Roman Catholic, down from about 78% in Census 2016.
About 14% of people in the Republic had no religion in Census 2022.
The 1937 Constitution recognised the special position of the Catholic Church in Article 44, and the Fifth Amendment referendum of December 1972 removed it with about 84% voting yes.
About nine in ten primary schools are under Catholic patronage.
A free health plan for mothers and children in 1950 to 1951 was dropped after opposition from the Catholic hierarchy, and Minister for Health Noel Browne resigned.
Pope John Paul II visited Ireland in 1979, drawing huge crowds.

The marriage equality referendum of 22 May 2015 passed with about 62% yes, and the Eighth Amendment was repealed on 25 May 2018 with about 66% yes.
Educate Together, a multi-denominational school patron, was founded in 1984.
Orthodox Christians and Muslims are growing groups in Ireland through immigration.
Aspect 3 – Religion and conflict:
Where religion overlaps with national identity and political power it can feed conflict.
The Plantation of Ulster from 1609 settled English and Scottish Protestants on confiscated land.
The Government of Ireland Act 1920 led to partition, and Northern Ireland was established in 1921 with a Protestant and unionist majority.
Catholics in Northern Ireland faced discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting, including gerrymandering in Derry.
The Troubles from the late 1960s to 1998 killed over 3,500 people.

The Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998 set up a power-sharing Assembly and Executive.
