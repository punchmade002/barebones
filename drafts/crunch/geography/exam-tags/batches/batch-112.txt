PART 7B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named developed economy – Ireland:
Ireland is a developed economy with the second-highest GDP per head in the EU after Luxembourg.
Change over time – Ireland since the 1950s:
In the 1950s Ireland was a poor, mainly agricultural economy with protected industry and heavy emigration.
From 1958 the State turned towards attracting foreign investment, using IDA grants and serviced sites, and the IDA became an independent agency in 1969.
Free secondary education from 1967 raised the skills of the workforce.
Membership of the EEC from 1973 gave farmers guaranteed prices and a market, so farm incomes rose.
Stage 2 – Structural funding and high technology:
Ireland was a single Objective 1 region from 1989 to 1999 because its income was below 75% of the EU average, and it received large structural funds.
These funds built roads, ports and water services, which helped to attract investment.
Intel opened at Leixlip in 1989, marking the arrival of high-technology manufacturing.
Stage 3 – Celtic Tiger and crash:
In the 1990s rapid growth, known as the Celtic Tiger, was driven by foreign investment, a skilled English-speaking workforce, and access to the EU single market.
Corporation tax of 12.5% from 2003 attracted technology and pharmaceutical firms, and employment shifted from farming towards services and high-tech industry.
After the 2008 crash unemployment rose to about 15% in 2012 and emigration returned.
Dell moved manufacturing from Limerick to Poland in 2009, with about 1,900 jobs lost.
Stage 4 – Recovery to the 2020s:
Unemployment fell below 5% by 2022.
Ireland became a net contributor to the EU budget in 2014, the first time since joining, because of its economic growth.
Irish GDP grew by about 26% in 2015 mainly because multinationals relocated intellectual property and aircraft leasing assets, a result called leprechaun economics.
The CSO introduced GNI* in 2017 and it was only a little over half of GDP in the early 2020s, so GDP overstates Irish prosperity.
Universities supply graduates to pharmaceuticals, medical devices and information technology, and Ireland has one of the highest shares of young adults with third-level education in the EU.

PART 8B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: GNP as a measure of development:
Gross national product is the value of output owned by a country's residents, calculated as GDP plus income earned abroad minus income sent out to foreign owners.
GNP per head divides the total by the population, so countries of different size can be compared.
An advantage is that it is a simple single figure available for almost every country and it can be tracked over time.
It is also linked to a government's ability to fund health, education and other services.
A disadvantage is that it is an average, so it hides inequality between rich and poor and between regions.
It also ignores the informal economy, subsistence farming and unpaid work, and it ignores environmental damage.
Example 1 – Ireland:
Multinationals send profits abroad, so Irish GNP has long been well below GDP.
Irish GDP grew by about 26% in 2015 because of relocated intellectual property and aircraft leasing assets, not because Irish people became richer.
The CSO introduced GNI* from 2017, and in the early 2020s it was only a little over half of GDP.
HDI as a measure of development:
The Human Development Index was first published by the UNDP in 1990 and combines life expectancy at birth, years of schooling, and GNI per head at PPP dollars.
Each dimension is turned into an index from 0 to 1, and the categories run from very high at 0.800 and above to low below 0.550.
An advantage is that it is broader than income and focuses on people, so Cuba ranks higher on HDI than on income because of strong health care and education.
A disadvantage is that it is a national average that hides inequality and leaves out freedom, gender equality and the environment.
Example 2 – India, Switzerland and Somalia:
Switzerland ranked first with 0.967 in the 2023/24 report, while India ranked 134th of 193 with 0.644, a medium HDI.
Somalia ranked last of 193 with 0.380 in the 2023/24 report, showing very low human development.
Equatorial Guinea has far higher GNI per head than its HDI rank suggests because of oil wealth, so using both measures with other indicators gives the fullest picture.

PART 8C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: EU policy – Common Agricultural Policy:
The Common Agricultural Policy was launched in 1962 and has supported Irish farm incomes since Ireland joined in 1973.
Impact 1 – Rising incomes and modernisation:
Guaranteed prices after 1973 raised farm prices and incomes sharply, in the golden age of the 1970s.
A guaranteed market opened for beef and dairy.
Farms modernised with machinery, fertiliser, silage, drainage and farm buildings.
Impact 2 – Controls on production:
Milk quotas from 1984 to 2015 capped output and held back Irish dairying for 30 years.
After quotas ended on 1 April 2015, milk output rose from about 5.5 billion litres in 2014 to about 8.3 billion litres in 2020.
The MacSharry reform of 1992 introduced direct payments, and the 2003 Fischler reform decoupled payments from production.
Payments are now linked to environmental rules through cross-compliance, REPS from 1994 to 2009 and the ACRES scheme.
Negative effects of the CAP:
Payments historically favoured bigger farmers, and many beef and sheep farms depend heavily on direct payments.
The number of farms has fallen, with 135,037 in 2020, and 33% of holders were aged 65 or over while only 7% were under 35.
Intensive farming has damaged water quality through nitrates, so the Nitrates Directive of 1991 limits the nitrogen spread per hectare.
LEADER, from 1991, funds rural enterprise such as farm diversification.
EU policy – Common Fisheries Policy:
The Common Fisheries Policy of 1983 created a common pond, so Ireland shares its rich waters with larger fleets from Spain, France and the Netherlands.
Total allowable catches are set each December and divided into national quotas, and Ireland's share is small relative to the size of its waters.
The policy brought EU funding for boats, harbours and processing, and Killybegs in Co. Donegal became the largest port for pelagic fish such as mackerel.
Brexit cut Irish quotas, as Ireland lost about 26% of its mackerel quota, and boats were decommissioned with job losses in coastal communities.
The 2013 reform introduced a landing obligation that bans discarding of fish.

[geo-economic-development-s1.5.3] From 2017 the Central Statistics Office (CSO) published modified gross national income (GNI*), which removes the profits of redomiciled firms (firms that moved their legal base to Ireland), depreciation (loss of value through wear and age) on foreign-owned intellectual property and leased aircraft.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.4] In the early 2020s Ireland's GNI* was only a little over half of its GDP, which shows how large the distortion is.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.5] Ireland has the second-highest GDP per head in the EU, after Luxembourg (Eurostat, early 2020s), so GDP overstates Irish prosperity. Use GNI* as the better guide to Irish living standards.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.b] The HDI combines health, education and income into one value. It was designed to measure people's well-being, not just the economy.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.0] The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) first published the HDI in its Human Development Report in 1990.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.2] A long and healthy life is measured by life expectancy at birth (how many years a newborn can expect to live).
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.4] A decent standard of living is measured by GNI per head at PPP dollars. The income indicator is GNI per head, not GDP.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.5] Each dimension is turned into an index from 0 to 1, and the three are combined into one value between 0 and 1. The closer the value is to 1, the higher the development.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.6] The four categories are very high (0.800 and above), high (0.700 to 0.799), medium (0.550 to 0.699) and low (below 0.550).
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.b] HDI is highest in the richer regions and lowest in parts of Africa. One named example of each category is enough for an exam answer.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.1] Switzerland ranked 1st (0.967) in the Human Development Report 2023/24, using 2022 data. Ireland was in the top ten in that report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.2] High HDI: Brazil, at about 0.76 in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.3] Medium HDI: India, 134th of 193 countries (0.644) in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.4] Low HDI: mostly sub-Saharan Africa (the countries south of the Sahara desert), including Niger, Chad, South Sudan, Somalia and the Central African Republic.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.5] Niger was last of 189 countries (0.394) in the 2020 report, and Somalia was last of 193 (0.380) in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.b] The HDI was built to fix the weaknesses of income per head, so its strengths mostly come from being broader and people-centred.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.0] It is broader than income alone, combining health, education and income.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.3] It can be tracked over time, showing progress or decline.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.4] It helps governments and aid agencies target spending, for example on schooling or health, because it shows which area is weakest.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.b] Like any single number, the HDI leaves things out and hides differences. The UNDP has tried to answer some of these criticisms.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.0] As a national average it hides inequality between rich and poor, men and women, regions and ethnic groups.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.1] It leaves out political freedom, human rights, gender equality, environmental sustainability, safety and happiness.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.b] GNP and HDI measure different things, so a country can rank very differently on each. Using both, with other indicators, gives the fullest picture.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.0] GNP measures output and income; HDI measures human outcomes in health, education and living standards.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.1] Equatorial Guinea has far higher GNI per head than its HDI rank suggests, because of oil wealth; the gap between its income rank and its HDI rank is large.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.2] Oil-rich Gulf states generally rank higher on income than on HDI.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.3] Cuba ranks higher on HDI than on income, because of strong public health care and education.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.4] GNI per head is one of the four HDI indicators, so the two measures overlap; the HDI adds life expectancy and schooling to it.
[geo-economic-development-s1.11.1] Economic indicators: share of workers in the primary sector (farming and other raw-material work, high in developing economies), unemployment rate, energy use per head, and internet and phone access.
[geo-economic-development-s1.12.4] Lifestyle: diet, smoking, alcohol and obesity shorten life in some rich countries.
[geo-economic-development-s1.14.4] Ireland has one of the highest shares of 25 to 34-year-olds with third-level education in the EU (early 2020s, Eurostat). Free secondary education was introduced in 1967.
[geo-economic-development-s1.15.b] The unemployment rate is the share of people who want work but cannot find it. A high rate signals a weak economy and causes problems of its own.
[geo-economic-development-s1.16.2] To calculate a difference, subtract the lower value from the higher and state the unit: for example, an illustrative country with a life expectancy of 80 years and another with 60 years differ by 20 years.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.5.0] Kinsale Head gas field, off Co. Cork, was discovered in 1971 and began production in 1978 as Ireland's first commercial gas field. Production ended in 2020 when the field was depleted (used up), so it is no longer a working field.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.7.1] Peat-fired stations at Lanesborough (Lough Ree) and Shannonbridge (West Offaly) closed in December 2020. Bord na Móna ended all peat harvesting in January 2021.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.b] Governments set targets to push renewables up and cut emissions. Ireland missed its 2020 target and has set tougher ones for 2030 and 2050.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.11.0] Ireland's 2020 EU target was 16% of gross final energy consumption (all the energy used by final users such as homes, industry and transport) from renewables.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.12.b] Ireland's renewable share fell short of its 2020 target for six linked reasons. Several come from how Irish people live and travel, and some from delays in building.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.18.2] Germany still burns lignite (brown coal) from huge open-cast mines (mines dug from the surface) in the Rhineland. A 2020 law sets 2038 at the latest as the end of coal-fired power.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.b] Denmark shows a country that has built its electricity supply around wind.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.0] Wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity (2020s).
[geo-economic-environment-s1.20.1] Denmark was an early leader in wind power and is home to the turbine maker Vestas.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.b] The EU sets renewable targets for all its members. It met its 2020 target and has raised the goal for 2030.
[geo-economic-environment-s1.22.0] The EU target of 20% renewable energy by 2020 was achieved, at about 22%.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.b] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas formed from ancient living things) supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy (the energy taken straight from nature before conversion) in the 2020s. Their production is concentrated in a few places, so most countries must import them.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.0.3] China produces and consumes about half the world's coal.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.1.2] China has been the world's largest CO2 (carbon dioxide) emitter in total since the mid-2000s, but the USA emits far more CO2 per person.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.b] Burning a fossil fuel releases the carbon and impurities locked inside it. Each pollutant causes a different problem, so keep them apart.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.2.0] Carbon dioxide (CO2) is the main greenhouse gas released, and it drives global warming.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.b] The greenhouse effect is the natural trapping of heat by gases in the atmosphere. Burning fossil fuels adds extra gas to it.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.3.0] CO2 traps heat in the atmosphere, so extra CO2 from burning fossil fuels enhances the greenhouse effect and causes global warming. There is more on this in Environmental pollution.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.7.0] Burning coal and turf in homes releases smoke and fine particles, because the fuel burns incompletely in an open fire.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.9.b] Beyond the air pollutants, getting and using fossil fuels harms land, water and rivers.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.1] Denmark: wind supplies about half of Denmark's electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.10.2] Advantage: strong, frequent Atlantic winds give Ireland a good wind resource, and wind is the main renewable source in Irish electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.4] Advantage: output can be raised quickly at peak times (when demand is highest), as at Turlough Hill.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.11.6] Norway: hydroelectric power supplies about 90% of its electricity, for example.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.13.0] Wave: the Atlantic Marine Energy Test Site off Belmullet, Co. Mayo, is a wave test site.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.b] Geothermal energy uses heat from the Earth's interior, to heat water or to generate steam.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.14.2] Advantage: output does not depend on the weather. Disadvantage: high-temperature sources are found only where the crust is hot, as in Iceland.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.b] Biomass is organic material (from plants or animal waste) burned or converted to energy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.15.3] Advantage: carbon-neutral in principle, because the crops absorb as much CO2 as they release when burned. Disadvantage: energy crops compete with food production for land.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.0] France: nuclear power supplies about two-thirds of its electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.16.1] Advantages: low carbon emissions, so little CO2, plus reliable and large output.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.b] Renewable energy comes from sources that are naturally replaced. These advantages apply to wind, hydro, solar, wave, tidal and geothermal power.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.0] Renewables are inexhaustible, so supply is secure: the sun, wind, water and tides will not run out.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.18.2] They reduce the fossil-fuel import bill and improve energy security.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.b] Renewables also have costs and limits, and these explain why fossil fuels are still widely used.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.19.0] Renewables are intermittent (not constant), because wind and sun vary, so backup or storage is needed.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.b] Ireland imports a large share of its energy, so renewable energy matters for both its environment and its economy.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.3] Renewables provide jobs and income in rural areas, because wind farms, solar farms and dams are built there.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.4] Green power attracts multinational companies that want renewable electricity.
[geo-economic-environment-s2.20.5] Cleaner air and water follow from burning less fossil fuel.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.0.0] Ireland was a net recipient of EU budget money from joining on 1 January 1973 until about 2014, receiving over €40 billion more than it paid in, mostly through the CAP (the Common Agricultural Policy, the EU's farm policy).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.2.b] The Common Agricultural Policy was launched in 1962 to run farming for all EU members as one system.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.3.0] Guaranteed (intervention) prices were set above world levels, so farmers were sure of a good price, and intervention buying (the EU buying up produce) took up surpluses that the market could not absorb.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.4.b] Ireland joined in 1973, so its farmers suddenly gained access to CAP guaranteed prices. For Irish farming this was a major boost.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.4.0] Farm prices and incomes rose sharply, because Irish farmers now received the higher EU guaranteed prices.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.4.1] A guaranteed market opened for beef and dairy, so farmers knew they could sell what they produced.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.4.2] Farms modernised with machinery, fertiliser, silage (stored grass for winter feed), land drainage and farm buildings, in what is remembered as the golden age of the 1970s.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.b] Because the CAP caused surpluses, the EU limited how much milk each farm could produce. Irish dairying was held back until the limit was removed.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.0] Milk quotas, introduced in 1984, capped each farm's milk output to cut surpluses. They held back Irish dairying for 30 years.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.1] The quotas were abolished on 1 April 2015; the last quota year ended on 31 March 2015.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.2] Once the cap went, output rose: Irish milk output went from about 5.5 billion litres in 2014 to about 8.3 billion litres in 2020.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.5.3] From 2013 to 2023 dairy cow numbers rose about 41% and milk output about 56% (EPA, from CSO data).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.0] The MacSharry reform of 1992, led by Ray MacSharry, EU Agriculture Commissioner from Sligo, replaced price cuts with direct payments (money paid straight to farmers) and introduced set-aside (paying farmers to leave some land unused to cut output).
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.2] The Fischler reform, agreed in 2003, decoupled payments from production, so farmers were no longer paid according to how much they produced.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.6.4] Cross-compliance links payments to environmental and animal-welfare rules, so a farmer who breaks the rules can lose payment.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.0] Ireland's CAP Strategic Plan (its national plan for the CAP) is worth about €9.8 billion from EU and national funds, with about 45% for environmental and climate measures.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.1] Basic Income Support for Sustainability (BISS, the main direct payment to farmers) is worth about €3.6 billion. The eco-scheme (a payment for environmentally friendly farming) is worth about €1.5 billion, 25% of direct payments.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.7.3] Payments for young farmers and the ACRES agri-environment scheme (paying farmers for farming that protects nature) are included in the plan.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.8.0] REPS, the Rural Environment Protection Scheme, ran from 1994 to 2009 as an agri-environment scheme, paying farmers to farm in ways that protect the environment.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.8.1] LEADER, from 1991, funds local rural enterprise such as farm diversification (farmers earning from new activities), rural tourism and crafts.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.8.2] Grants support forestry and young farmers, such as those taking over family farms.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.9.b] Exams ask you to name types of farm and may ask about the number and size of farms. The Census of Agriculture 2020 (CSO) gives the picture.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.9.0] The Census of Agriculture 2020 counted 135,037 farms, down about 3% on 2010, with an average size of about 33 hectares.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.9.1] On a third (33%) of farms the holder was aged 65 or over; only 7% of holders were under 35, which shows the farming population is ageing.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.9.2] The main farm types are dairy, cattle (beef) rearing, sheep, tillage (growing crops) and mixed. Specialist beef is the most common type.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.b] For Ireland the CAP has brought security and support to farming and the countryside. These points suit questions on the positive effects of EU policies.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.0] Stable farm incomes and a guaranteed market, so farmers can plan with confidence.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.1] Modernisation of farms and food quality standards, so Irish produce meets EU rules and sells in the single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.2] Environmental schemes and support for rural communities, such as LEADER and REPS, so the countryside keeps people and income.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.10.3] Dependence on direct payments is high for many beef and sheep farms, which is a limit as well as a support, because those farms struggle if payments change.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.b] The same policy also has costs. To get full marks, pair each positive effect with a negative one.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.0] Payments have historically favoured bigger farmers, so most of the money went to a minority.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.2] Intensification (farming more intensively, with more animals and inputs) has damaged water quality through nitrates and raised greenhouse gas emissions as the dairy herd grew.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.11.3] The number of farms has fallen and farmers are ageing; small western farms remain uneconomic, meaning they cannot make a viable living.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.12.b] Intensive farming puts nitrogen into water, so the EU limits it. This shows EU environmental rules shaping Irish farming.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.12.0] The Nitrates Directive of 1991 limits the organic nitrogen (for example from slurry and manure) that can be spread per hectare.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.12.1] Ireland has a derogation (a permitted exception) that allows higher stocking (more animals per hectare) on intensive farms, conditional on water quality. EU environmental rules therefore shape farming directly.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.13.b] The Common Fisheries Policy treats EU seas as shared resources. This suited countries with big fleets more than Ireland.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.13.0] The Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) was agreed in full in 1983. It creates a common pond of EU waters, giving all members' fleets equal access beyond coastal limits.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.13.2] Ireland had to share its rich waters with much larger fleets, notably those of Spain, France and the Netherlands. Spain and Portugal joined in 1986.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.14.b] The CFP controls how much fish can be taken and how, through quotas and rules on fishing effort.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.14.0] Total Allowable Catches (TACs, the maximum amount of each species that can be caught) are set each December by EU fisheries ministers and divided into national quotas using fixed shares.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.14.1] A coastal band of 12 nautical miles is reserved mainly for local boats.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.15.0] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, is Ireland's largest fishing port by volume and value, specialising in pelagic fish (species living near the surface or mid-water): mackerel, herring and blue whiting.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.16.b] For Ireland the CFP has brought funding, protection for stocks and market access.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.16.0] EU funding for boats, harbours and processing plants, now through the European Maritime, Fisheries and Aquaculture Fund.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.16.3] The Hague Preferences protect Ireland's share when quotas are cut.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.0] Ireland's quota share is small relative to the size and richness of its waters.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.2] Discards, the throwing back of dead fish, were common until the landing obligation, which wasted fish.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.3] Decommissioning of boats (paying owners to take boats out of service) and job losses in coastal communities.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.17.4] Quota cuts under Brexit.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.18.b] The 2013 reform tackled two weaknesses: wasteful discarding and overfishing.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.18.0] The reform was agreed in 2013. It introduced a landing obligation, which bans discarding, phased in between 2015 and 2019.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.18.1] It aims to fish at maximum sustainable yield, the largest catch that can be taken without running down stocks.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.b] Brexit (the UK's leaving of the EU) hit Irish fishing hard because EU fishing quota was handed to the UK.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.1] Ireland lost about 26% of its mackerel quota. The government's Seafood Task Force valued the annual quota loss at about €43 million by 2026.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.2] Irish ministers said Ireland's losses were about double those of other EU fleets in proportion.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.19.4] A €25.6 million pelagic support scheme was funded from the reserve, and a voluntary permanent cessation (decommissioning) scheme ran in 2022-23.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.20.2] The Structural Funds (ERDF, the European Social Fund and the farm guidance fund) doubled after the 1988-89 reform, to prepare poorer regions for the single market.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.23.b] Regional funds paid for infrastructure and enterprise, the things poorer regions lacked.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.26.0] The funds helped Ireland converge with EU incomes and modernised its infrastructure.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.33.b] As with regional funds, social funding has both strengths and weaknesses that you can use in an evaluation.
[geo-economic-eu-s3.33.2] Social funding is now much smaller relative to the size of the Irish economy, because Ireland is a wealthy member.
