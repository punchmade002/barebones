PART "1B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Prediction of earthquakes:
Earthquakes cannot be predicted exactly, because scientists cannot say the precise time, place and magnitude in advance.
Scientists map active faults and plate boundaries to show where earthquakes are most likely, such as the San Andreas Fault in California.
They study the history and frequency of past earthquakes to estimate how often a region is struck.
Seismic gap theory holds that a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time has the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next.
Foreshocks, which are small tremors before the main shock, are monitored as a possible warning sign.
GPS, tiltmeters and laser measurements across faults detect ground deformation as stress builds up.
Radon gas release and changes in well-water levels are monitored as possible warning signs.
At Haicheng in China in 1975, foreshocks led to an evacuation hours before a magnitude 7.3 earthquake, which saved many lives.
Japan's early-warning system detects the fast P waves, giving seconds of warning to stop trains and shut off gas supplies.
Reducing the effects of earthquakes:
Base isolation places rubber and steel bearings under a building, so the ground moves beneath it without shaking the structure.
Flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames with cross-bracing and shear walls sway rather than crack.
A tuned mass damper, such as the giant steel ball in Taipei 101, offsets the sway of a tall building.
Automatic shut-off valves on gas mains and flexible joints on pipes reduce the risk of fires.
Older buildings are retrofitted to make them stronger, as happened in Kobe after the 1995 earthquake.
Strict building codes and land-use planning that avoids soft ground and fault lines reduce collapse and liquefaction.
Emergency drills, education and emergency supplies prepare people, as on Japan's Disaster Prevention Day each 1 September.
Tsunami walls, evacuation routes and warning systems, such as the Indian Ocean system operational from 2006, protect coastal areas.

PART "1C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named sedimentary rock – limestone:
Limestone is an organic sedimentary rock made from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures.
Carboniferous limestone formed about 360 to 300 million years ago, when Ireland lay near the equator under a warm, shallow, clear sea.
Sea creatures died, and their shells and skeletons sank and built up on the sea floor.
Sediment accumulated in layers called strata over millions of years in a subsiding basin.
The weight of the overlying layers squeezed out water and air and packed the grains together, which is compaction.
Minerals such as calcite, carried by groundwater, bound the grains together, which is cementation.
The conversion of loose sediment into solid rock by compaction and cementation is called lithification.
The Burren in County Clare is a named location of Carboniferous limestone.
The finished rock shows bedding planes and joints and often contains fossils.
Named metamorphic rock – marble:
Marble is a metamorphic rock formed when existing rock is changed by great heat or pressure without melting.
The parent rock of marble is limestone.
In thermal metamorphism, the heat of a nearby igneous intrusion bakes the limestone and forms a ring of altered rock called a metamorphic aureole.
In regional metamorphism, heat and pressure act over huge areas during plate collision and mountain building.
The minerals in the limestone recrystallise while the rock stays solid.
The interlocking crystals make marble harder and denser than limestone, with a sparkling appearance and no fossils.
Connemara marble in County Galway is a named location where marble is quarried.

PART "2B(i)" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named landform – waterfall and gorge:
A waterfall forms in the upper course where a band of resistant rock lies over less resistant rock.
The steep gradient of the upper course gives the river high energy, so vertical erosion dominates.
Process of erosion – hydraulic action:
Hydraulic action is the force of moving water dislodging loose material and forcing air into cracks until rock breaks away.
It erodes the soft rock beneath the hard cap faster than the hard rock above.
Process of erosion – abrasion:
Abrasion is the grinding of the river's load against the bed and banks.
Abrasion by the load also undercuts the soft rock and leaves an overhang of hard rock.
Falling water and its load scour a deep plunge pool at the base by hydraulic action and abrasion.
Pebbles swirled in the plunge pool drill it deeper.
Retreat and gorge formation:
The unsupported overhang collapses.
The fallen blocks are broken down by attrition and carried away downstream.
The waterfall retreats upstream as this process repeats.
Repeated retreat leaves a steep-sided gorge with a narrow floor downstream of the falls.
Erosion is greatest in flood, when discharge and energy are high.
The load is carried away by traction, saltation, suspension and solution.
Example – Niagara Falls:
Niagara Falls on the USA–Canada border has a resistant cap of dolomite over weaker shale.
The retreat of the falls has cut the Niagara Gorge below them.
Powerscourt Waterfall on the River Dargle in County Wicklow, about 121 m high, is another example.
Torc Waterfall near Killarney in County Kerry is a further Irish example.

PART "2B(ii)" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Mass movement process – slump:
Slump is a fast, rotational mass movement in which saturated material slides along a curved slip plane and rotates backward.
It leaves a crescent-shaped scar at the top, a back-tilted block and a bulging toe at the foot.
Slumps are common on clay cliffs undercut by the sea.
Mass movement is the downslope movement of weathered material under gravity, and it happens when the downslope force exceeds the resistance.
Factor 1 – Water content:
Heavy or prolonged rain saturates the material on the slope.
Saturation fills the pores, adds weight and reduces friction, so the downslope force increases.
Water acts as a lubricant on the slip plane, so the mass slides.
Clay becomes slippery and plastic when wet.
Factor 2 – Rock type and structure:
Permeable rock lying over impermeable clay concentrates water at the junction.
Rainwater soaks through the jointed rock above and saturates the clay below.
The weak clay gives way, and the rock above slumps.
Factor 3 – Undercutting, gradient and vegetation:
Erosion by the sea at the base of a slope removes support and steepens the slope.
Steeper slopes increase the downslope pull of gravity.
Loss of vegetation removes the roots that bind the soil, so bare slopes move more easily.
Example – Antrim coast:
On the Antrim coast between Larne and Cushendall, hard basalt lies on chalk, which lies on soft Lias clay.
Garron Point is a classic landslip site where rotational slides have repeatedly blocked the coast road.
Engineers respond with retaining walls, drainage and rock-catch fences to protect the road.

PART "2C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Constructive boundaries – movement and cause:
At constructive boundaries two plates move apart and new crust is formed in the gap.
Convection currents in the mantle carry the plates apart.
Pressure drops as the plates separate, so the mantle partly melts.
Magma rises into the gap and cools to form new basaltic oceanic crust.
Features of constructive boundaries:
A mid-ocean ridge forms, with a rift valley along its crest.
Frequent shallow earthquakes occur along the ridge.
Volcanic activity includes fissure eruptions of runny basalt lava.
Transform faults cut across the ridge and offset it in sections.
Where a ridge rises above sea level it forms volcanic islands.
Examples – Mid-Atlantic Ridge and Iceland:
The Mid-Atlantic Ridge separates the North American and Eurasian plates in the north and the South American and African plates in the south.
Iceland is a volcanic island on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the rift is visible on land at Thingvellir.
Surtsey formed as a new volcanic island off the south coast of Iceland in an eruption that began in 1963.
The East Pacific Rise is another mid-ocean ridge.
Continental rifting – East African Rift:
Where a continent starts to split, the crust stretches and a block drops between parallel faults to form a rift valley with volcanoes nearby.
The East African Rift Valley is where the African plate is splitting apart, and Mount Kilimanjaro is a volcano associated with it.
If spreading continues the sea floods in and a new ocean begins, as in the Red Sea.
