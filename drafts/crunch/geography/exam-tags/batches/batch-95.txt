QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q113 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q13, 80 marks)

PART 13 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sustainable development, which meets the needs of the present without compromising the needs of future generations, should be the priority for human and economic development, because growth that destroys the resources it depends on cannot last, as the use of resources in Ireland, economic growth in Costa Rica and fair trade for farmers in Ghana all show.
Aspect 1 – Protecting resources in Ireland:
Renewable resources such as fish must be used no faster than they regenerate, which is the sustainable yield.
Overfishing shows the danger, as the Grand Banks cod moratorium of 2 July 1992 cost about 30,000 jobs off Newfoundland.
The North Sea herring fishery was closed from 1977 to the early 1980s and recovered under quotas, which proves that sustainable management works.
The Common Fisheries Policy was reformed in 2013 to introduce maximum sustainable yield and a landing obligation that bans discards.
The Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority, set up in 2007, enforces these rules and protects the income of ports such as Killybegs, Co. Donegal, Ireland's largest fishing port.
On land, Bord na Móna was founded in 1946 to harvest peat for power, but the peat stations at Lanesborough and Shannonbridge closed in December 2020 and harvesting ended in January 2021.
Under its Brown to Green strategy, cutaway bogs are being rehabilitated and rewetted, and peatland is valued as a store of carbon.
Bellacorick, Co. Mayo, has wind farms on cutaway bog, which shows that power and jobs can continue from renewable energy.
The plastic bag levy of 2002 cut plastic bag use by over 90%, which shows that policy can change behaviour quickly.
Aspect 2 – Economic development in Costa Rica:
In the 1970s and 1980s Costa Rica cleared forests for cattle pasture, and by the late 1980s forest covered only about a fifth of the country.
Its Payment for Environmental Services programme, started in 1997 and run by FONAFIFO, pays landowners to protect and replant forest.
The programme is paid for largely by a tax on fuel, so those who use resources help to fund conservation.
Forest cover has since risen to over half of the country.
About a quarter of the land is in national parks and other protected areas.
Ecotourism is one of the largest sources of foreign income, so protecting nature creates jobs and earnings.
Since 2015 almost all of the country's electricity has come from renewable sources, mainly hydroelectric, geothermal and wind.
Costa Rica abolished its army in 1948 and spends instead on education and health, which builds human development.
The limits show that sustainability is a continuing task, because transport still runs mainly on imported oil and tourism puts pressure on protected areas.
Aspect 3 – Human development through fair trade:
Small farmers in developing countries receive a tiny share of the final price, because commodity prices are low and unstable and middlemen take a large share.
Fair trade is a trading partnership that seeks greater equity, and the Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production.
The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative, which members spend on projects they choose democratically.
Kuapa Kokoo, a Fairtrade cocoa farmers' co-operative in Ghana founded in 1993, shows this in practice.
Ghana is the world's second-largest cocoa producer, so many families depend on the crop.
Premium spending has included wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
Kuapa Kokoo part-owns Divine Chocolate, set up in Britain in 1998, so farmers share in the profits of the brand and not just the sale of beans.
The co-operative encourages women to take leadership roles, which gives women a voice and an income.
Labour standards ban forced and child labour, and environmental standards restrict pesticides and protect water and forests, so fair trade links human and environmental sustainability.
Its limits are that it is only a tiny share of world trade and does not change trade rules such as tariff escalation.

QUESTION geography-pp-2024-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2024 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART 14 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Decisions and actions taken in one area, such as demand for beef, soya and fossil fuels in rich regions or the overuse of land and water, have effects in other areas, as deforestation, global warming and desertification show.
Aspect 1 – Deforestation in the Amazon:
The Amazon is the world's largest tropical rainforest, with about 60% of it in Brazil.
Close to a fifth of the original forest has been cleared, and cattle ranching is the largest cause.
Soya, much of it exported as animal feed to Europe and China, has spread north from Mato Grosso, so demand in other regions drives clearance.
The Trans-Amazonian Highway was begun in the early 1970s, and roads, mining and dams add to the clearance.
The carbon released by clearing and burning forest adds to global warming for the whole planet.
Reduced rainfall recycling by the forest affects farming beyond the forest.
Biodiversity is lost and indigenous peoples are threatened.
In Indonesia and Malaysia, clearance of peat swamp forest for palm oil caused regional haze in 1997 and 2015 that affected Singapore and Malaysia.
Other countries have responded, through the Amazon Soy Moratorium of 2006 agreed by traders and the Amazon Fund of 2008, with Norway as the largest donor.
Aspect 2 – Global warming:
Greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels in industrialised and emerging economies, from deforestation and from agriculture cause global warming.
Carbon dioxide has risen from about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s.
China has been the largest CO2 emitter since the mid-2000s, but the USA emits far more per person, and rich countries bear historical responsibility for much of the build-up.
Countries that emitted least suffer most, such as Bangladesh in the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta, which faces floods, cyclones and sea-level rise.
Pakistan's floods of 2022 covered about a third of the country, killed over 1,700 people and affected about 33 million, although Pakistan produces under 1% of global greenhouse gas emissions.
Small island states such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives are threatened by sea-level rise, as global sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018.
Drought in the Sahel, rising food prices when harvests fail, and climate-driven migration spread the effects across regions.
The Paris Agreement of 2015 aims to keep warming well below 2 degrees Celsius, which needs co-operation between countries.
The Loss and Damage Fund agreed at COP27 in 2022 helps countries hit by climate damage that they did little to cause.
Aspect 3 – Desertification in the Sahel:
Desertification is land degradation in drylands, not the desert advancing, and drylands cover about 41% of the earth's land and are home to about 2 billion people.
The Sahel is the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, where unreliable rainfall and drought, notably in the 1970s and 1980s, are physical causes.
Human causes include population growth, overgrazing, overcultivation and fuelwood collection.
Cash crops such as groundnuts in Senegal and cotton take the best land for export, so demand abroad pushes people onto poorer land.
Global warming, linked to emissions in other regions, adds to drought in the Sahel.
The effects spread beyond the region, with famine, conflict over land and water, and migration to cities and to Europe.
Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s, according to a widely quoted UN figure.
The African Union's Great Green Wall, begun in 2007, is a planned band about 8,000 km long from Senegal to Djibouti, aiming to restore 100 million hectares by 2030.
Farmer-managed natural regeneration in Niger has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, which shows that local action can reverse degradation.

PART 15 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Views of development and underdevelopment have changed over time, and the simple division of the world into a developed North and a developing South should be challenged.
Aspect 1 – Changing definitions of development:
Development was first seen as economic growth, measured by GNP per head.
In the 1950s and 1960s Rostow's modernisation model claimed that every society passes from traditional society through take-off to high mass consumption.
South Korea seemed to fit this model, as export-led industrialisation under Park Chung-hee from the 1960s turned a country devastated by the Korean War into one reclassified as developed by UNCTAD in 2021.
Modernisation was challenged as Eurocentric, because it assumed that the Western path was the only path and that poor countries were simply behind.
Dependency theorists such as Andre Gunder Frank argued in 1966 that poor countries were made poor by their unequal links with the rich core, which drained wealth through cheap raw material exports, debt and profit repatriation.
Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grow over half of the world's cocoa but export mostly beans rather than chocolate, which leaves them dependent on low and unstable primary-product prices.
In the 1970s the basic needs approach put food, water, shelter, health and education ahead of GNP growth.
From 1990 the UNDP's Human Development Report and the HDI, built on the ideas of Amartya Sen, judged progress by people's health, education and choices rather than national output.
Since the Brundtland Report of 1987 sustainable development has added the idea that growth must not harm future generations, and the 17 Sustainable Development Goals were adopted in 2015 with a target year of 2030.
Aspect 2 – The North–South divide challenged:
The Brandt Report of 1980 divided the world along a line at roughly 30°N into a rich, industrialised North and a poor South.
The gap remains large, as the World Inequality Report 2022 found that the richest 10% of people own about 76% of global wealth and the poorest half about 2%.
The model is now outdated because many countries of the South are high-income or industrial powers, such as Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan and Hong Kong.
China has been the world's second-largest economy since 2010, and India is among the five largest.
The BRIC term was coined in 2001 by Jim O'Neill of Goldman Sachs and grew into BRICS when South Africa joined in 2011, with more members joining from 2024.
The G20, set up in 1999, includes China, India, Brazil, South Africa, Indonesia, Mexico and Saudi Arabia, which gives the South a stronger voice in world affairs.
Hans Rosling argued in Factfulness that most of the world's people now live in middle-income countries, so a two-part division hides the real picture.
Poverty and deprivation also exist within the North, so wealth does not follow the line neatly.
In 2016 the World Bank announced that it would stop dividing countries into developing and developed in its main data tables and would keep its four income groups instead.
Aspect 3 – Eurocentric views and images of Africa:
Eurocentric thinking sees the world from a European point of view and judges other societies by European standards, as if Europe's path were the norm.
The Mercator projection of 1569 places Europe at the top centre and makes Africa, which is about 14 times the area of Greenland, look similar in size to it, while the Peters projection of 1973 shows true areas.
Environmental determinism claimed that temperate climates produced advanced peoples and tropical climates produced backward ones, but Singapore, a tropical country, is one of the richest in the world.
The 1984–85 Ethiopian famine, brought to the world by Michael Buerk's BBC report and by Live Aid in 1985, fixed an image of Africa as helpless and dependent and hid the political causes of civil war and government policy.
Such images lead to stereotypes, such as the belief that Africa is a country, and can make donors see themselves as saviours.
Dóchas, the Irish association of development NGOs, drew up a Code of Conduct on Images and Messages that requires dignified images and the full context.
Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's 2009 talk The Danger of a Single Story argued that one story about a place distorts it.
Rwanda has rebuilt since the 1994 genocide, and since the 2013 election women have held over 60% of the seats in its lower house, the highest share in the world.
Kenya's M-Pesa, launched in 2007 by Safaricom, lets people without bank accounts send, receive and save money by text message, which shows African innovation.
