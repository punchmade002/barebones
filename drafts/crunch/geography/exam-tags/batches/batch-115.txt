PART 13 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Views of development and underdevelopment differ on what causes poverty and how progress should be achieved, as the modernisation model, dependency theory and human and sustainable development show.

Aspect 1 – Modernisation (Rostow's model):
Modernisation theory holds that all societies follow one path from traditional to modern, as Western Europe and North America did.
It sees underdevelopment as being not yet developed, so poor countries are simply behind and need to catch up.
Walt Rostow set out five stages in 1960, from traditional society through preconditions for take-off, take-off and drive to maturity to high mass consumption.
Take-off is reached when investment rises above 10% of national income and a few leading industries grow fast.
Poor countries are expected to catch up through investment, technology, industry and Western values.
Ireland fits the model, with the First Programme for Economic Expansion in 1958 as the preconditions, industrial growth in the 1960s and 1970s as take-off and the Celtic Tiger as high mass consumption.
South Korea industrialised through export-led growth from the 1960s, led by chaebol such as Samsung and Hyundai, and was reclassified as a developed country in 2021.
Critics say the model is Eurocentric because it assumes the Western path is the only path.
It ignores colonialism and unequal trade as causes of poverty, and Ireland's crash from 2008 shows that the stages can be reversed.

Aspect 2 – Dependency theory:
Dependency theory holds that poor countries are poor because of how they were drawn into the world economy, not because they are backward.
Rich core countries develop by draining wealth from poor periphery countries, so development and underdevelopment are two sides of one process.
Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer argued in 1950 that the terms of trade of primary-product exporters tend to decline.
Andre Gunder Frank argued in 1966 that Latin America was underdeveloped by its links with the core.
Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire grow over half of the world's cocoa, but most is exported as beans rather than chocolate.
Tariff escalation means rich countries tax processed goods more heavily than raw materials, so producers cannot add value.
Zambia depends on copper for most of its exports, so a fall in copper prices damages its whole economy.
Multinationals send their profits home and debt repayments drain income that could fund development.
Critics say the theory cannot explain how the Asian Tigers and China developed through trade with the core.

Aspect 3 – Human and sustainable development:
Human development judges progress by people's health, education, freedom and choice, not by national output alone.
Amartya Sen argued in Development as Freedom in 1999 that development means widening people's choices and capabilities.
The first UNDP Human Development Report appeared in 1990 and introduced the Human Development Index.
The basic needs approach of the 1970s put food, water, shelter, health and education ahead of GNP growth.
E. F. Schumacher's Small Is Beautiful in 1973 argued for appropriate technology that local people can afford, use and repair.
Bottom-up development puts participation, co-operatives and women's groups at the centre, in contrast to top-down projects.
The Brundtland Report of 1987 added sustainable development, which meets today's needs without harming future generations.
The 17 Sustainable Development Goals were adopted in 2015 with a target year of 2030.
Bhutan's Gross National Happiness puts well-being ahead of GNP as a goal.

PART 14 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sustainability, which meets present needs without harming future generations, offers a model for future human and economic development, as resource use, fair trade and self-reliance show.

Aspect 1 – Resource use:
Sustainable resource use means using renewable resources no faster than they regenerate, which is the sustainable yield.
Non-renewable resources should be used efficiently, and waste should be reduced, reused and recycled.
Costa Rica cleared forests for cattle pasture, and by the late 1980s forest covered only about a fifth of the country.
Its Payment for Environmental Services programme, started in 1997, pays landowners to protect and replant forest.
The programme is paid for largely by a tax on fuel.
Forest cover has since risen to over half of the country, and ecotourism is now one of its largest sources of foreign income.
Almost all of Costa Rica's electricity now comes from renewable sources, mainly hydroelectric, geothermal and wind.
In Ireland, Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in 2021 under its Brown to Green strategy and is rewetting cutaway bogs.
The Aral Sea shows unsustainable use, because rivers diverted from the 1960s to irrigate Soviet cotton caused the sea to shrink to a fraction of its size.

Aspect 2 – Fair trade:
Fair trade tackles low and unstable commodity prices, which leave small farmers with a tiny share of the final price.
The Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production.
The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative and spent on projects chosen democratically by its members.
Fairtrade standards ban forced and child labour and restrict pesticides to protect water and forests.
Kuapa Kokoo is a Fairtrade cocoa farmers' co-operative founded in 1993 in Ghana, the world's second-largest cocoa producer.
It part-owns Divine Chocolate, so farmers share in the profits of the brand and not just the price of their beans.
Premium spending has paid for wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
Kuapa Kokoo encourages women to take leadership roles in its village societies.
Fair trade remains a tiny share of world trade and does not change trade rules such as tariff escalation.

Aspect 3 – Self-reliance:
Self-reliance means communities and countries meet their own needs using their own resources, skills and decisions.
It reduces dependence on aid, loans, foreign companies and imports.
Tanzania under Julius Nyerere set out self-reliance in the Arusha Declaration of 1967, known as Ujamaa.
Ujamaa villages and collective farming were created, together with education for self-reliance.
Literacy and school enrolment rose and Kiswahili was promoted as a uniting national language.
Forced villagisation from 1973 to 1976 and falling food output caused an economic crisis, and Tanzania agreed an IMF programme in 1986.
The Green Belt Movement, founded in Kenya in 1977 by Wangari Maathai, has seen women's groups plant tens of millions of trees for fuel, soil and income.
Zaï pits in Burkina Faso restore degraded farmland by trapping water and organic matter.
Complete self-reliance cuts countries off from trade, investment and technology, so communities still need fair trade rules and some outside support.
