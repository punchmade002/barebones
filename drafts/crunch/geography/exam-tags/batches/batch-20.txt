QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q119 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q19, 80 marks)

PART "19" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Conflicts arise when state boundaries and governments ignore or suppress the identity of cultural groups, as in Northern Ireland, Spain, Kurdistan and China.
Aspect 1 – Partition and Northern Ireland:
Northern Ireland was established in 1921 from six of Ulster's nine counties, which created a unionist majority with a large nationalist minority.
The border is about 499 km long and follows old county boundaries rather than physical features, so it cuts through communities and farms.
Discrimination against nationalists fuelled the civil rights movement from 1968, and the Troubles from the late 1960s to 1998 killed over 3,500 people.
The Good Friday Agreement was signed on 10 April 1998 and approved by about 71% in Northern Ireland and about 94% in the Republic in referendums on 22 May 1998.
It set up a power-sharing Assembly and Executive, a North/South Ministerial Council and the consent principle, without moving the border.
Articles 2 and 3 of the Irish Constitution were changed to an aspiration to unity by consent, in force from December 1999.
Brexit made the openness of the border an issue after Northern Ireland voted about 56% Remain in 2016, and the Windsor Framework of 2023 keeps the land border free of checks.
Aspect 2 – Basques and Catalans in Spain:
Basque and Catalan were banned from public life and schools under Franco from 1939 to 1975.
Spain's 1978 constitution created 17 autonomous communities, and the Basque Autonomous Community and Catalonia received statutes of autonomy in 1979.
ETA, founded in 1959, killed over 800 people in its campaign for an independent Basque state, and it declared an end to violence in 2011 and dissolved in 2018.
In 2010 Spain's Constitutional Court struck down parts of Catalonia's 2006 autonomy statute, including its description of Catalonia as a nation, which fuelled the independence movement.
On 1 October 2017 a Catalan independence referendum was declared illegal and police tried to stop the voting, and about 90% of those voting backed independence on a turnout of about 43%.
Spain imposed direct rule using Article 155 of its constitution, and the regional president Carles Puigdemont fled to Belgium.
Nine Catalan leaders were jailed in 2019 and pardoned in 2021, and an amnesty law was passed in 2024.
Aspect 3 – The Kurds:
The Kurds, commonly estimated at 30 million or more, are the largest ethnic group without a state, and their homeland of Kurdistan is divided between Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria.
The Treaty of Sèvres in 1920 provided for a possible Kurdish state, but the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923 drew the borders of modern Turkey with none.
Turkey long denied a separate Kurdish identity and banned Kurdish in public life until 1991 and in broadcasting until the early 2000s.
The PKK, founded in 1978, began an armed campaign in 1984 that cost about 40,000 lives, and it announced its dissolution in May 2025.
On 16 March 1988 Saddam Hussein's forces attacked the Iraqi Kurdish town of Halabja with chemical weapons and killed about 5,000 people.
Iraq's 2005 constitution recognised the Kurdistan Region as autonomous, with its parliament in Erbil, but after about 93% voted for independence on 25 September 2017 Iraqi forces retook Kirkuk.
The Kurdish language and Newroz on 21 March are major expressions of Kurdish identity.
Aspect 4 – Tibetans and Uyghurs in China:
China's army entered Tibet in 1950, and after a failed uprising in 1959 the Dalai Lama fled to India, where Tibet's government-in-exile is based at Dharamsala.
The Tibet Autonomous Region was set up in 1965.
Tibetans see Han Chinese migration, controls on monasteries and Mandarin-medium schooling as threats to their culture.
The Uyghurs are a Turkic-speaking, mainly Muslim people of Xinjiang in north-west China.
Since 2017 an estimated one million or more Uyghurs and other Muslims have been held in detention, or re-education, camps.
A UN human rights office report of August 2022 found serious human rights violations that may amount to crimes against humanity.

QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q120 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q20, 80 marks)

PART "20" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Language is an important cultural indicator because it carries a group's identity, can be classified and mapped, and survives or declines according to the strength of its speakers and of state support.
Aspect 1 – Language and identity:
Language carries a group's history, literature, songs, place names and ways of thinking, and it marks who belongs to the group.
Place names preserve the language of the past, as Irish elements such as baile, cill, dún, inis and cluain survive in names like Kilkenny (Cill Chainnigh), Dundalk (Dún Dealgan) and Clonmel (Cluain Meala).
The Gaelic League, founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill, led a language revival that fed the Irish independence movement.
Suppressing a language has been used to weaken a group, as with the Welsh Not in 19th-century schools, the ban on Basque and Catalan under Franco from 1939 to 1975 and the ban on Kurdish in Turkey until the early 1990s.
Colonial languages became official languages, such as English in India and Nigeria, French in Senegal and Spanish across most of Latin America.
A shared language can unite a state, but it can also divide, as the language border divides Belgium.
French is spoken in France, in Wallonia in Belgium, in western Switzerland and in Quebec, so languages cross borders.
Quebec's Charter of the French Language of 1977 made French the sole official language of Quebec, which shows language used to protect identity.
Article 8 of the 1937 Irish Constitution makes Irish the national language and the first official language.
Aspect 2 – Language families and the spread of languages:
About 7,000 languages are spoken in the world, grouped into families descended from a common ancestor, and each family divides into branches.
Indo-European is the largest family, spoken by nearly half the world's people, and it covers Europe, the Americas, Iran and South Asia.
Other families include Sino-Tibetan with Mandarin and Tibetan, Afro-Asiatic with Arabic and Hebrew, Niger-Congo with Swahili, Yoruba and Zulu, and Dravidian with Tamil and Telugu.
The main European branches of Indo-European are Germanic with English and German, Romance with French and Spanish, Slavic with Russian and Polish, and Celtic with Irish and Welsh.
The Celtic branch divides into Goidelic, with Irish, Scottish Gaelic and Manx, and Brythonic, with Welsh, Breton and Cornish.
Basque is an isolate unrelated to any other language, and Finnish, Estonian and Hungarian belong to the Uralic family.
Languages spread through migration, conquest and empire, as Latin spread with the Roman Empire and English, Spanish, French and Portuguese spread with colonialism.
Trade and religion also spread languages, as Arabic spread with Islam as the language of the Qur'an.
Today the media and internet are dominated by English, which has about 1.5 billion speakers counting second-language speakers, while Mandarin has the most native speakers.
Aspect 3 – Minority languages, decline and survival:
About 40% of the world's languages are endangered, because of conquest, the need for a dominant language for jobs and emigration, urbanisation, mass media and parents not passing the language on.
The 1851 census recorded about 1.5 million Irish speakers, about 23% of the population, before the Famine and emigration weakened the language.
In Census 2022 about 1.87 million people, about 40%, said they could speak Irish, but only about 72,000 spoke it daily outside the education system.
A 2007 study found that Irish is viable as a community language in the Gaeltacht only where at least 67% speak it daily.
Supports for Irish include Údarás na Gaeltachta from 1980, the Official Languages Act of 2003, the Language Commissioner from 2004, Gaelscoileanna and compulsory study in schools.
Raidió na Gaeltachta began in 1972 and TG4 was launched on 31 October 1996, showing the role of media in protecting a minority language.
In Census 2021 about 538,000 people, 17.8%, could speak Welsh, down from 19.0% in 2011, although S4C was launched in 1982 and the Welsh Language Measure of 2011 gave Welsh official status.
Wales has set a target of 1 million Welsh speakers by 2050, and Welsh is compulsory in schools up to age 16.
Manx was revived after its last native speaker, Ned Maddrell, died in 1974, while Breton has declined sharply where state support is weak, as France has not ratified the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages.

PART "21" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: People express their culture and identity in everyday life through visible, practised customs such as sport, music, festivals, food and dress, which mark belonging and pass culture from one generation to the next.
Aspect 1 – Sport:
The Gaelic Athletic Association was founded on 1 November 1884 in Hayes' Hotel, Thurles, Co. Tipperary, by Michael Cusack and others to promote native Irish games such as hurling and Gaelic football.
GAA clubs are based on parishes and teams represent counties, so the GAA reinforces local and county identity and supporters wear their county colours.
Members were banned from playing foreign games such as soccer, rugby, cricket and hockey until 1971.
On Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920, British forces opened fire at a football match in Croke Park and killed 14 people, and Croke Park now holds about 82,300 spectators.
Hurling was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2018.
In Northern Ireland sporting loyalties are divided between Irish and British teams, so the choice of team can express national identity.
FC Barcelona's motto Més que un club reflects its role as a symbol of Catalan identity under Franco, and Athletic Bilbao fields only players with Basque connections.
Aspect 2 – Traditional music and dance:
Traditional instruments include the fiddle, uilleann pipes, tin whistle, bodhrán and harp, and the harp is Ireland's national symbol on coins and the presidential seal.
Sessions are informal gatherings where musicians play together, and they are the main setting in which traditional music is shared.
Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann was founded in 1951, and the first Fleadh Cheoil na hÉireann was held in Mullingar in 1951.
The Fleadh is now held in a different town each August and draws hundreds of thousands of visitors.
Sean-nós is unaccompanied traditional singing in Irish, strongest in Conamara.
Riverdance began as the interval act of the Eurovision Song Contest in Dublin on 30 April 1994 and carried Irish dance to audiences worldwide.
Uilleann piping was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2017, and Irish harping in 2019.
Aspect 3 – Festivals and traditions:
St Patrick's Day on 17 March is marked by parades worldwide, as in New York, which makes it a global expression of Irish identity in the diaspora.
St Brigid's Day became a public holiday in 2023, the first named after a woman.
Samhain on 31 October and 1 November is the Celtic origin of Halloween.
Puck Fair in Killorglin, Co. Kerry, is held every August, when a wild goat is crowned king, and Ballinasloe Horse Fair in Co. Galway, held every October, is important to Travellers.
The Rose of Tralee, held since 1959, is open to women of Irish birth or descent worldwide, which links the diaspora to Ireland.
Orange Order parades on 12 July commemorate the Battle of the Boyne in 1690 and express unionist identity, but they can divide communities.
San Fermín in Pamplona, Spain, runs from 6 to 14 July with the running of the bulls, and castells, the human towers of Catalonia, express Catalan identity.
Aspect 4 – Food, dress and pressures on culture:
Traditional Irish dishes include soda bread, Irish stew, colcannon, boxty and coddle, which is a Dublin dish.
Guinness has been brewed at St James's Gate in Dublin since 1759.
The Aran sweater from the Aran Islands and the Claddagh ring from Galway are craft items that express Irish identity.
In Scotland the kilt, worn with tartan, is the best-known national dress, and Burns Night on 25 January celebrates Robert Burns with haggis and poetry.
Oktoberfest in Munich has been held since 1810, and visitors wear lederhosen and dirndl.
Globalisation spreads the same brands and foods, as McDonald's opened its first Irish restaurant on Grafton Street in 1977.
Migration adds new expressions, as Census 2022 recorded Polish as the most common home language after English and Irish, and Polish shops and food now sit beside traditional dishes.
