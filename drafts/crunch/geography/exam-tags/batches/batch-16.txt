PART "9B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Developed economy – Ireland:
Ireland is a developed economy with high incomes and a large services and high-technology sector.
Footloose industries are not tied to raw materials or a market, because their products are small, light and high in value, so they can choose from many possible sites.
Development – Government and EU support:
IDA Ireland, set up in 1949, attracts foreign direct investment by offering grants, serviced sites and advance factories.
Corporation tax of 12.5% from 2003, rising to 15% for very large groups from 2024, attracted multinational firms.
EU membership, since 1973, gives access to the single market.
Lower transport costs, containerisation, air freight and instant communications make footloose firms more mobile.
Example – Intel at Leixlip:
Intel opened its plant at Leixlip, Co. Kildare, in 1989, and it is Ireland's largest private-sector investment, with over €30 billion invested by 2023.
The plant is on the M4 motorway west of Dublin and close to Dublin Airport, which suits exporting light, high-value chips.
Skilled graduates are available from Maynooth University nearby and from the universities in Dublin.
It occupies a large greenfield site with a supply of electricity and water, which fabs need in huge volumes.
Fab 34, opened in 2023, makes advanced chips using extreme ultraviolet lithography, showing the plant moving up the value chain.
Example – Pharmaceutical cluster at Cork Harbour:
Pfizer, Janssen and Novartis are at Ringaskiddy and Eli Lilly is at Kinsale, forming a cluster around Cork Harbour.
The Port of Cork has deep-water berths at Ringaskiddy, large flat sites are zoned for industry, and skilled labour comes from UCC and MTU.
Pharmaceuticals and chemicals are Ireland's largest goods exports.
Limits and risks:
Footloose firms depend on decisions at distant headquarters, so jobs can leave quickly.
Dell moved manufacturing from Limerick to Łódź in Poland in 2009, with about 1,900 jobs lost.
Global job cuts announced by Intel in 2024 and 2025 affected Leixlip.

PART "9C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact 1 – Structural Funds and infrastructure:
The European Regional Development Fund, set up in 1975, funds infrastructure, enterprise and research in poorer regions, which is the main impact of EU policy on regional development in Ireland.
Ireland was a single Objective 1 region from 1989 to 1999 because its income per head was below 75% of the EU average, and EU funds co-financed the National Development Plans.
The funds built inter-urban motorways, bypasses, water and wastewater treatment, ports, airports and broadband.
The Cohesion Fund, begun in 1993 to 1994, financed transport and environmental projects while Ireland's income was below 90% of the EU average.
Better access for peripheral regions helped to attract foreign direct investment and contributed to the Celtic Tiger.
From 2000 to 2006 the Border, Midland and Western region kept Objective 1 status, which targeted the poorer parts of the country.
Impact 2 – Common Agricultural Policy and rural areas:
The Common Agricultural Policy, launched in 1962, gave Irish farmers guaranteed prices after 1973, and farm incomes rose sharply in rural areas.
Farms modernised with machinery, fertiliser and drainage, which supported rural communities.
Areas of Natural Constraint payments support farms on disadvantaged land.
LEADER, from 1991, funds local rural enterprise such as farm diversification, rural tourism and crafts.
Payments historically favoured bigger farmers, small western farms remain uneconomic and farmers are ageing, so regional gaps persist.
Impact 3 – Common Fisheries Policy and coastal areas:
The Common Fisheries Policy gives EU funding for boats, harbours and processing plants, which supports peripheral coastal areas such as Killybegs in Co. Donegal.
It opened Irish waters to larger fleets from Spain, France and the Netherlands, and decommissioning of boats caused job losses in coastal communities.
Brexit cost Ireland about 26% of its mackerel quota, which hit fishing ports.
Impact 4 – Social funding and the persisting gap:
The European Social Fund co-financed training, including Youthreach from 1989, and the skilled workforce helped to attract foreign direct investment.
The Just Transition Fund for 2021 to 2027 targets the Midlands peat region after Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting for electricity.
The funds helped Ireland to converge with EU incomes, but the gap between the Dublin-centred east and the Northern and Western region has persisted.

PART "10B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact of falling fertility – Ireland:
In Ireland the total fertility rate fell from about 4 in the early 1970s to below replacement level around 1990 and was about 1.5 in 2023.
Fertility fell because of free secondary education from 1967, more women in third-level education and paid work, the removal of the marriage bar in 1973 and legal contraception from 1979.
Births peaked at about 74,000 in 1980 and were about 55,000 in 2023, so the base of the population pyramid has narrowed.
About one-fifth of the population is now under 15, far fewer than when large families were the norm.
The pyramid has moved from a young, wide-based shape to an ageing one.
Impact of falling mortality – Ireland:
Life expectancy at birth is about 82 to 83 years and infant mortality is about 3 per 1,000 live births.
A good diet and housing, effective health services, vaccination and clean water explain the low mortality.
Rising life expectancy widens the top of the pyramid, so Census 2022 recorded about 776,000 people aged 65 and over, about 15% of the population.
The average age rose from 37.4 in 2016 to 38.8 in 2022, which raises the old-age dependency ratio and the costs of pensions and health care.
Second example – Japan:
Japan has a total fertility rate of about 1.2 and about 29% of its people are aged 65 or over, the highest share in the world.
Its pyramid is top-heavy, with a narrow base and a wide top.
The population peaked at about 128 million around 2008 to 2010 and is falling, with a shrinking workforce and rising pension and health costs.
Contrast – Niger:
Niger has a total fertility rate of about 6 and a median age of about 15, so its pyramid has a very wide base.
High fertility and falling death rates give growth of over 3% a year, which places Niger in stage 2 of the demographic transition model.
Niger's population is about 27 million, so a large young population needs schools and future jobs.

PART "10C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named settlement – Dublin:
Dublin, the capital, has grown rapidly, and the Greater Dublin Area had about 2.1 million people in Census 2022.
Issue 1 – Planning issues from urban sprawl:
Planning issues arise when growth is uncontrolled, as in the sprawl of Dublin into the commuter counties of Meath, Kildare and Wicklow.
Causes include rising car ownership, motorways, high inner-city prices and the zoning of greenfield land, with tax incentives during the Celtic Tiger.
The Wright Report of 1967 proposed the new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown on the western edge of the city.
The towns were built before services, jobs and transport, so residents depended on distant jobs and facilities.
The Square shopping centre in Tallaght opened only in 1990 and the Luas Red Line to Tallaght opened in 2004, decades after the first homes.
A Government survey in 2010 found almost 3,000 unfinished housing estates, called ghost estates, after the 2008 property crash.
Housing supply is also a planning issue, as over 15,000 people were in State-funded emergency accommodation in 2025 and rents remain high.
The National Planning Framework of 2018 responds by aiming for 40% of new homes within the built-up footprint of existing settlements.
Issue 2 – Environmental issues from traffic and pollution:
Traffic congestion arises because radial roads converge on the centre and there are few crossings of the River Liffey.
Stop-start traffic produces air and noise pollution and carbon emissions from idling engines.
Planners responded with the Luas in 2004, the Port Tunnel in 2006 and the M50 orbital motorway, built in stages from 1990.
Sprawl causes loss of farmland and habitats at the edge of the city.
Long commutes from dormitory towns raise carbon emissions and car dependence.
Smoke and smog from burning smoky coal were a serious problem, and the smoky coal ban introduced in Dublin on 1 September 1990 largely removed it before it was extended nationwide in October 2022.
The Ringsend wastewater treatment plant was upgraded to reduce discharges into Dublin Bay.
Nitrogen dioxide from traffic and particulates from solid fuel remain problems, so the Clean Air Strategy was published in 2023.
