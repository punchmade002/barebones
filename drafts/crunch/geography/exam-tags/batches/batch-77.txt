PART 1B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Rock 1 – Granite, a plutonic rock:
Granite is a plutonic igneous rock formed when magma rises into the crust and cools and crystallises below the surface.
The magma collects in a huge dome-shaped mass called a batholith deep in the crust.
The surrounding rock insulates the magma, so it cools very slowly over millions of years.
Slow cooling gives time for large interlocking crystals to grow.
The crystals are quartz, feldspar and mica, which give granite its speckled appearance.
Magma can also force into cracks to form vertical dykes that cut across the rock layers and horizontal sills along bedding planes.
The granite reaches the surface only after the rock above it has been eroded away.
An Irish example is the Leinster batholith, the core of the Wicklow Mountains, which is about 400 million years old and formed when the Iapetus Ocean closed during Caledonian mountain building.
Rock 2 – Basalt, a volcanic rock:
Basalt is a volcanic igneous rock formed from lava erupted onto the earth's surface.
Magma rises through cracks and fissures to the surface at constructive boundaries and hotspots.
Basalt lava is runny, so it flows a long way and spreads out in wide sheets.
The lava is exposed to air or water at the surface and cools quickly.
Rapid cooling allows only small crystals to form, so basalt is dark and fine-grained.
As thick lava flows cool and contract, they can crack into columns.
An Irish example is the Antrim plateau, which was built by lava flows about 60 million years ago as the Atlantic began to open, and the Giant's Causeway is formed in it.

PART 1C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Isostasy explained:
Isostasy is the balance between the crust, which floats on the denser mantle, and the mantle beneath it.
During the Ice Age the weight of ice sheets pressed the crust down, and when the ice melted the land slowly rose again in isostatic rebound.
Impact 1 – Raised beaches in the north:
The north of Ireland had thicker ice, so it was depressed more and has undergone more isostatic rebound.
Rebound lifted former beaches above present high tide, forming raised beaches on an emergent coast.
Each raised beach has an old cliff line inland of the sea, with a wave-cut notch cut at the former sea level.
A flat wave-cut platform and beach shingle lie in front of the old cliff, above the reach of the tide.
Sea caves and stacks may also stand stranded above the present tide reach.
An Irish example is the raised beach at Malin Head on the Inishowen peninsula, Co. Donegal.
Rebound lagged behind melting, and meltwater also raised sea level worldwide, so the land level relative to the sea is the combined result of isostatic and eustatic change.
Impact 2 – Drowned coast in the south-west:
The south-west shows the opposite pattern to the north, because its coast is drowned.
Where the sea stands higher relative to the land, the coast is submergent and valleys and lowlands are flooded.
A ria is a drowned river valley with a V-shaped cross-section and a winding outline.
Irish examples of rias are Bantry Bay and the Kenmare River in Co. Cork and Co. Kerry.
Drowned drumlins form where lowland between drumlins floods, so the crests stand as islands, as in Clew Bay on the west coast in Co. Mayo.
In fluvial landscapes a fall in base level gives rejuvenation, so the river cuts down again and forms knickpoints, incised meanders and river terraces.
A rise in base level drowns the lower course of a river, which becomes an estuary or a ria.

PART 2B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option (i) – delta, a fluvial landform:
A delta is a fan-shaped or finger-shaped landform of deposited sediment at the mouth of a river where it enters a sea or lake.
The river must carry a heavy load of sediment from its upper and middle courses.
When the river enters the sea or lake its velocity drops sharply.
The river loses energy, so it can no longer carry its load and deposits it, which is the process of deposition.
The heaviest material is deposited first and the finest last, so the deposits are sorted by size.
Calm water with a small tidal range and weak currents is needed so the deposits are not removed.
Deposits build up until they block the channel, forcing the river to split into smaller channels called distributaries.
Topset beds are flat, coarser deposits laid down on top of the delta.
Foreset beds are steeply sloping deposits at the front of the delta, built out into the water.
Bottomset beds are fine, flat layers laid down beyond the front of the delta.
Repeated deposition builds the delta outward into the water over time.
An arcuate delta is fan-shaped, as in the Nile delta, Egypt.
A bird's-foot delta has long fingers of deposits along its distributaries, as in the Mississippi delta, USA.
An Irish example is the delta between the Upper and Lower Lakes at Glendalough, Co. Wicklow, which divided what was once a single lake.
At Glendalough the inflowing river dropped its load as it entered the still water of the lake and built up the delta over time.
The resulting landform is a low, flat area of sediment crossed by the river's channels.

PART 2C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Process – Carbonation:
The chemical weathering process that shapes karst is carbonation.
Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form soluble calcium bicarbonate, which is carried away in solution.
Limestone is permeable because water moves along its bedding planes and joints, so carbonation works along these lines of weakness.
Landform 1 – Limestone pavement:
Ice scraped away the soil cover, exposing the jointed, bare limestone to the rain.
Carbonation widened the joints into deep, narrow vertical cracks called grikes.
The flat-topped blocks left standing between the grikes are called clints.
Rainwater running across the clints cuts small grooves called karren, also known as runnels.
Rainwater collects in small hollows on the clints and dissolves the rock to form solution hollows.
Landform 2 – Swallow holes and dolines:
A surface stream flowing off impermeable rock reaches the limestone and disappears down a swallow hole widened by carbonation along a joint.
A doline is a hollow formed by solution of the surface or by collapse of a cave roof.
Several dolines can merge into one larger hollow called an uvala.
Named example – The Burren, Co. Clare:
The Burren is Ireland's best-known karst landscape, formed on Carboniferous limestone laid down in a warm, shallow sea about 360 to 300 million years ago.
Its extensive limestone pavement of clints and grikes is the surface karst landscape, and its name comes from the Irish Boireann, meaning rocky place.

[geo-rivers-s1.15.3] Braided channels form where the load is large and discharge varies, so the river cannot carry all its sediment and drops it as islands in the channel.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.b] Every fluvial landform is formed by erosion, by deposition, or by both. Sorting them this way is a skill that exams test.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.1] Erosion and deposition: meander, oxbow lake.
[geo-rivers-s1.16.2] Deposition: floodplain, levee, delta, point bar, estuary mudflats.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.b] Carbonation is a chemical weathering process. It dissolves limestone, and it works along joints and bedding planes (the flat surfaces between layers of rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.0] Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.1] The acid reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble, so it is carried away in solution (dissolved in water).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.2] Word equation: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide gives calcium bicarbonate.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.b] Karst is a landscape on limestone shaped mainly by solution (rock dissolving in water), with few surface streams and many underground features. The name comes from the Kras plateau of Slovenia. Limestone suits this because of its chemistry and the way water moves through it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.0] Limestone is calcium carbonate, which dissolves in weak acid. Carbonation: rainwater containing carbon dioxide forms weak carbonic acid, which reacts with the rock to form soluble calcium bicarbonate: CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 gives Ca(HCO3)2.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.2] Limestone is permeable because water moves along its bedding planes (horizontal layers) and joints (vertical cracks), not through the solid rock itself.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.4] Percolation is the downward movement of water through soil and rock, which is how rainwater reaches the limestone's cracks.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.b] Limestone pavement is a bare, flat limestone surface. It exists because glacial ice scraped away the soil, leaving jointed rock open to the rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.0] Stage 1: ice removes the soil cover, exposing the jointed and bedded rock (rock with horizontal layers and vertical cracks) to rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.1] Stage 2: carbonation, the reaction of the rock with carbonic acid in rainwater, widens the joints into grikes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.2] Stage 3: the blocks left standing between the grikes are clints, because the rock there was not dissolved away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.3] Burren, Co. Clare: Ireland's best-known karst landscape, of Carboniferous limestone with extensive pavement of clints and grikes. Its name comes from the Irish 'Boireann', meaning rocky place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.4] Mullaghmore is a Burren hill with folded, terraced limestone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.b] A pavement is made of a few small features, and exam photographs ask you to name each one.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.0] Grikes: deep, narrow vertical cracks, because carbonation widens the rock along joints, where water collects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.1] Clints: flat-topped blocks of limestone between grikes, so together they look like paving.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.2] Karren (runnels): small grooves cut into clint surfaces because rainwater running across them slowly dissolves the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.3] Solution hollows (kamenitza or solution pans): small enclosed hollows on clint surfaces where rainwater collects and dissolves the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.4] Diagram: plan of clints and grikes; cross-section showing bedding planes and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.0] Swallow hole (slugga): a surface stream flowing off impermeable rock reaches limestone and disappears underground down a hole widened by carbonation along a joint.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.1] Doline (sinkhole): a hollow formed by solution of the surface, or by collapse of a cave roof. A doline formed by cave collapse is still a doline.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.2] Uvala: several dolines merged into one larger hollow, as their edges dissolve or collapse into each other.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.1] Turloughs are common in the Burren and east Co. Galway. Rahasane turlough, near Craughwell, Co. Galway, is one of the largest.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.2] River of resurgence: an underground river that re-emerges at the surface, usually where limestone meets impermeable rock below. It is the reverse of a swallow hole, which takes a surface stream underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.3] Diagram: cross-section from impermeable rock, through a swallow hole, underground to the resurgence.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.b] Below the surface, the same solution widens cracks in the limestone into hollow spaces that can be large enough to walk through.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.0] Caves and caverns: water dissolves passages along bedding planes and joints, and underground rivers enlarge them by erosion (wearing away rock). A cavern is a very large cave.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.1] Caves occur where limestone is dissolved, for example: Aillwee Cave, Co. Clare (Burren); Mitchelstown Cave, Co. Tipperary.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.2] Doolin Cave, Co. Clare, holds the Great Stalactite, about 7.3 m long, one of the longest free-hanging stalactites in the world.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.0] Dripstone formation: water dripping from the roof carries dissolved calcium bicarbonate. When it loses carbon dioxide and evaporates, it deposits calcite (calcium carbonate).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.4] Curtains are sheets of calcite formed where water trickles along a crack in the roof.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.7.b] A karst landscape develops through three stages.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.7.0] Youth: surface drainage begins to sink, so swallow holes form.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.7.1] Maturity: most drainage goes underground because the swallow holes now take it, with caves, dolines and uvalas.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.b] Ireland has three named karst settings in the syllabus. Besides the Burren, they show streams sinking into limestone and water travelling through it unseen.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.1] Streams flowing off the impermeable shale and sandstone of Cuilcagh sink into swallow holes where they reach the limestone, flow through the caves and re-emerge as the Cladagh River.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.9.b] Karst occurs wherever limestone is dissolved, and each international setting is known for a different feature.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.2] Stage 2: the rotted material was later stripped away, e.g. by mass movement (downslope movement of material) under periglacial conditions (cold conditions beside ice), leaving solid, widely jointed blocks standing as tors.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.3] 4. The stored energy is released as seismic waves, which are vibrations that travel through the Earth and shake the ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.b] Four key terms describe where an earthquake starts and how its energy spreads. Learn which is underground and which is on the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.0] Focus (hypocentre): the point inside the crust where the rupture begins, so it is always underground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.1] Epicentre: the point on the surface directly above the focus, where shaking is usually strongest.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.2] Fault (fault line): the fracture along which the rocks move.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.3] Seismic (shock) waves: waves of energy spreading out from the focus in all directions.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.4] Diagram: draw a cross-section showing the fault, the focus below ground, seismic waves spreading out, and the epicentre on the surface directly above the focus.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.5] Depth matters: a shallow-focus earthquake causes more surface damage than a deep-focus one of the same magnitude (the energy released), because the waves have less rock to travel through before reaching the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.0] P (primary) waves are the fastest seismic waves, so they arrive first.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.b] Scientists can record seismic waves with an instrument and use the records from several places to pinpoint where an earthquake struck.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.0] A seismograph (seismometer) is the instrument that detects and records seismic waves; the record it produces is a seismogram.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.1] A barometer measures air pressure and is not an earthquake instrument.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.0] The Richter scale measures magnitude, the energy released, from the size of the waves on a seismogram.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.1] The Richter scale is logarithmic (each step is a multiplication, not an addition): each whole number up means ten times the wave amplitude (the height of the wave) and about 30 times more energy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.2] The moment magnitude scale is the modern replacement for large earthquakes. Either scale is an accepted answer for a scale that measures magnitude.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.3] The modified Mercalli scale measures intensity, the observed effects and damage at a place, on a scale of I to XII.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.10.0] Building and bridge collapse, deaths and injuries, and ground rupture (the ground splitting open along the fault).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.b] Two earthquakes of the same magnitude can do very different damage. The outcome depends on the earthquake itself, on the ground, and on the people and buildings in its path.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.0] Magnitude: larger earthquakes release more energy.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.1] Depth of focus: a shallow-focus earthquake causes more surface damage than a deep-focus one of the same magnitude.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.12.7] Haiti, 12 January 2010: magnitude 7.0 near Port-au-Prince. Poorly built concrete buildings collapsed, and the country, a poor one, had little capacity to respond. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, all over 100,000.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.b] Kobe shows how a moderate-magnitude earthquake with a shallow focus, close to a big city, can be devastating. Begin a case-study answer with the date, place, plate setting and magnitude.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.0] Place: the Great Hanshin earthquake, Kobe, Japan, 17 January 1995. Its epicentre was near the northern tip of Awaji Island, and its focus was shallow.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.13.1] Magnitude 6.9 on the moment magnitude scale.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.14.1] Many older wooden houses with heavy tiled roofs collapsed, because the heavy roofs sat on weak frames.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.15.b] This was an undersea earthquake at a subduction zone, and the seabed movement triggered a tsunami.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.2] Seismic gap theory: a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time has the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.2] Japan has a national earthquake early-warning system.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.b] Buildings can be designed to survive shaking, which limits deaths and damage. Each measure either stops a building from being shaken or lets it move without breaking.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.2] Flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames that sway rather than crack, with cross-bracing and shear walls (extra supports that stiffen the structure).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.4] Automatic shut-off valves on gas mains, and flexible joints on pipes, so pipes bend rather than break and gas does not leak.
