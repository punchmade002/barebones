India is a developing economy of South Asia that opened up to world trade and investment after 1991.

After the 1991 crisis, reforms removed most licences, cut tariffs and welcomed foreign investment, which opened India to globalisation.
India became a founding member of the World Trade Organisation in 1995, giving it access to world markets.
Services now make up over half of Indian GDP.
Bengaluru is the base of the IT and outsourcing industry, with firms such as TCS and Infosys serving overseas clients.
English, inherited from British rule, is an asset because it lets Indian firms serve customers in English-speaking markets.
Multinationals invest in India, encouraged by the Make in India programme of 2014.
India is now among the five largest economies in the world by GDP.
India is the world's largest recipient of remittances, which bring foreign money to families.

Growth from globalisation has been uneven, because about nine-tenths of Indian workers are in the informal sector without contracts, pensions or legal protection.
Agriculture still employs about 55 per cent of workers and has low productivity, so most farmers gained little from the IT boom.
Inequality exists between states, as literacy was about 94 per cent in Kerala but about 62 per cent in Bihar in Census 2011.
Inequality also exists between rich and poor, so globalisation has widened the gap between urban professionals and rural workers.
Infrastructure gaps and pollution remain serious problems, because growth has been faster than investment in roads, power and clean air.
Many Indian firms and workers depend on overseas demand, so a downturn in the world economy hits Indian jobs.

MODEL ANSWER: Named developed economy – Ireland:
Ireland is a developed economy where footloose industries such as software, internet services, data processing and financial services are not tied to raw materials or power.
Factor 1 – Human factors:
Skilled graduates come from Trinity College Dublin, University College Dublin, Dublin City University, TU Dublin and Maynooth University, so firms find the workers they need.
Ireland has a young, educated, English-speaking workforce, which suits US firms serving Europe.
Dublin Airport, with over 30 million passengers a year in 2023, and fibre connections link the region to the world.
Agglomeration occurs when firms gain from being close together, so Microsoft's presence since 1985 and Google's European headquarters in Dublin since 2003 drew in Meta, LinkedIn and Salesforce.
Silicon Docks at Grand Canal Dock is the best known cluster of digital firms in Ireland.
The cluster provides well-paid jobs in offices and related services.
Demand for offices and housing has pushed rents up, and the concentration of workers adds to congestion in Dublin.
High housing and living costs now make it harder to recruit staff, which can limit further growth.
Factor 2 – Political factors:
Corporation tax of twelve and a half per cent, rising to fifteen per cent for very large groups from 2024, attracts footloose firms.
IDA Ireland, set up in 1949, offers grants and serviced sites and promotes Ireland to foreign investors.
Ireland joined the EEC in 1973, and the Single Market from 1993 gives firms in Ireland access to the whole EU.
The Shannon Free Zone, set up in 1959, offered tax relief and duty-free imports of components, and aircraft leasing grew from it.
Ireland now manages about half of the world's leased commercial aircraft.
The International Financial Services Centre opened in the Dublin Docklands in 1987, and the Dublin Docklands Development Authority was set up in 1997 to plan regeneration.
Political decisions can also limit growth, as EirGrid said in 2022 that it would not connect new data centres in the Dublin region until about 2028.
Ireland's public finances depend on corporation tax from a few large multinationals, so a policy change abroad can hit the economy.

PART 9C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Policy 1 – Common Agricultural Policy:
The CAP raised farm prices and incomes sharply after Ireland joined the EEC in 1973.
It gave Irish beef and dairy producers a guaranteed market.
Farms modernised with machinery, fertiliser, silage, land drainage and farm buildings, in what is remembered as the golden age of the 1970s.
Milk quotas from 1984 capped farm output and held back Irish dairying for 30 years.
When quotas ended on 1 April 2015, Irish milk output rose from about 5.5 billion litres in 2014 to about 8.3 billion litres in 2020.
Intensification damaged water quality and raised greenhouse gas emissions as the dairy herd grew.
Direct payments, begun after the 1992 MacSharry reform, now support farm incomes, and the CAP plan for 2023 to 2027 is worth about 9.8 billion euro.
Policy 2 – Regional and social funds:
The European Regional Development Fund, set up in 1975, funded infrastructure and enterprise in poorer regions.
Ireland was a single Objective 1 region from 1989 to 1999 because its income per head was below 75 per cent of the EU average.
The Cohesion Fund from 1993 financed transport and environmental projects, including roads, bypasses and motorways.
The European Social Fund co-financed training through FÁS, and the skilled workforce created this way helped attract foreign direct investment.
Ireland became a net contributor to the EU budget in 2014 because its economy had grown, so it now receives far less.
Policy 3 – Common Fisheries Policy:
The Common Fisheries Policy, agreed in 1983, gives all member states' fleets access to EU waters and sets total allowable catches divided into national quotas.
Ireland's quota share is small relative to the size and richness of its waters, which is a negative effect.
Under Brexit the EU–UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement transferred quota to the UK, and Ireland lost about 26 per cent of its mackerel quota.

[geo-economic-development-s1.0.0] Economic development means growth in a country's output (the goods and services it produces) and income, with rising living standards for its people.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.1] Development is wider than income: it also includes health, education, political freedom, equality and quality of life. A country can grow richer without improving these, which is why income alone is not enough.
[geo-economic-development-s1.0.3] The World Bank classes countries as low, lower-middle, upper-middle or high income by GNI per head (gross national income divided by population). The thresholds change each year, so do not quote any.
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.1] Gross national product (GNP) is GDP plus income earned abroad by the country's residents and firms, minus income sent out of the country to foreign owners as profits, interest and dividends (payments to shareholders).
[geo-economic-development-s1.1.3] Gross national income (GNI) is the term the World Bank and UN now use, and it is essentially the same as GNP. Data tables may use either term, so treat them as the same measure.
[geo-economic-development-s1.4.5] It says nothing directly about health, education, freedom or happiness, which is why a broader measure was needed.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.3] From 2017 the Central Statistics Office (CSO) published modified gross national income (GNI*), which removes the profits of redomiciled firms (firms that moved their legal base to Ireland), depreciation (loss of value through wear and age) on foreign-owned intellectual property and leased aircraft.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.4] In the early 2020s Ireland's GNI* was only a little over half of its GDP, which shows how large the distortion is.
[geo-economic-development-s1.5.5] Ireland has the second-highest GDP per head in the EU, after Luxembourg (Eurostat, early 2020s), so GDP overstates Irish prosperity. Use GNI* as the better guide to Irish living standards.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.b] The HDI combines health, education and income into one value. It was designed to measure people's well-being, not just the economy.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.0] The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) first published the HDI in its Human Development Report in 1990.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.2] A long and healthy life is measured by life expectancy at birth (how many years a newborn can expect to live).
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.3] Knowledge is measured by mean (average) years of schooling for adults aged 25 and over, and expected years of schooling for children starting school.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.4] A decent standard of living is measured by GNI per head at PPP dollars. The income indicator is GNI per head, not GDP.
[geo-economic-development-s1.6.7] Older textbooks use adult literacy and gross enrolment (the share of children enrolled in school) for education; these were replaced by mean and expected years of schooling in 2010.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.0] Very high HDI: Europe (especially Scandinavia and Switzerland), North America, Australia and Japan. Norway ranked 1st (0.957) in the Human Development Report 2020, using 2019 data. Ireland and Switzerland were joint 2nd (0.955) in the same report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.2] High HDI: Brazil, at about 0.76 in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.7.3] Medium HDI: India, 134th of 193 countries (0.644) in the 2023/24 report.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.0] It is broader than income alone, combining health, education and income.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.2] It shows countries whose income is high but whose human development lags, such as oil-rich states, and countries that achieve good health and education on modest incomes.
[geo-economic-development-s1.8.4] It helps governments and aid agencies target spending, for example on schooling or health, because it shows which area is weakest.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.b] Like any single number, the HDI leaves things out and hides differences. The UNDP has tried to answer some of these criticisms.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.2] Data are poor or out of date in some developing countries, and the figures are published about two years after the data year.
[geo-economic-development-s1.9.4] Very high-income countries cluster close to 1, so small differences between them mean little.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.b] GNP and HDI measure different things, so a country can rank very differently on each. Using both, with other indicators, gives the fullest picture.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.0] GNP measures output and income; HDI measures human outcomes in health, education and living standards.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.1] Equatorial Guinea has far higher GNI per head than its HDI rank suggests, because of oil wealth; the gap between its income rank and its HDI rank is large.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.2] Oil-rich Gulf states generally rank higher on income than on HDI.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.3] Cuba ranks higher on HDI than on income, because of strong public health care and education.
[geo-economic-development-s1.10.4] GNI per head is one of the four HDI indicators, so the two measures overlap; the HDI adds life expectancy and schooling to it.
