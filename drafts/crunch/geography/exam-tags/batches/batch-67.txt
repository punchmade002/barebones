PART "2B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option (ii) – Bog burst, a mass movement process:
A bog burst is a rapid mass movement in which saturated blanket peat bursts and flows downhill as a black slurry.
It happens on sloping blanket bog in the uplands of western and north-western Ireland.
The flowing peat blocks rivers, kills fish and destroys roads, bridges and land.
Factor 1 – Heavy rainfall and saturation:
Prolonged or very intense rain saturates the blanket bog.
The saturated peat gains weight, which increases the downslope force of gravity.
Water fills the pores and lubricates the base of the peat, so friction between the peat and the underlying mineral soil or rock is reduced.
When the downslope force exceeds the resistance, the peat bursts and flows as a liquid slurry.
Factor 2 – Steep gradient and slope material:
The steeper the slope, the greater the downslope pull of gravity, so bog bursts occur on sloping uplands.
Peat is a loose, spongy material that holds large volumes of water, so it moves easily once it is saturated.
Impermeable rock or mineral soil beneath the peat stops water draining away and forms a slippery surface for the peat to slide on.
Bare or poorly vegetated slopes have fewer roots to bind the peat, so it is easily released.
Example – Pollatomish, Co. Mayo:
At Pollatomish on Dooncarton Mountain in Co. Mayo, an intense thunderstorm on 19 September 2003 triggered about 40 landslides and peat slides in a few hours.
Houses, roads, a bridge and a graveyard were damaged and several families were evacuated.
At Knocknageeha near Rathmore in Co. Kerry in December 1896, a bog burst swept away a house and killed a family of eight.

PART "2C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named Irish rock – Quartzite:
Quartzite is a metamorphic rock formed when sandstone is changed by great heat and pressure.
Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by heat and pressure without melting.
The minerals in the sandstone recrystallise while the rock stays solid, so the quartzite becomes pale, very hard and glassy.
Quartzite forms Errigal in Co. Donegal.
It also forms Croagh Patrick in Co. Mayo, the Great Sugar Loaf in Co. Wicklow and the Twelve Bens in Connemara.
Regional metamorphism:
Regional metamorphism happens when heat and pressure act over huge areas during plate collision and mountain building.
The closing of the Iapetus Ocean during Caledonian mountain building produced regional metamorphism in Ireland.
In regional metamorphism the rock is squeezed and recrystallised, so it may develop banding or cleavage.
Shale or mudstone becomes slate and, with more heat and pressure, becomes schist.
Mica schist is found in Donegal and Connemara as a result.
Thermal metamorphism and a second rock – Mica schist:
Thermal or contact metamorphism happens when rock beside an igneous intrusion is baked by its heat.
This forms a ring of altered rock called a metamorphic aureole, which is small in scale.
A metamorphic aureole of mica schist surrounds the Leinster granite in the Wicklow area.
The Leinster batholith, the granite core of the Wicklow Mountains, is about 400 million years old.
Other parent rocks and the oldest rock:
Limestone becomes marble when it is heated and squeezed.
Granite becomes gneiss under heat and pressure.
Gneiss at Inishtrahull, off Co. Donegal, is about 1.7 billion years old and is the oldest rock in Ireland.

PART "3B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Reference to temperature:
Temperature acts as a trigger in physical weathering through the expansion and contraction that cause stress in rock.
Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals.
It depends on temperature changes, not simply on warmth.
Process 1 – Freeze-thaw action:
Freeze-thaw action needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often.
Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
At night, when the temperature falls below freezing, the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
By day, when the temperature rises above freezing, the ice thaws and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular fragments.
It is most effective on mountain summits such as Carrauntoohil in Co. Kerry and in periglacial areas.
The result is scree slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields.
Process 2 – Exfoliation:
Exfoliation is an insolation process in hot deserts such as the Sahara, where the daily temperature range is large.
The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day, then cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
Stress builds up and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion, leaving rounded boulders.
Process 3 – Granular disintegration:
In a rock such as granite the dark and light minerals heat and expand at different rates.
The differing expansion loosens individual grains, which fall away leaving gravelly sand.
Second reference to temperature:
Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, while hot deserts with a large daily range favour exfoliation, so the dominant process depends on the temperature regime.

PART "3C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact 1 – Deposition in the reservoir:
A dam changes river processes because it stores water in a reservoir and regulates the flow downstream.
Velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load there.
The reservoir slowly fills with sediment, which reduces its capacity.
A delta can build where the river enters the reservoir.
Impact 2 – Clear-water erosion downstream:
Water released from the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy.
This water erodes the bed and banks below the dam.
The river bed below the Aswan High Dam on the Nile is scoured in this way.
Impact 3 – Loss of floodplain deposition:
Discharge is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
Less flooding means less deposition of fresh silt on the floodplain.
Silt is trapped in Lake Nasser, so farmers in Egypt downstream rely on artificial fertilisers.
The delta receives no new silt, so the Nile delta coast is eroding, for example at the Rosetta promontory.
Location – Aswan High Dam, River Nile, Egypt:
The Aswan High Dam was completed in 1970 and its reservoir is Lake Nasser, which extends into Sudan.
The dam provides flood control, year-round irrigation and hydro-electricity.
Irish example – Ardnacrusha on the Shannon:
At Ardnacrusha near Limerick, Parteen Weir diverts most of the Shannon's water into a headrace canal to the power station.
The original channel between Parteen and Limerick, the Old River Shannon, carries much less water as a result.
Salmon and eel stocks declined because the dam blocks migrating fish.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.b] Nobody has drilled through the earth, so its structure is worked out from earthquake (seismic) waves. Their speed and direction change as they pass through layers of different density.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.0] Seismic waves reveal the earth's structure because the changes in their speed and direction at each boundary are how the discontinuities were found.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.2] S-waves (secondary waves) pass only through solids, so they are stopped by any liquid layer.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.3] S-waves do not pass through the outer core, which shows that the outer core is liquid.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s1.7.4] This leaves an S-wave shadow zone (an area where S-waves are not detected) on the far side of the earth from an earthquake.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.6] Sea-floor spreading supplied the missing mechanism and turned continental drift into the theory of plate tectonics.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.1] The earth has seven major plates: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian and Antarctic. The Pacific plate is the largest.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.3] Some maps split the Indo-Australian plate into the Indian plate and the Australian plate; both versions are accepted.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.6.0] Ridge push is new, hot crust at the ridge sliding away from it under gravity, so the plate is shoved away from the ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.8.b] Ireland's position on the plate map determines its present geological activity.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.8.0] Ireland lies on the Eurasian plate, near its western edge and far from any plate boundary, which means it sits in the interior of a plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.8.1] The nearest plate boundary is the Mid-Atlantic Ridge to the west, which separates the Eurasian and North American plates in the North Atlantic, with Iceland on the ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.8.3] Because Ireland is far from a plate boundary, it has no active volcanoes and only rare, minor earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.9.0] About 500 million years ago, before the Caledonian folding, Ireland was in two parts on opposite sides of the Iapetus Ocean: the north-west part on the North American (Laurentian) side and the south-east part on the European side.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.0] Constructive (also called divergent or separating) boundaries: plates move apart and crust is created, because magma fills the gap.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.b] At constructive boundaries plates move apart, and new crust is formed in the gap. The process runs in three steps, driven by the mantle (the hot layer beneath the crust).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.1.0] 1. Convection currents (slow circulating flows in the hot mantle) carry the plates apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.b] Where the plates separate under the ocean, the new crust builds up a long underwater mountain chain called a mid-ocean ridge. This is the main oceanic example of a constructive boundary.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.0] A rift valley (a long valley formed where the crust splits and drops) runs along the crest of the ridge, with frequent shallow earthquakes (earthquakes starting close to the surface) and volcanic activity.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.2] Where a mid-ocean ridge rises above sea level it forms volcanic islands, such as Iceland.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.3] The Mid-Atlantic Ridge also separates the South American and African plates in the South Atlantic.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.4] Iceland is a volcanic island on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the rift is visible on land at Þingvellir (Thingvellir).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.5] The Azores are also on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the East Pacific Rise is another mid-ocean ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.6] Surtsey, a new volcanic island, formed off the south coast of Iceland in an eruption that began in 1963.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.b] These are the landforms and activity to name when asked what is found at a constructive boundary. These features are found where plates pull apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.b] Where two plates converge, one plate is forced down into the mantle. Which one sinks depends on the density of the crust.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.0] The older, denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the other, because denser crust sinks more easily.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.3] The Indo-Australian plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate, forming the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau; the collision began about 50 million years ago and continues.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.b] At passive (transform) boundaries two plates slide past each other horizontally along a transform fault. Because nothing is pulled apart or pushed under, no crust is made or lost.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.0] Constructive boundaries: plates move apart and crust is created. Examples: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the East African Rift Valley.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.1] The three measures studied here are hydro-electric dams, canalisation and flood control, each with Irish and international examples.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.b] A hydro-electric dam turns the energy of falling water into electricity. It does this by holding the river back and then releasing it in a controlled way.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.0] The dam stores water in a reservoir (an artificial lake behind the dam), and the water is then released so that it falls through turbines (wheels that spin and drive generators).
[geo-rivers-s4.2.b] Upstream of a dam the river meets still water in the reservoir. This changes its processes completely, because a river can only carry material while it keeps moving.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.0] Velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load (the sand, silt and stones it was carrying) there.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.1] The reservoir slowly fills with sediment, a process called sedimentation, which reduces its capacity (how much water it can hold).
[geo-rivers-s4.2.2] A delta (a fan of deposited sediment) can build where the river enters the reservoir, because that is where the flow slows and drops its load.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.b] Below the dam the river has lost its sediment and its natural rhythm of floods and low flows. Both changes alter erosion and deposition.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.0] Water released from the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam. This is called clear-water erosion.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.1] The floodplain (the flat land beside the river) and delta receive no new silt, so deltas and coasts erode because nothing replaces the material lost.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.2] Discharge (the volume of water flowing past a point) is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.3] Less flooding means less deposition on the floodplain, because floodwater is what normally lays down silt there.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.4] Where water is diverted out of the old channel, the bypassed channel carries much less water.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.5] Dams block migrating salmon and eels, which cannot pass the wall. Fish passes (routes around the dam) and trapping and transporting fish around dams are responses.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.b] The Shannon Scheme is one power station at Ardnacrusha, near Limerick, fed by a weir and a canal. It is not a series of dams.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.1] Parteen Weir (a low barrier across the river) diverts most of the Shannon's water into a headrace canal to Ardnacrusha. A tailrace (the channel carrying used water away) returns it to the river near Limerick.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.2] Lough Derg's level is controlled by Parteen Weir, so the lough acts as the scheme's reservoir.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.3] The original channel between Parteen and Limerick, the Old River Shannon, carries much less water because most of it is diverted.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.4] Salmon and eel stocks declined. The ESB built a fish pass, and it traps silver eels (adult eels migrating downstream) upstream and transports them past the dam.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.b] Ardnacrusha is not the only Irish example. Four more dams and power stations can be named in an answer.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.0] Poulaphouca dam, River Liffey: created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, Co. Wicklow. It generates electricity and supplies drinking water to Dublin.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.b] The Aswan High Dam is the main international case study. It holds back the Nile in a huge reservoir to give Egypt control over a river that once flooded and shrank unpredictably.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.0] Aswan High Dam, River Nile, Egypt, was completed in 1970. Its reservoir is Lake Nasser, which extends into Sudan.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.1] Benefits are flood control, year-round irrigation (which allows more than one crop a year), hydro-electricity, and protection from drought.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.b] The dam's effects follow from the same processes as any dam: trapped sediment, clear-water erosion and regulated flow. They then spread into farming, fishing, health and settlement.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.0] Silt is trapped in Lake Nasser, so farmers downstream rely on artificial fertilisers instead of the natural silt that floods once left.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.1] The river bed below the dam is scoured (worn away) by clear water, which has spare energy to erode.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.2] The Nile delta coast is eroding, e.g. at the Rosetta promontory (a headland on the delta coast), because no new sediment arrives to replace what the sea removes.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.3] The sardine fishery off the delta in the Mediterranean declined sharply.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.b] The Three Gorges Dam is a second international example, built on the Yangtze to produce power and to reduce floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.3] It traps sediment that once reached the river's lower course and delta, so those areas receive less of the sediment they depended on.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.b] Canalisation modifies an existing river channel. It is not the same as building a canal.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.1] Purposes are navigation, faster drainage of land, and flood control.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.1] Water and floods are passed downstream faster, so flood peaks downstream are higher and arrive sooner.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.2] Faster flow has more energy, so it erodes the bed and banks.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.1] Arterial drainage effects were changes to fish spawning beds, bank erosion and downstream flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.b] Hard engineering uses built structures to hold floodwater back or move it away. Each one changes river processes, and some pass the problem on downstream.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.0] Embankments and levees (raised banks) confine flow to the channel, so deposition raises the bed and the banks must be raised again.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.1] With embankments and levees the floodplain gets no silt, and failure gives sudden, severe floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.4] Dredging and deepening increase channel capacity, but the flow is faster and downstream flooding can increase.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.0] Floodplain zoning uses planning controls on building, so that homes are kept off land that floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.4] Effect on processes: natural flooding and floodplain deposition continue.

[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.3.3] Uses: granite is used as building stone, kerbs, worktops and headstones; basalt is crushed for road chippings and aggregate (crushed stone used in construction).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.0] Basalt is the commonest volcanic rock and forms from runny lava, which flows easily before it sets.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.5.1] Basalt forms at constructive (divergent) boundaries, where plates move apart, and at hotspots.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.9.4] Chalk lies beneath the basalt along the Antrim coast, so the chalk is the older rock and the lava flowed over it.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.1] Granite outcrops (shows at the surface) in Wicklow, Donegal and the Mournes; basalt covers Co. Antrim; metamorphic rocks lie in Donegal, Connemara and Mayo.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.13.4] Volcanic example: the Antrim plateau was built by lava flows about 60 million years ago, as the Atlantic began to open. The Giant's Causeway, Co. Antrim, is basalt columns.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s1.15.1] Basalt: dark and fine-grained because it cooled quickly; it may form columns, as at the Giant's Causeway.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.2] Regolith is the layer of loose, weathered rock fragments lying on top of solid bedrock (the unbroken rock beneath).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.3] Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals (the chemical substances the rock is made of).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.4] Chemical weathering changes or dissolves the minerals in the rock, so the rock itself is altered, not just broken.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.0.5] Biological weathering by plants and animals works through both physical and chemical means.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.b] Freeze-thaw action, also called frost shattering, is a physical weathering process. It needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, together with water and jointed rock (rock with natural cracks called joints).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.0] 1. Water enters cracks and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.1] 2. At night the water freezes and expands by about 9%, which widens the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.2] 3. By day the ice thaws, and the water seeps deeper into the crack.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.3] 4. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing split off angular (sharp-edged) fragments.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.4] It is most effective on mountains in upland Ireland, on high mountains and in periglacial areas (cold areas where the ground is frozen for much of the year). Freeze-thaw is active on Irish mountain summits such as Carrauntoohil, Co. Kerry.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.1.5] Result: scree (talus) slopes of angular fragments at the foot of mountain cliffs, and jagged peaks and blockfields (areas covered in broken angular rock).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.b] In hot deserts the rock surface swings between hot days and cold nights. Both processes here are physical weathering driven by that heating and cooling, called insolation (heating by the sun).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.0] Exfoliation, or onion weathering, is an insolation process in hot deserts such as the Sahara, where the daily temperature range (the gap between day and night temperature) is large.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.1] The outer layer of rock heats and expands by day, and cools and contracts at night more than the interior, because rock conducts heat poorly.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.2] Stress builds up, and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion, leaving rounded boulders.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.3] Many geographers stress that some moisture, such as dew, must be present for exfoliation to work effectively.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.2.4] Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.b] Rock can also break because the forces on it change, or because growing crystals push it apart. Neither process changes the minerals, so both are physical.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.1] The sheets peel off to leave domes, such as Half Dome, Yosemite, California, USA, a granite dome shaped by sheeting.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.3.2] Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores (tiny holes) and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.b] Plants and animals break rock down in two ways. Their physical action is a form of physical weathering, and the acids they produce cause chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.4.0] Physical: plant roots grow into cracks and widen them, and burrowing animals expose fresh rock to weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.5.3] Carbonation is the process behind karst (distinctive limestone scenery); it operates on the Carboniferous limestone of the Burren, Co. Clare.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.b] Four more chemical processes each work on different minerals. Each changes or dissolves the mineral, so each is chemical weathering.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.0] Hydrolysis: water reacts with feldspar (a mineral in granite) and turns it into clay (kaolin, or china clay); the quartz grains (another mineral in granite) are left as sand, and the granite crumbles.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.6.4] Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.0] Climate: temperature and rainfall decide which type of weathering dominates.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.2] Cold climates with temperatures around freezing favour freeze-thaw, and hot deserts favour exfoliation and salt crystallisation.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.7.3] Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.0] Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults (breaks in the rock where blocks have moved) let water in, so heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.8.4] Aspect (the direction a slope faces): shady slopes stay frozen longer, so freeze-thaw is stronger on them.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.b] Temperature links the processes above. It matters in two different ways, and the type of weathering that results depends on the temperature pattern of the place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.0] Temperature acts in two ways: as a trigger, through the expansion and contraction that cause stress, and as a control on the rate of chemical reactions.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.1] Freeze-thaw depends on temperature crossing the freezing point, and exfoliation and granular disintegration depend on a large daily range; both need changes in temperature, not just warmth.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.2] Warmth speeds the chemical reactions of carbonation and hydrolysis, so the same temperature can favour chemical weathering in one place and freeze-thaw in another.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s3.9.3] Method: name the process and its type, state the temperature condition it needs, explain the stress or rate change it produces, and link this to the result, such as scree from freeze-thaw.
