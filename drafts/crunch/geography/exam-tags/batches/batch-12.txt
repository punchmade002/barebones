PART "3A(ii)" (4 marks)

PART "3A(iii)" (4 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Lithification: the process that turns loose sediment into solid sedimentary rock.
It happens through compaction, where the weight of overlying layers presses the grains together, and cementation, where minerals carried in water bind the grains.

PART "3A(iv)" (4 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Plutonic rock: an igneous rock formed when magma cools slowly deep underground.
Because it cools slowly, it has large crystals, for example granite.

PART "3A(v)" (4 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Thermal metamorphism: rock near a magma intrusion is heated by the magma.
The heat recrystallises the minerals without melting the rock, so limestone changes into harder marble.

PART "3B(i)" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option (i) – Waterfall and gorge:
A waterfall is a fluvial landform of the upper course, formed by river erosion where the river crosses bands of rock of differing resistance.
Process of erosion – Hydraulic action:
Hydraulic action is the force of moving water that dislodges loose material and forces air into cracks in the bed, widening them until rock breaks away.
Process of erosion – Abrasion:
Abrasion occurs when the river's load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks.
Vertical erosion dominates in the upper course because the steep gradient gives the river high energy.
Formation of the waterfall:
A waterfall forms where a band of resistant rock lies over less resistant rock.
Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft rock below faster than the hard cap, so the hard rock is undercut.
This leaves an overhang of hard rock with nothing to support it.
Falling water and its load swirl at the base and scour a deep plunge pool by hydraulic action and abrasion.
Pebbles swirled in the plunge pool drill the bed deeper by abrasion.
The unsupported overhang eventually collapses into the plunge pool, and the waterfall retreats upstream.
The fallen blocks are ground down by abrasion and attrition, which supplies more tools for erosion at the base.
Gorge and diagram:
Repeated retreat of the waterfall leaves a steep-sided gorge downstream of the falls.
A labelled cross-section shows the resistant caprock, the softer rock beneath, the overhang, the plunge pool and the gorge.
Named examples:
Powerscourt Waterfall on the River Dargle, Co. Wicklow, is Ireland's highest waterfall at about 121 m.
Torc Waterfall near Killarney, Co. Kerry, is a second Irish example.
Niagara Falls has a resistant cap of dolomite over weaker shale, and the retreat of the falls has cut the Niagara Gorge below them.

PART "3B(ii)" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Mass movement process – Slump (option ii):
Slump is a fast rotational mass movement in which saturated material slides downslope along a curved slip plane.
Mass movement is the downslope movement of weathered material under gravity without a transporting agent such as a river, glacier or wind.
Slump occurs when the downslope force of gravity becomes greater than the friction and cohesion holding the material in place.
Factor 1 – Water content and rainfall:
Heavy or prolonged rainfall soaks into the slope and fills the pore spaces of the soil and rock.
Saturation adds weight to the slope and so increases the downslope pull of gravity.
Water acts as a lubricant, which reduces friction and cohesion and allows the material to slide.
Clay becomes slippery and plastic when it is wet, so a saturated clay layer forms the curved slip plane along which the slump moves.
Factor 2 – Geology and rock type:
Slumps are common where permeable rock lies over impermeable clay or shale.
Water passes down through the permeable rock but cannot drain through the clay, so water concentrates at the junction between the two layers.
The wet clay below is weak and gives way, so the rock above rotates backward on a concave slip plane.
Factor 3 – Undercutting and gradient:
Erosion by the sea or a river at the base of a slope removes support from the material above.
A steep gradient increases the downslope force of gravity, so slopes that have been steepened by undercutting are most likely to slump.
Additional loading at the top of a slope, from rock, spoil or buildings, adds further weight and increases the chance of failure.
Example – Antrim coast between Larne and Cushendall:
On the Antrim coast hard jointed basalt lies on chalk, which lies on soft Lias clay.
Rainwater soaks through the basalt and chalk and saturates the clay, which becomes slippery, so the rock above slumps and rotates seaward, as at Garron Point.
These slides have repeatedly blocked the Antrim Coast Road, and engineers have built retaining walls and drainage and fitted rock-catch fences to protect it.
Diagram points in words:
The slump leaves a crescent-shaped scar, called a scarp, at the top of the slope.
The slumped block is tilted backward on the curved slip plane, and the material bulges out as a toe at the foot of the slope.
A slump moves on a curved plane, whereas a landslide moves on a flat plane.

PART "3C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Occurrence of seismic activity:
Earthquakes occur when plates lock together by friction and stress builds up in the rocks around the boundary.
When the stress exceeds the strength of the rock, it slips suddenly along a fault and the stored energy is released as seismic waves.
Prediction – Monitoring and history:
Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely, because their exact time, place and magnitude are unknown, so scientists identify where they are likely and how often they occur.
Mapping active faults and plate boundaries shows which areas, such as the Pacific Ring of Fire, are at greatest risk.
Studying the history and frequency of past earthquakes helps estimate how often a place is struck.
Seismic gap theory says that a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time holds the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next.
Scientists monitor foreshocks, ground deformation using GPS, tiltmeters and lasers, radon gas release and changes in well-water levels.
In Haicheng, China, in 1975 foreshocks led to the city being evacuated hours before a magnitude 7.3 earthquake, which saved many lives.
In Tangshan, China, in 1976 no warning was given, and the official death toll was about 240,000.
Prediction – Early warning:
Early-warning systems detect the fast P waves, giving seconds of warning before the destructive surface waves arrive.
Those seconds are enough to stop trains, shut off gas supplies and take cover, and Japan has a national early-warning system.
Reducing effects – Buildings:
Earthquake-resistant buildings have deep foundations anchored to bedrock.
Base isolation places rubber and steel bearings under a building so that the ground moves beneath it without shaking the structure.
Flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames with cross-bracing sway rather than crack, and tall buildings such as Taipei 101 use a tuned mass damper.
Automatic shut-off valves on gas mains reduce fires, which destroyed much of San Francisco after the earthquake of 1906.
Reducing effects – Planning and preparation:
Strict building codes and land-use planning avoid building on fault lines and soft ground, where liquefaction badly damaged Christchurch in 2011.
Emergency drills, education and emergency supplies prepare people, and Japan holds national Disaster Prevention Day drills each 1 September.
After the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami killed about 230,000 people, a tsunami warning system was set up there, operational from 2006.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.0] 1. Plates (the huge slabs of crust) moving past, towards or away from each other lock together by friction, because their rough edges grip and cannot slide.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.1] 2. Stress builds up in the rocks around the locked boundary, because the plates keep moving while the edges stay stuck.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.2] 3. When the stress exceeds the strength of the rock, the rock fractures or slips suddenly along a fault (a crack in the crust along which rock can move).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.0.3] 4. The stored energy is released as seismic waves, which are vibrations that travel through the Earth and shake the ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.3] Seismic (shock) waves: waves of energy spreading out from the focus in all directions.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.4] Diagram: draw a cross-section showing the fault, the focus below ground, seismic waves spreading out, and the epicentre on the surface directly above the focus.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.1.5] Depth matters: a shallow-focus earthquake causes more surface damage than a deep-focus one of the same magnitude (the energy released), because the waves have less rock to travel through before reaching the surface.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.0] P (primary) waves are the fastest seismic waves, so they arrive first.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.1] S (secondary) waves are slower than P waves and arrive second.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.2.2] Surface waves travel along the surface, arrive last and cause most of the damage, because they shake the ground and the buildings on it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.b] Scientists can record seismic waves with an instrument and use the records from several places to pinpoint where an earthquake struck.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.3.0] A seismograph (seismometer) is the instrument that detects and records seismic waves; the record it produces is a seismogram.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.b] Earthquakes are measured in two different ways: how much energy they release, and how much damage they do at a particular place. Two different scales do these jobs.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.4.3] The modified Mercalli scale measures intensity, the observed effects and damage at a place, on a scale of I to XII.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.5.b] Most earthquakes occur in narrow belts along plate boundaries, the lines where plates meet, because that is where plates lock and then slip. Each boundary type produces a different kind of earthquake.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.5.0] The Pacific Ring of Fire, the horseshoe of plate boundaries around the Pacific Ocean, has about 90% of the world's earthquakes, including most of the most powerful ones.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.9.0] These earthquakes are shallow and often very damaging.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.b] Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely: their exact time, place and magnitude are not known. Scientists identify where they are likely and estimate how often they occur.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.0] Mapping active faults and plate boundaries, to show where earthquakes are likely.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.1] Studying the history and frequency of past earthquakes, to estimate how often they occur.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.2] Seismic gap theory: a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time has the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.17.3] Monitoring foreshocks, ground deformation (GPS, tiltmeters and laser measurements across faults), radon gas release and changes in well-water levels. Tiltmeters measure small changes in the slope of the ground.

[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.b] Prediction cannot give a time, but warning systems can give a short notice once an earthquake has started or a tsunami has been triggered.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.0] Early-warning systems detect the fast P waves, giving seconds of warning before the destructive waves arrive, because P waves outrun the surface waves.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.1] Those seconds are enough to stop trains, shut off gas supplies and take cover.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.2] Japan has a national earthquake early-warning system.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.19.3] Tsunami warning systems use seabed pressure sensors and buoys, so that a tsunami can be detected at sea, as in the Indian Ocean system set up after 2004.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.b] Buildings can be designed to survive shaking, which limits deaths and damage. Each measure either stops a building from being shaken or lets it move without breaking.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.0] Deep foundations anchored to bedrock (solid rock below the soil), so the building does not sit on soft ground.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.1] Base isolation: rubber and steel bearings or rollers under the building, so the ground moves beneath it.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.2] Flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames that sway rather than crack, with cross-bracing and shear walls (extra supports that stiffen the structure).
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.3] Counterweights (tuned mass dampers) in tall buildings, which swing against the shaking. Taipei 101, Taiwan, has a giant tuned mass damper, a steel ball hung near the top.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.4] Automatic shut-off valves on gas mains, and flexible joints on pipes, so pipes bend rather than break and gas does not leak.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.20.5] Retrofitting older buildings, which means strengthening them to modern standards.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.0] Strict building codes, so that new buildings can survive shaking, and land-use planning that avoids soft ground (which shakes more and can liquefy) and fault lines.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.1] Emergency drills, education and emergency supplies.
[geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2.21.3] Japan holds national Disaster Prevention Day drills each 1 September, the anniversary of the 1923 Great Kanto earthquake.
