QUESTION geography-pp-2021-higher-q123 (LC Geography Higher 2021 — Part Two — Q23, 80 marks)

PART "23" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The exchange of water between the oceans and the atmosphere supplies the moisture for precipitation, and the way that moisture cools, condenses and is carried by winds and pressure belts produces the world pattern of rainfall.
Aspect 1 – Evaporation from the oceans:
The hydrological cycle is a closed system in which the total amount of water stays the same, driven by solar energy and gravity.
Oceans hold about 97% of the earth's water.
Roughly 85 to 90% of global evaporation comes from the oceans, and roughly three-quarters of global precipitation falls on them.
The surplus evaporated over the oceans is carried over the land by wind, a process called advection, and returns to the sea through rivers and groundwater.
Higher temperature, strong sunshine, dry air, wind and a large water supply all increase evaporation.
Evaporation is greatest over the subtropical oceans under clear skies and steady trade winds and along warm currents such as the Gulf Stream, and it is least over cold polar seas.
Evaporation takes latent heat from the surface, and this energy is released again when the vapour condenses.
Water stays in the atmosphere for only about 9 days on average, so atmospheric water is recycled quickly while ocean water changes slowly.
Evaporation exceeds precipitation over the subtropical oceans, which explains their high salinity, while precipitation exceeds evaporation in the equatorial and mid-latitude westerly belts.
Aspect 2 – Condensation and types of rainfall:
Condensation is water vapour turning into liquid droplets when air cools to its dew point, and it needs condensation nuclei such as dust, sea salt or smoke.
The main cause of cloud is adiabatic cooling, as rising air expands and cools without losing heat to its surroundings.
Unsaturated air cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate of about 10 °C per 1,000 m, and once condensation begins the latent heat released slows the cooling to about 5 to 7 °C per 1,000 m.
Convectional rainfall occurs when strong heating makes warm air rise, cool and form cumulonimbus clouds, giving heavy afternoon showers as in the equatorial belt every day.
Relief rainfall occurs when moist sea air is forced to rise over mountains, giving heavy rain on the windward slope and a rain shadow on the leeward side.
Ireland's west coast mountains receive relief rain, and in India the Western Ghats force the monsoon to rise, giving heavy rain on the Malabar coast with the Deccan in rain shadow.
Frontal rainfall occurs when warm moist air rises over cold air in a depression, giving long steady rain at the warm front and short heavy showers at the cold front, and it is Ireland's main source of rain.
Convergence rainfall occurs where air streams meet and are forced to rise, as where the trade winds converge at the ITCZ.
Raindrops grow by the Bergeron–Findeisen ice-crystal process in cold clouds like Ireland's, and by collision and coalescence in warm tropical clouds.
Aspect 3 – Global distribution of precipitation:
The equatorial belt receives over 2,000 mm a year from low pressure, rising air, convergence at the ITCZ and high evaporation, and Manaus in Brazil receives about 2,300 mm.
Savanna and monsoon lands between about 10 and 20 degrees have rain in summer when the ITCZ moves over them and a dry winter.
The subtropical high-pressure belts at about 20 to 30 degrees have very low rainfall because of sinking, warming, stable air, giving the great hot deserts such as the Atacama, the driest non-polar desert on earth.
The Namib receives much of its moisture as fog from the cold Benguela Current.
Mid-latitudes at about 40 to 60 degrees have moderate to heavy rain from depressions in the westerlies, and it is wettest on west coasts facing onshore winds.
Polar regions have very low precipitation because cold air holds little vapour and air sinks under the polar high pressure, so Antarctica is a cold desert.
Continental interiors are dry because they lie far from the moisture of the oceans.
Warm currents add moisture and instability, while cold currents chill the air and make it stable, so rain is rare on coasts such as those of the Atacama and Namib.
In Ireland the west is wetter than the east, with about 750 mm around Dublin, 1,000 to 1,250 mm across much of the west and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
The pressure belts move north and south with the overhead sun, so the rain belts shift seasonally and give regimes such as the monsoon with a wet summer and the Mediterranean with a wet winter.

PART "24" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: The Mediterranean climate, found around the Mediterranean Basin, has hot dry summers and mild wet winters, and its characteristics are described and explained below.
Aspect 1 – Location and temperature:
The Mediterranean climate lies on the west sides of continents, in the Mediterranean Basin, central and southern California, central Chile, the Cape Town area of South Africa and south-western and southern Australia.
Summers are hot, with lowland July means of about 25 °C or more, for example in southern Italy.
Winters are mild, with coastal January means of about 9 to 12 °C, and frost is rare on the coasts.
Inland and upland areas have hotter summers and colder winters than the coast.
In summer the sun is high in the sky, overhead near the Tropic of Cancer at the June solstice, so insolation is high.
Clear skies in summer, caused by sinking air, let in strong sunshine and raise temperatures.
The sea heats and cools slowly, so it moderates winters on the coast and gives a smaller annual range than in continental interiors.
On some of these coasts cold currents such as the California, Humboldt, Benguela and Canary currents keep summers cooler and drier.
The sirocco is a hot, dry, dusty wind from the Sahara that raises temperatures.
The mistral and the bora are cold winds that affect the northern Mediterranean.
Aspect 2 – Precipitation and pressure:
Rainfall is moderate, under 600 mm a year on parts of the Puglia lowlands and Sicily.
Most rain falls from October to March, and summers are dry with drought for several months.
In summer the subtropical high pressure belt moves poleward over the region, bringing sinking, warming air, clear skies and no rain.
Sinking air is compressed and warms, so clouds evaporate and rain is unlikely.
In winter the pressure belts shift towards the equator, so the westerlies and their depressions bring rain.
The warm Mediterranean Sea also spawns its own winter depressions.
Rain often falls in intense downpours that cause flash floods and soil erosion.
Cold currents chill the air and make it stable, so summer rain is rare on coasts such as California's.
The seasonal shift of the pressure belts with the overhead sun explains why the wet season is winter, the opposite of the monsoon with its wet summer.
Aspect 3 – Vegetation, hazards and human response:
Vegetation is drought-tolerant, with olives, vines, cork oak and maquis scrub.
Citrus fruits grow where they are irrigated.
Deforestation since ancient times for shipbuilding, fuel, farming and goat grazing degraded evergreen oak and pine forest to maquis and garrigue, a low open scrub on thin soils.
Summer water shortage is the main hazard of the climate.
Water storage and irrigation are the human response to the summer drought.
Wildfires occur in hot, dry, windy summers.
The Mati wildfire near Athens in July 2018 killed about 100 people, one of the deadliest wildfires in Europe.
Intense winter downpours on cleared slopes cause soil erosion.
Tourism is a major human response to the sunny, dry summers.

PART "1B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Influence 1 – Folding by compression:
Tectonic activity has shaped the Irish landscape through folding, volcanic extrusion and igneous intrusion, all caused by plate movement.
Compression where plates converged squeezed layers of rock into folds, forming anticlines and synclines.
Caledonian folding produced the north-east to south-west trend of the mountains of Donegal, Mayo and Wicklow.
Armorican folding produced the east to west ridges and valleys of Munster, with the Armorican front marking the northern limit of strong folding.
Resistant Old Red Sandstone survives on the anticlines as ridges such as Macgillycuddy's Reeks, the Galtee Mountains and the Knockmealdown Mountains.
Weaker Carboniferous limestone lies in the synclines as valleys such as those of the Blackwater and the Lee, and the Blackwater turns sharply south at Cappoquin where it cuts across a ridge.
The coastal ends of the valleys in Cork and Kerry were drowned to form rias, including Bantry Bay, Dingle Bay and the Kenmare River.
Influence 2 – Volcanic activity (Antrim plateau):
About 60 million years ago, linked to the opening of the North Atlantic, basic lava poured out of fissures in the crust in Co. Antrim.
Repeated flows built up the Antrim plateau, a high plateau of basalt.
Along the Antrim coast the dark basalt lies on top of white chalk, which it protected from erosion.
The Giant's Causeway formed when a thick lava flow cooled and contracted, creating about 40,000 mainly six-sided basalt columns that sea erosion has exposed.
Slemish, Co. Antrim, is a volcanic plug of resistant magma left standing as a steep-sided hill after the softer cone was eroded.
Influence 3 – Plutonic intrusion:
The Leinster batholith is the largest granite mass in Ireland, formed when magma cooled slowly underground about 400 million years ago during the Caledonian period.
Erosion removed the overlying slate and exposed the granite as the rounded Wicklow Mountains, with Lugnaquilla at 925 m, surrounded by a band of mica schist that was altered by the heat.
The Mourne Mountains in Co. Down are granite intrusions from the same period as the Antrim basalts, and Slieve Donard is their highest peak.
Granite is resistant, so these intrusions stand out as uplands once the weaker rock around them has been eroded.

PART "1C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath:
Tara Mines is an underground zinc and lead mine at Navan, Co. Meath, and it is Europe's largest zinc mine.
The mine has been in production since 1977 and is owned by the Swedish company Boliden.
The ore lies in Carboniferous limestone, where minerals were concentrated by hot fluids moving through the rock.
Interaction 1 – Extraction of rock from the crust:
Humans interact with the rock cycle by removing metal ores from the crust far faster than natural weathering and erosion could.
People therefore act as an exogenic agent, with extraction as the human equivalent of erosion.
Exploration begins with geological surveys and test drilling before a mine is developed.
Underground mining reaches the deep ore through shafts and tunnels, as at Tara.
The ore is crushed and ground, and the zinc and lead are separated from the waste rock.
Extraction exposes fresh rock to weathering and can cause subsidence above underground workings.
Interaction 2 – Waste and deposition:
Waste rock is stored in spoil heaps and fine waste called tailings is stored in tailings ponds, which is the human equivalent of deposition.
These deposits take up land and must be lined and capped to stop seepage into groundwater.
At Avoca, Co. Wicklow, centuries of copper mining left acid mine drainage that discolours the Avoca River and pollutes it with heavy metals.
At Aberfan in south Wales in 1966 a colliery spoil tip collapsed in a flow slide after heavy rain, killing 144 people.
Impacts and management:
Mining brings jobs, exports and local spending, but it also brings dust, noise and heavy traffic for people living nearby.
Environmental licensing and monitoring control waste handling and the quality of water leaving the site, and after Lisheen mine in Co. Tipperary closed in 2015 the site was rehabilitated.
