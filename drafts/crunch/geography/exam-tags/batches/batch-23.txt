PART "3B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option – Extracting building materials:
Humans interact with the rock cycle by quarrying rock close to the surface for cement, aggregate and building stone.
Quarrying removes rock far faster than natural weathering and erosion, so people act as an exogenic agent.
Named example – Irish Cement quarry at Platin:
Irish Cement quarries limestone at Platin near Drogheda, Co. Meath, to make cement.
The rock is Carboniferous limestone, a sedimentary rock formed from the shells and skeletons of marine organisms.
Process of extraction:
First the overburden of soil and glacial till above the rock is removed.
Benches are then cut into the rock face, and the rock is drilled and blasted.
The rock is crushed and screened to grade it by size.
Links to the rock cycle:
The crushed limestone is mixed with shale and heated in a kiln to make clinker, which is ground with gypsum to make cement.
Heating limestone releases carbon dioxide, which returns to the atmosphere carbon that had been locked in the rock for hundreds of millions of years.
Cement is mixed with sand and gravel from glacial deposits to make concrete, a new human-made rock used in roads and buildings.
Quarrying exposes fresh rock to weathering, and spoil and waste are stored on the surface in the human equivalent of deposition.
Impacts:
Quarries leave permanent scars in the landscape and cause loss of farmland and habitat.
Dust, noise, blasting vibration and heavy lorries on rural roads affect people living nearby.
Quarrying can lower the local water table.
Positive impacts are jobs and the materials needed for housing and roads.
Management:
Planning permission comes with conditions on working hours and limits on dust and noise.
Earth banks and trees screen the quarry from view, and worked-out quarries are rehabilitated as lakes, wildlife habitats or amenity areas.

It also shows a spoil heap beside the quarry.

PART "3C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Destructive plate boundary – Convergence and subduction:
A destructive boundary occurs where two plates move towards each other and crust is destroyed.
Convection currents in the mantle drive the plates together.
Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so where the two meet the oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle.
This process is subduction, and the zone where the plate sinks is the subduction zone.
Crust is destroyed as the sinking plate melts in the hot mantle.
Type 1 – Oceanic plate meeting continental plate:
An ocean trench forms where the oceanic plate bends down.
Sediments are scraped off the sinking plate and crumpled against the continent into fold mountains.
Melting of the sinking plate produces magma that rises to form a chain of explosive volcanoes on the continent.
Earthquakes occur along the sinking plate in the Benioff zone, shallow near the trench and deeper inland.
Named example – Nazca plate and South America:
The Nazca plate subducts beneath the South American plate, forming the Peru-Chile Trench offshore and the volcanic Andes on land.
Named example – Juan de Fuca plate:
The Juan de Fuca plate subducts beneath the North American plate, producing the Cascade Range volcanoes including Mount St Helens, which erupted on 18 May 1980 and killed 57 people.
Type 2 – Two oceanic plates:
The older, denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the other, forming an ocean trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands called an island arc.
The Mariana Trench, about 11 km deep, lies where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the Philippine plate, and the Mariana Islands form an island arc.
The Aleutian Islands of Alaska are an island arc formed where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the North American plate.
Earthquakes are frequent and can cause tsunamis, as in Japan on 11 March 2011 when a magnitude 9.0 earthquake caused a tsunami that killed about 18,000 people.
Type 3 – Two continental plates colliding:
Both plates are too light to subduct, so the crust and the ocean sediments between them are crushed, folded and pushed up into high fold mountains.
Earthquakes are common but there is little or no volcanic activity.
The Indo-Australian plate is colliding with the Eurasian plate to form the Himalayas, including Mount Everest at 8,849 m.
The Nepal earthquake of 25 April 2015 had a magnitude of 7.8 and killed about 9,000 people on this collision boundary.

PART "4B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Aspect of culture – Language:
Culture is the shared way of life of a group of people, including their language, religion, customs, music and sport.
Language is the cultural trait used to define a region because it can be mapped from census data and marks one group off from its neighbours.
Where one language is common to the people of a defined area, that area can be treated as a cultural region.
Cultural regions rarely have sharp edges because the shared trait thins out gradually across a transition zone.
Named example – the Gaeltacht:
The Gaeltacht is the officially recognised area where Irish is, or was, the community language.
It is not one continuous region but a set of scattered pockets in seven counties, namely Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, Cork, Waterford and Meath.
The largest area is Conamara and the Aran Islands in County Galway, and other main areas are Gaoth Dobhair in Donegal and Corca Dhuibhne in Kerry.
Irish survived mainly along the western seaboard because remote, mountainous areas with poor farmland and poor communications had least contact with English-speaking towns and trade.
The two Meath Gaeltachtaí at Ráth Chairn and Baile Ghib were created in the 1930s when the State resettled Irish-speaking families from Connemara.
The State supports the Gaeltacht through Údarás na Gaeltachta, set up in 1980 to promote jobs and the language there.
Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972 and TG4 in 1996, so Irish is heard in the media.
Irish-medium schools and summer colleges bring learners into the Gaeltacht and help to sustain the language.
Threats to the Gaeltacht include out-migration of young people, in-migration of English speakers, holiday homes and English-language media.
Second example – Belgium:
Belgium is divided by a language border running east to west, with Dutch-speaking Flanders in the north and French-speaking Wallonia in the south.
The Brussels-Capital Region is officially bilingual, and a small German-speaking community lives in the east around Eupen.

PART "4C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Region 1 – Paris Basin, France:
The Paris Basin has a cool temperate oceanic climate that becomes more continental towards the east, with rainfall of about 600 to 650 mm spread fairly evenly through the year.
Even rainfall means crops rarely suffer drought, so cereal yields are reliable and high.
Warm summers, with July averaging about 20°C, ripen wheat, barley and grapes.
Dry harvest weather lets crops be gathered in good condition, which protects quality and income.
In the Beauce, known as the granary of France, the climate and deep fertile limon soils support wheat, barley and maize on farms often over 100 hectares.
Champagne lies near the northern limit of commercial vine growing, so its warm summers are essential for the grapes grown on the chalk slopes around Reims and Épernay.
Wetter conditions in the west, in Normandy, favour grass and dairy farming, which produces Camembert cheese.
Sugar beet and oilseed rape are also grown on the plateaux because the long growing season gives crops time to develop.
This favourable climate is why the Paris Basin is the main arable region of France, and France is the largest wheat producer in the EU.
Region 2 – Mezzogiorno, southern Italy:
The Mezzogiorno has a Mediterranean climate with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters.
Summers are hot and dry because the subtropical high-pressure belt moves north over the region, and lowland July temperatures average about 25°C or more.
Most rain falls between October and March, so farmers depend on winter rain for crops such as durum wheat.
Summer drought causes water shortage, so irrigation is needed, for example for the market gardening of San Marzano tomatoes on the Campanian plain.
Annual rainfall is under 600 mm on parts of the Puglia lowlands and Sicily, which suits drought-tolerant tree crops, and Puglia is Italy's leading olive-oil region.
Durum wheat for pasta is grown on the Tavoliere di Puglia around Foggia, where the dry summer ripens the grain.
The hot summers suit citrus fruit, as in the lemons around Sorrento and the blood oranges of the Catania plain, though these crops need irrigation.
