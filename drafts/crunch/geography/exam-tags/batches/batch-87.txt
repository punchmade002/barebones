PART "24" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Climate characteristics such as temperature, rainfall and its reliability influence economic development through agriculture, drought and water supply, and tourism.

Aspect 1 – Agriculture:
Temperature and rainfall decide what farming is possible and how productive it is.
Ireland's mild temperatures and rainfall give a long grass-growing season, because grass grows when the soil is above about 6 °C, so most farmland is grassland for dairy, beef and sheep.
Rainfall is about 750 mm in the driest east and over 2,000 mm on western mountains, so tillage crops such as barley and wheat are concentrated in the drier south and east, for example Co. Wexford.
Too much rain waterlogs soils, which are poached by cattle, shortens the grazing season and forces farmers to house animals longer and feed silage, which raises costs.
The exceptionally wet summer of 2012 and a cold, late spring in 2013 caused a fodder crisis, and fodder was imported with a Government transport subsidy.
Too little rain also harms farming, because grass almost stopped growing in the long anticyclonic spell of summer 2018 and fodder had to be imported.
In India the south-west monsoon reaches Kerala around 1 June and brings about three-quarters of the annual rainfall, so a late or weak monsoon cuts harvests, farm incomes and GDP.
Rich economies overcome climate limits with technology, as in California's Central Valley, where dry summers mean that fruit and nuts depend on irrigation.
Poor rain-fed economies are the most exposed, as in the Sahel, which has one short wet season, so any shortfall in rain means crop failure and loss of livestock.
Climate change lengthens the growing season in Ireland, but wetter winters and more frequent summer droughts add risk.

Aspect 2 – Drought and water supply:
Drought and unreliable water supply damage economies, especially poorer ones.
Drought is a long period with much less precipitation than normal, so that water supply falls short of demand, and it is caused by blocking anticyclones, failure of the ITCZ or monsoon, El Niño and climate change.
Economic effects include crop failure, livestock losses, higher food prices and loss of farm income and rural jobs.
Drought also cuts hydroelectricity and cooling water for power stations and disrupts river transport.
Drought across the Horn of Africa in 2011 followed two failed rainy seasons linked to La Niña.
Crops failed, livestock died and food prices soared, and the UN declared famine in parts of southern Somalia in July 2011.
A later UN-backed study estimated about 260,000 deaths in Somalia, and large numbers fled to the Dadaab camps in Kenya.
A developed economy copes better, as in the Millennium Drought in south-eastern Australia, which lasted from the late 1990s to 2009 and hit the Murray–Darling Basin, but losses were absorbed through insurance, government aid, water trading and technology.
Australian cities, including Melbourne, built large desalination plants in response.
Poorer countries use early warning systems, dams and tanks, drought-tolerant crops and drip irrigation, and rich countries with harsh climates, such as Israel, the Gulf states and Australia, overcome limits through capital and technology, so climate influences development without deciding it alone.

Aspect 3 – Tourism:
Climate attracts or deters tourists and so shapes the income and jobs that tourism brings.
Sunshine, warmth, low rainfall and a warm sea attract sun, sea and sand tourism, and climate decides the length of the tourist season.
Hot, dry, sunny summers have made the Mediterranean one of the world's most visited regions, and mass package tourism grew from the 1960s with cheap jet flights.
Spain received about 85 million international tourists in 2023, a record, making it the world's second most visited country after France.
Tourism brings jobs, foreign earnings and infrastructure to such regions.
There are climate problems, because tourists arrive in the driest season, so water demand from hotels, pools and golf courses peaks when supply is lowest, and jobs are seasonal.
The Canary Islands, at about 28° N, have mild, dry winters and the cooling Canary Current, which give them a year-round tourist season.
Alpine ski tourism depends on reliable snow, and climate change is raising the snow line and shortening seasons, so lower resorts struggle or close and others invest in snow-making.
Ireland's cool, cloudy, wet summers limit beach tourism, so it markets scenery, golf, walking and surfing, for example through the Wild Atlantic Way of about 2,500 km, launched in 2014.
In July 2023 wildfires on the Greek island of Rhodes forced thousands of tourists to be evacuated, which shows how hotter summers can harm Mediterranean tourism.

PART "1B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Chosen option – flood control measures:
Flood control measures such as levees, dams and relief channels change how the river processes of erosion, transport and deposition operate.
Impact 1 – Levees and embankments:
Levees confine the river to its channel, so floodwater can no longer spread over the floodplain.
Deposition of silt on the floodplain stops, so the floodplain receives no new fertile alluvium.
The river deposits its load inside the confined channel, so the bed rises and the banks must be raised again.
If a levee fails, the flood is sudden and severe.
Impact 2 – Dams and reservoirs:
Dams store floodwater and regulate discharge, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
In the reservoir velocity falls to near zero, so the river deposits its load and the reservoir slowly fills with sediment.
Water released below the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam.
Cutting off the sediment supply starves the delta and coast of silt, which can cause coastal erosion.
Impact 3 – Dredging and relief channels:
Dredging and deepening increase channel capacity, but the faster flow can increase erosion and flooding downstream.
Relief channels take floodwater around a town, so the main channel carries less water and erodes and transports less.
Example – Mississippi levees:
The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 led to the US federal levee and floodway system run by the US Army Corps of Engineers.
Levees stop flood silt reaching the delta, which contributes to the loss of Louisiana's coastal wetlands.
In August 2005 Hurricane Katrina caused levee and floodwall failures in New Orleans, and about 80% of the city flooded.
Example – River Lee at Cork:
In November 2009 the ESB released large volumes of water from Inniscarra dam on the River Lee because the reservoir was full, adding to the flooding of Cork city.
Soft engineering and natural processes:
Soft measures such as floodplain zoning, afforestation and washlands work with natural processes, so natural flooding and floodplain deposition continue.
The Netherlands' Room for the River scheme moves dykes back and digs side channels so the river can spread out safely.

PART "1C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact identified – earthquakes:
Earthquakes are the main impact of destructive boundaries, as the sinking plate locks by friction and the stress is released suddenly.
Named example – Nazca and South American plates:
The Nazca plate subducts beneath the South American plate along the Peru-Chile Trench.
Impact – Landforms:
Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so the oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle in a process called subduction.
An ocean trench forms offshore where the oceanic plate bends down, such as the Peru-Chile Trench.
Sediments scraped off the sinking plate are crumpled against the continent into fold mountains, which form the Andes.
Melting of the sinking plate in the hot mantle produces magma that rises to form a chain of volcanoes in the Andes.
Impact – Earthquakes:
Earthquakes occur along the sinking plate, shallow near the trench and deeper inland, in the inclined Benioff zone.
Destructive boundaries produce the most powerful earthquakes, with foci ranging from shallow to very deep.
The Pacific Ring of Fire contains about 90% of the world's earthquakes and about 75% of its active volcanoes.
Impact – Tsunamis:
Undersea earthquakes at subduction zones thrust up the seabed and displace the ocean, causing tsunamis.
The magnitude 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra on 26 December 2004, where the Indo-Australian plate subducts, killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries.
The Tōhoku earthquake of 11 March 2011 in Japan was magnitude 9.0, and its tsunami killed about 18,000 people dead and missing and caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster.
Impact – Volcanic eruptions:
Volcanoes above the subducting Juan de Fuca plate form the Cascade Range, including Mount St Helens in Washington, USA.
Mount St Helens erupted on 18 May 1980 and killed 57 people.
Impact – Island arcs:
Where two oceanic plates converge, the older plate subducts and a curved chain of volcanic islands called an island arc forms, such as the Mariana Islands.
The Mariana Trench, about 11 km deep, is the deepest place on earth.
Impact – Continental collision:
Where two continental plates collide, the crust is crushed and folded into fold mountains with powerful earthquakes, as in the Himalayas.
The Nepal earthquake of 25 April 2015 was magnitude 7.8 and killed about 9,000 people.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.0.1] Destructive (also called convergent or colliding) boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, because one plate sinks into the mantle, or modified where two continental plates collide.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.3] The Mid-Atlantic Ridge also separates the South American and African plates in the South Atlantic.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.2.4] Iceland is a volcanic island on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the rift is visible on land at Þingvellir (Thingvellir).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.b] These are the landforms and activity to name when asked what is found at a constructive boundary. These features are found where plates pull apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.4.0] Features of constructive boundaries, where plates pull apart, are the mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, volcanic islands, fissure eruptions (eruptions from long cracks rather than one vent) of runny basalt lava, and shallow earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.b] Where two plates converge, one plate is forced down into the mantle. Which one sinks depends on the density of the crust.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.0] Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so where the two meet the oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.1] This process is subduction, and the zone where the plate sinks is the subduction zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.2] Crust is destroyed as the subducting plate melts in the hot mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.5.3] The place where the two plates meet at the surface is an ocean trench, a deep, narrow valley in the sea floor.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.b] When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks. This one collision produces a trench, fold mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes, which is why such coasts are so active.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.0] An ocean trench forms where the oceanic plate bends down, because the plate is forced into the mantle.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.1] Sediments (loose material laid down on the sea floor) are scraped off the subducting plate and crumpled against the continent into fold mountains.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.2] Melting of the sinking plate produces magma that rises to form a chain of volcanoes on the continent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.3] Earthquakes occur along the sinking plate because it grinds past the rock around it, shallow near the trench and deeper inland; this inclined zone is the Benioff zone.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.4] The Nazca plate subducts beneath the South American plate: the Peru-Chile Trench lies offshore and the Andes, with many volcanoes, rise on land.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.5] The Juan de Fuca plate subducts beneath the North American plate, producing the Cascade Range volcanoes, including Mount St Helens, Washington, USA.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.6.6] Diagram: cross-section with ocean trench, subducting plate, melting zone, rising magma, volcano, fold mountains and earthquake foci (the points underground where earthquakes start).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.b] When two oceanic plates meet, neither is light enough to resist sinking, so one is forced under the other. The result is a trench and a curved line of volcanic islands rather than a mountain chain on a continent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.0] The older, denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the other, because denser crust sinks more easily.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.1] An ocean trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands, called an island arc, form, and earthquakes are frequent.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.2] The Mariana Trench, about 11 km deep, is the deepest place on earth; it lies where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the Philippine plate, and the Mariana Islands form an island arc.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.3] The Aleutian Islands, Alaska, are an island arc where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the North American plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.7.4] Japan lies where the Pacific, Philippine, Eurasian and North American plates meet.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.0] Both plates are too light to subduct, so the crust between them, including sediments from the ocean that once separated them, is crushed, folded and pushed up into high fold mountains.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.8.1] Earthquakes are common, but there is little or no volcanic activity, because no plate sinks and melts to make magma.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.b] Destructive boundaries shape the land and also cause hazards such as powerful earthquakes, tsunamis and explosive volcanic eruptions. For a discussion of their impacts, give landforms and hazards, each with a named example.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.0] Landforms: ocean trenches, fold mountains, volcanic mountain chains and island arcs, such as the Andes and the Mariana Trench.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.1] Earthquakes: Nepal, 25 April 2015, magnitude 7.8 (a measure of earthquake size), killed about 9,000 people on the Himalayan collision boundary.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.2] Undersea earthquakes and tsunamis (huge sea waves caused by earthquakes under the sea): the Tōhoku, Japan, earthquake of 11 March 2011 had magnitude 9.0; its tsunami killed about 18,000 people (dead and missing) and caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.3] The Indian Ocean earthquake of 26 December 2004 had magnitude 9.1 off Sumatra, Indonesia, where the Indo-Australian plate subducts; its tsunami killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.9.4] Volcanic eruptions: Mount St Helens erupted on 18 May 1980, killing 57 people.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.0] Friction locks the plates until stress builds up, and the stress is released suddenly as an earthquake.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.1] No magma is produced, so there is no volcanic activity, and no crust is created or destroyed.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.10.5] The North and East Anatolian Faults in Turkey are transform faults; the earthquake of 6 February 2023 on the East Anatolian Fault, magnitude 7.8, killed over 50,000 people in Turkey and Syria.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.0] Crust is created at ridges, carried across the ocean floor, and destroyed at subduction zones, so the ocean floor is continually recycled.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.11.1] Continental crust is too light to be subducted, which is why it survives for so long.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.1] Destructive boundaries: plates move together and crust is destroyed, or modified where two continental plates collide. Examples: the Andes, the Mariana Trench and the Himalayas.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.12.2] Transform (passive) boundaries: plates slide past and crust is conserved. Example: the San Andreas Fault.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.b] Earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains are concentrated in narrow belts along plate boundaries, with very few in plate interiors. This is how plate tectonics explains where they occur.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.0] The Pacific Ring of Fire is the belt of destructive and some transform boundaries around the edge of the Pacific plate and its neighbours.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.1] The Ring of Fire, because it follows destructive boundaries, contains about 75% of the world's active volcanoes and about 90% of its earthquakes.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.2] Ridges, subduction zones, collision zones and transform faults are the boundaries where these belts form, because plates interact there.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s3.13.3] Fold mountains are found only at convergent boundaries, as long chains along the plate edge; the Andes, Himalayas and Alps are examples.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.b] Rivers are useful, so people change them. Every change alters how the river's processes work: its velocity (speed of flow), erosion (wearing away of bed and banks), transport (carrying of material), deposition (dropping of material) and flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.0.1] The three measures studied here are hydro-electric dams, canalisation and flood control, each with Irish and international examples.
[geo-rivers-s4.1.0] The dam stores water in a reservoir (an artificial lake behind the dam), and the water is then released so that it falls through turbines (wheels that spin and drive generators).
[geo-rivers-s4.2.0] Velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load (the sand, silt and stones it was carrying) there.
[geo-rivers-s4.2.1] The reservoir slowly fills with sediment, a process called sedimentation, which reduces its capacity (how much water it can hold).
[geo-rivers-s4.2.2] A delta (a fan of deposited sediment) can build where the river enters the reservoir, because that is where the flow slows and drops its load.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.b] Below the dam the river has lost its sediment and its natural rhythm of floods and low flows. Both changes alter erosion and deposition.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.0] Water released from the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam. This is called clear-water erosion.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.1] The floodplain (the flat land beside the river) and delta receive no new silt, so deltas and coasts erode because nothing replaces the material lost.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.2] Discharge (the volume of water flowing past a point) is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.3] Less flooding means less deposition on the floodplain, because floodwater is what normally lays down silt there.
[geo-rivers-s4.3.4] Where water is diverted out of the old channel, the bypassed channel carries much less water.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.b] The Shannon Scheme is one power station at Ardnacrusha, near Limerick, fed by a weir and a canal. It is not a series of dams.
[geo-rivers-s4.4.3] The original channel between Parteen and Limerick, the Old River Shannon, carries much less water because most of it is diverted.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.b] Ardnacrusha is not the only Irish example. Four more dams and power stations can be named in an answer.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.0] Poulaphouca dam, River Liffey: created the Blessington Lakes reservoir, Co. Wicklow. It generates electricity and supplies drinking water to Dublin.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.1] Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid dams, River Lee, Co. Cork.
[geo-rivers-s4.5.2] Cathaleen's Fall power station, River Erne, at Ballyshannon, Co. Donegal.
[geo-rivers-s4.6.1] Benefits are flood control, year-round irrigation (which allows more than one crop a year), hydro-electricity, and protection from drought.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.b] The dam's effects follow from the same processes as any dam: trapped sediment, clear-water erosion and regulated flow. They then spread into farming, fishing, health and settlement.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.1] The river bed below the dam is scoured (worn away) by clear water, which has spare energy to erode.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.2] The Nile delta coast is eroding, e.g. at the Rosetta promontory (a headland on the delta coast), because no new sediment arrives to replace what the sea removes.
[geo-rivers-s4.7.3] The sardine fishery off the delta in the Mediterranean declined sharply.
[geo-rivers-s4.8.3] It traps sediment that once reached the river's lower course and delta, so those areas receive less of the sediment they depended on.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.b] Canalisation modifies an existing river channel. It is not the same as building a canal.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.0] Methods are straightening, deepening and widening the channel, sometimes lining it with concrete, cutting through meanders (bends in the river), dredging (scooping sediment from the bed) and building locks and weirs for navigation.
[geo-rivers-s4.9.1] Purposes are navigation, faster drainage of land, and flood control.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.0] A straighter, shorter course has a steeper gradient (slope) and less friction (drag from the bed and banks), so velocity rises.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.2] Faster flow has more energy, so it erodes the bed and banks.
[geo-rivers-s4.10.3] Where the channel widens or the flow slows again, deposition increases and dredging is needed to keep the channel clear.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.1] Arterial drainage effects were changes to fish spawning beds, bank erosion and downstream flooding.
[geo-rivers-s4.11.2] Mississippi: cut-offs (new short channels across bends) made by the US Army Corps of Engineers shortened the river.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.b] Flood control measures respond to the causes of river flooding, so it helps to know what those causes are.
[geo-rivers-s4.12.2] Building on floodplains puts homes in the path of floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.0] Embankments and levees (raised banks) confine flow to the channel, so deposition raises the bed and the banks must be raised again.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.1] With embankments and levees the floodplain gets no silt, and failure gives sudden, severe floods.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.3] Dams and reservoirs store floodwater.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.4] Dredging and deepening increase channel capacity, but the flow is faster and downstream flooding can increase.
[geo-rivers-s4.13.5] Relief (bypass) channels take floodwater around the town.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.b] Soft engineering works with the river instead of against it. It slows water or gives it space, so natural processes carry on.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.2] Wetlands and bogs can be restored, and washlands (land that is allowed to flood) give the river more room.
[geo-rivers-s4.14.4] Effect on processes: natural flooding and floodplain deposition continue.

[geo-rivers-s4.15.1] The releases were not the sole cause, because the rain was exceptional. The University College Cork and the city water treatment works were flooded, and many homes lost their water supply.
[geo-rivers-s4.15.3] December 2015: Storm Desmond caused prolonged flooding on the Shannon, in towns such as Athlone.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.b] The Mississippi is the international case study for levees. It shows both what they protect and how they can fail.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.0] The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 led to the US federal levee and floodway system run by the US Army Corps of Engineers.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.1] The 1993 flood breached many levees along the upper Mississippi and Missouri.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.2] In August 2005 Hurricane Katrina caused levee and floodwall failures in New Orleans, and about 80% of the city flooded.
[geo-rivers-s4.16.3] Levees stop flood silt reaching the delta, which contributes to the loss of Louisiana's coastal wetlands.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.b] The Netherlands shows a soft-engineering response to flooding: instead of building higher walls, it gives the river space.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.0] Room for the River (Ruimte voor de Rivier), Netherlands, started after near-disastrous high water on the Rhine and Maas in 1993 and 1995.
[geo-rivers-s4.17.1] Measures include moving dykes (embankments) back, lowering floodplains and digging side channels so the river can spread out safely.
[geo-rivers-s4.18.2] There is a trend towards working with natural processes.
