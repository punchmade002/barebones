QUESTION geography-pp-2023-higher-q119 (LC Geography Higher 2023 — Part Two — Q19, 80 marks)

PART "19" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Many cultural groups, such as the Kurds, Tibetans and Catalans, share a distinct identity but have no nation state of their own, because borders were drawn or enforced by others.
Aspect 1 – The Kurds:
The Kurds are commonly estimated at 30 million or more, the largest ethnic group without its own state.
They live in a mountainous region, Kurdistan, that is divided between Turkey, which has the largest number in the south-east, Iran, Iraq and Syria.
The Treaty of Sèvres of 1920 provided for a possible Kurdish state, but the Treaty of Lausanne of 1923 replaced it and drew the borders of modern Turkey without one.
Kurdish is an Indo-European language of the Iranian group, and Newroz, the Kurdish New Year on 21 March, is a major expression of Kurdish identity.
Turkey long denied a separate Kurdish identity and banned Kurdish in public life until 1991 and in broadcasting until the early 2000s.
The PKK, founded in 1978, began an armed campaign in 1984 that cost about 40,000 lives, and it announced its dissolution in May 2025.
On 16 March 1988 Saddam Hussein's forces attacked the Kurdish town of Halabja with chemical weapons, killing about 5,000 people.
Iraq's 2005 constitution recognised the Kurdistan Region as autonomous, with its own government and parliament in Erbil.
On 25 September 2017 about 93% voted for independence in a referendum that Iraq's government rejected, and Iraqi forces then retook Kirkuk.
Aspect 2 – The Tibetans:
Tibetans have a distinct language, culture and religion centred on Buddhist monasteries, but they have no state of their own.
China's army entered Tibet in 1950.
After a failed uprising in 1959 the Dalai Lama fled to India, and Tibet's government-in-exile is based in Dharamsala.
The Tibet Autonomous Region was set up in 1965 within China, so Tibet has autonomy in name but not independence.
Tibetans see migration of Han Chinese into Tibet as a threat to their culture.
Controls on monasteries limit the practice of Tibetan religion.
Mandarin-medium schooling threatens the Tibetan language.
Tibetans keep their identity alive through language, festivals and diaspora organisations, and by peaceful political campaigns.
The outcome in Tibet has been repression rather than autonomy, in contrast with Catalonia and Iraqi Kurdistan.
Aspect 3 – The Catalans:
Catalans have their own language and a strong regional identity, and Barcelona is the capital of Catalonia.
Catalan was suppressed under Franco from 1939 to 1975.
Spain's 1978 constitution created 17 autonomous communities, and Catalonia's autonomy statute dates from 1979 and was revised in 2006.
In 2010 Spain's Constitutional Court struck down parts of the 2006 statute, including its description of Catalonia as a nation, which fuelled the independence movement.
On 1 October 2017 an independence referendum was declared illegal and police tried to stop the voting, and about 90% of those voting backed independence on a turnout of about 43%.
Spain then imposed direct rule using Article 155 of its constitution, and the regional president Carles Puigdemont fled to Belgium.
Nine Catalan leaders were jailed in 2019 and pardoned in 2021, and an amnesty law was passed in 2024.
Catalonia shows that a nation without a state can keep strong autonomy without independence.
This contrasts with the Kurds in Turkey, who for decades had no recognition at all.

PART "20" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Everyday life expresses culture and identity through sport, traditional music and dance, and festivals and traditions, as the Irish example shows.

Aspect 1 – Sport and the GAA:
Sport, especially the Gaelic Athletic Association, is one of the clearest everyday expressions of Irish identity.
The GAA was founded on 1 November 1884 in Hayes' Hotel, Thurles, Co. Tipperary, by Michael Cusack and others to preserve and promote native Irish games.
Hurling and Gaelic football are played in clubs in almost every parish, so taking part passes the culture from one generation to the next.
Clubs are based on parishes and teams represent counties, so the GAA reinforces local and county identity.
Supporters wear their county jerseys and colours in everyday life, which shows where they belong.
Players are amateurs, so the games remain rooted in the community.
Croke Park in Dublin holds about 82,300 spectators and is one of Europe's largest stadiums, so it is a national focus for Gaelic games.
On Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920, British forces opened fire at a match in Croke Park and killed 14 people, which tied the GAA to the national struggle.
The GAA is organised on an all-island basis and has clubs among the Irish diaspora abroad, so it carries Irish identity overseas.
Hurling was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2018, which recognises its cultural value.
In Northern Ireland sporting loyalties are divided between Irish and British teams, so the choice of team can express national identity.

Aspect 2 – Traditional music and dance:
Traditional music and dance are practised in ordinary social life and express a distinct Irish identity.
Traditional instruments include the fiddle, uilleann pipes, tin whistle, concertina, bodhrán and harp.
Sessions are informal gatherings where musicians play together, so the music is part of everyday social life.
The harp is Ireland's national symbol and appears on coins and the presidential seal.
Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann was founded in 1951 to promote traditional music.
The first Fleadh Cheoil na hÉireann was held in Mullingar in 1951, and it is now held in a different town each August and draws hundreds of thousands of visitors.
Sean-nós is unaccompanied traditional singing in Irish, strongest in Conamara, so it expresses both music and language.
Riverdance began as the interval act of the Eurovision Song Contest in Dublin on 30 April 1994 and became a worldwide touring show that carried Irish dance to audiences abroad.
Uilleann piping was added to UNESCO's list of living traditions in 2017 and Irish harping in 2019.
Irish popular music with traditional roots combines traditional instruments and melodies with modern styles, which shows the culture adapting to change.

Aspect 3 – Festivals and traditions:
Festivals and traditions are repeated each year and show people's sense of belonging to a place or a community.
St Patrick's Day, 17 March, is marked by parades across the world, for example in New York, and landmarks are lit green, so it is a global expression of Irish identity.
Dublin's St Patrick's Festival began in 1996.
Puck Fair in Killorglin, Co. Kerry, is held from 10 to 12 August and a wild goat is crowned king, which shows a local tradition kept alive over time.
Samhain, 31 October to 1 November, is the Celtic origin of Halloween, so a custom practised today has Irish roots.
Ballinasloe Horse Fair in Co. Galway is held every October, is one of Europe's oldest horse fairs and is important to Irish Travellers.
The Rose of Tralee has been held since 1959 and is open to women of Irish birth or descent worldwide, so it links the diaspora to Ireland.
Bloomsday on 16 June celebrates James Joyce's Ulysses, which is set on 16 June 1904.
St Brigid's Day became a public holiday in 2023, the first named after a woman.
Festivals bring tourists and income to the places where they are held, which gives communities a reason to keep them going.

QUESTION geography-pp-2023-higher-q121 (LC Geography Higher 2023 — Part Two — Q21, 80 marks)

PART "21" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Minority languages can be kept alive through education, media and legal status, as the cases of Irish, Welsh and Basque show.

Aspect 1 – Education through the language:
Teaching in and through a minority language is a key strategy because it repairs the break in passing the language from parents to children.
Immersion schools teach all or most subjects through the minority language, which creates new fluent speakers.
In Ireland Gaelscoileanna are Irish-medium schools outside the Gaeltacht, and their umbrella body was founded in 1973.
Irish is a compulsory subject through primary and post-primary school, with exemptions.
Gaeltacht summer colleges bring learners to live with Irish-speaking families.
In the Basque Country the ikastolak began as clandestine Basque-medium schools under Franco, and Model D, taught all through Basque, is now chosen for most pupils.
Euskaltzaindia, the Royal Academy of the Basque Language founded in 1919, created standard Basque, called Batua, from 1968, so one form could be taught in schools.
Welsh is compulsory in all schools in Wales up to age 16, and Welsh-medium schools are found across Wales.
Urdd Gobaith Cymru, a youth movement founded in 1922, and the National Eisteddfod promote Welsh among the young.
Breton shows the opposite, since the Diwan immersion schools are run by a voluntary association and in 2021 France's Constitutional Council struck down parts of a law that permitted immersion teaching in public schools, and Breton has declined sharply.

Aspect 2 – Broadcasting and media:
Media in the minority language give daily exposure and show that the language is modern and valued.
Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting in 1972.
TG4 launched on 31 October 1996, is based in Baile na hAbhann in Conamara, and broadcasts Irish-language drama, sport and music.
S4C, the Welsh-language television channel, launched on 1 November 1982 after Gwynfor Evans threatened a hunger strike in 1980 when the government backed away from its promise.
BBC Radio Cymru, the Welsh-language radio service, began in 1977.
BBC Alba, the Scottish Gaelic television service, launched in 2008.
EITB, the Basque public broadcaster, began television in 1982.
Berria, a daily newspaper in Basque founded in 2003, gives news in the language every day.
Social media and online content let small languages reach young people.

Aspect 3 – Official status and legislation:
Giving a language official status in law protects its use in public life and obliges the state to support it.
Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution makes Irish the national language and the first official language.
The Official Languages Act 2003 gives the public the right to deal with public bodies in Irish, and its 2021 amendment sets a target that 20% of new public-service recruits be competent in Irish by 2030.
An Coimisinéir Teanga, the Language Commissioner set up in 2004, enforces the Act.
Údarás na Gaeltachta, set up in 1980, grant-aids firms in the Gaeltacht, such as on the Gaoth Dobhair industrial estate, so that jobs help young speakers to stay.
The Gaeltacht Act 2012 requires language planning in Gaeltacht areas.
The Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave Welsh official status and led to a Welsh Language Commissioner from 2012.
The Statute of Gernika in 1979 made Basque co-official with Spanish in the Basque Autonomous Community.
Irish became an official EU language in 2007, with full working-language status from 1 January 2022, and the Council of Europe adopted the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 1992.
Law alone is not enough, because the 2022 census showed about 1.87 million people able to speak Irish but only about 72,000 speaking it daily outside the education system.
