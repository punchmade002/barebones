PART 13 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Empowering people by giving them control over resources and a voice in decisions turns economic growth into human development, as aid programmes and land ownership patterns show.
Aspect 1 – Aid programmes:
Aid is money, goods or expertise given by richer countries and organisations to poorer ones, and it links growth to development only when it empowers people rather than just raising GDP.
Emergency aid such as food and shelter after famine and conflict saves lives but does not on its own give people lasting control over resources.
Long-term development aid funds wells, schools, clinics and vaccination programmes, which improve health and education, the core of human development.
Uganda introduced Universal Primary Education in 1997 and in 1998 became the first country to receive HIPC debt relief, with the savings directed to schools, health and water.
Tanzania abolished primary school fees in 2001, which empowered poorer families to educate their children.
Zambia reached HIPC completion point in 2005 and in 2006 abolished user fees for health care in rural areas.
Irish NGOs such as Concern Worldwide, founded in 1968 in response to the Biafra famine, and Self Help Africa, which supports smallholder farmers, work bottom-up with communities.
Bóthar gives farm animals to poor families who pass on the first female offspring to another family, which spreads ownership and self-help.
Irish Aid, whose bilateral programme began in 1974, is untied and works in partner countries such as Uganda, Tanzania, Malawi and Zambia, so recipients can spend it where it best meets their needs.
Tied aid, which must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, returns much of the money to donor firms and so does little to empower recipients.
UK aid for the Pergau Dam in Malaysia was linked to a British arms deal and was ruled unlawful by the English High Court in 1994 because it was not for sound development.
Corruption and elite capture can divert aid to powerful groups, so growth does not reach the poor.
In the mid-1990s Haiti cut its rice tariff under pressure from lenders, cheap subsidised US rice flooded in and many Haitian rice farmers were ruined, and Bill Clinton apologised for the policy in 2010.
Aid is most effective when it is untied, targeted at the poorest, locally owned, transparent and supportive of self-help, because then people control the resources that link growth to development.
Aspect 2 – Land ownership patterns:
Land is the main source of food, income, security and status in rural societies, so who owns it decides who benefits from economic growth.
In nineteenth-century Ireland a small, mainly Protestant landlord class, many of them absentee, owned most of the land, and tenants-at-will had no security and could be evicted.
Rack-rents and the subdivision of holdings into tiny plots, many worked under conacre or rundale, left families dependent on the potato.
When blight struck, the Great Famine of 1845–49 killed about 1 million people and about 1 million emigrated, which shows how unequal ownership blocks human development.
The Land League, founded in 1879 and led by Michael Davitt and Charles Stewart Parnell, empowered tenants by demanding the Three Fs of fair rent, fixity of tenure and free sale.
Gladstone's Land Act of 1881 granted the Three Fs, and the Ashbourne Act of 1885 and the Wyndham Land Act of 1903 let tenants buy their holdings with government loans repaid by annuities.
By the early twentieth century most Irish farmers owned their land, which gave them security, income and political power, though many farms stayed small and the west remained poor.
Latin America shows the opposite pattern, with latifundia or large estates alongside minifundia or tiny plots, and Brazil has one of the most unequal patterns of land ownership in the world.
The MST, the Landless Workers' Movement founded in Brazil in 1984, empowers landless families by campaigning for land.
South Africa's Natives Land Act of 1913 limited Black South Africans to reserves of about 7% of the country, and land reform since 1994 has transferred land slowly.
Land reform raised development where it worked, as in South Korea from 1950 and Taiwan from 1953, where tenant farmers gained ownership and rural incomes rose before industrialisation.
Kerala abolished landlord tenancy through land reform from 1970 and has about 94% literacy compared with about 62% in Bihar (Census 2011), which shows high human development.
Badly managed reform damages development, as in Zimbabwe, where land seizures from 2000 cut farm output and hyperinflation peaked in 2008.
Women make up about 43% of the agricultural labour force in developing countries but fewer than one in five agricultural landholders worldwide, so without land they cannot get credit or make farm decisions.

QUESTION geography-pp-2023-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2023 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART 14 (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Views of development and underdevelopment differ on what causes poverty and what counts as progress, as the modernisation, dependency and human development models show.
Aspect 1 – Modernisation theory:
Modernisation theory holds that all societies pass along one path from traditional to modern, as Western Europe and North America did, so poor countries are simply behind.
Walt Rostow set out five stages in 1960, namely traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity and high mass consumption.
In take-off investment rises above 10% of national income and a few leading industries grow fast.
On this view underdevelopment means not yet developed, and the remedy is investment, technology, industry and Western values.
South Korea seems to fit the model, with export-led industrialisation from the 1960s led by chaebol such as Samsung and Hyundai, and it was reclassified as a developed country by UNCTAD in 2021.
Ireland is often fitted to the stages, with Whitaker's First Programme for Economic Expansion in 1958 as a precondition and EEC membership in 1973 helping take-off.
Critics say the model is Eurocentric because it assumes the Western path is the only path to development.
It is linear, but countries can stall or go backwards, as Zimbabwe after 2000 and Syria after 2011 show.
It ignores colonialism and unequal trade, and it assumes wealth trickles down from growth to the poor, which often does not happen.
Aspect 2 – Dependency theory:
Dependency theory holds that poor countries are poor because of how they were drawn into the world economy, not because of their own backwardness.
The rich core drains wealth from the poor periphery, so the development of the core and the underdevelopment of the periphery are two sides of one process.
On this view underdevelopment means made poor by others.
The theory arose among Latin American thinkers, and Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer argued in 1950 that the terms of trade of primary-product exporters tend to fall.
Andre Gunder Frank's The Development of Underdevelopment (1966) argued that Latin America was underdeveloped by its links with the core.
Colonial extraction continues as dependence on one or two primary exports, such as Zambia on copper and Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, which grow over half of the world's cocoa, mostly exported as beans.
Rich countries tax processed goods more heavily than raw materials, multinationals send profits home and debt repayments drain income that could fund development.
Wallerstein's world-systems theory divides the world into core, semi-periphery and periphery, and countries such as South Korea and China have moved up.
Critics say dependency theory cannot explain the Asian Tigers, which developed through trade with the core, and that withdrawal from world trade has rarely worked.
Aspect 3 – Human development and alternative views:
A third view judges progress by people's lives and not by national output alone.
The basic needs approach of the 1970s put food, water, shelter, health and education ahead of GNP growth, and the ILO World Employment Conference of 1976 placed it at the centre of policy.
E. F. Schumacher's Small Is Beautiful (1973) argued for small-scale appropriate technology that local people can afford, use and repair.
Amartya Sen's Development as Freedom (1999) argued that development means widening people's choices and capabilities.
The UNDP's first Human Development Report appeared in 1990, and the HDI, created by Mahbub ul Haq with Sen, measures health, education and income.
Bottom-up development puts participation, co-operatives, NGOs and women's groups at the centre, in contrast with top-down projects.
Sustainable development, set out in the Brundtland Report of 1987, adds that today's needs must be met without harming future generations, and the 17 Sustainable Development Goals were adopted in 2015 with a target year of 2030.
Alternative measures such as Bhutan's Gross National Happiness, put forward in the 1970s, challenge GNP as the main goal.
Eurocentric views are also challenged, since the Mercator projection of 1569 makes Africa look similar in size to Greenland although Africa is about 14 times larger.

MODEL ANSWER: Social and political decisions in one country shape refugee flows, migration patterns and human rights in others, which shows how interdependent the global economy and society have become.
Aspect 1 – Economic and political refugees:
Political refugees flee persecution or war that results from political decisions, while economic refugees flee poverty that is often caused by decisions elsewhere in trade, debt or conflict, and the line between them is often blurred.
More than 120 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide in 2024 (UNHCR), and most refugees stay in neighbouring low and middle income countries, not in Europe.
In Syria the government's use of force against protests in March 2011 led to civil war, and over 6 million Syrians became refugees abroad from a pre-war population of about 22 million.
Turkey hosted over 3 million Syrian refugees, and Lebanon has the most refugees per head of population of any country.
Germany's decision to admit refugees in August 2015 contrasted with Hungary's border fence with Serbia, and about 1.3 million asylum applications were made in the EU in 2015.
The EU–Turkey statement of March 2016 returned irregular migrants from the Greek islands to Turkey, with €6 billion promised for refugees in Turkey.
Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 was a political decision that displaced millions, and the EU activated its Temporary Protection Directive for the first time on 4 March 2022.
Ireland's Irish Refugee Protection Programme of September 2015 offered protection to up to 4,000 people in response to the Syrian crisis.
Direct provision, introduced in Ireland in 2000, housed asylum seekers in state-run accommodation while their applications were processed.
Aspect 2 – Migration patterns:
About 281 million international migrants were counted in 2020, about 3.6% of the world's population.
Most international migrants move within their own region, and the main corridors are Mexico to the USA, North and West Africa to Europe and South Asia to the Gulf.
On 1 May 2004 Ireland, the UK and Sweden opened their labour markets at once to workers from the new EU member states, which changed migration patterns across Europe.
Census 2022 recorded about 12% of residents in Ireland as non-Irish nationals, and Ireland has returned to net immigration.
Political and economic decisions also drove Irish emigration, as about 1 million people emigrated during the Famine of 1845–49 and about 89,000 left in the year to April 2013 after the 2008 crash.
Migrant workers, mainly from India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Pakistan and the Philippines, make up most of the population and workforce of Qatar and the United Arab Emirates under the kafala sponsorship system.
On the Atlantic route migrants from West Africa travel by boat to Spain's Canary Islands, and arrivals reached a record in 2024.
Remittances to low and middle income countries were about $650 billion in 2023, and India is the largest recipient.
Source regions lose doctors and nurses through brain drain, while receiving regions gain workers in health care, construction and farming and a younger population, but face pressure on housing and services.
Aspect 3 – Human rights issues:
The Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 10 December 1948 gives the right to seek and enjoy asylum from persecution in Article 14.
Non-refoulement, set out in Article 33 of the 1951 Refugee Convention, says that refugees must not be returned to danger.
Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law left most Rohingya stateless, and from August 2017 a military campaign drove about 740,000 of them into Bangladesh.
About 1 million Rohingya live in camps around Cox's Bazar, including Kutupalong, the world's largest refugee camp, where work and movement are restricted.
Rana Plaza near Dhaka collapsed on 24 April 2013, killing about 1,130 garment workers who made clothes for Western brands, and the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh was signed in response.
Qatar was criticised over conditions for workers building World Cup stadiums and in 2020 introduced reforms including a minimum wage and the right to change jobs without the employer's permission.
The UN Human Rights Office reported in 2022 serious violations against Uyghurs in Xinjiang, and the US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act presumes that goods made there use forced labour and bans their import.
In July 1984 Mary Manning, a Dunnes Stores worker in Henry Street, Dublin, refused to handle South African grapefruit in protest against apartheid, and in 1987 the Irish government banned imports of South African fruit and vegetables.
Long stays in direct provision in Ireland raise questions of dignity and human rights, and the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive of 2024 requires large companies to address human rights risks in their supply chains.
