QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q113 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q13, 80 marks)

PART "13" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Economic growth only becomes human development when people are empowered with control over resources and a voice in decisions, as national debt, aid programmes and land ownership patterns show.
Aspect 1 – National debt:
Developing countries became indebted when the oil-price rises of the 1970s gave banks large deposits, which they lent on as petrodollars.
Interest rates soared about 1980 and commodity prices fell, so the loans became far harder to repay, and Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts.
Debt disempowers because repayments take up government revenue that would otherwise be spent on health, schools and water.
The cycle of poverty follows, as poor health and education keep productivity and incomes low, low tax and export earnings force more borrowing, and the debt grows.
Structural adjustment programmes tied to IMF and World Bank loans forced governments to cut public spending, introduce fees for schools and clinics and remove food subsidies.
The poorest people and women bore most of the cost of these cuts, so growth did not become development for them.
Debt relief began to empower people when the HIPC Initiative of 1996 offered relief on condition that the savings went to poverty reduction.
Uganda, the first country to receive HIPC relief in 1998, put the savings into schools, health and water, having introduced Universal Primary Education in 1997.
Zambia reached HIPC completion point in 2005 and in 2006 abolished user fees for health care in rural areas.
Ireland shows the weight of debt even in a rich country, as the EU–IMF bailout of €85 billion in 2010 brought austerity, unemployment of about 15% in 2012 and emigration of about 89,000 in the year to April 2013.
Aspect 2 – Aid programmes:
Aid links growth to development when it is untied, targeted at the poorest, locally owned and supports self-help.
Emergency aid saves lives after famine, floods or conflict, while long-term development aid funds projects in water, health, education and farming.
Bilateral aid goes directly from one government to another, while multilateral aid is pooled through bodies such as the EU and UN agencies.
The UN target of 1970 asks rich countries to give 0.7% of their gross national income as aid, which Norway, Sweden, Luxembourg and Denmark have reached but Ireland has not.
Irish Aid began its bilateral programme in 1974, and its untied aid goes to partner countries such as Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia and Vietnam.
Irish NGOs such as Concern Worldwide, founded in 1968 in response to the Biafra famine, and Trócaire, founded in 1973, work close to communities and empower them through co-operatives, women's groups and microcredit.
The EU institutions and member states together are the world's largest aid donor, and the Samoa Agreement of 2023 links the EU with African, Caribbean and Pacific states.
Aid can harm recipients, as when Haiti cut its rice tariff in the mid-1990s under pressure from lenders, cheap subsidised US rice flooded in and many Haitian rice farmers were ruined.
Tied aid serves donors, as when UK aid for the Pergau Dam in Malaysia was linked to a British arms deal and in 1994 the English High Court ruled it unlawful.
Aid can be diverted by corrupt elites and can breed dependency, which is the argument made by the Zambian economist Dambisa Moyo in Dead Aid (2009).
Aspect 3 – Land ownership patterns:
In rural societies land is the main source of food, income, security and status, so whoever owns it decides who benefits from growth.
In nineteenth-century Ireland a small, largely absentee landlord class held the land, while tenants-at-will had no security, could be evicted and paid rack-rents.
Holdings were subdivided into tiny plots and families depended on the potato, so the Great Famine of 1845–49 killed about 1 million people and about 1 million emigrated.
The Irish National Land League, founded in 1879 and led by Michael Davitt and Charles Stewart Parnell, demanded the Three Fs of fair rent, fixity of tenure and free sale.
Gladstone's Land Act of 1881 granted the Three Fs, and the Ashbourne Act of 1885 and the Wyndham Land Act of 1903 let tenants buy their holdings with government loans.
Ownership gave tenants security, income and political power, which is how land reform empowered them, although many farms stayed small and the west stayed poor.
Brazil has one of the most unequal patterns of land ownership in the world, with large latifundia beside tiny minifundia, and the Landless Workers' Movement (MST), founded in 1984, campaigns for land for landless families.
South Korea's land reform from 1950 and Taiwan's from 1953 gave tenant farmers ownership and raised rural incomes before industrialisation.
Kerala abolished landlord tenancy through land reform from 1970 and has about 94% literacy against about 62% in Bihar (Census 2011), showing land reform linked to human development.
Women make up about 43% of the agricultural labour force in developing countries but fewer than one in five agricultural landholders worldwide, so without land they cannot get credit or make farm decisions.

QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART "14" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Global warming, the rise in the earth's temperature caused by an enhanced greenhouse effect, is a global environmental issue with both human causes and widespread impacts.
Aspect 1 – Causes:
The greenhouse effect occurs when gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide trap long-wave radiation and warm the lower atmosphere.
Burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas in power stations, industry and transport since the Industrial Revolution is the main source of added carbon dioxide.
Industrial emissions are a cause in Ireland, where Moneypoint in Co. Clare, the country's only coal-fired power station, burned coal until 2025.
Deforestation adds carbon dioxide because burning and decay of felled trees release the carbon the forest stored, as in the clearance of the Brazilian Amazon.
Agriculture produces methane from cattle and rice paddies and nitrous oxide from fertiliser, and it produces over a third of Irish greenhouse gas emissions.
Rotting waste in landfill releases methane.
Cement manufacture releases carbon dioxide when limestone is heated.
Growing populations and rising consumption in industrialising countries increase the demand for energy and so the emissions.
The gases mix throughout the atmosphere, so emissions in any country affect the whole world, which makes the issue global.
Aspect 2 – Impacts on the physical environment:
The global average temperature has risen by more than 1 °C since pre-industrial times.
Glaciers and ice sheets are melting and Arctic sea ice is shrinking.
Sea level is rising because ocean water expands as it warms and because melting land ice adds water to the sea.
Warm air can hold about 7% more water vapour for each 1 °C rise, so global warming intensifies the water cycle and brings heavier downpours and more flooding.
Drought and fire become more frequent in dry regions such as the Sahel, where rainfall is roughly 200 to 600 mm and unreliable.
Thawing of tundra permafrost releases stored methane and carbon dioxide, which adds to the warming.
Warmer seas cause coral bleaching, which damages reefs such as the Great Barrier Reef.
Climate belts and biomes shift, which stresses species that cannot move or adapt quickly.
Scientists such as Carlos Nobre and Thomas Lovejoy warn that losing about 20 to 25% of the Amazon forest could push parts of it past a tipping point towards degraded savanna.
Aspect 3 – Impacts on people:
Low-lying islands such as the Maldives, only a metre or two above sea level, are threatened by rising seas.
Coastal flooding threatens delta farmland and cities, as in Bangladesh.
Rising seas push salt water into the Mekong Delta, which damages rice fields.
Drought lowers crop yields and causes food shortages, which can lead to famine and migration.
Heatwaves, storms and floods damage health, homes and infrastructure and cost economies heavily.
Melting glaciers in the Himalaya threaten the dry-season river flow that millions of people in South Asia depend on.
Warmer conditions let diseases such as malaria and dengue spread into areas that were once too cool for the mosquitoes that carry them.
The poorest people suffer most because they have least ability to adapt, although they have added least to the problem.
People displaced by rising seas, drought or floods become climate migrants.

QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q115 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q15, 80 marks)

PART "15" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Fair trade contributes to sustainable development by giving small producers a fairer price and a voice, which supports the economic, social and environmental sides of development.
Aspect 1 – Economic sustainability:
Commodity prices are low and unstable, small farmers receive a tiny share of the final price, and middlemen take a large share of the value of their crops.
The Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production, so farmers can plan and invest with more security.
When the market price is above the minimum, producers receive the market price plus the Fairtrade Premium.
Long-term contracts and pre-finance give producers credit before the harvest.
The Oromia Coffee Farmers Co-operative Union in Ethiopia, founded in 1999 and led by Tadesse Meskela, sells members' coffee directly to buyers and so cuts out middlemen.
Divine Chocolate, set up in Britain in 1998 and part-owned by the Ghanaian co-operative Kuapa Kokoo, gives farmers a share of profits as well as the Fairtrade price and premium.
Producers learn processing and marketing skills, which helps them to keep more of the value of their crops.
Aspect 2 – Social sustainability:
The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative and spent on projects that the members choose democratically.
Kuapa Kokoo, founded in Ghana in 1993, has spent its premium on wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
The Oromia union's premium has paid for schools, clinics and water.
Labour standards ban forced and child labour, protect the freedom to join unions and require safe conditions.
Child labour can fall when families earn a fairer income.
Women's participation in co-operatives gives women a voice and an income, and Kuapa Kokoo encourages women to take leadership roles in its village societies.
Democratic co-operatives empower producers to take decisions together.
Aspect 3 – Environmental sustainability:
Environmental standards restrict pesticides, protect water and ban GM crops.
Fairtrade standards forbid clearing primary forest to plant crops, which protects habitats and biodiversity.
Organic farming and reduced clearing of land protect soil and biodiversity.
Organic Fairtrade crops earn an extra price on top of the minimum, which rewards farmers for avoiding chemical inputs.
Fairtrade coffee is often grown under shade trees, which shelter birds and other wildlife and protect the soil from heavy rain.
A more stable income lessens the pressure on farmers to overuse their land in order to survive.
The Fairtrade Mark guarantees to consumers that these standards have been met.
Aspect 4 – Limits on its contribution:
Fair trade is only a tiny share of world trade.
Higher prices put off some consumers, and certification costs exclude the poorest farmers.
Benefits go mainly to co-operative members rather than hired labourers, and a 2014 study by SOAS of Ethiopia and Uganda found hired workers on some certified farms paid less than on comparable non-certified farms, though Fairtrade disputed parts of it.
Market prices sometimes exceed the minimum price, so the floor then gives no extra protection.
Fair trade does not change trade rules such as subsidies and tariff escalation, where roasted coffee faces higher tariffs than coffee beans.
Cadbury Dairy Milk carried the Fairtrade Mark from 2009 but switched in 2017 to Mondelēz's own Cocoa Life programme.
Its overall impact depends on its scale and on wider changes in trade rules.
