PART 2B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sedimentary rock – Carboniferous limestone:
Carboniferous limestone formed about 360 to 300 million years ago when Ireland lay near the equator under a warm, shallow, clear sea.
Shells and skeletons of marine animals built up on the sea floor as the sediment.
The sediment accumulated in layers called strata over millions of years.
The weight of the overlying layers squeezed out water and compressed the lower layers by compaction.
Dissolved calcium carbonate acted as a natural cement that bound the particles together by cementation.
The conversion of loose sediment into solid rock in this way is called lithification.
Because it formed from the remains of living things, it is an organic sedimentary rock that contains fossils and shows bedding planes between layers.
A named location is the Burren in Co. Clare, where Carboniferous limestone is exposed over a wide area.
Metamorphic rock – Quartzite:
Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by great heat and pressure without melting.
Quartzite forms from the parent rock sandstone.
The sandstone is made of quartz grains cemented together.
The heat and pressure came from plate collision during the Caledonian mountain building as the Iapetus Ocean closed.
Under these conditions the minerals recrystallise while the rock stays solid.
The heat is not enough to melt the rock, so it changes in the solid state.
The sand grains fuse into a hard, dense, interlocking rock that is much harder than sandstone.
Because it is so resistant to erosion, quartzite stands out as high peaks above the surrounding rock.
A named location is Errigal in Co. Donegal, a quartzite peak.
Croagh Patrick in Co. Mayo and the Great Sugar Loaf in Co. Wicklow are also quartzite.

PART 2C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Option – Human impact on river processes – Dams:
A dam changes how a river erodes, transports and deposits by trapping its load and regulating its flow.
Named example – Aswan High Dam:
The Aswan High Dam on the River Nile in Egypt, completed in 1970, is an example of human activity that changes river processes.
Its reservoir, Lake Nasser, lies behind the dam in southern Egypt and extends into Sudan.
Impact upstream:
Velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load there.
The reservoir slowly fills with sediment, which reduces its capacity.
A delta can build up where the river enters the reservoir.
Impact downstream:
Water released from the dam is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam.
The river bed below the dam is scoured by this clear-water erosion.
The floodplain and delta receive no new silt, so natural deposition on the floodplain has stopped.
Farmers downstream now rely on artificial fertilisers because the annual silt no longer arrives.
The Nile delta coast is eroding, for example at the Rosetta promontory, because sediment no longer replaces material removed by waves.
Discharge is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out.
The sardine fishery off the delta in the Mediterranean declined sharply after the dam was built.
Benefits and costs:
The dam gives flood control, year-round irrigation for more than one crop a year, and hydro-electricity.
Irrigated land has suffered waterlogging and salinisation.
Irish example – Ardnacrusha:
At Ardnacrusha near Limerick, built 1925 to 1929, Parteen Weir diverts most of the Shannon's water into a headrace canal to the power station.
The original channel between Parteen and Limerick, the Old River Shannon, now carries much less water, and Lough Derg's level is controlled by the weir.

PART 3A(ii) (4 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Plucking: ice freezes onto blocks of jointed rock at the base or sides of the glacier.
As the glacier moves, it pulls these blocks away, enlarging the joints into cavities.

PART 3A(iii) (4 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Basal sliding: the weight of the ice melts a thin layer of ice at the base of the glacier, forming a film of water.
The glacier slides forward over the bedrock on this water, carrying the ice with it.

PART 3B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Rock 1 – Limestone and the Burren karst:
Carboniferous limestone is calcium carbonate and floors the central lowland and the Burren in Co. Clare.
Rainwater picks up carbon dioxide to form weak carbonic acid, which dissolves limestone by carbonation.
Limestone is permeable because water moves along its bedding planes and joints, so surface streams are few.
Glaciers scraped the soil from the Burren, exposing the jointed rock as limestone pavement.
Carbonation widens the joints into deep grikes and leaves flat-topped blocks called clints.
Underground, water dissolves caves such as Aillwee Cave in the Burren, where dripstone forms stalactites and stalagmites.
Turloughs are seasonal lakes that fill in winter when the water table rises and drain through the floor in summer, and they are common in the Burren and east Galway.
Rock 2 – Granite and the Wicklow Mountains:
Granite is hard, crystalline and resistant to erosion, and the Leinster batholith, the largest granite mass in Ireland, forms the Wicklow Mountains.
The Wicklow uplands are rounded, dome-shaped hills worn down from the rock that once covered the granite.
Granite is impermeable, so water runs off it and there are many surface streams.
Impermeable granite and high rainfall give thin, acidic soils and blanket bog, for example around the Sally Gap.
Ice cut deep valleys into the granite.
Resistant granite forms the uplands while weaker limestone forms the lowland, so Ireland has a saucer shape with a low centre ringed by hills.

PART 3C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named theorist – Alfred Wegener:
Alfred Wegener, a German meteorologist, proposed continental drift in 1912 and published it in 1915.
He proposed that the continents were once joined in one supercontinent, Pangaea, which began to break up about 200 million years ago.
The theory was rejected at first because Wegener could not explain what force moved the continents.
Evidence for continental drift:
The coastlines of South America and Africa fit together like a jigsaw, and the fit is even better at the edge of the continental shelves.
Fossils of Mesosaurus, a freshwater reptile, are found in both Brazil and southern Africa, and it could not have crossed an ocean.
The Appalachian Mountains of North America match the Caledonian mountains of Ireland, Scotland and Norway in rock type, age and structure.
Glacial deposits about 300 million years old occur in India, Africa, South America and Australia, places that are now warm.
Sea-floor spreading:
Arthur Holmes suggested in the late 1920s that mantle convection could move the continents.
Harry Hess proposed sea-floor spreading in the early 1960s, in which magma rises at mid-ocean ridges and cools to form new basalt crust.
The new crust pushes older crust away on both sides of the ridge, and old crust is destroyed again at ocean trenches.
Oceanic rocks are youngest at the ridge and get older with distance from it, symmetrically on both sides.
Stripes of normal and reversed magnetism mirror each other on either side of the ridge, as explained by Vine and Matthews in 1963.
Named location – Mid-Atlantic Ridge:
The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is about 16,000 km long and separates the Eurasian and North American plates.
The Atlantic is widening by about 2.5 cm a year, so Ireland on the Eurasian plate is moving slowly away from North America.
Plates and movement:
A plate is a rigid slab of lithosphere that floats and moves on the asthenosphere, and the earth has seven major plates including the Pacific, Eurasian and African plates.
Convection currents in the mantle drag the plates along, helped by ridge push and by slab pull at subducting edges.
Plates meet in three ways, moving apart, moving together or sliding past each other, and endogenic forces are concentrated along these boundaries.

[geo-glaciation-s1.1.b] Glacier ice is not frozen water poured into a valley. It is built up slowly from snow that never melts away.
[geo-glaciation-s1.1.1] Firn becomes glacier ice as the weight of overlying snow squeezes out the air and the ice crystals grow larger and interlock, producing dense solid ice.
[geo-glaciation-s1.2.0] Accumulation zone: the upper part of the glacier, where snow and ice are gained, because more snow falls there than melts.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.0] Basal slip: meltwater at the base lubricates the bed (the rock floor beneath the ice), so the whole glacier slides over the rock.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.1] Internal deformation (plastic flow): ice crystals slip past one another under the weight of the ice, so the glacier flows downhill.
[geo-glaciation-s1.3.3] Ice moves fastest at the centre of the surface and slowest at the sides and base, where friction with the valley walls and bed is greatest.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.b] The snout's position depends on whether more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts at the snout. This balance decides whether the glacier is getting longer or shorter.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.0] Advance: when more ice arrives from the accumulation zone than melts, the snout moves forward.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.2] Heavier snowfall or cooler summers, which reduce melting, cause the glacier to advance.
[geo-glaciation-s1.4.3] Less snowfall or warmer summers, which increase melting, cause the glacier to retreat.

[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.0.b] The plate tectonics model grew in two stages, and the first was continental drift, the idea that the continents move. Alfred Wegener, a German meteorologist (a scientist who studies weather), proposed it in 1912 and published it in his book The Origin of Continents and Oceans in 1915.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.0.0] Wegener proposed that the continents were once joined in one supercontinent (a single giant landmass), Pangaea, surrounded by one ocean, Panthalassa, and have since drifted apart.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.0.1] Pangaea split into Laurasia in the north and Gondwanaland in the south, separated by the Tethys Sea; these broke up further into today's continents, such as South America and Africa.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.0.2] Pangaea began to break up about 200 million years ago.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.0.3] Wegener could not explain what force moved the continents, so the theory was rejected at first, because without a cause few accepted that continents could move.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.1.b] Wegener backed his idea with four kinds of evidence. Each shows that lands now far apart were once joined or lay in different climate zones.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.1.0] Jigsaw fit: the coastlines of South America and Africa fit together, and the fit is even better at the edge of the continental shelves (the shallow sea floor around a continent's edge).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.1.1] Fossils: Mesosaurus, a small freshwater reptile, is found in both Brazil and southern Africa. Glossopteris, a fern-like plant, is found in South America, Africa, India, Antarctica and Australia. Neither could have crossed an ocean, so the lands must once have been joined.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.1.2] Rocks and mountains: the Appalachian Mountains of eastern North America match the Caledonian mountains of Ireland, Scotland and Norway in rock type, age and structure, as if one range had been split by the Atlantic.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.1.3] Past climates: glacial deposits (material left by ice) and striations (scratches cut into rock by moving ice) about 300 million years old occur in India, Africa, South America and Australia, places now warm or near the equator.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.2.b] Continental drift lacked a force to move the continents. The clue came from the ocean floor, which was mapped in detail only after the Second World War.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.2.1] The mapping revealed long mid-ocean ridges (underwater mountain chains) with a central rift valley, and deep ocean trenches (long, very deep gashes in the sea floor).
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.2.2] The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is about 16,000 km long.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.b] Sea-floor spreading supplied the mechanism that continental drift lacked, by showing that the ocean floor itself is moving and carrying the continents with it.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.0] Arthur Holmes, a British geologist, suggested in the late 1920s that mantle convection (slow circulation of heated rock in the layer beneath the crust) could move continents.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.1] Harry Hess, an American geologist, proposed sea-floor spreading in the early 1960s, in four stages.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.2] 1. Magma (molten rock) rises at mid-ocean ridges.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.3] 2. It cools to form new oceanic crust (the crust under the oceans) made of basalt, a dark volcanic rock.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.4] 3. The new crust pushes older crust away on both sides of the ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.5] 4. Old crust is destroyed again at ocean trenches, so the ocean floor is constantly recycled.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.3.6] Sea-floor spreading supplied the missing mechanism and turned continental drift into the theory of plate tectonics.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.4.b] Three pieces of evidence from the ocean floor support Hess: the age of the rocks, the thickness of the sediment and the pattern of magnetism.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.4.0] Age of rocks: oceanic rocks are youngest at the ridge and get progressively older with distance from it, symmetrically on both sides. This fits new crust forming at the ridge and moving outwards.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.4.3] So the ocean floor holds stripes of normal and reversed magnetism that mirror each other on either side of the ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.4.4] Magnetic striping was explained by Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews in 1963.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.4.5] Diagram: plan view of a ridge with symmetrical magnetic stripes and arrows showing movement away from the ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.b] Sea-floor spreading showed that the earth's surface is divided into moving pieces called plates. Here you meet the definition of a plate, the main plates by name and the force that moves them.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.0] A plate is a rigid slab of lithosphere (the crust plus the solid upper part of the mantle) that floats and moves on the asthenosphere (the softer, slowly flowing layer beneath). A plate can carry continental crust, oceanic crust or both.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.1] The earth has seven major plates: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian and Antarctic. The Pacific plate is the largest.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.2] Smaller plates include Nazca, Cocos, Caribbean, Juan de Fuca, Philippine, Arabian and Scotia.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.4] Convection currents in the mantle (rising and sinking flows of heated rock) drag the plates along, because the plates sit on top of the moving rock.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.5] Plates move a few centimetres a year, so the map of continents and oceans changes over geological time. The Atlantic is widening by about 2.5 cm a year, roughly the rate a fingernail grows.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.5.6] Plates meet in three ways: moving apart (constructive, or divergent), moving together (destructive, or convergent), and sliding past (passive, conservative, or transform). There is more on this in the next subsection.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.6.0] Ridge push is new, hot crust at the ridge sliding away from it under gravity, so the plate is shoved away from the ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.6.1] Slab pull is the cold, dense subducting edge of a plate (the edge sinking back into the mantle) pulling the rest of the plate after it.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.7.0] Plate movement drives endogenic forces, which, for example, cause folding, faulting, volcanic activity and earthquakes and are concentrated mostly along plate boundaries.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.7.1] The continental drift and sea-floor spreading evidence above is why the plate tectonics model replaced the earlier theory.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.8.0] Ireland lies on the Eurasian plate, near its western edge and far from any plate boundary, which means it sits in the interior of a plate.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.8.1] The nearest plate boundary is the Mid-Atlantic Ridge to the west, which separates the Eurasian and North American plates in the North Atlantic, with Iceland on the ridge.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.8.2] As the Atlantic widens, Ireland moves slowly away from North America.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.9.2] The collision caused the Caledonian folding (the crumpling of rock layers into fold mountains), about 400 million years ago, which built mountains running north-east to south-west.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.10.b] As plates carried Ireland north, it passed through different climates, and the rocks that formed record them.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.10.0] Ireland has drifted north over hundreds of millions of years, which is why rocks formed in very different climates are found there.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.11.0] The Armorican (Variscan) folding, about 300 million years ago, came from the collision of Africa, part of Gondwanaland, with Europe as Pangaea came together.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.12.0] As North America and Greenland separated from Europe after Pangaea split, magma rose through cracks in north-east Ireland and formed the Antrim basalts about 60 million years ago.
[geo-plate-tectonics-s2.12.2] Alpine folding did not build Ireland's mountains; the two periods that did are Caledonian and Armorican.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.0] Limestone is calcium carbonate, which dissolves in weak acid. Carbonation: rainwater containing carbon dioxide forms weak carbonic acid, which reacts with the rock to form soluble calcium bicarbonate: CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 gives Ca(HCO3)2.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.0.2] Limestone is permeable because water moves along its bedding planes (horizontal layers) and joints (vertical cracks), not through the solid rock itself.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.b] Limestone pavement is a bare, flat limestone surface. It exists because glacial ice scraped away the soil, leaving jointed rock open to the rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.0] Stage 1: ice removes the soil cover, exposing the jointed and bedded rock (rock with horizontal layers and vertical cracks) to rain.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.1] Stage 2: carbonation, the reaction of the rock with carbonic acid in rainwater, widens the joints into grikes.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.2] Stage 3: the blocks left standing between the grikes are clints, because the rock there was not dissolved away.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.3] Burren, Co. Clare: Ireland's best-known karst landscape, of Carboniferous limestone with extensive pavement of clints and grikes. Its name comes from the Irish 'Boireann', meaning rocky place.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.1.4] Mullaghmore is a Burren hill with folded, terraced limestone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.0] Grikes: deep, narrow vertical cracks, because carbonation widens the rock along joints, where water collects.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.1] Clints: flat-topped blocks of limestone between grikes, so together they look like paving.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.2] Karren (runnels): small grooves cut into clint surfaces because rainwater running across them slowly dissolves the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.3] Solution hollows (kamenitza or solution pans): small enclosed hollows on clint surfaces where rainwater collects and dissolves the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.2.4] Diagram: plan of clints and grikes; cross-section showing bedding planes and joints in the rock.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.3.b] Once water can sink into limestone, the surface develops holes and hollows where streams vanish or the ground dissolves or collapses.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.b] Water that sinks underground can also come back, and in Irish limestone the water table rising and falling makes lakes appear and vanish.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.0] Turlough: a seasonal lake in a hollow in limestone. It fills in winter when the water table rises and drains through the floor in summer.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.4.1] Turloughs are common in the Burren and east Co. Galway. Rahasane turlough, near Craughwell, Co. Galway, is one of the largest.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.0] Caves and caverns: water dissolves passages along bedding planes and joints, and underground rivers enlarge them by erosion (wearing away rock). A cavern is a very large cave.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.1] Caves occur where limestone is dissolved, for example: Aillwee Cave, Co. Clare (Burren); Mitchelstown Cave, Co. Tipperary.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.5.2] Doolin Cave, Co. Clare, holds the Great Stalactite, about 7.3 m long, one of the longest free-hanging stalactites in the world.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.6.5] Diagram: labelled cross-section of a cave showing stalactites, stalagmites, a pillar, a swallow hole and an underground river.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.b] Ireland has three named karst settings in the syllabus. Besides the Burren, they show streams sinking into limestone and water travelling through it unseen.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.8.1] Streams flowing off the impermeable shale and sandstone of Cuilcagh sink into swallow holes where they reach the limestone, flow through the caves and re-emerge as the Cladagh River.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.9.b] Karst occurs wherever limestone is dissolved, and each international setting is known for a different feature.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.9.3] Jamaica: Cockpit Country has cockpit karst, steep conical hills separated by deep, star-shaped hollows.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.b] Granite is hard, crystalline and resistant to erosion, and its landscapes reflect how it drains and weathers. Unlike limestone, it keeps water on the surface.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.0] Granite is impermeable: water cannot enter it and runs off, so it has many surface streams and boggy ground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.3] The Leinster batholith, the largest granite mass in Ireland and Britain, forms the Wicklow Mountains.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.4] Its rounded, dome-shaped uplands are worn down from rock that once covered the granite.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.5] Impermeable granite with high rainfall gives thin, acidic soils and blanket bog (a deep cover of wet peat), e.g. on the Sally Gap area of Co. Wicklow.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.10.6] Ice cut deep valleys into the granite. There is more on this in Glacial processes, patterns and associated landforms (Glaciation).
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.0] Tor: a block of granite standing on a hilltop.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.1] Two-stage formation, put forward by D. L. Linton in 1955. Stage 1: under warm, wet conditions, deep chemical weathering (hydrolysis) attacked the granite along its joints, rotting it most where the joints were closest together.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.11.5] Dartmoor, Devon, England: a granite upland with tors such as Haytor and Hound Tor, clitter on its slopes and blanket bog.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.b] Rock type changes across Ireland, and the landscape changes with it. Resistant rock stands up, weaker rock is worn low, and permeability decides whether water flows on the surface or underground.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.0] Ireland's saucer shape: a low central lowland of Carboniferous limestone is ringed by uplands of harder, more resistant rocks such as granite, quartzite and sandstone.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.1] Differential erosion (different rocks wearing away at different rates): resistant rock forms uplands, headlands and peaks, while weaker rock forms lowlands, valleys and bays.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.3] Rock type and drainage: impermeable granite and shale give many surface streams and bog. Permeable limestone takes drainage underground, leaving few rivers and dry surfaces.
[geo-rocks-weathering-s4.12.4] Lough Corrib roughly divides the limestone lowland of east Co. Galway from the granite and metamorphic uplands of Connemara to the west.
