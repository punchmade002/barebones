QUESTION geography-pp-2025-higher-q113 (LC Geography Higher 2025 — Part Two — Q13, 80 marks)

PART "13" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Social and political decisions made in one country shape the lives of refugees, the direction of migration and the protection of human rights in countries far away.
Aspect 1 – Economic and political refugees:
Political refugees flee persecution or war that results from political decisions, and economic refugees flee poverty that is often caused by decisions on trade, debt or conflict elsewhere.
Under the 1951 UN Refugee Convention a refugee is a person outside their country with a well-founded fear of persecution, so the legal definition itself is a political decision.
More than 120 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide in 2024, according to UNHCR.
In Syria, protests in March 2011 were met with force by the government of Bashar al-Assad, which led to civil war.
Over half of Syria's pre-war population of about 22 million were displaced, including over 6 million refugees abroad.
Turkey hosted over 3 million Syrian refugees, which shows that most refugees stay in neighbouring countries and not in Europe.
Germany's decision in August 2015 to admit refugees and Hungary's decision to build a border fence with Serbia show how different political decisions in receiving countries shaped refugee journeys.
The EU–Turkey statement of March 2016 returned irregular migrants from the Greek islands to Turkey, and the EU promised €6 billion for refugees in Turkey.
In March 2022 the EU activated its Temporary Protection Directive for the first time, giving Ukrainians fleeing Russia's invasion immediate rights to live and work.
Aspect 2 – Migration patterns:
Migration patterns are the flows of people between regions, and they are shaped by government decisions on borders, labour markets and rights.
About 281 million international migrants were counted in 2020, which is about 3.6% of the world's population.
Most international migrants move within their own region, and the main corridors run from Mexico to the USA, from North and West Africa to Europe and from South Asia to the Gulf.
On 1 May 2004 Ireland, the UK and Sweden opened their labour markets at once to workers from the new EU member states, which drew large flows from Eastern to Western Europe.
Migrant workers from India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Pakistan and the Philippines make up most of the population and workforce of Qatar and the United Arab Emirates.
The kafala system in Gulf states ties a migrant worker's legal status to an employer-sponsor, which controls who migrates and for how long.
On the Atlantic route, migrants from Senegal, Mauritania and Mali travel by boat to Spain's Canary Islands, and arrivals reached a record in 2024.
Remittances to low- and middle-income countries were about $650 billion in 2023, more than foreign direct investment and official aid to those countries combined, excluding China.
Source regions lose young workers and doctors and nurses through brain drain, while receiving regions fill jobs in health care, construction and farming and offset ageing populations.
Aspect 3 – Human rights issues:
Political decisions can protect or violate human rights, and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 10 December 1948 gives every person the right to seek asylum from persecution.
Non-refoulement, set out in Article 33 of the 1951 Refugee Convention, says refugees must not be returned to a country where they face danger.
Detention, pushbacks and deaths at sea, such as the Lampedusa shipwreck of October 2013 that killed over 360 people, raise human rights concerns for refugees.
The Rohingya of Myanmar were left stateless by the 1982 Citizenship Law, which did not recognise them as one of the country's national groups.
About 740,000 Rohingya fled into Bangladesh after a military campaign began in August 2017.
Rana Plaza near Dhaka, whose factories supplied Western brands, collapsed on 24 April 2013 and killed about 1,130 garment workers, which shows how the sourcing decisions of rich-country firms affect workers' safety.
The Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh was signed in May 2013 in response to Rana Plaza.
In 1984 Mary Manning, a Dunnes Stores worker in Dublin, refused to handle South African grapefruit, and in 1987 the Irish government banned imports of South African fruit and vegetables in protest against apartheid.
The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive of 2024 requires large companies to address human rights risks in their supply chains.

QUESTION geography-pp-2025-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2025 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART "14" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Empowering people gives them control over resources and a voice in decisions, which is how economic growth is turned into human development, as national debt, aid programmes and gender roles show.
Aspect 1 – National debt:
Debt blocks the link between growth and development because repayments take national wealth away from the people it should serve.
Oil price rises in the 1970s gave banks large deposits of petrodollars, which they lent to developing countries.
Interest rates soared about 1980 and commodity prices fell, so loans became far harder to repay, and Mexico announced in August 1982 that it could not pay its debts.
Debt repayments take up government revenue, so less is spent on health, schools and water, which keeps productivity and incomes low and leads to more borrowing in a cycle of poverty.
Structural adjustment programmes attached to IMF and World Bank loans in the 1980s and 1990s required governments to cut public spending, privatise state firms and introduce fees for schools and clinics.
The poorest people and women bore most of the cost of these cuts.
The Jubilee 2000 campaign and the HIPC Initiative of 1996 won debt relief for heavily indebted poor countries, with the savings required to go on poverty reduction.
Uganda introduced Universal Primary Education in 1997 and in 1998 became the first country to receive HIPC relief, and it spent the savings on schools, health and water.
Debt is rising again, as Sri Lanka defaulted in 2022 and shortages of fuel, food and medicine led to mass protests.
Aspect 2 – Aid programmes:
Aid can empower people when it is targeted, untied and locally owned, but it can also create dependency.
Emergency aid saves lives after famine, floods or conflict, while long-term development aid funds water, health, education and farming.
The UN target of 1970 asks rich countries to give 0.7% of gross national income as aid, and Norway, Sweden, Luxembourg and Denmark have met it while Ireland has not.
Tied aid must be spent on goods or services from the donor country, so it returns money to donor firms.
UK aid for the Pergau Dam in Malaysia was linked to an arms deal, and the English High Court ruled in 1994 that it was unlawful.
Aid can harm recipients, as in Haiti, where a tariff cut on rice in the mid-1990s under pressure from lenders let cheap subsidised US rice flood in and ruin many Haitian rice farmers.
Irish Aid began its bilateral programme in 1974, and its aid is untied, with partner countries mostly in sub-Saharan Africa such as Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia.
Concern Worldwide, founded in 1968 in response to famine in Biafra, and Trócaire, founded in 1973, deliver emergency relief and long-term development.
NGOs work close to communities and empower them through co-operatives, women's groups and microcredit.
Aspect 3 – Differing gender roles in society:
Gender roles are the tasks and behaviour a society expects of men and women, and empowering women links economic growth to human development.
The ILO found in 2018 that women perform about three-quarters of all unpaid care work worldwide.
In many developing countries women grow much of the food and collect water and fuel, while men control cash crops and income.
Women make up about 43% of the agricultural labour force in developing countries but fewer than one in five agricultural landholders, so they struggle to get credit.
Girls receive less schooling because of early marriage, school fees and household chores.
After the Taliban returned to power in August 2021, Afghanistan banned girls from secondary school in 2022 and women from universities in December 2022.
Educated, earning women tend to have fewer, healthier and better-educated children, and household income spent by women is more likely to go on food, health and schooling.
Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, founded as a bank by Muhammad Yunus in 1983, lends small sums without collateral mostly to poor women in groups, and it shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006.
Rwanda shows empowerment in politics, as women have held over 60% of seats in its lower house since 2013.

QUESTION geography-pp-2025-higher-q115 (LC Geography Higher 2025 — Part Two — Q15, 80 marks)

PART "15" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sustainable development is a model for the future because it balances economic growth with the protection of resources for future generations, as the concept, the example of Costa Rica and fair trade show.
Aspect 1 – The concept of sustainable development:
Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, as defined by the Brundtland Report of 1987.
It rests on three pillars, environmental, economic and social, which must be balanced against each other.
The Limits to Growth, a report for the Club of Rome in 1972, warned that unchecked population and economic growth would exhaust resources within about a century.
The Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in 1992 produced Agenda 21 and the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change.
The 17 Sustainable Development Goals adopted in September 2015 set a target year of 2030 and followed the eight Millennium Development Goals of 2000 to 2015.
It is a model for the future because resources are finite and growth that destroys the resources it depends on cannot last.
The poorest people suffer first when resources are degraded, so sustainability also promotes fairness.
The model is hard to apply because it is vague and open to greenwashing, where firms and governments claim sustainability without real change.
Poorer countries argue that rich countries grew without limits and now ask them to slow down.
Aspect 2 – Resource use in Costa Rica and Ireland:
Sustainable resource use means using renewable resources no faster than they regenerate, which is the sustainable yield.
In the 1970s and 1980s Costa Rica cleared forest for cattle pasture, and by the late 1980s forest covered only about a fifth of the country.
Its Payment for Environmental Services programme, started in 1997 and run by FONAFIFO, pays landowners to protect and replant forest.
The programme is paid for largely by a tax on fuel.
Forest cover has since risen to over half of the country, and about a quarter of the land is in national parks and other protected areas.
Ecotourism is now one of Costa Rica's largest sources of foreign income, which links the environmental and economic pillars.
Since 2015 almost all of Costa Rica's electricity has come from renewable sources, mainly hydroelectric, geothermal and wind.
In Ireland Bord na Móna, founded in 1946, ended peat harvesting in January 2021 under its Brown to Green strategy and is rehabilitating cutaway bogs such as Lough Boora Discovery Park in Co. Offaly.
The Irish Climate Act 2021 requires a 51% cut in emissions by 2030 and net zero by 2050.
Aspect 3 – Fair trade and Kuapa Kokoo in Ghana:
Fair trade is a trading partnership based on dialogue, transparency and respect that seeks greater equity in international trade.
It addresses low and unstable commodity prices, which leave small farmers with a tiny share of the final price.
The Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production, and when the market price is higher producers receive the market price.
The Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative and spent on projects chosen democratically by its members.
Kuapa Kokoo, founded in 1993, is a Fairtrade cocoa farmers' co-operative in Ghana, the world's second-largest cocoa producer.
It part-owns Divine Chocolate, set up in Britain in 1998, so farmers share in the profits of the brand and not just the beans they sell.
Premium spending has paid for wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
Kuapa Kokoo encourages women to take leadership roles in its village societies.
Fair trade is only a tiny share of world trade and does not change trade rules such as subsidies and tariff escalation, so its impact depends on its scale.
