PART 11B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Galway:
Galway is the regional capital of the West and a high-order central place, with a university and a large hinterland across the West.
Central place theory – Description:
Central place theory was published by the German geographer Walter Christaller in 1933, based on a study of southern Germany.
A central place is a settlement that provides goods and services to people in its surrounding area.
Threshold is the minimum number of customers needed to keep a service in business.
Range is the maximum distance people will travel to obtain a service.
A service locates where its threshold falls within its range.
High-order goods such as jewellers, department stores and hospitals have a high threshold and are found only in large centres.
Central place theory – Explanation:
The theory assumes a flat, uniform plain with an evenly spread population, equal purchasing power and equal ease of transport in all directions.
Market areas are hexagonal, because circles overlap or leave gaps while hexagons fit together.
This produces a hierarchy with many small, closely spaced low-order centres and fewer, more widely spaced high-order centres.
Each higher-order centre also supplies all the lower-order goods.
Nesting follows k = 3 under the marketing principle, k = 4 under the transport principle and k = 7 under the administrative principle.
Diagram described in words – Hexagonal hierarchy:
A labelled diagram would show a city at the centre with its hexagonal market area, surrounded by six towns, with villages between them, each with a smaller hexagon.
Second example – Noordoostpolder:
In the Netherlands the polder Noordoostpolder, drained in 1942, was planned with Emmeloord as the central town and ten villages around it.
The villages, including Marknesse and Bant, were spaced so that farmers could reach one by bicycle, giving a planned two-level hierarchy.
Limitations – Ireland:
In parts of the Irish Midlands, market towns serving farming areas are regularly spaced, which supports the theory.
Relief, rivers and coasts are not uniform and transport routes are linear, so real patterns differ from the regular hexagons.
Dublin's primacy breaks the regular hierarchy, because the capital is about six times the size of Cork.

PART 11C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact identified – Medical technology lowers deaths:
Medical technology such as vaccines, antibiotics and clean water cut death rates far faster than birth rates fell, causing rapid population growth.
Second impact – Agricultural technology:
Agricultural technology such as the Green Revolution increased food supply and the number of people the land can support.
Example of location – India:
India is a country where technology has strongly affected population change.
Examination – Medical technology:
Penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928, allowed deadly infections to be treated.
Smallpox was declared eradicated by the World Health Organization in 1980, through vaccination.
Death rates fell while birth rates stayed high, which is stage 2 of the Demographic Transition Model, the population explosion.
As a result India's population grew rapidly in the 20th century.
Examination – The Green Revolution:
From the mid-1960s high-yield wheat bred in Mexico by Norman Borlaug was introduced in India under M. S. Swaminathan.
The core areas were Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh.
High-yield seeds, irrigation and fertilisers raised yields.
Food-grain output rose from about 51 million tonnes in 1950–51 to about 330 million tonnes in 2022–23.
This raised the carrying capacity of the land, so far more people could be fed and the risk of famine fell.
There were side effects, including falling water tables, soil degradation and debt among small farmers, which limit further growth.
Examination – Contraceptive technology:
Contraceptive technology lowered birth rates.
India's total fertility rate fell from about 6 children per woman in the 1950s to about 2.0 in 2019–21, so population growth is slowing.
Contrast – Lack of technology:
Where technology is lacking, as with subsistence farming in the Sahel, carrying capacity stays low.

PART 12C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Dublin:
Dublin, with about 1.26 million people in its city and suburbs in Census 2022, has grown outwards and strongly affected the surrounding counties.
The Greater Dublin Area of Dublin, Meath, Kildare and Wicklow has about 2.1 million people, about 40% of the State's population.
Impact 1 – Urban sprawl onto rural land:
Sprawl has extended Dublin's low-density housing into the commuter counties beyond the city boundary.
Causes include high prices in the inner city, demand for houses with gardens, and car ownership and motorways such as the M50, built in stages from 1990.
Farmland and habitats are lost as greenfield land is developed.
A Government survey in 2010 found almost 3,000 unfinished housing estates, known as ghost estates, after the 2008 property crash.
Impact 2 – Growth of commuter towns:
Towns such as Navan, Drogheda, Naas and Newbridge have become commuter towns.
Many residents of these dormitory towns sleep there and work in Dublin, leaving little local activity.
Services such as roads, water and schools are costly to provide when spread thinly over a large area.
The new towns of Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown, planned in the Wright Report of 1967, were built before services, jobs and transport were in place.
Impact 3 – Traffic and congestion:
Long commutes by car bring heavy traffic and congestion on the radial routes into the city.
Public transport arrived late, as the Luas Red Line to Tallaght opened only in 2004, decades after the first homes.
Car dependence raises carbon emissions and air pollution in the surrounding areas.
Impact 4 – Retail and business change:
Suburban shopping centres beside the M50, such as Blanchardstown Centre in 1996, Liffey Valley in 1998 and Dundrum Town Centre in 2005, draw trade from the city centre, leaving vacant units.
Business parks near the M50 and N7, such as Grange Castle and Citywest, bring jobs to the surrounding area.
Response – Compact growth:
The National Planning Framework aims for 40% of new homes nationally to be within the built-up footprint of existing settlements, which would reduce sprawl.

[geo-urban-s2.1.b] Dispersed settlement is the dominant rural pattern in Ireland. It exists for farming, historical and physical reasons.
[geo-urban-s2.1.0] Irish farms are family-owned, and each family lives on its own holding (its own farm), so the houses end up spread out across the countryside.
[geo-urban-s2.1.1] The Land Commission was set up in 1881 under the Land Law (Ireland) Act 1881. After the Land Acts, consolidation of holdings (joining scattered plots into one compact farm) moved houses out of clusters onto individual farms.
[geo-urban-s2.1.3] Fertile, well-drained lowland with plentiful water lets houses be built almost anywhere, because no site is much better than another.
[geo-urban-s2.2.0] Route focus: a crossroads where roads meet brings travellers and trade, so buildings gather there.
[geo-urban-s2.2.6] A harbour is the reason for a fishing village, because the boats and the catch are landed there.
[geo-urban-s2.2.7] Services such as a shop, pub, school and Garda station (police station) gather in the cluster, which then attracts further settlement.
[geo-urban-s2.3.1] Rundale was a landholding system in which families worked land in common, in scattered strips of infield (the better land near the houses) and outfield (poorer land further out). It explains the clachan; it is not a settlement pattern.
[geo-urban-s2.3.5] The Land Commission also moved houses onto individual farms, which is how dispersed settlement replaced the clachans.
[geo-urban-s2.3.6] A few clachans survive in the west.
[geo-urban-s2.4.b] Linear settlement is the third pattern. Houses line up along a feature that offers a route or dry, firm ground.
[geo-urban-s2.4.1] Linear settlement avoids marshy or steep land, because it is hard and costly to build on.
[geo-urban-s2.5.0] Uplands are steep, exposed and have poor soils, so they are hard to build on and farm.
[geo-urban-s2.5.1] Bogs are wet, poorly drained land, so houses cannot be built safely on them.
[geo-urban-s2.5.3] Lakes take up land that could hold houses.
[geo-urban-s2.5.4] Forestry plantations are large areas of planted trees with no houses.
[geo-urban-s2.7.b] Ribbon development is houses built one plot deep along the roads leading out of towns and villages, and along rural roads generally. Several pressures made roadsides the favoured building sites.
[geo-urban-s2.7.1] Road frontage gives access to services such as electricity, water mains and phone or broadband lines, which already run along the road.
[geo-urban-s2.7.2] Farmers sold roadside sites, because these were the easiest plots to sell.
