PART 4B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Region 1 – Paris Basin, a core region in France:
Transport is vital to the economic development of the Paris Basin because it links the largest market and labour force in France to suppliers and customers.
The road and rail network is radial, centred on Paris, so the national transport network is focused on the region.
The Boulevard Périphérique ring road, completed in 1973, carries traffic around the city and eases congestion.
The TGV's first high-speed line, Paris–Lyon, opened in 1981 and speeds up the movement of people and business.
The Channel Tunnel opened in 1994 and Eurostar links Paris with London, which improves access to EU markets.
The RER regional express network brings workers from across the city region to jobs in Paris.
Charles de Gaulle Airport, opened in 1974, is France's busiest airport and attracts head offices and services.
The Port of Paris at Gennevilliers is one of Europe's largest river ports, and the Seine lets bulk goods reach factories by water.
Good transport lowers the cost of getting inputs in and products out, so it reinforces industry along the Lower Seine and the concentration of head offices in the region.
Region 2 – Mezzogiorno, a peripheral region in Italy:
Poor transport keeps the Mezzogiorno remote from European markets and the industrial North, which raises the cost of moving goods and limits economic development.
The Autostrada del Sole, completed in 1964, links Milan, Rome and Naples and opened the South to investment and tourists.
The A2 Salerno–Reggio Calabria motorway has been notorious for decades of slow, costly upgrading, which held back the south-west.
High-speed rail reaches Naples and Salerno but the Naples–Bari line is still being upgraded, so rail links in the South remain limited.
Gioia Tauro in Calabria is Italy's largest container transhipment port and brings trade and port jobs.
Ferries cross the Strait of Messina and sail from Bari and Brindisi to Greece and Albania, which links the islands and the east to other markets.
Mountainous relief makes road and rail building difficult and costly in the interior.
Better transport brings investors and tourists, so poor links keep the region less developed than the core.

PART 4C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Culture as a criterion:
A cultural region is an area where people share cultural traits such as language or religion that set them apart from neighbouring areas.
Cultural regions rarely match political borders and their edges are usually transition zones.
Example 1 – The Gaeltacht in Ireland:
The Gaeltacht is the officially recognised area where Irish is, or was, the community language.
It is not one continuous region but a set of scattered pockets, mainly along the western seaboard in seven counties.
The main areas are Gaoth Dobhair in Co. Donegal, Conamara and the Aran Islands in Co. Galway, Corca Dhuibhne in Co. Kerry and Múscraí in Co. Cork.
Irish survived in remote, mountainous areas with poor farmland and poor communications because they had least contact with English-speaking trade, towns and administration.
Udarás na Gaeltachta, set up in 1980, promotes employment and the language in the Gaeltacht.
Raidió na Gaeltachta and TG4 broadcast in Irish, and Irish-medium schools and summer colleges sustain the language.
Threats include out-migration of young people, in-migration of English speakers and holiday homes, and English-language media.
Example 2 – Language regions of Belgium:
Belgium is split by a language border running east to west, creating separate language areas.
Flanders in the north is Dutch-speaking and Wallonia in the south is French-speaking.
A small German-speaking community lives in the east around Eupen.
The Brussels-Capital Region is officially bilingual in French and Dutch although it lies inside Flanders.
Belgium became a federal state in 1993 with three regions and three language communities, each with its own parliament.
Schools, media and political parties are divided by language, and the university at Leuven split in 1968 with its French-speaking section moving to Louvain-la-Neuve.
The language divide matches an economic divide because Flanders, with the port of Antwerp, became the wealthier region.

PART 5B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Factor 1 – Raw materials:
The availability of raw materials has strongly influenced where secondary activity developed in India.
Coal, iron ore, limestone and manganese of the Chota Nagpur Plateau support iron and steel.
The Tata Iron and Steel Company, founded in 1907 at Jamshedpur in Jharkhand, was India's first large steel works and was sited on Chota Nagpur coal and iron ore.
Locating beside bulky raw materials cuts transport costs, so heavy industry clustered in the Chota Nagpur and Damodar valley region.
Cotton grown on black soils supports cotton textiles in Mumbai and Ahmedabad.
Jute grown in the Ganges delta supports jute mills along the Hooghly near Kolkata.
Factor 2 – Government policy:
Government policy has changed both the scale and the location of secondary activity in India.
After 1947 industrialisation was state-led, and the state built steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur.
The First Five-Year Plan began in 1951 and imports were protected from foreign competition.
The Licence Raj required permits to start or expand a factory, which made industry inefficient.
Reform in 1991 removed most licences, cut tariffs and opened India to foreign investment, so manufacturing grew rapidly.
Special Economic Zones, created by an Act in 2005, offer incentives that steer firms to chosen sites.
Make in India, launched in 2014, and production-linked incentive programmes from 2020 pay subsidies for electronics, cars and pharmaceuticals.
Maruti Suzuki, a joint venture with Suzuki of Japan, has plants at Gurugram and Manesar in Haryana.
Foxconn assembles Apple iPhones at Sriperumbudur near Chennai.

PART 5C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Region studied – Mezzogiorno, southern Italy:
Population in the Mezzogiorno is concentrated on the coastal lowlands and in cities and thins out in the interior uplands.
Campania has about 400 people per km², one of Italy's most densely populated regions.
Basilicata in the interior has about 55 people per km² and only about 540,000 people.
Factor 1 – Relief:
Mountains and hills cover most of the region and the Apennines form its spine, so the lowlands are small and mostly coastal.
Steep slopes limit farming and settlement to small areas and push villages onto hilltops.
Lowlands such as the Campanian plain around Naples, the Tavoliere di Puglia around Foggia and the plain of Catania in Sicily attract dense settlement because they are flat and easy to farm and build on.
Factor 2 – Soils and climate:
Fertile volcanic soils on Vesuvius and Etna support vineyards and orchards and attract people despite the volcanic danger.
Alluvial soils on the lowlands are used for intensive farming and support larger populations.
Thin, stony soils and dry uplands support few people, as in Basilicata.
Hot, dry summers and under 600 mm of rain on parts of Puglia and Sicily limit farming without irrigation.
Factor 3 – Cities and jobs:
Cities offer jobs in services and industry, drawing people from the interior.
Naples is the South's largest city, with about 900,000 people in the city and about 3 million in the metropolitan city.
Naples grew because of its port on the Bay of Naples, its role as capital of the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies until 1861 and its fertile volcanic hinterland.
Palermo in Sicily has about 630,000 people.
Factor 4 – Migration and ageing:
Emigration to the USA and Argentina from the late 19th century, and later to northern Italy and northern Europe, drained the interior.
Out-migration continues as young graduates leave, so inland villages are ageing and depopulating.
Very low fertility, about 1.2 children per woman in Italy, reinforces the decline of inland villages.
