QUESTION geography-pp-2018-higher-q114 (LC Geography Higher 2018 — Part Two — Q14, 80 marks)

PART "14" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Views of development have changed from growth-centred modernisation to dependency, human development and sustainability, and each view gives a different explanation of why some countries are underdeveloped.
Aspect 1 – Modernisation theory:
Modernisation theory holds that all societies pass along one path from traditional to modern, as Western Europe and North America did, so poor countries are simply behind and need investment, technology and industry to catch up.
Walt Rostow set out five stages of growth in 1960, running from traditional society through preconditions for take-off, take-off and drive to maturity to the age of high mass consumption.
At take-off, investment rises to over 10% of national income and a few leading industries grow fast.
South Korea seems to fit the model, since export-led industrialisation under Park Chung-hee and firms such as Samsung and Hyundai lifted it from poverty after the Korean War of 1950–53 to developed-country status by 2021.
Ireland can be read in the same way, with the First Programme for Economic Expansion in 1958, EEC membership in 1973 and the Celtic Tiger from the mid-1990s to 2007.
The model is criticised as Eurocentric and linear, because Zimbabwe and Syria show that countries can go backwards, and the Irish crash of 2008 reversed earlier gains.
It also ignores colonialism and unequal trade as causes of poverty and assumes that wealth will trickle down to the poor, which often does not happen.
Aspect 2 – Dependency theory:
Dependency theory argues that poor countries are poor because of how they were drawn into the world economy, not because of their own backwardness.
Rich core countries develop by draining wealth from poor periphery countries, so development and underdevelopment are two sides of one process.
The theory began in Latin America, where Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer argued in 1950 that the terms of trade of primary-product exporters tend to decline, and Andre Gunder Frank wrote The Development of Underdevelopment in 1966.
Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grow over half of the world's cocoa, mostly exported as beans, while most of the value is added in rich countries.
Zambia depends on copper for most of its exports, which leaves it exposed to price changes.
Other mechanisms include tariff escalation on processed goods, profit repatriation by multinationals, debt repayments and brain drain.
Critics point out that the Asian Tigers and China developed through trade with the core, and that countries which cut their links with the world economy stayed poor.
Aspect 3 – Basic needs and human development:
The basic needs approach of the 1970s put food, water, shelter, health and education ahead of growth in GNP, and the ILO World Employment Conference of 1976 placed it at the centre of policy.
E. F. Schumacher's Small Is Beautiful of 1973 argued for small-scale appropriate technology that local people can afford, use and repair.
Amartya Sen argued in Development as Freedom in 1999 that development means widening people's choices and capabilities.
The first Human Development Report appeared in 1990, and the Human Development Index judges progress by people's lives and not by national output alone.
Bhutan put forward Gross National Happiness in the 1970s as a goal ahead of GNP.
The Washington Consensus of the 1980s and 1990s pushed privatisation, free trade and cuts in state spending from the top down, whereas bottom-up development puts participation, co-operatives and NGOs at the centre.
Rwanda rebuilt after the genocide of 1994 and women have held over 60% of the seats in its lower house of parliament since the 2013 election.
Aspect 4 – Sustainability and a changing view of the world:
Sustainable development, set out in the Brundtland Report of 1987, means meeting today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
The 17 Sustainable Development Goals were adopted in 2015 with a target year of 2030, following the eight Millennium Development Goals of 2000–2015.
Environmental determinism, argued by Ellen Churchill Semple and Ellsworth Huntington, once claimed that tropical climates produced poor and backward societies, and it was used to justify colonialism.
That view is now rejected because Singapore is tropical yet one of the richest countries, and Botswana became an upper-middle-income country through diamonds and stable government.
The North–South divide of the 1980 Brandt Report is outdated because many countries in the South, such as Singapore and South Korea, are high-income, and China has been the second-largest economy since 2010.
Eurocentric thinking, such as the Mercator projection that makes Africa look similar in size to Greenland although it is about 14 times larger, shaped how the world viewed development.
Media images of famine in Africa, such as the 1984–85 Ethiopian famine, fixed a view of the continent as helpless, which counter-images such as Kenya's M-Pesa mobile money service launched in 2007 now challenge.

PART "15" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Sustainability is a model for future development because it meets the needs of the present without harming future generations, as resource use, fair trade and self-reliance show.
Aspect 1 – Resource use:
Renewable resources should be used no faster than they regenerate, which is known as the sustainable yield.
Costa Rica shows how a country can reverse resource loss, because by the late 1980s forest covered only about a fifth of the country after clearance for cattle pasture.
Its Payment for Environmental Services programme, started in 1997 and run by FONAFIFO, pays landowners to protect and replant forest.
The programme is paid for largely by a tax on fuel.
Forest cover has since risen to over half of the country, and about a quarter of the land is in protected areas.
Since 2015 almost all of Costa Rica's electricity has come from renewable sources, mainly hydroelectric, with geothermal and wind.
Ecotourism is one of Costa Rica's largest sources of foreign income.
In Ireland Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in January 2021 and is rehabilitating and rewetting cutaway bogs, as at Lough Boora Discovery Park in Co. Offaly.
The cod moratorium off Newfoundland on 2 July 1992 cost about 30,000 jobs and is the warning for overfishing, and the Common Fisheries Policy reform of 2013 introduced maximum sustainable yield and a landing obligation.
Limits remain, since Costa Rica's transport still runs mainly on imported oil.
Aspect 2 – Fair trade:
Commodity prices are low and unstable, and small farmers receive a tiny share of the final price because middlemen take a large share.
Fair trade is a trading partnership based on dialogue, transparency and respect that seeks greater equity in international trade.
The Fairtrade Minimum Price is a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production, and the Fairtrade Premium is an extra sum paid to the co-operative.
The premium is spent on projects the members choose democratically, such as schools, clinics and clean water.
Kuapa Kokoo in Ghana, the world's second-largest cocoa producer, is a farmers' co-operative founded in 1993 that part-owns Divine Chocolate, so farmers share in profits as well as selling beans.
Kuapa Kokoo's premium spending has included wells for clean water, school buildings and mobile health clinics.
The Oromia Coffee Farmers Co-operative Union in Ethiopia, founded in 1999, sells directly to buyers and cuts out middlemen.
Labour standards ban forced and child labour, and environmental standards restrict pesticides and protect water and forests.
Fair trade is only a tiny share of world trade, and it does not change trade rules such as subsidies and tariff escalation.
Benefits go mainly to co-operative members rather than hired labourers, and certification costs exclude the poorest farmers.
Aspect 3 – Self-reliance:
Self-reliance means communities and countries meet their own needs with their own resources, skills and decisions, which reduces dependence on aid, loans and imports.
Julius Nyerere's Arusha Declaration of 1967 set out socialism and self-reliance in Tanzania, known as Ujamaa or familyhood.
Ujamaa villages and collective farming were created, with education for self-reliance.
Tanzania achieved national unity through the promotion of Kiswahili, and literacy and school enrolment rose.
Villagisation from 1973 to 1976 moved millions of people, often by force, and food and export output fell.
Nyerere stepped down in 1985 and Tanzania agreed an IMF programme in 1986, which showed that complete self-reliance cuts countries off from trade, investment and technology.
The Green Belt Movement, founded in Kenya in 1977 by Wangari Maathai, has seen women's groups plant tens of millions of trees for fuel, soil and income.
Maathai won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004, the first African woman to do so.
In Yatenga, Burkina Faso, farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo use zaï pits and stone lines to restore degraded farmland, and farmer-managed natural regeneration in Niger has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s.
In Ireland Muintir na Tíre was founded in 1937 by Fr John Hayes in Co. Tipperary, and co-operative creameries and credit unions show the same self-help.

MODEL ANSWER: Soil characteristics such as colour, depth, humus content, acidity and fertility are shaped by processes such as podzolisation, laterisation and calcification, which depend on climate, parent material and vegetation.
Aspect 1 – Podzolisation:
Podzolisation is an intense form of leaching that occurs in a cool, wet climate where precipitation is well above evapotranspiration.
It occurs on acid parent material such as granite or sandstone, under coniferous forest or heath, as on the granite Wicklow Mountains in Ireland.
Acid litter releases organic acids into the percolating water, which builds up a thick, acidic mor humus layer on the surface.
These acids combine with iron and aluminium by chelation and carry them down the profile along with bases and humus.
The A/E horizon is left as a bleached, ash-grey, sandy layer of quartz.
Iron, aluminium and humus are deposited in the B horizon, which turns dark reddish-brown.
A thin, hard iron pan may form in the B horizon, which blocks drainage and roots so that water lies above it and peat may form on top.
The resulting podzol is strongly acidic, at about pH 4 to 4.5, and infertile, with sharply defined horizons.
Podzols lie in the boreal forest belt of northern Canada, Scandinavia, Finland and Siberia.
Their poor fertility limits their use to rough grazing for sheep and forestry, and deep ploughing and heavy liming are needed to improve them.
Aspect 2 – Laterisation:
Laterisation occurs in hot, wet tropical climates such as the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia.
High temperatures and heavy rain cause intense chemical weathering to great depth, so the soils are often many metres deep.
Silica and bases are leached out because silica is soluble in warm water under these conditions.
Insoluble iron and aluminium oxides called sesquioxides are left behind, which gives the soil a deep red or yellow colour.
Litter decomposes so quickly that very little humus accumulates, so the soil has only a thin O layer.
Nearly all nutrients are held in the living vegetation, so the latosol is acidic, clay-rich and infertile despite the lush forest above it.
Where forest is removed and the soil dries out, the iron-rich layer can harden into a brick-like crust called laterite.
Extreme laterisation of aluminium-rich rock forms bauxite, which is mined in Guinea, Jamaica and north Queensland in Australia.
When the forest is cleared, the soil loses fertility within a few years and erodes under heavy rain.
Shifting cultivation has traditionally used the soil for a few years before moving on.
Aspect 3 – Calcification:
Calcification occurs in dry continental climates where evapotranspiration roughly equals or exceeds precipitation, as on the steppes of Ukraine and southern Russia and the Prairies of North America.
The climate has hot summers, very cold winters and low rainfall of roughly 250 to 500 mm a year.
Rain penetrates only part of the way down the soil, so leaching is weak.
Calcium carbonate is dissolved and carried a short distance down, then deposited in the B horizon as white nodules or a lime-rich layer.
In dry periods capillary action draws water and calcium back up towards the surface.
Deep grass roots take up calcium and return it to the surface in litter, and dying roots supply large amounts of organic matter.
Burrowing animals such as susliks and prairie dogs mix the soil, leaving darker patches called krotovinas.
The result is the chernozem, a neutral to slightly alkaline, base-rich soil with a very thick black humus-rich A horizon commonly about 1 m deep.
Its fertility makes it some of the world's main wheat and maize land, and Ukraine has been called the breadbasket of Europe.
Ploughing the prairie soils for wheat, followed by drought in the 1930s, caused the Dust Bowl in the Great Plains of the USA.
