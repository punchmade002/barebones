QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q122 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q22, 80 marks)

PART "22" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Exchanges of water between the oceans and the atmosphere, through evaporation, condensation and precipitation, produce distinctive weather and climate regimes around the world.
Aspect 1 – Evaporation from the oceans:
Roughly 85 to 90% of global evaporation comes from the oceans, and roughly three-quarters of global precipitation falls on them, with the surplus carried over land by wind.
Evaporation is greatest over the subtropical oceans under clear skies and steady trade winds and along warm currents such as the Gulf Stream, and least over cold polar seas.
Higher temperature, drier air and wind all increase evaporation.
Evaporation exceeds precipitation over the subtropical oceans, which helps to explain their high salinity.
Evaporation takes latent heat from the sea surface, and the energy is released again on condensation, which transfers energy into the atmosphere.
Water stays in the atmosphere for only about 9 days on average, so it is recycled quickly, while ocean water changes over thousands of years.
Warm air can hold about 7% more water vapour for each 1 °C rise, so global warming intensifies the water cycle and brings heavier downpours.
Aspect 2 – Condensation and precipitation:
Condensation occurs when air cools to its dew point, and it needs condensation nuclei such as dust and sea salt.
Rising air cools adiabatically at about 10 °C per 1,000 m until it reaches dew point, and cloud forms at the condensation level.
Convectional rainfall occurs when heated ground warms the air, which rises, cools and forms cumulonimbus clouds that give afternoon showers.
Relief rainfall occurs when moist sea air is forced up over mountains, as when the Western Ghats force the Arabian Sea branch of the monsoon to rise and give heavy rain on the Konkan and Malabar coasts, while the Deccan lies in rain shadow.
Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in the Khasi Hills are among the wettest places on earth.
Frontal rainfall occurs in depressions when warm, moist air rises over cold polar air, and it is Ireland's main source of rain.
The ice-crystal process is the main way raindrops grow in mid-latitude clouds, while collision and coalescence do so in warm tropical clouds.
Aspect 3 – Equatorial, monsoon and desert regimes:
The equatorial belt has heavy rain all year, over 2,000 mm, because of low pressure, rising air, convergence at the ITCZ and high evaporation, and Manaus in the Amazon receives about 2,300 mm a year.
In the equatorial regime morning heating gives way to afternoon storms every day.
In the monsoon regime moist sea air brings a wet summer and dry land air brings a dry winter over India as the wind reverses.
Savanna and monsoon lands receive rain in summer when the ITCZ moves over them and are dry in winter.
In the Sahel rainfall is roughly 200 to 600 mm in one short wet season as the ITCZ moves north, and it varies greatly from year to year.
The subtropical high-pressure belts have sinking, warming, stable air and clear skies, which give very low rainfall and the world's great hot deserts.
The rain belts migrate north and south with the overhead sun, shifting the regimes with them.
Aspect 4 – Irish maritime regime, currents and fog:
Ireland has a cool, cloudy, wet maritime climate fed by moisture from the Atlantic and warmed by the North Atlantic Drift.
Rain falls all year with a maximum in autumn and winter, mostly from frontal rain in Atlantic depressions carried by south-westerly winds.
The west is wetter than the east, with about 1,000 to 1,250 mm across much of the west and over 2,000 mm on western mountains, compared with about 750 mm around Dublin in the rain shadow.
There are about 150 wet days a year on the east and south-east coasts and up to about 225 in parts of the west.
Warm currents add moisture and instability, while cold currents chill the air and make it stable, so rain is rare but fog is common on their coasts.
Advection fog forms on the Grand Banks off Newfoundland, one of the foggiest places on earth, where warm moist air from over the Gulf Stream crosses the cold Labrador Current.
The Atacama in Chile is the driest non-polar desert, and the Namib receives much of its moisture as fog from the cold Benguela Current.

QUESTION geography-pp-2017-higher-q123 (LC Geography Higher 2017 — Part Two — Q23, 80 marks)

PART "23" (80 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Atmosphere-ocean phenomena such as temperature, pressure, wind and humidity are measured with standard instruments on land, at sea and in the air, and these measurements are vital for forecasting, safety, economic activity and the study of climate.
Aspect 1 – Measuring temperature:
Air temperature is measured with a liquid-in-glass thermometer, in which mercury or alcohol expands and rises up a narrow tube as it warms.
A Six's maximum-minimum thermometer uses small metal indexes that are pushed up to the highest and lowest points reached, so the day's extremes are read once and the indexes are reset with a magnet.
Thermometers are placed in a Stevenson screen, a white, louvred box on legs about 1.25 m above short grass with its door facing away from the sun, so that the reading is a true shade temperature.
Automatic stations use electronic sensors whose electrical resistance changes with temperature, and a thermograph gives a continuous trace.
In the oceans, Argo floats drift at depth, rise to the surface and measure temperature and salinity down to about 2,000 m before transmitting the data by satellite, and moored buoys record sea temperature as well.
Temperature records give frost warnings that protect crops and help farmers to time sowing, spraying and silage making.
Long, consistent records such as that of Valentia Observatory in Co. Kerry, and the carbon dioxide record kept at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958, show global warming and ocean warming and are the evidence used by the IPCC.
Aspect 2 – Measuring air pressure:
A mercury barometer, invented by Torricelli in 1643, measures pressure because the weight of the atmosphere supports a column of mercury in a glass tube, which rises as pressure rises.
An aneroid barometer has a sealed, partly emptied metal capsule that is squeezed in as pressure rises and expands as it falls, and levers move a needle on a dial.
Because an aneroid barometer is portable, it is used in homes and on ships.
A barograph links an aneroid to a pen that draws a continuous record on a rotating drum, so the trend of rising or falling pressure can be seen.
Readings are corrected to mean sea level and given in hPa, with an average of about 1013 hPa, so that stations at different heights can be compared and isobars drawn.
Falling pressure warns of an approaching depression with rain and gales, while rising pressure signals improving weather, so pressure is the most important single reading in forecasting.
Pressure readings are the starting point for computer forecast models and for the storm warnings issued by Met Éireann, which save lives and property, and aviation also needs them to set aircraft altimeters.
Aspect 3 – Measuring wind:
Wind speed is measured with a cup anemometer, in which three or four cups on arms spin faster as the wind rises, and the turns are counted electrically and converted to speed.
Sonic anemometers have no moving parts and are used on some modern stations.
The standard height for measuring surface wind is 10 m above open ground, away from buildings and trees, so that readings can be compared worldwide.
Wind direction is measured with a wind vane, whose large tail is pushed downwind so that its pointer faces the direction the wind blows from, and windsocks show direction and strength at airports.
Wind measurements are used to site wind farms and to predict their output, which is important for energy supply.
Buoys of the Irish Marine Data Buoy Observation Network, run by the Marine Institute with Met Éireann, record wind and wave height off the Irish coast, so that shipping and fishing receive sea area forecasts and gale warnings.
Airports need accurate wind and gust readings for safe take-off and landing, and tropical cyclones are tracked using wind and pressure data.
Aspect 4 – Measuring humidity:
Humidity is measured with a wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer, which has two thermometers side by side, and the wet bulb is wrapped in muslin kept wet from a water reservoir.
Evaporation from the muslin cools the wet bulb, so it reads lower than the dry bulb.
The drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the bigger the difference, which is called the wet bulb depression, and if both readings are the same the air is saturated.
Relative humidity, given as a percentage, is read from tables using the dry bulb reading and the difference between the two readings.
A hair hygrometer uses human hair, which lengthens in humid air and shortens in dry air, to move a pointer, and automatic stations use electronic capacitance sensors.
Humidity readings show how close the air is to saturation, so they help forecasters to predict cloud, fog, rain and frost.
Balloons carry radiosondes that measure humidity, temperature and pressure in the upper air and radio them back, and farmers and airports use the resulting forecasts of fog and wet weather to plan their work.

MODEL ANSWER: The cool temperate oceanic climate of north-west Europe, as found in Ireland, has specific characteristics of temperature, precipitation, wind and changeable weather that are accounted for by its position on a west coast in the path of the westerlies.
Aspect 1 – Temperature:
This climate lies on the west coasts of continents in the path of the westerlies, including Ireland, Britain, north-west France and coastal Norway.
Winters are mild, with Ireland's mean January temperature about 4 to 7 °C, and frost and snow are infrequent on low ground.
Summers are cool, with a mean July temperature of about 14 to 16 °C, so the annual range is only about 10 °C.
The North Atlantic Drift, the extension of the Gulf Stream, warms the winter air and seas around Ireland.
The sea has a high specific heat capacity, so it heats and cools slowly and moderates the seasons.
By contrast, Verkhoyansk in the continental interior of Siberia has an annual range of over 60 °C, because it is far from the moderating influence of the sea.
The prevailing south-westerly winds blow from the warm ocean over the land, so the moderating influence of the sea reaches well inland.
Aspect 2 – Precipitation:
Rain falls in all months, with a maximum in autumn and winter, and there are many wet days, about 150 a year in the east of Ireland and up to about 225 in parts of the west.
Annual rainfall is about 750 mm in the driest east around Dublin, about 1,000 to 1,250 mm across much of the west, and over 2,000 mm on many western mountains.
Most rain is frontal, because depressions form frequently on the polar front and are steered across Ireland by the jet stream.
At the fronts of a depression, warm moist air rises over colder air, cools and condenses to give cloud and rain.
Relief rain falls on west-facing mountains, as moist Atlantic air is forced to rise, cool and condense.
The air is heavily laden with moisture, because it has crossed the warm Atlantic and picked up water vapour by evaporation.
Rainfall totals decrease from west to east, because the air loses much of its moisture over the western mountains and the east lies in their rain shadow.
Aspect 3 – Winds and air masses:
Prevailing winds are south-westerly and bring moist Atlantic air.
These winds are part of the westerlies, which blow from the subtropical high pressure belt towards the low pressure of the polar front and are deflected to the right by the Coriolis force.
Tropical maritime and polar maritime air masses are the most common, and both are humid because they have crossed the Atlantic.
Tropical maritime air brings mild, damp and cloudy weather, while polar maritime air brings cooler, showery weather.
Occasionally cold, dry polar continental air from the east, linked to the Siberian High in winter, brings frost and snow.
The west coast is exposed to Atlantic gales, and Storm Éowyn in January 2025 caused widespread damage and power cuts in the west of Ireland.
The strongest winds come in winter, when depressions steered by the jet stream are deepest and most frequent.
Aspect 4 – Pressure and changeable weather:
Ireland lies in the path of depressions, which form frequently on the polar front and cross the Atlantic towards north-west Europe.
The weather is changeable, because depressions and ridges of high pressure alternate within a few days.
Winds blow anticlockwise and inwards around a depression, with isobars close together giving strong winds, cloud and rain.
A warm front brings steady rain, and the cold front that follows brings heavier showers and then clearer, cooler air.
Occasional anticyclones bring dry, settled spells, most often in summer.
Because Ireland can experience several different air masses in one week, the weather is hard to predict.
The mild, moist climate with rare frost gives a long growing season, which supports grass and deciduous woodland on brown earths.
