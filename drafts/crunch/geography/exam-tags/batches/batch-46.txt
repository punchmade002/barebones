PART 3B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Isostasy and the Irish landscape:
Isostasy is the state of balance between the crust, which floats on the denser mantle, and the mantle beneath it.
During the ice age the weight of the ice sheets pressed the crust down, which is called isostatic depression.
When the ice melted the load was removed, and the crust slowly rose in isostatic rebound.
Water released by melting ice also raised sea level worldwide, which is eustatic change.
The height of the land relative to the sea is the combined result of isostatic and eustatic change.
Feature 1 – Raised beaches in the north:
The north of Ireland had thicker ice, so it was depressed more and has undergone more isostatic rebound.
Where land rose faster than sea level, the coast is emergent and old shore features lie above present high tide.
Raised beaches have a flat former wave-cut platform, an old cliff line, beach shingle and sometimes sea caves above the reach of the tide.
A raised beach is found at Malin Head, Inishowen, Co. Donegal.
Feature 2 – Drowned coastline in the south-west:
The south-west had thinner ice and the opposite pattern, so the sea rose relative to the land and flooded valleys and lowlands.
A ria is a drowned river valley with a V-shaped cross-section and a winding outline.
Bantry Bay and the Kenmare River are rias in Co. Cork and Co. Kerry.
A fjord is a drowned glacial trough, as at Killary Harbour on the Galway and Mayo border.
Drowned drumlins form where the sea floods the lowland between drumlins, leaving their crests as islands.
Clew Bay, Co. Mayo, and Strangford Lough, Co. Down, have drowned drumlins.
Feature 3 – River rejuvenation:
Where land rises relative to the sea, base level falls and rivers cut down again with renewed erosive power.
This rejuvenation produces knickpoints, incised meanders and river terraces.

PART 3C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named example – Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath:
Tara Mines is an underground zinc and lead mine at Navan, Co. Meath, and it is Europe's largest zinc mine.
It has been in production since 1977 and is owned by the Swedish company Boliden.
The ore lies in Carboniferous limestone, where minerals were concentrated by hot fluids moving through the rock.
Extraction and processing:
Geological surveys and test drilling located the ore body before the mine was developed.
Underground mining reaches the deep ore through shafts and tunnels, because the ore does not lie near the surface.
Zinc is used to galvanise steel, and lead is used in batteries, so the metals have a steady market.
The ore is crushed and ground, and the zinc and lead minerals are separated from the waste rock.
Impact on the rock cycle:
Mining removes rock far faster than natural weathering and erosion, so people act as an exogenic process.
It exposes fresh rock to weathering.
Fine waste called tailings is stored in tailings ponds, which is the human equivalent of deposition.
Positive impacts:
Tara Mines provides jobs, exports and local spending in Navan and Co. Meath.
The mine has operated for decades, so it is a long-term employer in the Boyne valley region.
Negative impacts:
Underground workings can cause subsidence, and spoil and tailings take up land.
Dust, noise and heavy traffic affect people living near the mine.
Old mines such as Avoca, Co. Wicklow, left acid mine drainage that pollutes rivers with heavy metals.
Management:
Environmental licensing and monitoring control waste handling and the quality of water leaving the site.
Tailings ponds are lined to stop seepage and are capped when they close, and the Lisheen mine in Co. Tipperary was rehabilitated after it closed in 2015.

[geo-glaciation-s2.5.2] Sea level rising faster than the land: the sea reaches features that were cut when it stood lower.

PART 4B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Region – Paris Basin, France:
The Paris Basin is a low sedimentary lowland in northern France, centred on Paris and the Seine, and it is a core European region.
Secondary activity examples:
The car industry is a major secondary activity, with Renault at Flins and Stellantis at Poissy, both west of Paris.
Oil refining and petrochemicals are located on the Seine estuary at Gonfreville-l'Orcher near Le Havre.
Food and drink processing, such as Champagne production at Reims and Épernay, uses local farm output.
Factor 1 – Markets:
The Paris region is the largest market in France, so firms locate close to their customers.
Île-de-France has about 12 million people, about 18 per cent of the population of France.
It produces about 30 per cent of French GDP, so incomes and spending power are high.
Car makers at Flins and Poissy sell to this large market on their doorstep, which keeps transport costs low.
The Paris region also has the largest labour force in France, so the large market draws industry and the workers it needs.
Paris holds the head offices of large firms and their finance, and its universities and research institutes supply skills and ideas.
Food processors supply the city market, and Rungis wholesale market, opened south of Paris in 1969, distributes fresh food to shops and restaurants.
The basin's central position in the EU gives firms access to a wider European market.
Factor 2 – Infrastructure:
Paris is the hub of road, rail, river and air transport in France.
The road and rail network is radial, centred on Paris, and the Boulevard Périphérique ring road was completed in 1973.
The TGV line from Paris to Lyon opened in 1981, and the Channel Tunnel opened in 1994.
Charles de Gaulle Airport, opened in 1974, is the busiest airport in France.
The Seine is navigable, so bulk goods reach factories by water, and the Port of Paris at Gennevilliers is one of Europe's largest river ports.
Le Havre is France's largest container port, and refineries and petrochemical plants lie along the Lower Seine between Rouen and Le Havre.
Good transport lowers the cost of getting raw materials in and finished goods out, which reinforces the advantage of the region.

MODEL ANSWER: Urban area – Galway city, Co. Galway, in the West:
Galway is the regional capital of the West, with about 86,000 people in Census 2022.
Factor 1 – Site and situation:
Galway grew at the mouth of the River Corrib, where it enters Galway Bay.
The sheltered harbour at the river mouth gave access to sea routes and trade.
It was founded as a Norman town by the de Burgo family in the 13th century.
Fourteen merchant families, the Tribes of Galway, governed the town and traded wine with Spain and France.
The Spanish Arch, built in 1584, survives from the town walls.
Galway surrendered to Cromwellian forces in 1652 and went into a long decline.
Factor 2 – Industry and multinationals:
Digital Equipment Corporation opened a plant in Galway in 1971 and closed its manufacturing there in 1993.
This left a skilled workforce, and Boston Scientific opened in Galway in 1994, with Medtronic at Parkmore.
Suppliers and start-ups clustered around these multinationals, so Galway is now a leading medical devices centre in Europe.
These jobs attract in-migrants and increase the demand for housing and services.
Factor 3 – Education, services and culture:
Queen's College Galway was founded in 1845 and is now the University of Galway, which supplies graduates and students.
Galway has the main shops, hospitals and services for a large hinterland across the West.
Galway was European Capital of Culture in 2020, and the Galway Arts Festival and the Galway Races at Ballybrit attract visitors.
Factor 4 – Government policy and transport:
The National Spatial Strategy of 2002 named Galway a gateway.
The National Planning Framework of 2018 aims for at least 50 per cent growth in Galway by 2040.
The M6 motorway to Dublin was completed in 2009, and the M17 and M18 to Tuam opened in 2017.
