PART "11B" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Named city – Dublin:
Dublin is Ireland's capital and its land uses are arranged into distinct zones around a central core.
Zone 1 – Central business district:
Land values are highest in the CBD because accessibility is greatest, so the most valuable sites are used for the most profitable activities.
Tall buildings with several floors of offices or shops make intensive use of the expensive land.
Grafton Street south of the Liffey and Henry Street north of it are pedestrianised shopping streets with department stores and chain stores.
Banks, offices and company head offices cluster in the centre, and O'Connell Street is the city's main street.
Few people live in the CBD, so it is crowded during the working day and quiet at night.
Zone 2 – Suburbs:
Outer suburbs and western new towns such as Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan and Blanchardstown were planned in the Wright Report of 1967.
Housing here is lower in density, mainly semi-detached houses, built later and further from the centre where land is cheaper.
Suburban shopping centres such as Dundrum Town Centre and Blanchardstown Centre serve these areas and lie beside major roads or the M50.
Zone 3 – Industrial zones:
Dublin Port is a major industrial and transport land use at the mouth of the Liffey.
Old inner-city industry moved out, and Jacob's biscuit factory moved from Bishop Street to Tallaght in the 1970s.
Modern business and industrial parks such as Grange Castle, Citywest and Park West lie near the M50 and the N7, where land is cheaper and road access is better.
Zone 4 – Open space and recreation:
Phoenix Park, established in 1662, covers about 700 hectares and is one of Europe's largest enclosed city parks.
The Dublin Mountains and Dublin Bay are further open spaces at the edge of the city.

PART "11C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Impact identified – society and culture:
Society and culture shape attitudes to family size, marriage, contraception and the role of women, so they directly affect birth rates and therefore population growth.
Named example – Ireland:
Ireland is the example studied, a country now in stage 4 of the demographic transition model.
Examination – Catholic culture and high fertility:
Catholic culture favoured marriage and large families, with little access to contraception, so fertility stayed high into the 1970s.
The total fertility rate was about 4 in the early 1970s, which gave strong natural increase, and births peaked at about 74,000 in 1980.
Heavy net emigration of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961 held back growth even though birth rates were high.
Examination – The changing role of women:
Free secondary education, introduced in 1967 by Donogh O'Malley, raised the number of girls and women in education.
The marriage bar, which forced women in the public service to resign on marriage, was removed in 1973, so more women stayed in paid work.
More women in paid work led to later marriage and later first births, which lowered fertility.
Examination – Contraception and secularisation:
The Health (Family Planning) Act 1979 legalised contraception on prescription, and access was widened in 1985 and the early 1990s.
Secularisation reduced the influence of the Church on family decisions, and Catholics fell from about 78% of the population in 2016 to about 69% in 2022.
The total fertility rate fell below replacement level of 2.1 around 1990 and was about 1.5 in 2023, so population growth from births has slowed.
Births were about 55,000 in 2023 against about 35,000 deaths, giving a natural increase of only about 20,000.
Examination – Second impact, cultural diversity and migration:
A more open and diverse society attracted immigrants from the 1990s, and about one in five people in Census 2022 were born outside Ireland.
Between 2016 and 2022 net migration added about 190,000 to the population, slightly more than the natural increase of about 170,000.
As a result the population reached about 5.15 million in 2022, above 5 million for the first time since 1851.

MODEL ANSWER: Ethnic issue – racism and segregation:
Migrants from Algeria, Morocco and Tunisia came to France as post-war labour, and large-scale Algerian migration followed Algerian independence in 1962.
Visible difference such as skin colour makes minorities targets of racism and discrimination in jobs and housing.
Many migrants live in banlieues, segregated high-rise estates on the edge of cities such as Paris, where unemployment is high.
Segregation reinforces poverty and separation, because concentration in deprived areas limits contact with the majority population.
Riots began in Clichy-sous-Bois in October 2005 after two teenagers died fleeing police, and they spread across France.
The anti-immigration National Front, founded in 1972 and renamed the National Rally in 2018, exploits fear of immigrants for political support.
Competition for jobs, housing and services, especially in recessions, fuels hostility towards minorities.
Religious issue – laïcité and Islam in France:
France has the largest Muslim population in western Europe, and its principle of laïcité separates religion and state strictly under the 1905 law.
A 2004 law banned conspicuous religious symbols, including the Islamic headscarf, in state schools, which many Muslims see as discrimination.
A 2010 law banned face coverings such as the burqa and niqab in public places, in force from 2011.
Religious practice in public space, such as dress, halal food and holy days, can clash with secular norms.
Islamophobia rose after the Charlie Hebdo attack in January 2015 and the Paris attacks of 13 November 2015, in which 130 people were killed.
Named example – Northern Ireland:
The Plantation of Ulster began in 1609, when land taken from Catholic Irish was settled by Protestant colonists from Scotland and England, and the religious division it created led to the Troubles until 1998.
Most children there still attend schools of their own community, and peace walls still divide some Belfast neighbourhoods.
Further issue – Ireland:
After a knife attack on children outside a school on Parnell Square, a riot in Dublin on 23 November 2023 saw buses, a Luas tram and shops burned and looted, showing how hostility to migrants can turn violent.

PART "12C" (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Reason 1 – Bridging point on the River Sullane:
On the OS map the N22 crosses the River Sullane in the town centre at about 338730, which shows a bridge at the crossing.
A river is a barrier to travellers, so the place where it could be bridged or forded drew routes together.
Traders and travellers had to pass through the crossing, so inns, shops and houses grew up beside it.
The crossing allowed trade between the farming areas on each side of the river, which helped the market function to develop.
Settlement at the crossing attracted further roads, so the bridge point steadily grew into a town.
Reason 2 – Route focus where roads meet:

Reason 3 – Defence and river site:
