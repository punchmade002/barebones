PART 7B (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Multinational – Dell:
Dell is an American computer multinational with its headquarters in Round Rock, Texas.
Named locations – Limerick and Łódź:
Dell opened a plant at Raheen in Limerick in 1990.
In January 2009 Dell announced that manufacturing would move to Łódź in Poland, with about 1,900 jobs lost in Limerick.
Dell kept sales and service functions in Ireland.
Mobility – transport and communications:
Containerisation and air freight cut transport costs, so computers can be made far from their markets.
The internet and video calls let Dell manage plants at a distance from its headquarters.
Mobility – production and trade:
Standardised assembly can be done by low-skilled workers, so work can go wherever labour is cheap.
Removing trade barriers through the EU single market means goods made in Łódź can be sold across Europe.
Poland joined the EU on 1 May 2004, so its plants lay inside the enlarged EU with lower costs.
Dell can compare countries and move capital quickly.
Mobility – company strategy:
Branch plants open when a firm wants access to a trade bloc, lower costs or skilled labour, as Dell did in Limerick to serve the EU market.
Plants close through rationalisation, relocation, outsourcing, automation or mergers, and the Limerick closure was relocation to a lower-cost country.
Branch plants are vulnerable because decisions are made at a distant headquarters.
Consequences for Ireland:
Investment brings rapid job creation, but jobs can leave just as quickly, as Limerick showed.
Countries compete for investment by offering tax and grants.
Ireland responded by attracting firms earlier in new product cycles and by funding education and research, such as Science Foundation Ireland, set up in 2000.
Diagram of the mobility of Dell:
A diagram would show Dell's headquarters in Texas with arrows to Limerick in 1990 and from Limerick to Łódź in 2009.
It would also show the factors behind the move, cost, EU access and low-wage labour, and the sales and service jobs that stayed in Ireland.

PART 7C (30 marks)
MODEL ANSWER: Local example – Lough Neagh, 2023:
Economic activities can pollute water, air and land, and farming is the main local example.
Lough Neagh, the largest lake on the island of Ireland, suffered a huge toxic blue-green algal bloom in summer 2023.
The bloom was blamed on nutrient pollution from farm runoff, sewage and septic tanks, with warm weather and invasive zebra mussels adding to it.
Nitrogen and phosphorus from slurry and fertilisers wash into water, especially after heavy rain or spreading on wet ground.
The nutrients cause eutrophication, as algae grow quickly and then die.
Bacteria decomposing the dead algae use up dissolved oxygen, so fish suffocate in fish kills.
Lough Neagh supplies a large share of Northern Ireland's drinking water, so costly treatment is needed, and angling and tourism suffer.
The EU Nitrates Directive of 1991 set closed periods for spreading slurry, and buffer strips and slurry storage are required.
Global example – global warming:
Global warming is the main global-scale impact, caused by the enhanced greenhouse effect.
Carbon dioxide comes from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and cement production, and methane comes from cattle, rice paddies and landfill.
Atmospheric carbon dioxide was about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution and was over 420 ppm by the mid-2020s.
Global average temperature has risen by about 1.1 to 1.2 °C since the late 1800s, and 2024 was the warmest year on record.
Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 13% per decade since 1979, and sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018.
Responses include global agreements, EU targets and individuals and communities reducing emissions.
Second impact – marine oil pollution:
Oil spills are another global impact, as with Exxon Valdez, which ran aground in Prince William Sound, Alaska, on 24 March 1989 and spilled about 11 million US gallons.
Oiled seabirds and otters died, shorelines were smothered and the herring fishery collapsed.
Diagram of the greenhouse effect:
A diagram would show incoming short-wave solar radiation passing through, and outgoing long-wave radiation trapped by greenhouse gases.
Extra gases from human activity trap more heat, raising temperature.

MODEL ANSWER: Financial services in Ireland, a developed economy:
Ireland is a developed economy with a high income per person in which services employ about three-quarters of workers.
Named example – the IFSC, Dublin:
The International Financial Services Centre was set up in 1987 on the derelict Custom House Docks on the north bank of the Liffey.
It hosts banking, insurance, fund administration, asset management and aircraft leasing firms.
Development – origins:
The Haughey government created the IFSC to attract international finance and to regenerate a declining dock area.
Approved firms paid a low 10% corporation tax rate, later replaced by the 12.5% national rate.
Development – why firms located there:
Financial services are footloose, so firms can locate anywhere that offers the right conditions.
Ireland offered an educated, English-speaking workforce.
Membership of the EU gave firms access to the Single Market.
Ireland's time zone suits trading with both Asian and American markets.
Government incentives reduced the costs of approved firms.
Development – growth:
The IFSC became one of the world's largest centres for investment funds, with Irish-domiciled funds holding over €3 trillion in assets.
Aircraft leasing became a second specialism.
