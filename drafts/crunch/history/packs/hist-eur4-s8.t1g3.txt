TARGET hist-eur4-s8: 8. The communist economies: problems and outcomes
[hist-eur4-s8.0.b] After 1945 Europe split into a capitalist west and a communist east, and the two sides competed over whose economic system could deliver more. To follow the problems of the communist economies, you first need the system they were built on and the dates of the leaders who ran it.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.0] Communism, as used here, means one-party rule combined with a publicly owned, centrally planned economy, in which the state rather than private owners decides what is made and at what price.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.1] Capitalism rests on private ownership, markets and profit, whereas communism rests on public ownership and state direction. This difference made the economy a central battleground of the Cold War, because each side claimed to offer people a better life.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.2] After 1945 the Soviet Union dominated eastern Europe, and communist parties took power there in 1945–48. Sovietisation means remodelling these states, their economies included, on the Soviet pattern.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.3] The Marshall Plan (1947) offered American aid to rebuild Europe and deepened the division of the continent, because the Soviet bloc refused Marshall aid and set up its own economic arrangements instead.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.4] Stalin died in March 1953. Khrushchev then led the Soviet Communist Party from 1953 until he was removed in October 1964, Brezhnev led from 1964 to 1982 (he died in November 1982), and Gorbachev became General Secretary on 11 March 1985.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.b] Communist economies were run by central planning, in which the state set production targets and allocated resources instead of leaving decisions to markets. The system was built in the Soviet Union first, at enormous human cost.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.0] Gosplan, the Soviet state planning committee (set up in 1921), drew up five-year plans that set output targets for industry, agriculture and trade. Prices were also set by the state, so there were no market signals telling factories what people wanted.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.1] Priority was given to heavy industry (steel, coal, machinery and armaments), while consumer goods and housing were neglected, because the plans aimed to build industrial and military strength first.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.2] Stalin's first Five-Year Plan began in 1928 and built heavy industry and cities at great human cost.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.3] Collectivisation, the forcing of peasants to give up private farms and work in large state-controlled farms, was carried through in the Soviet Union in the early 1930s. Together with the famine of 1932–33 it killed millions, though estimates of the total vary widely.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.4] Output was driven by pressing workers to exceed their norms (set work quotas). The Stakhanovite campaigns, which began in the Soviet Union in 1935 and were named after the miner Alexei Stakhanov, held up record-breaking workers as models, and they were copied in eastern Europe from the late 1940s.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.b] Once communist parties held power in eastern Europe, the Soviet economic model was copied there. This tied the satellite economies, the states under Soviet influence, closely to Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.0] In eastern Europe, industry, banks and large estates were nationalised (taken into state ownership) in 1945–48. Plans on the Soviet pattern followed from about 1949–51, for example Poland's Six-Year Plan (1950–55) and East Germany's first Five-Year Plan (1951).
[hist-eur4-s8.2.1] Showpiece new industrial towns were built to symbolise the new system, such as Nowa Huta, a steel town and combine near Kraków begun in 1949, and Stalinstadt in East Germany, a steel town founded in 1950 and renamed Eisenhüttenstadt in 1961.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.2] Collectivisation was imposed on most eastern European farms after 1945, and was pressed hard in Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, Romania and East Germany. Poland was the major exception, because most Polish farms stayed in private hands.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.3] Comecon, founded in January 1949 in Moscow (the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance), coordinated trade and planning among the Soviet bloc. It is usually seen as a Soviet answer to the Marshall Plan.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.4] Comecon trade used a non-convertible accounting currency, one that could not be exchanged for Western money (bilateral clearing at first, then the transferable rouble from 1963), at prices set by planners.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.5] In practice the Soviet Union supplied cheap raw materials and energy and took manufactured goods from the satellites. This is why the east European economies became dependent on Soviet supplies.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.6] In the early years the Soviet Union also took resources out of the region through reparations (payments in goods) and Soviet-controlled joint companies in Hungary, Romania and East Germany in the late 1940s and early 1950s. In 1949–53 eastern Europe's trade shifted from the West to the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.b] Central planning produced chronic shortages of goods, poor quality and slow adoption of new technology. Each problem had a cause in how the system rewarded managers, and together they undermined the economies over time.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.0] Planners set targets in gross output terms, meaning the total quantity produced. This encouraged factories to produce heavy and low-quality goods rather than goods that consumers wanted, because quantity was what earned the reward.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.1] Prices were fixed by the state rather than by supply and demand, so shortages in shops coexisted with surpluses of unwanted goods in warehouses. Nothing in the system adjusted production to what people would actually buy.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.2] Shortages of consumer goods, meat, housing and cars were common, and long queues and black markets (illegal trade in scarce goods) were a permanent feature of daily life.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.3] Innovation was slow because managers had little incentive to adopt new technology, and the state discouraged risk-taking. A manager who changed methods risked missing the target and gained little if the change worked.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.4] Housing was built in standard blocks and was often overcrowded. In East Germany buyers had to wait many years for a car such as the Trabant, which shows how little the system answered consumer demand.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.b] Beyond the daily shortages, the system had deeper structural weaknesses. Agriculture stayed unproductive, industry damaged the environment, growth depended on adding more inputs, and military spending drained resources.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.0] Soviet agriculture remained unproductive, with a large share of the labour force employed on farms. The Soviet Union bought large amounts of Western grain after the poor harvest of 1963 and again in the 1970s.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.1] Environmental damage was severe. Industrial regions such as northern Bohemia and Upper Silesia were polluted by heavy industry, and in the Aral Sea basin irrigation for cotton shrank the Aral Sea.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.2] The economies relied on extensive growth, which comes from adding more labour and raw materials, and could not move to intensive growth, which comes from better technology and productivity. Once the supply of new workers dried up in the 1970s, growth slowed.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.3] The arms race and the cost of maintaining an army and military industries absorbed a large share of national output, especially in the Soviet Union. This was at the cost of consumers and investment, because resources spent on weapons could not be spent on housing or modern factories.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.b] Soviet leaders knew the system performed badly, and tried to improve it without giving up central control. Both main reforms were partial, and neither lasted in its original form.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.0] Khrushchev's reform of 1957 replaced industrial ministries with regional economic councils (sovnarkhozy), aiming to bring decisions closer to local industry. Ministries were restored after he fell in October 1964, so the reform was reversed.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.1] Khrushchev also tried to lift the production of consumer goods and housing. He launched a mass housebuilding drive from the mid-1950s and in 1957 pledged to overtake the USA in output of goods such as meat, milk and butter, but this was not achieved.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.2] Alexei Kosygin, Soviet prime minister from 1964 to 1980, announced his economic reform in September 1965. It gave enterprises more control over profits and pricing, but central control of investment stayed in place, and the reform also restored the ministries.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.3] By the late 1960s Kosygin's reform had been watered down and it faded. A common explanation is that ministries and the party feared losing control, and the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968 also discouraged further experiment.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.b] Some bloc states went further than the Soviet Union in introducing market elements. Even so, none gave up one-party rule, which limited how far reform could go.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.0] Czechoslovakia's New Economic Model of the mid-1960s was designed by the economist Ota Šik and proposed market mechanisms. The Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia in August 1968 ended it, and also warned other bloc states against going too far.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.1] Hungary's New Economic Mechanism came into force on 1 January 1968. It allowed limited market prices and private enterprise in services, and it survived in part into the 1980s, which made it a model of partial reform.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.2] Hungary's policy under János Kádár, who led the country from 1956 to 1988, was nicknamed goulash communism. It tolerated small private plots and enterprises and gave citizens more consumer goods than in other bloc states.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.3] Hungary's reforms gave managers more freedom and allowed a second economy, private work done after hours alongside the official one, but the state kept control of the main industries and prices.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.4] Yugoslavia under Tito was expelled from the Cominform (the Soviet-led communist bloc organisation) in 1948. It left Soviet alignment and was never a full member of Comecon. From 1950 it developed worker self-management and market socialism, and it allowed its citizens to travel and work abroad.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.5] Yugoslavia's system had costs. It ran up heavy foreign debt and suffered high inflation in the 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.b] Reforms stalled because they threatened the control of the party and the ministries. Under Leonid Brezhnev, who led from 1964 to 1982, the Soviet leadership avoided reform and the economy grew more slowly.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.0] Fear of losing party and ministry control is a common explanation of why reforms stopped, and the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia served as a warning of the risks of loosening central control.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.1] Growth in the Soviet economy slowed in the 1970s, as the labour force grew more slowly and the rise in productivity fell. The period became known as stagnation (zastoi), a label applied later, especially by Gorbachev.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.2] The Soviet Union earned large hard currency (convertible Western money) revenues from oil and gas exports after the oil price rises of 1973 and 1979. This let the leadership buy Western grain and technology and postpone reform.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.3] When oil prices fell sharply in 1986 this support vanished, so the economy's weaknesses were exposed. Living standards for most people had improved only slowly during the 1970s.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.4] Under Brezhnev the Soviet Union reached rough nuclear parity with the USA and increased its military spending, which took a large share of national output at the cost of consumer goods and investment. Soviet forces entered Afghanistan in December 1979, which added to the burden.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.5] Rewarding party loyalty rather than performance led to corruption and an ageing leadership.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.6] Consumers experienced shortages of basic goods. The black market, bribery and blat, the use of personal contacts and favours to obtain scarce goods and services, became part of ordinary life.
[hist-eur4-s8.8.b] Poland's attempts to raise living standards by borrowing from the West ended in debt and repeated waves of protest. Three price-rise crises, in December 1970, June 1976 and summer 1980, mark the story.
[hist-eur4-s8.8.0] Price rises announced in December 1970 led to riots and strikes in Gdańsk, Gdynia and Szczecin on the Baltic coast. They brought the fall of Władysław Gomułka, who was replaced by Edward Gierek on 20 December 1970.
[hist-eur4-s8.8.1] Gierek borrowed heavily from Western banks to finance imports of machinery and consumer goods. These were intended to modernise industry and win public support.
[hist-eur4-s8.8.2] Price rises announced on 24 June 1976 led to strikes and riots, notably at Radom and Ursus. The government withdrew the increases the next day.
[hist-eur4-s8.8.3] Gierek's strategy failed for several reasons. The new factories produced goods that were not competitive in Western markets, so export earnings did not pay off the loans, and agricultural output suffered from a lack of investment.
[hist-eur4-s8.8.4] Poland's debt to the West was over 20 billion dollars by 1980. By then it had become unsustainable, the economy had shrunk and shops were bare of meat.
[hist-eur4-s8.8.5] Price rises announced on 1 July 1980 set off strikes that spread to Gdańsk in August. The shipyard strike there began in mid-August, the Gdańsk Agreement was signed on 31 August 1980, and Solidarity, led by Lech Wałęsa, was founded in September 1980.
[hist-eur4-s8.8.6] Poland's economic crisis was a cause of Solidarity, and the economic collapse in the 1980s helped to bring the communists to the Round Table talks with Solidarity, which began in February 1989.
[hist-eur4-s8.9.b] Mikhail Gorbachev tried to rescue the Soviet economy by restructuring it. His reforms weakened the old system without putting a working new one in its place.
[hist-eur4-s8.9.0] Gorbachev launched perestroika, meaning restructuring of the economy, in 1986 (27th Party Congress, February–March 1986). It coincided with falling oil prices in 1986, which cut the Soviet Union's hard currency earnings.
[hist-eur4-s8.9.1] The Law on State Enterprise, passed in 1987 and in force from 1988, gave factories more freedom. The 1988 law on cooperatives allowed private business.
[hist-eur4-s8.9.2] These laws weakened central control without creating a functioning market. Planners no longer directed supply reliably, but prices and competition did not yet replace them.
[hist-eur4-s8.9.3] By 1989–90 the Soviet economy was suffering from shortages, a growing budget deficit and falling output, and rationing (coupons) for basic goods appeared in several cities.
[hist-eur4-s8.10.b] By the late 1980s the gap between the communist states and the West was plain to see, and several bloc economies survived only on Western credit or severe austerity. Their end left problems for the successor states.
[hist-eur4-s8.10.0] Romania under Nicolae Ceaușescu announced in 1989 that its foreign debt was repaid. The cost was cuts in imports and in food, heating and electricity for the population, who suffered shortages.
[hist-eur4-s8.10.1] East Germany was the strongest industrial economy in the bloc by common assessment, but it depended on West German credit and trade concessions, including a billion-mark loan arranged in 1983, with a second in 1984.
[hist-eur4-s8.10.2] The German economic, monetary and social union of 1 July 1990 exposed East German industries to competition and led to mass closures.
[hist-eur4-s8.10.3] The gap with the West was visible by 1989. East German and Czechoslovak citizens could see how much better West Germans and Austrians lived on television and through travel, and living standards in the Soviet Union lagged far behind.
[hist-eur4-s8.10.4] The end of the Soviet bloc's economic system left a legacy of obsolete industry, environmental damage and unemployment that the successor states had to address.
[hist-eur4-s8.11.b] The bloc was not uniform, and examiners reward comparison of different states. Each started from a different level of development and handled planning differently.
[hist-eur4-s8.11.0] Czechoslovakia was the most industrialised state in the region before the war, because the Czech lands were the industrial heartland of the Habsburg Empire and of interwar Czechoslovakia.
[hist-eur4-s8.11.1] Czechoslovakia's living standards were among the highest in the bloc in the 1950s, but it stagnated under rigid planning in the 1960s and was the home of Ota Šik's reform.
[hist-eur4-s8.11.2] East Germany was among the most advanced economies in the bloc and the showcase of socialism, but it lost skilled workers to the West until the Berlin Wall was built in August 1961, after millions of people had left since 1949.
[hist-eur4-s8.11.3] Bulgaria and Romania were still largely agricultural in 1945 and were industrialised rapidly. Romania developed heavy industry, oil and chemicals and pursued an independent foreign policy under Ceaușescu.
[hist-eur4-s8.11.4] Ceaușescu's Romania exported food and fuel to pay its debts and rationed food and electricity in the 1980s. His systematisation programme, announced in 1988, planned to demolish villages.
[hist-eur4-s8.11.5] Hungary's partial reforms and Poland's private farming made those states more flexible than the Soviet Union. However, their dependence on Western credit made them vulnerable when interest rates and prices rose around 1980.
[hist-eur4-s8.12.b] Communist economies achieved rapid industrialisation and social welfare, but they failed to sustain growth or satisfy consumers. Judgement answers need both sides and a clear verdict.
[hist-eur4-s8.12.0] Achievements: rapid industrialisation, near-universal literacy, free health care and education, full employment including high employment of women, and cheap housing and basic goods.
[hist-eur4-s8.12.1] Limits: chronic shortages, low quality, poor housing, environmental damage and a standard of living that fell further behind the West.
[hist-eur4-s8.12.2] Eastern Europe's communist states did expand education, cheap housing and health care, but their living standards fell behind West European levels by the 1970s.
[hist-eur4-s8.12.3] Contemporary Western accounts often stressed the failure of the planned economy, while Soviet and bloc officials emphasised the achievements of industrialisation and social welfare.
[hist-eur4-s8.12.4] Later historians stress both the system's real successes in mobilising resources and the structural limits that made it impossible to sustain growth without political reform.
[hist-eur4-s8.12.5] The final judgement of historians is that the planned economy could build basic industry, but could not adapt to the information-based economies of the 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s8.12.6] Reform within the bloc was partial, because leaders would not give up party control. Economic failure was one cause, not the only cause, of the events of 1989.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s4: 4. Reform movements in Eastern Europe, the collapse of the Soviet Union, and fragmentation and realignment, 1953–1992
[hist-eur4-s4.0.b] After 1945 the Soviet Union kept control of eastern Europe through communist parties loyal to Moscow. Reform movements kept appearing inside this system, and knowing how it held together explains why they were suppressed for so long and then succeeded so quickly.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.0] Communism, as practised in the Soviet bloc, meant rule by a single communist party and a state-owned, centrally planned economy. It is the system that reformers tried to change and that collapsed in 1989-91.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.1] The bloc consisted of one-party 'satellite' states (formally independent countries controlled by Moscow): Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and East Germany. Soviet troops were stationed in several of them, so the control was real and not just a matter of influence.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.2] Two alliances tied the satellites to the USSR: Comecon (1949), an economic alliance, and the Warsaw Pact (1955), a military alliance.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.3] The Soviet leaders in order were Stalin (died March 1953), Khrushchev (removed 1964), Leonid Brezhnev (1964-82), Yuri Andropov (1982-84), Konstantin Chernenko (1984-85) and Mikhail Gorbachev (1985-91).
[hist-eur4-s4.0.4] Hungary in 1956 was the standing warning: a reform movement that left the Warsaw Pact was crushed by Soviet tanks, so every later reformer knew how far Moscow would go.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.5] By the 1970s and 1980s the command economy (where the state plans production instead of the market) brought shortages, low living standards, stagnation and, in Poland, heavy foreign debt, which steadily cost the regimes public support.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.6] The Helsinki Final Act of 1975, signed by the USA, Canada and almost all European states, East and West, contained human-rights commitments. Dissidents (open critics of the regime) later used them against their own governments.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.7] In the early 1980s came a 'second Cold War': the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979), then Ronald Reagan, US president 1981-89, called the USSR an 'evil empire' (1983) and announced the Strategic Defense Initiative ('Star Wars') in 1983, raising the cost of the arms race.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.b] Within a few years of Stalin's death, workers in two satellite states challenged their governments. How Moscow answered each one set a pattern that lasted until 1968.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.0] On 17 June 1953 building workers in East Berlin protested against increased work norms (more work for the same pay, in effect a wage cut). With shortages and Stalin's death in March, the protests spread to hundreds of towns across East Germany.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.1] Soviet tanks put the rising down, so it failed, and dozens were killed (no reliable exact figure). Walter Ulbricht, the East German communist leader, stayed in power, and emigration westward continued until the Berlin Wall closed the border.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.2] In June 1956 workers protested in Poznań, Poland, because of poor conditions, and the army put the protest down, killing dozens (no exact figure).
[hist-eur4-s4.1.3] In October 1956 Władysław Gomułka, a communist purged in 1948-49, became party First Secretary after Khrushchev and other Soviet leaders flew to Warsaw. Soviet troops moved but did not intervene.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.4] Moscow held back because Poland stayed in the Warsaw Pact and the party kept control of the country, so the change of leader did not threaten Soviet interests.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.5] Gomułka ended forced collectivisation (most farms returned to private hands), released Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński from internment and gave the Church and intellectuals more freedom, though the party kept one-party rule.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.6] By the 1960s Gomułka had tightened controls again, so the 1956 settlement proved limited because the party never gave up its monopoly.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.7] The pattern was Poland 1956 (concession), Hungary 1956 (crushed) and Czechoslovakia 1968 (invaded): reform was tolerated only if the party kept its monopoly and the country stayed in the Pact.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.b] In Czechoslovakia in 1968 reform came from inside the communist party itself. The reformers wanted 'socialism with a human face' (communism made freer and more humane), but their Warsaw Pact allies ended the experiment by force.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.0] Czechoslovakia had a hardline leader, Antonin Novotný, a stagnant economy and Slovak grievances. On 5 January 1968 the Slovak Alexander Dubček replaced Novotný as First Secretary of the party, which opened the way to reform.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.1] The Action Programme of April 1968 proposed freedom of speech and the press, the rehabilitation (official clearing of the names) of victims of the 1950s and curbs on the secret police. Censorship ended in practice in spring 1968.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.2] Dubček's government also planned economic reform with market mechanisms and a federal settlement for Czechs and Slovaks. The federal republics were agreed in 1968 and took effect on 1 January 1969, the one reform that survived.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.3] In June 1968 the writer Ludvík Vaculík's 'Two Thousand Words' manifesto urged faster reform.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.4] Dubček did not propose leaving the Warsaw Pact or ending communism: he insisted that Czechoslovakia stayed in the Pact and that the party kept its leading role. This is the key difference from Imre Nagy's step in Hungary in 1956.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.5] Soviet leaders feared that reform would spread to Poland, East Germany and Ukraine. In July 1968 the Soviet, East German, Polish, Hungarian and Bulgarian leaders sent the Warsaw Letter of warning, and talks followed at Čierna nad Tisou and Bratislava in late July and early August.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.6] On the night of 20-21 August 1968 Soviet, Polish, Hungarian and Bulgarian forces invaded Czechoslovakia. East German troops were prepared but kept back, apart from a token presence.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.7] Dubček and other leaders were seized and flown to Moscow and forced to sign the Moscow Protocol. Resistance was mainly passive, such as removing street signs and talking to the soldiers, so there was little fighting.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.b] The invasion split the communist world and gave Moscow a justification for intervening again. Inside Czechoslovakia it was followed by a hard-line clean-up, while a small dissident movement kept the ideas of reform alive.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.0] Romania, under Nicolae Ceaușescu, refused to take part in the invasion and condemned it, and Albania formally left the Warsaw Pact in September 1968 in protest.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.1] The Brezhnev Doctrine was set out in a Pravda article on 26 September 1968 and in Brezhnev's speech to the Polish party congress in November. It held that when socialism is threatened in one socialist country, it is the concern of all, so Moscow claimed a right to intervene.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.2] Normalisation (the restoration of strict party control) followed. Gustáv Husák replaced Dubček as First Secretary in April 1969, and hundreds of thousands of party members were expelled or left in the purges.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.3] Dubček himself was expelled from the party in 1970 and worked as a forestry official, which shows how completely the reformers were removed.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.4] In January 1969 the student Jan Palach set himself on fire in Prague in protest at the crushing of reform, and died days later.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.5] Charter 77, published in January 1977, demanded that the government respect the human rights it had signed up to in the Helsinki Final Act of 1975 and in UN covenants. Dissidents were using the regime's own promises against it.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.6] Its first spokesmen were Václav Havel, Jan Patočka and Jiří Hájek, so it had no single leader.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.7] Havel, a playwright and the Charter's best-known figure, was imprisoned several times, the longest term being 1979-83. Charter 77 mattered because it kept reform alive through years of repression.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.b] Karol Wojtyła, elected pope in 1978, gave moral support to the Polish opposition to communism and so became a political figure as well as a religious leader. Here we cover only his political role.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.0] Born in Wadowice, Poland, in 1920, Karol Wojtyła became Archbishop of Kraków in 1964 and a cardinal in 1967. He was elected pope on 16 October 1978, taking the name John Paul II, the first non-Italian pope since Adrian VI (1522-23).
[hist-eur4-s4.4.1] His first visit to Poland as pope, 2-10 June 1979, drew huge crowds. At the open-air Mass in Victory Square, Warsaw, on 2 June he prayed: 'Let your Spirit descend, and renew the face of the earth, the face of this land.'
[hist-eur4-s4.4.2] The visit showed Poles that they were many and could organise independently of the state, and many took his prayer for the renewal of their land as encouragement to demand change.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.3] In August 1980 the pope's picture hung on the Gdańsk shipyard gate during the strike, which shows that his election and visit had given many Poles the confidence to organise.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.4] He returned to Poland in June 1983, when he met Lech Wałęsa, and in June 1987. His sermons defended workers' rights and human dignity without calling for revolt, which gave Solidarity moral support while the authorities tried to suppress it.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.5] He was shot in St Peter's Square, Rome, on 13 May 1981 by Mehmet Ali Ağca, a Turkish gunman, and recovered. Claims of Bulgarian or Soviet involvement have never been proven.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.6] The Polish and Soviet governments saw him as a threat, and the Polish secret police monitored the Church closely. On 1 December 1989 he received Gorbachev in the Vatican, the first Soviet Communist Party leader (General Secretary) to meet a pope.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.7] Historians debate how far he caused 1989: many see a moral leader who strengthened Polish civil society (groups organised outside the state), others stress economic and Soviet factors. Gorbachev wrote in 1992 that events in eastern Europe would have been impossible without the pope.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.b] Poland's economic troubles produced the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc. Solidarity grew from a shipyard strike into a national movement, forcing the communist state to deal with an organised opposition.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.0] In December 1970 price rises caused strikes on the Baltic coast (Gdańsk, Gdynia, Szczecin); troops fired on workers and Gomułka was replaced by Edward Gierek. Gierek borrowed heavily from the West in the 1970s, protests followed price rises in 1976, and meat-price rises came in July 1980.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.1] The strike at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk began on 14 August 1980, sparked by the sacking of the crane operator Anna Walentynowicz.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.2] The inter-factory strike committee made 21 demands, including free trade unions, the right to strike, the release of political prisoners, access to the media and better supplies.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.3] The Gdańsk Agreement, signed on 31 August 1980, gave the strikers the right to form free trade unions and to strike, a concession forced on the state by the strike.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.4] Solidarity (Solidarność) was founded in September 1980 and registered by the courts in November 1980. Its leader was Lech Wałęsa, an electrician at the shipyard who had been sacked in 1976.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.5] At its peak in 1981 it had about ten million members, about a quarter of the population, and Rural Solidarity was formed for farmers in 1981. With Church and intellectual support, this made it a national movement rather than just a union.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.b] The state struck back in December 1981, but it could not destroy Solidarity. Eight years later the same regime had to negotiate with it.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.0] General Wojciech Jaruzelski, prime minister from February 1981 and party First Secretary from October 1981, declared martial law on 13 December 1981. Wałęsa and other Solidarity leaders were interned, the union was suspended, and a curfew and army rule through the Military Council of National Salvation followed.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.1] The union was formally banned in October 1982 so that it could not revive, and martial law was lifted in July 1983.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.2] Jaruzelski later claimed martial law was the 'lesser evil' that prevented a Soviet invasion. Moscow had pressed for firm action, but Soviet Politburo records from December 1981 suggest it was unwilling to send troops.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.3] Historians still debate how to read those records and whether the invasion threat was real, and many add that Jaruzelski also acted to keep communist control. Martial law was control by proxy: Poles, not Soviet troops, did the repressing.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.4] Wałęsa was released in November 1982 and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983. Father Jerzy Popiełuszko, a pro-Solidarity priest, was murdered by secret police officers in October 1984, and the officers were tried and convicted.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.5] Strike waves in 1988 showed that Solidarity's support had survived the years of repression, which is why the government had to talk to it.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.6] Round Table talks between the government and the Solidarity-led opposition ran from 6 February to 5 April 1989. They agreed partly free elections (a set share of Sejm, the lower house, seats reserved for the communists and their allies, and a new freely elected Senate) and a new presidency.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.7] Solidarity was legalised again on 17 April 1989, which opened the way to the June elections because it could now stand candidates.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.b] Mikhail Gorbachev, Soviet leader from 1985, began reforms that he meant to renew socialism but that ended with the collapse of communist rule in eastern Europe and the break-up of the USSR.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.0] He was born on 2 March 1931 in Privolnoye, in the Stavropol region, to a peasant family. He took a law degree at Moscow State University (1955), where he met his wife Raisa.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.1] He rose through the party in Stavropol (regional First Secretary in 1970), became Central Committee secretary for agriculture in 1978 and a full Politburo member in 1980, as a protege of Yuri Andropov.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.2] He became General Secretary on 11 March 1985, on Chernenko's death, as the youngest Politburo member. He was head of state from October 1988 (chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet), chairman of the new Supreme Soviet in 1989 and President of the USSR in March 1990.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.3] He was chosen by the party in 1985 and by the Congress of People's Deputies in 1990, never by popular vote; Boris Yeltsin, elected in June 1991, was the one with a popular mandate. Gorbachev resigned on 25 December 1991 and died in 2022.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.4] His aim was to save and renew socialism, not to end it. As a participant he chose not to use force in eastern Europe, unlike Khrushchev in 1956, and most historians agree that the consequences, the end of communist rule and of the Union, were ones he did not foresee.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.5] Contemporaries abroad welcomed him. After meeting him in December 1984 Margaret Thatcher said: 'I like Mr Gorbachev. We can do business together.' Reagan negotiated arms agreements with him, 'Gorbymania' (popular enthusiasm) swept the West, and he won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1990.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.6] At home conservatives blamed him for destroying the system, radicals such as Yeltsin (dismissed in 1987 after attacking the slow pace of reform) thought him too slow, and many Russians blamed him for shortages and economic collapse.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.7] Historians differ. Archie Brown (The Gorbachev Factor, 1996) sees a genuine transformer of the system; Stephen Kotkin (Armageddon Averted, 2001) argues the system was already doomed and that his achievement was that it ended without war.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.b] By 1985 the Soviet Union was stagnating, and Gorbachev set out to renew it from above. His economic policies moved from a simple push for speed to a wider restructuring, and neither cured the shortages.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.0] Reform was needed because of economic stagnation, the cost of the arms race and of the war in Afghanistan (Soviet troops there since December 1979), and an ageing leadership.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.1] World oil prices fell sharply in 1986, cutting Soviet export earnings, so the state had less money to meet its costs.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.2] He relied on younger reformers, notably Alexander Yakovlev, his key adviser on reform and glasnost, and Eduard Shevardnadze, foreign minister from 1985.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.3] His first economic slogan, in 1985-86, was uskorenie (acceleration of economic growth). It tried to speed up the existing planned economy, and when it failed he moved to perestroika.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.4] The anti-alcohol campaign of May 1985 belonged to this early effort, but it was unpopular and cost the state tax revenue.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.5] Perestroika (restructuring) set out to reform the economy, and later the political system, by adding limited market elements to central planning. The Law on State Enterprise (1987) gave factories more freedom over output and prices.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.6] The Law on Cooperatives (1988) allowed small private businesses, for example restaurants and workshops, so private enterprise returned in a limited form.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.7] Perestroika failed because it was a set of half-measures: markets were added but planning was not abandoned, so by 1990-91 there were empty shops, rationing, rising prices and falling output.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.b] Gorbachev found that economic reform needed honest information and public pressure on officials. Glasnost opened up discussion, and democratisation then gave voters a real say.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.0] Glasnost means openness: freer discussion, less censorship and criticism of officials and of the Soviet past. It was proclaimed at the 27th Party Congress in February-March 1986.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.1] The reactor explosion at Chernobyl, in Soviet Ukraine (not Russia), on 26 April 1986 exposed the secrecy of the system: the authorities said nothing for about two days, until radiation was detected in Sweden. The concealment strengthened the case for glasnost.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.2] Banned works were published, which showed the new openness, such as Boris Pasternak's Doctor Zhivago (1988) and Solzhenitsyn's writings (1989-90), and Stalin's crimes were openly discussed.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.3] Dissidents (open critics of the regime) were released, for example the nuclear physicist Andrei Sakharov, freed from internal exile in Gorky in December 1986.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.4] Democratisation (demokratizatsiya) meant partly free elections and the end of the party's monopoly of power.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.5] The elections to the Congress of People's Deputies in March 1989 were the first partly free elections in the Soviet Union since 1917. Several party bosses were defeated and the sessions were televised.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.6] In February-March 1990 the party gave up its leading role (Article 6 of the constitution was amended in March 1990), and in March 1990 Gorbachev became the first executive president of the USSR.
[hist-eur4-s4.9.7] Glasnost also released nationalism in the republics, so the success of freeing speech carried a limit that Gorbachev could not control.
[hist-eur4-s4.10.b] Gorbachev wanted to cut the military burden that was crippling the economy. His 'new thinking' in foreign policy, helped by Shevardnadze, ended the arms race and eased tension with the West.
[hist-eur4-s4.10.0] He held summits with Reagan at Geneva (November 1985), Reykjavik (October 1986) and Washington (December 1987). At Reykjavik they came close to agreeing deep cuts in nuclear weapons but failed over SDI.
[hist-eur4-s4.10.1] The INF Treaty, signed in Washington on 8 December 1987, abolished all US and Soviet intermediate-range (500-5,500 km) land-based missiles, the first treaty to remove a whole class of nuclear weapons.
[hist-eur4-s4.10.2] In his United Nations General Assembly speech on 7 December 1988 he announced that Soviet armed forces would be cut by 500,000 men and some divisions withdrawn from eastern Europe, which signalled a lower military threat.
[hist-eur4-s4.10.3] The last Soviet troops left Afghanistan on 15 February 1989, ending a costly war that had begun in December 1979, so the USSR no longer had to pay for it.
[hist-eur4-s4.10.4] At the Malta summit with President George H. W. Bush on 2-3 December 1989 (Bush, not Reagan) the leaders met as the bloc collapsed, and it is often seen as marking the end of the Cold War.
[hist-eur4-s4.10.5] START I (strategic arms reduction) was signed in July 1991 to cut the long-range nuclear weapons of both sides, which continued arms control after the INF Treaty.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.b] In 1989 communist rule collapsed across eastern Europe in a few months, almost entirely without Soviet intervention. Poland and Hungary went first, and Hungary's actions opened a route to the West for East Germans.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.0] The Polish elections of 4 and 18 June 1989 were not fully free, because a set share of Sejm seats was reserved for the communists and their allies. Solidarity won all but one of the Sejm seats it could contest (161) and 99 of the 100 Senate seats.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.1] Jaruzelski was elected president by parliament in July 1989. In August 1989 Tadeusz Mazowiecki, a Catholic journalist and Solidarity adviser, became prime minister of the first non-communist-led government in the Soviet bloc in over forty years.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.2] Wałęsa was elected President of Poland in December 1990.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.3] In Hungary János Kádár, leader since 1956, was removed in May 1988. The dismantling of the border fence with Austria began on 2 May 1989, and on 27 June the Hungarian and Austrian foreign ministers, Gyula Horn and Alois Mock, cut the wire publicly.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.4] Imre Nagy, leader of the 1956 uprising, was reburied with honours in Budapest on 16 June 1989 before a huge crowd, which turned the memory of 1956 into a national symbol and a challenge to the regime.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.5] At the Pan-European Picnic near Sopron on 19 August 1989 several hundred East Germans crossed to Austria, and on 11 September 1989 Hungary opened its border to East Germans so that they could escape west.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.6] Round-table talks agreed multi-party elections, the party renamed itself the Hungarian Socialist Party in October 1989, and the Republic of Hungary was proclaimed on 23 October 1989, the anniversary of 1956.
[hist-eur4-s4.11.7] Free elections in March-April 1990 were won by the Hungarian Democratic Forum, and József Antall became prime minister.
[hist-eur4-s4.12.b] East Germany's government collapsed under pressure from its own people, who first left in thousands and then marched. The opening of the Berlin Wall became the symbol of the end of the division of Europe.
[hist-eur4-s4.12.0] Thousands of East Germans fled through Hungary and Czechoslovakia and camped in the West German embassy in Prague, until they were allowed to leave for the West at the end of September 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.12.1] New Forum, an opposition group, was founded in September 1989, and the Leipzig Monday demonstrations began that month. On 9 October about 70,000 marched and the security forces did not fire, which showed that the regime had lost the will to shoot.
[hist-eur4-s4.12.2] Gorbachev attended the GDR's 40th anniversary celebrations on 7 October 1989 and urged reform, a sign that Moscow would not back the hardliners with force.
[hist-eur4-s4.12.3] Erich Honecker, East German leader since 1971, resigned on 18 October 1989 because the demonstrations could not be stopped, and Egon Krenz succeeded him.
[hist-eur4-s4.12.4] A mass rally in East Berlin on 4 November 1989 followed. The slogan 'We are the people' later became 'We are one people', showing the shift from demanding reform to demanding unity.
[hist-eur4-s4.12.5] On the evening of 9 November 1989 Günter Schabowski, Politburo spokesman, told a press conference that new travel rules applied 'immediately'. The announcement was confused, and crowds gathered at the crossings.
[hist-eur4-s4.12.6] Guards at Bornholmer Strasse opened the gate that night, so the Wall was opened, not knocked down; demolition came in 1990.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.b] The last regimes fell in a rush in November and December 1989. Czechoslovakia's change was peaceful, Bulgaria's came from inside the party, and Romania's was the only violent revolution of 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.0] The Velvet Revolution began on 17 November 1989, when police beat a student march in Prague. It is called 'velvet' because it was peaceful.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.1] Mass demonstrations and a general strike on 27 November followed. Civic Forum, with Havel, led the opposition in the Czech lands, and Public Against Violence did so in Slovakia.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.2] The party gave up its leading role at the end of November, and Husák resigned as president in December 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.3] Dubček was elected chairman of the Federal Assembly on 28 December and Václav Havel president on 29 December 1989, so the leader of 1968 and the Charter 77 spokesman stood together at the end.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.4] In Bulgaria, Todor Zhivkov, leader since 1954, was removed by his own party on 10 November 1989 and replaced by Petar Mladenov.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.5] In Romania protests began in Timișoara on 16 December 1989 over the eviction of the Hungarian Protestant pastor László Tőkés, and security forces fired on the crowds.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.6] A rally in Bucharest on 21 December turned against Nicolae Ceaușescu. He fled by helicopter on 22 December, the army changed sides, and there was fighting with Securitate (secret police) loyalists.
[hist-eur4-s4.13.7] The National Salvation Front, led by Ion Iliescu, took power. Nicolae and Elena Ceaușescu were tried by a military court and shot on 25 December 1989. About 1,100 people died in the revolution.
[hist-eur4-s4.14.b] The speed and calm of 1989 need explaining, because earlier risings had been crushed. The central change was that Moscow no longer backed its allies with force.
[hist-eur4-s4.14.0] In October 1989 Gennady Gerasimov, the Soviet foreign ministry spokesman, said the Brezhnev Doctrine had been replaced by the 'Sinatra Doctrine' (after the song 'My Way'), meaning that eastern European states could choose their own path.
[hist-eur4-s4.14.1] Non-intervention was Gorbachev's choice. He meant to renew socialism, not to free eastern Europe, so the fall of the regimes was a consequence he did not foresee.
[hist-eur4-s4.14.2] The economic failure of the regimes had already cost them public support, so there was little loyalty left to defend them.
[hist-eur4-s4.14.3] Each neighbour's example mattered: success in Poland and Hungary showed that change was possible, which encouraged protesters in East Germany and Czechoslovakia.
[hist-eur4-s4.14.4] Television and travel showed eastern Europeans the prosperity of the West, which made their own living standards look worse.
[hist-eur4-s4.14.5] The regimes lost the will to shoot because they knew Soviet tanks would not come, so the police and army did not fire on the crowds.
[hist-eur4-s4.15.b] Glasnost let the republics of the USSR (the Soviet Union was a federation of fifteen republics) air grievances that had long been suppressed. National demands and a rival leader in Russia weakened Gorbachev's authority.
[hist-eur4-s4.15.0] Nagorno-Karabakh was the scene of an Armenian-Azerbaijani conflict from 1988, which showed that glasnost let old ethnic quarrels resurface.
[hist-eur4-s4.15.1] In Tbilisi, Georgia, in April 1989, troops killed peaceful demonstrators, which hardened nationalist feeling.
[hist-eur4-s4.15.2] On 23 August 1989, the 50th anniversary of the Nazi-Soviet Pact, about two million people formed a human chain across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, called the Baltic Way.
[hist-eur4-s4.15.3] Lithuania, led by Vytautas Landsbergis, declared independence on 11 March 1990, and other Soviet republics followed in demanding sovereignty. In January 1991 Soviet troops killed fourteen people at the Vilnius television tower.
[hist-eur4-s4.15.4] Boris Yeltsin, Moscow party chief, attacked the slow pace of reform and was dismissed in 1987. He was elected chairman of the Russian parliament in May 1990, left the Communist Party in 1990, and Russia declared its sovereignty in June 1990.
[hist-eur4-s4.15.5] On 12 June 1991 Yeltsin was elected President of the Russian republic by popular vote. This gave him a mandate Gorbachev lacked and made Russian sovereignty a rival to Soviet authority.
[hist-eur4-s4.16.b] In 1991 the institutions of the Soviet system fell apart one after another. A coup by hardliners meant to save the Union instead hastened its end.
[hist-eur4-s4.16.0] Comecon was dissolved in June 1991 and the Warsaw Pact was formally dissolved on 1 July 1991 in Prague. Soviet troops left Hungary and Czechoslovakia in 1991, and the last left Germany in 1994.
[hist-eur4-s4.16.1] The coup of 19-21 August 1991 came on the eve of a new Union Treaty giving the republics more power, which hardliners feared would break up the Union.
[hist-eur4-s4.16.2] It was staged by the State Committee for the State of Emergency, eight hardliners including Vice-President Gennady Yanayev, KGB chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov and Defence Minister Dmitry Yazov. Gorbachev was held at his holiday home at Foros, Crimea.
[hist-eur4-s4.16.3] Yeltsin spoke from a tank outside the Russian parliament (the White House) in Moscow and rallied opposition. Troops would not attack, three civilians were killed, and the coup collapsed.
[hist-eur4-s4.16.4] Gorbachev returned on 22 August, weakened, and resigned as General Secretary on 24 August 1991. The Communist Party was suspended, and Yeltsin banned it in Russia in November 1991.
[hist-eur4-s4.16.5] The republics declared independence. The USSR recognised Estonian, Latvian and Lithuanian independence on 6 September 1991, and all three were admitted to the UN on 17 September 1991.
[hist-eur4-s4.17.b] After the coup the Union had no authority left, and the republics negotiated their way out. The end came in a few weeks.
[hist-eur4-s4.17.0] On 1 December 1991 Ukraine held an independence referendum with about 90 per cent in favour, which made it hard to keep the Union together.
[hist-eur4-s4.17.1] On 8 December 1991 the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus met at Belovezha (the Minsk agreement), declared the USSR at an end and founded the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS), a loose association of former republics.
[hist-eur4-s4.17.2] More republics joined the CIS at Alma-Ata on 21 December 1991, so the CIS replaced the Union.
[hist-eur4-s4.17.3] Gorbachev resigned as Soviet president on 25 December 1991 because the Union he led no longer existed in practice, and the red flag over the Kremlin was lowered that evening.
[hist-eur4-s4.17.4] The USSR was formally dissolved on 26 December 1991, a day after his resignation, leaving fifteen independent successor states.
[hist-eur4-s4.18.b] The fall of the Wall put unity on the agenda within weeks. Chancellor Helmut Kohl moved quickly, and Gorbachev's consent allowed a united Germany to remain in NATO.
[hist-eur4-s4.18.0] Helmut Kohl, West German chancellor from 1982 to 1998, pushed for rapid unity after the Wall fell, so he set out a Ten-Point Plan on 28 November 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.18.1] In the first free East German election on 18 March 1990 the Kohl-backed Alliance for Germany won, and Lothar de Maizière became prime minister, which showed that East Germans wanted unity.
[hist-eur4-s4.18.2] Monetary, economic and social union came into force on 1 July 1990, so that the Deutsche Mark became East Germany's currency.
[hist-eur4-s4.18.3] In July 1990 Gorbachev accepted that a united Germany could be in NATO, in return for German aid and limits on German forces.
[hist-eur4-s4.18.4] The Two Plus Four Treaty was signed in Moscow on 12 September 1990 by the two German states plus the USA, the Soviet Union, Britain and France.
[hist-eur4-s4.18.5] It gave Germany full sovereignty, confirmed the Oder-Neisse border, left a united Germany free to stay in NATO and required Soviet troops to leave.
[hist-eur4-s4.18.6] Unification took place on 3 October 1990, when the territory of the GDR joined the Federal Republic. Kohl won the first all-German election in December 1990.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.b] Yugoslavia was a federation of republics held together under Tito. When communism fell, nationalism broke it apart, and unlike most of 1989 the change was violent.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.0] Tito died in May 1980, and a rotating collective presidency followed, so no single leader held the federation together. Economic crisis and rising nationalism followed.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.1] Slobodan Milošević, Serbian leader from 1987 (president of Serbia from 1989), stirred Serb nationalism, and from 1991 Serbia opposed the secession of Slovenia and Croatia.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.2] Slovenia and Croatia declared independence on 25 June 1991, and Macedonia declared independence in 1991 too.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.3] The Ten-Day War in Slovenia (June-July 1991) ended with the Yugoslav army withdrawing, so Slovenia's break was brief.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.4] War in Croatia began in 1991 because Serbia opposed secession, with the siege and fall of Vukovar in November 1991.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.5] The European Community recognised Slovenia and Croatia on 15 January 1992, after Germany pressed for it.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.6] Bosnia-Herzegovina voted for independence in a referendum in February-March 1992, boycotted by most Bosnian Serbs, and was recognised in April 1992. War and the siege of Sarajevo began that month.
[hist-eur4-s4.19.7] The war in Bosnia lasted until 1995 and was the bloodiest in Europe since 1945.
[hist-eur4-s4.20.b] With the Soviet bloc gone, the former satellites and the Baltic states looked west, Czechoslovakia split peacefully, and the CIS took shape in the east.
[hist-eur4-s4.20.0] The Visegrád group of Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary, which was founded on 15 February 1991, coordinated their approach to integration with the West.
[hist-eur4-s4.20.1] The former satellites and the Baltic states turned to Western institutions: the Council of Europe, the European Community and NATO. Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic later joined NATO in 1999, and most of them and the Baltic states joined the EU in 2004.
[hist-eur4-s4.20.2] Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania regained independence in 1991 and sought membership of Western institutions.
[hist-eur4-s4.20.3] In the east the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS) linked former Soviet republics loosely, so Russia kept some influence without the old Union.
[hist-eur4-s4.20.4] Czechoslovakia divided peacefully into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on 1 January 1993, agreed in 1992 after the June elections won by Vladimír Mečiar's party in Slovakia and Václav Klaus's in the Czech lands.
[hist-eur4-s4.21.b] Examiners reward a judgement that weighs what Gorbachev achieved against what he lost. His record was far stronger abroad than at home.
[hist-eur4-s4.21.0] Success abroad: the INF Treaty, the UN troop cuts and START I ended the arms race. Failure at home: the economy got worse, with empty shops, rationing and falling output by 1990-91.
[hist-eur4-s4.21.1] Success: the withdrawal from Afghanistan in February 1989 ended a costly war, which freed money and effort for reform. Limit: shortages made him unpopular at home, so foreign admiration did not translate into support.
[hist-eur4-s4.21.2] Success: non-intervention let eastern Europe change peacefully and let Germany unite within NATO. Failure: the loss of the Union he meant to save, as nationalism grew in the republics and the August 1991 coup failed.
[hist-eur4-s4.21.3] Success: glasnost brought freedom of speech and partly free elections. Limit: it released nationalism, and he reformed the party's monopoly of power too slowly to keep control.
[hist-eur4-s4.21.4] Contemporary views: Western leaders welcomed him, while conservatives at home blamed him for destroying the system and radicals such as Yeltsin for being too slow.
[hist-eur4-s4.21.5] Later views: Archie Brown credits him as a genuine transformer, while Stephen Kotkin argues the system was already doomed and that his achievement was that it ended without war.
[hist-eur4-s4.21.6] Compared with Khrushchev, Gorbachev also reformed from above, met resistance and fell, but unlike Khrushchev he refused to use force, and his reforms ended the bloc and the Union.
[hist-eur4-s4.22.b] Historians explain the collapse through long-term failure, short-term triggers and the decision not to use force. The best answers rank these causes and weigh internal against external ones.
[hist-eur4-s4.22.0] Long-term causes: the command economy failed to deliver, and the regimes lacked legitimacy because they had never ruled by consent. Nationalism, the burden of the arms race, which the West pressed on through defence spending, and Western prosperity seen on television added to the pressure.
[hist-eur4-s4.22.1] Short-term causes: Gorbachev's reforms and non-intervention removed the threat of Soviet force, which had held the regimes up since 1953.
[hist-eur4-s4.22.2] Opposition movements such as Solidarity and Charter 77, and the Church, kept reform alive through repression, and the domino effect of 1989 carried change from one country to the next.
[hist-eur4-s4.22.3] Internal view: Stephen Kotkin and others stress the structural weakness of the system, while Archie Brown stresses Gorbachev's personal role.
[hist-eur4-s4.22.4] External view: contemporary Western conservatives credited Reagan's pressure, including the arms build-up, SDI and his 'tear down this wall' speech in Berlin in June 1987. This is one view, not proven fact, and later historians give more weight to the Soviet system's internal crisis.
[hist-eur4-s4.22.5] Soviet control of eastern Europe was kept by force in 1953, 1956 and 1968 and by proxy in 1981, but never by consent, and it was lost within months once Moscow would not use force.
