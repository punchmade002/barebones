[hist-eur2-s4.0.b] Serbia became fully independent in 1878. Its drive to unite the South Slavs threatened Austria-Hungary, and its link with Russia made a Serbian quarrel a Great Power matter.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.0] The Congress of Berlin (1878) recognised Serbia's full independence from the Ottoman Empire and enlarged its territory. Serbia became a kingdom in 1882 under the Obrenović dynasty.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.1] Austria-Hungary dominated Serbia's economy, which depended on exporting pigs and other livestock to the Habsburg market, and it kept Serbia in its sphere until the early 1900s.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.2] Serbian nationalists dreamed of uniting the South Slavs (Serbs, Croats, Slovenes and the Slavs of Bosnia) in a single state. This threatened a Habsburg empire with millions of South Slavs.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.3] In June 1903 a group of army officers murdered King Alexander Obrenović and Queen Draga in Belgrade. Peter I of the Karadjordjević dynasty became king.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.4] The new regime turned away from Vienna and towards Russia and France. Russia, the great Slav and Orthodox power, saw itself as protector of the Balkan Slavs and wanted to prevent Austrian control of the Balkans.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.5] The Pig War began in 1906, when Austria-Hungary closed its market to Serbian livestock to punish its trade links with France. Serbia found other markets, including Germany, Italy and France, and the war weakened Austrian influence.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.6] Austria-Hungary feared that a strong Serbia could encourage the Slav nationalities of the empire to leave, and Chief of Staff Conrad von Hötzendorf repeatedly urged a preventive war against it.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.0] Austria-Hungary had occupied and administered Bosnia-Herzegovina since 1878, though it remained under nominal Ottoman rule.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.1] The Young Turk revolution of July 1908 in the Ottoman Empire raised the possibility that Bosnia would be reclaimed, so Austria acted.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.2] In September 1908 the Austrian foreign minister Aehrenthal and the Russian foreign minister Izvolsky met at Buchlau and discussed a swap: Austria would annex Bosnia, and Russia would gain support for opening the Straits to its warships.

[hist-eur2-s4.1.4] Serbia, which regarded Bosnia as the heartland of Serb population, mobilised and demanded compensation; Serbian nationalism and anti-Austrian feeling grew, and the Narodna Odbrana society was formed to resist Austrian rule.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.5] In March 1909 Germany told Russia to accept the annexation and drop its support for Serbia, or face consequences. Russia, still weak after the war with Japan and revolution of 1905, gave way; Serbia also had to accept.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.6] The crisis humiliated Russia and Serbia, and left both resolved not to back down again. Russia accelerated its rearmament, and Austria-Hungary and Germany drew closer.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.0] In 1912 Russia helped to build the Balkan League of Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro, aimed at the Ottoman Empire. Italy had just taken Libya from the Ottomans in 1911–12.

[hist-eur2-s4.2.2] The Great Powers meeting at the London Conference (1912–13) created an independent Albania, supported by Austria-Hungary and Italy, which denied Serbia the Adriatic port it wanted.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.3] The League then quarrelled over the spoils. In the Second Balkan War (June to August 1913) Bulgaria attacked Serbia and Greece and was beaten by them, with Romania and the Ottomans joining in.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.4] By the Treaty of Bucharest (August 1913) Serbia doubled in size and took much of Macedonia, including Kosovo.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.5] A stronger Serbia, backed by Russia and unsatisfied over the Adriatic, alarmed Austria-Hungary further, and Conrad pressed for war against it.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.6] The Balkan Wars showed that a regional conflict could be contained by Great Power conference diplomacy (London 1912–13); but they left Austria-Hungary feeling that the next time it would have to fight.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.0] The Black Hand (Unification or Death) was a secret society of Serbian army officers founded in May 1911. Its leader was Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, chief of Serbian military intelligence.

[hist-eur2-s4.5.4] Jaurès, the main French pacifist, was assassinated in Paris by a nationalist on 31 July 1914.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.5] On 4 August 1914 the SPD Reichstag group voted for war credits. French socialists backed the 'sacred union' and joined the government, and British Labour largely supported the war.

[hist-eur2-s4.6.4] She spoke against war before 1914 and was jailed in 1914 for urging soldiers to disobey orders. When the SPD voted for war credits on 4 August 1914 she was shocked by its capitulation.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.5] With Karl Liebknecht she formed the International Group, later the Spartacus League (1916). From prison she wrote the Junius pamphlet (1915, published 1916), condemning the war as imperialist and the SPD's stance as betrayal.

[hist-eur2-s4.6.9] Contemporaries: the SPD leaders and conservatives viewed her as a dangerous agitator, 'Red Rosa'; to supporters, she was a courageous principled critic.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.10] Historians now see her as a major Marxist thinker and a critic of both militarism and dictatorship, although they argue over whether her ideas would have worked in practice.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s2: 2. The structures of European diplomacy, the New Imperialism and colonial rivalries, 1890–1914
[hist-eur2-s2.0.b] After Bismarck's fall, Germany's new leaders let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse. Russia and France, both isolated, drew together and ended France's isolation.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.0] Chancellor Caprivi and the Foreign Office official Holstein decided not to renew the Reinsurance Treaty in 1890, believing it was incompatible with the Dual Alliance and with good relations with Britain.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.1] Russia then turned to France: French banks began large loans to Russia from 1888 and the powers agreed to consult each other in a political understanding in 1891, after a French naval squadron visited Kronstadt.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.2] A military convention was agreed in August 1892 and the Franco-Russian Alliance was formally ratified in January 1894. It was a defensive alliance directed at the Triple Alliance powers.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.3] If France was attacked by Germany, or by Italy supported by Germany, Russia would attack Germany; if Russia was attacked by Germany, or by Austria-Hungary supported by Germany, France would attack Germany. Both would mobilise if any Triple Alliance member mobilised.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.4] The alliance gave Germany the risk of a war on two fronts, the danger Bismarck had tried to avoid, and it later shaped the Schlieffen Plan.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.5] The alliance was cemented by French investment in Russian railways and industry, and lasted until 1917.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.b] Britain stayed outside permanent alliances until the early twentieth century. The Boer War showed it had few friends, and it began to settle quarrels with its imperial rivals instead.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.0] Britain's policy of 'splendid isolation' lost its value after the Boer War, when most European opinion was hostile, and after Germany began to build a large navy.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.1] The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902) gave Britain support in the Far East against Russia and allowed it to concentrate its navy nearer home; it was renewed in 1905 and 1911.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.2] The Entente Cordiale (8 April 1904) settled colonial disputes: Britain was recognised in Egypt, France in Morocco, and the two settled questions over Siam, Madagascar, and the Newfoundland fisheries.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.3] The Entente was not an alliance, but after the first Moroccan crisis British and French military staffs began secret talks in 1906, and in 1912 their navies agreed to divide responsibilities in the Channel and the Mediterranean.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.4] The Anglo-Russian Entente (31 August 1907) settled rivalries in Asia: Persia was divided into a Russian northern zone, a British southern zone and a neutral middle; Afghanistan was recognised as a British sphere; both promised to stay out of Tibet.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.5] The Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia was therefore a loose understanding, without a common military commitment, facing the formal Triple Alliance.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.6] The Triple Alliance was weaker than it seemed: Italy signed a secret agreement with France in 1902 (the Prinetti-Barrère agreement) promising neutrality if France was attacked, and attacked the Ottoman Empire for Libya in 1911–12.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.7] From 1906 to 1914 each bloc grew more fearful of the other, and Germany spoke of 'encirclement' while Britain and France feared German ambitions; this made each crisis seem part of a struggle between the blocs.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.b] From about 1880 the European powers competed to acquire territory in Africa, Asia and the Pacific. Historians group the causes into economic, strategic, nationalist, missionary and ideological motives.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.0] Economic: industrial states needed raw materials such as rubber, cotton, palm oil, copper and later oil, and wanted protected markets when tariffs rose in Europe from the 1870s.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.1] Economic theory: the British writer J.A. Hobson argued in Imperialism: A Study (1902) that capitalists invested overseas because of surplus capital at home; Lenin built on this in Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1916). Historians dispute it, since most trade and investment went to areas outside the empires.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.2] Strategic: states sought naval bases, coaling stations and secure routes. Britain occupied Egypt in 1882 to protect the Suez Canal, the route to India, and then moved to control the Nile valley.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.3] Nationalist: colonies were seen as a mark of Great Power status, and newspapers, colonial societies and the public cheered each acquisition. After defeat in 1871, France sought compensation overseas under Jules Ferry.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.4] Missionary and humanitarian: churches wanted to spread Christianity and end the slave trade, and explorers such as David Livingstone and H.M. Stanley made Africa known to the public.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.5] Ideological: ideas of racial and cultural superiority, including Social Darwinism, and a belief in a duty to 'civilise' other peoples (France's mission civilisatrice; Kipling's poem The White Man's Burden, 1899) were used to justify rule.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.6] Technology made conquest easy: quinine against malaria, steamships, the telegraph and rifles and machine guns such as the Maxim gun gave Europeans overwhelming military advantage.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.7] Competition fed on itself: once one power claimed territory, others felt that they had to do the same or be shut out.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.0] In 1880 only about a tenth of Africa was under European control, mostly coastal areas such as Algeria, the Cape and Senegal; by 1914 about nine tenths of the continent had been taken.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.1] The scramble was triggered by Leopold II's claims on the Congo, France's advance in West Africa and Tunisia (1881), and Britain's occupation of Egypt in 1882.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.2] The Berlin Conference (November 1884 to February 1885), called by Bismarck and attended by fourteen powers including the United States, set rules for claims: notice to other powers, effective occupation, and freedom of trade on the Congo and Niger rivers.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.3] No African ruler was represented at the Conference, and it did not itself divide Africa, but it made the partition faster and gave it a legal shape.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.4] Britain's aim was a belt of territory from the Cape to Cairo, promoted by Cecil Rhodes, Prime Minister of the Cape Colony (1890–96); France sought to link its territories from Senegal to the Red Sea.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.5] These two ambitions crossed at Fashoda in 1898. Germany took South West Africa, Togo, Cameroon and East Africa; Italy took Eritrea and Somaliland and, in 1911–12, Libya.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.6] Africans resisted: Ethiopia defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa (1 March 1896), and only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent by 1914.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.0] Leopold II, King of the Belgians, founded the International African Association in 1876 and used Stanley to claim the Congo basin for himself.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.1] The Berlin Conference recognised the Congo Free State in 1885 as Leopold's personal possession, on the promise that he would end slavery and keep free trade.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.2] From the 1890s the state forced Africans to collect wild rubber under quotas, as demand rose with the bicycle and the motor car; failure was punished by hostage-taking, beatings, mutilation and the burning of villages.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.3] The Force Publique, the colonial army, enforced the system. Mortality rose sharply from violence, disease and starvation; the population fell by millions, though estimates vary widely.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.4] E.D. Morel, a shipping clerk who noticed the Congo's trade figures, and the British diplomat Roger Casement (whose 1904 report documented the abuses) led the Congo Reform Association (1904).
[hist-eur2-s2.4.5] Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness (1899) was based on his own time in the Congo, and Mark Twain wrote a satire on Leopold's rule.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.6] International pressure forced the Belgian parliament to take over the colony in 1908, ending Leopold's personal rule.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.0] France created French Indochina from Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos (1880s–1890s); Britain added Burma and Malaya; the Dutch held the East Indies.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.1] China was defeated by Japan in the Sino-Japanese War (1894–95). The powers then forced concessions: Germany leased Kiaochow (Tsingtao), Russia Port Arthur, Britain Weihaiwei and France Guangzhouwan, and each claimed a sphere of influence.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.2] The United States, which had defeated Spain in 1898 and taken the Philippines, Guam and Puerto Rico, proposed an 'Open Door' for trade in China (1899).
[hist-eur2-s2.5.3] The Boxer Rising (1899–1901) was a Chinese uprising against foreigners and Christians, which besieged the foreign legations in Beijing in 1900; an eight-nation force relieved them.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.4] Japan modernised after 1868, built a strong army and navy and defeated Russia in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05), the first time an Asian power beat a European Great Power in modern times.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.5] The defeat turned Russia's attention back towards the Balkans and Europe, and contributed to the revolution of 1905 (see the notes on Tsarist Russia).
[hist-eur2-s2.6.0] Fashoda: in July 1898 a small French expedition under Captain Marchand reached Fashoda on the Upper Nile in Sudan, hoping to claim a French East-West belt of Africa.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.1] In September 1898, after his victory at Omdurman, General Kitchener arrived with a far stronger force. Both sides refused to withdraw and war was feared.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.2] France, isolated and facing the Dreyfus Affair at home, backed down in November 1898, and an agreement in March 1899 set the boundary between British and French spheres.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.3] Fashoda poisoned Anglo-French relations until the settlement of 1904; but it also showed the danger of colonial clashes and encouraged both powers to solve their disputes.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.4] The Boer War began in October 1899 after disputes over British claims on the Boer republics, the Transvaal and the Orange Free State, whose gold and rights of foreign settlers were at issue; the failed Jameson Raid of 1895–96 had already soured relations.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.5] The Boers won early successes (Black Week, December 1899). Britain sent large reinforcements and, under Kitchener, burned farms and interned Boer civilians in camps, where over 20,000 died.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.6] The Treaty of Vereeniging (31 May 1902) ended the war and the two republics were absorbed into the British Empire.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.7] The war damaged British prestige, aroused hostility in Europe (Germany's Kruger telegram of 1896 had already caused outrage), and persuaded Britain to end its isolation.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.0] After the Entente Cordiale of 1904, France moved to control Morocco, one of the last independent states in Africa.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.1] Wilhelm II landed at Tangier on 31 March 1905 and spoke in support of Moroccan independence. Germany hoped to test the Entente and to win concessions from France.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.2] German pressure forced the resignation of the French foreign minister Delcassé in June 1905, but the crisis ended at the Algeciras Conference (January to April 1906).
[hist-eur2-s2.7.3] Algeciras was a diplomatic defeat for Germany: only Austria-Hungary supported it, Britain, Russia, Italy and the United States backed France, and France and Spain received the policing of Morocco's ports.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.4] The crisis strengthened the Entente: British and French generals began secret staff talks.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.5] In May 1911 France sent troops to Fez. Germany, in July 1911, sent the gunboat Panther to Agadir, demanding compensation, and Lloyd George warned in his Mansion House speech (21 July) that Britain would not be ignored.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.6] The Agadir crisis was settled in November 1911: Germany accepted French control of Morocco in return for part of the French Congo (the 'New Cameroon' strip).
[hist-eur2-s2.7.7] German opinion saw the settlement as a humiliation and Britain and France drew closer; the crisis led directly to the naval talks of 1912 between Britain and France.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.0] Imperial rivalry created crises (Fashoda, the Boer War and Morocco) that raised tension and made Britain and Germany suspicious of each other.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.1] Imperial agreements also reduced tension, as the Entente Cordiale and the Anglo-Russian Entente show; the powers were often able to settle colonial quarrels by compromise.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.2] Imperialism made the alliance blocs more rigid: the Moroccan crises turned the Entente Cordiale from a colonial settlement into a quasi-alliance.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.3] Imperial rivalries were mostly settled peacefully, so the war in 1914 came from the Balkans and Europe's own alliance system rather than from colonial clashes.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.4] Contemporaries in Germany blamed British and French encirclement for their sense of insecurity, while the British and French blamed German pressure for the Moroccan crises.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.5] Lenin and Hobson, writing from different positions, saw capitalism and imperialism as an underlying cause of the war; many later historians see imperialism as one factor alongside nationalism, militarism and alliances.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s1: 1. The Second Reich, Bismarck's foreign policy and the changing balance of power, 1871–1890
[hist-eur2-s1.0.b] The German Empire was proclaimed in 1871 after Prussia's wars against Denmark, Austria and France. It combined a strong monarchy with an elected parliament, and it upset the old balance of power in Europe.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.0] The German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, while Paris was still under siege in the Franco-Prussian War; King Wilhelm I of Prussia became German Emperor (Kaiser).
[hist-eur2-s1.0.1] The Empire was a federation of 25 states: four kingdoms (Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg), smaller duchies and principalities, and three free cities. Prussia held about two thirds of the territory and population.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.2] The Kaiser was hereditary King of Prussia, commanded the army, appointed and dismissed the Chancellor and could declare war with Bundesrat consent.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.3] The Chancellor was responsible to the Kaiser, not to parliament. Bismarck held the office from 1871 to 1890 and was also Prussian Minister-President for almost all of that time.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.4] The Bundesrat (federal council) represented the state governments. Prussia held 17 of its 58 votes in 1871, enough to block any proposed constitutional change.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.5] The Reichstag was elected by universal male suffrage (men aged 25 and over) by secret ballot. It could pass or reject laws and the budget, but it could not appoint or dismiss the Chancellor or ministers.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.6] The Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871) made France pay an indemnity of 5 billion francs and cede Alsace and part of Lorraine, including Strasbourg and Metz; German troops stayed until it was paid, which France did by 1873.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.7] A new Great Power in the centre of Europe, with a growing population and industry, changed the balance of power: France was weakened and embittered, while Britain, Russia and Austria-Hungary had to adjust to a united Germany.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.b] Bismarck governed by managing parliament and identifying 'enemies of the Reich' in turn. The Kulturkampf against the Catholic Church is covered in the notes on church-state tensions.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.0] Until 1878 Bismarck relied on the National Liberals in the Reichstag, who supported free trade and the Reich's unity.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.1] After two attempts on the life of Wilhelm I in May and June 1878 (by Hödel and Nobiling), Bismarck used the fear of revolution to pass the Anti-Socialist Law in October 1878. It banned socialist organisations, meetings and newspapers but still let socialists stand for the Reichstag.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.2] The law did not stop the SPD: the party's vote kept rising, and by the 1890 election it won about 1.4 million votes and 35 seats.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.3] The tariff of 1879 ended free trade by protecting German iron and steel and agricultural products. It split the National Liberals, and Bismarck now relied on the Conservatives and the Catholic Centre Party.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.4] To win workers away from socialism, Bismarck introduced state insurance: sickness insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old age and invalidity insurance (1889). Germany was the first major state to create such a scheme.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.5] Bismarck's mix of repression and welfare is often described as the 'carrot and stick'. The insurance schemes were limited in coverage and benefits, and they failed to reduce support for the SPD.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.6] Bismarck won army funding by repeated confrontations with the Reichstag; in the 1887 election, held after the Reichstag rejected the army bill (the Septennate), the pro-government Kartell of Conservatives and National Liberals won a majority.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.b] After 1871 Bismarck declared Germany a 'satiated' power that wanted no more territory in Europe. His aim was to keep the new Reich safe from a hostile coalition, above all one that included France.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.0] French feeling after the loss of Alsace-Lorraine produced revanche, the desire for revenge and the recovery of the lost provinces; Bismarck assumed that France would never become a reliable friend.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.1] His strategy was to keep France without allies by keeping the other powers friendly with Germany or at odds with each other. Contemporaries spoke of his fear of coalitions against Germany.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.2] Bismarck wanted the Great Powers to feel that they needed good relations with Berlin more than with each other; he also steered rivalries towards the periphery of Europe, such as the Balkans and Africa.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.3] In the War-in-Sight crisis of spring 1875 a German newspaper article suggested that Germany might attack France to stop its rearmament (France had passed a new army law). Britain and Russia warned Germany to hold back; historians disagree on whether Bismarck was really planning war or merely bluffing.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.4] The crisis showed Bismarck that Britain and Russia would not allow France to be crushed a second time, and it fed his fear of a hostile coalition.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.5] Franco-German tension flared again in 1886–87 under General Boulanger, a popular war minister who stirred up revanchist feeling, and in the Schnaebelé affair (April 1887), when a French frontier official was arrested by the Germans and later released.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.6] Bismarck backed French colonial expansion, such as the occupation of Tunisia in 1881, and cooperated briefly with France against Britain over Egypt and Africa in 1884–85, to turn French attention away from Alsace-Lorraine.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.0] The Three Emperors' League (October 1873) linked Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia as a conservative understanding to resist revolution and settle disputes among the three monarchies.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.1] The League was only an understanding, and it came under strain in the Balkan crisis of 1875–78, when Christian peoples rose against Ottoman rule and Russia fought Turkey in 1877–78.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.2] The Treaty of San Stefano (March 1878) created a large Bulgaria and gave Russia great influence in the Balkans. Austria-Hungary and Britain refused to accept it.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.3] At the Congress of Berlin (13 June to 13 July 1878), chaired by Bismarck, who called himself an 'honest broker', the powers cut Bulgaria down and divided it into three parts.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.4] The Congress recognised the full independence of Serbia, Montenegro and Romania. Austria-Hungary was allowed to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina, and Britain took Cyprus; Russia kept Bessarabia and gains in the Caucasus.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.5] Russians felt that Bismarck had sided against them at Berlin and had cheated them of the fruits of their victory over Turkey; Russian newspapers attacked Germany, and the League lapsed.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.6] The League was renewed secretly in June 1881 for three years (and again in 1884), with promises of neutrality if one member went to war with a fourth power and agreed Balkan spheres. It broke down in 1887 over the crisis in Bulgaria.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.0] The Dual Alliance of 7 October 1879 was a secret defensive treaty between Germany and Austria-Hungary: each promised to help the other if attacked by Russia, and to stay neutral if attacked by another power, unless Russia joined that power.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.1] Bismarck signed it to guard against a Russian attack and to bind Austria-Hungary to Germany; Wilhelm I accepted it reluctantly because it seemed to threaten his friendship with Tsar Alexander II.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.2] The Dual Alliance lasted until 1918 and was the permanent core of Germany's alliance system.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.3] The Triple Alliance (20 May 1882) added Italy, which was angered by France's occupation of Tunisia in 1881 and wanted support against France and recognition as a Great Power.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.4] Under the Triple Alliance, Germany and Austria-Hungary promised help if Italy was attacked by France; Italy would help Germany if attacked by France, and all three would act together if any faced two or more Great Powers.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.5] Italy declared that it would not fight Britain, and its relations with Austria-Hungary were poor because of the Italian-speaking territories (Trentino and Trieste) in Habsburg hands.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.6] The treaty was renewed regularly until 1914; Italy stayed out of the war in August 1914 and joined the Allies in May 1915.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.0] The crisis in Bulgaria (1885–87), where Russia and Austria-Hungary competed for influence after Prince Alexander of Battenberg was forced to abdicate in 1886, ended the Three Emperors' League in 1887.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.1] Bismarck replaced it with the Reinsurance Treaty of 18 June 1887, a secret agreement between Germany and Russia for three years. Each promised neutrality if the other was at war with a third power, except if Germany attacked France or Russia attacked Austria-Hungary.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.2] Germany also recognised Russia's interests in Bulgaria and promised diplomatic support over the Straits.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.3] Bismarck did not tell Austria-Hungary or Italy of the treaty, and it can be argued that it conflicted in spirit with the Dual Alliance.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.4] In November 1887 Bismarck banned the Reichsbank from accepting Russian bonds as security for loans (the Lombardverbot). Russia turned to France for loans, which helped to bring the two powers together.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.5] Britain, Italy and Austria-Hungary signed the Mediterranean Agreements in 1887 to hold the status quo in the Mediterranean and Near East, with Bismarck's encouragement. This tied Britain loosely to the Triple Alliance powers.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.6] The Reinsurance Treaty was not renewed in 1890 after Bismarck's fall, and Russia moved towards France (see the notes on the structures of European diplomacy).
[hist-eur2-s1.6.b] Bismarck was not an enthusiast for colonies. He changed his mind in 1884, partly because of pressure from merchants and the colonial movement and partly for diplomatic reasons.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.0] In 1884–85 Germany claimed South West Africa (Lüderitz), Togoland, the Cameroons, German East Africa and territory in the Pacific including north-east New Guinea.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.1] Bismarck preferred protectorates run by chartered companies at their own cost rather than colonies governed by the state.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.2] The colonial claims brought friction with Britain, especially over South West Africa and East Africa, and the Gladstone government protested; Bismarck used the disputes to build pressure on Britain and to cooperate with France.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.3] Bismarck summoned the Berlin Conference (November 1884 to February 1885) on West Africa; it set rules for the partition of the continent (see the notes on the New Imperialism).
[hist-eur2-s1.6.4] Relations with Britain improved after Lord Salisbury took office in 1885. Bismarck tried to keep Britain friendly through the Mediterranean Agreements and hoped that colonial quarrels would not damage the relationship.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.5] Anglo-German tension was also dynastic: the future Frederick III's wife, Victoria, was Queen Victoria's daughter, and Bismarck distrusted the liberal influence of the English-born crown princess.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.6] Bismarck is said to have told an explorer in 1888 that his map of Africa lay in Europe, meaning that colonies mattered to him mainly for their effect on European diplomacy.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.0] Wilhelm I died on 9 March 1888 aged 90. His son Frederick III, a liberal who was fatally ill with throat cancer, reigned for 99 days and died on 15 June 1888.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.1] The new Kaiser, Wilhelm II, aged 29, wanted to rule personally and disagreed with Bismarck about handling the workers; he wanted to ease the anti-socialist laws, which Bismarck wanted to make permanent.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.2] The Anti-Socialist Law was due to expire in September 1890. After the Reichstag election of February 1890, in which the Kartell parties lost seats and the SPD vote grew, Bismarck mooted a coup d'etat to change the constitution.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.3] Wilhelm II also objected to Bismarck's attempt to limit the ministers' access to the Kaiser through the Cabinet Order of 1852, and to the Chancellor's talks with the Catholic Centre leader Windthorst.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.4] Bismarck offered his resignation on 18 March 1890, after the Kaiser made it clear that he would be dismissed otherwise. It was accepted two days later, and General Leo von Caprivi became Chancellor.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.5] The British magazine Punch published the cartoon 'Dropping the Pilot' by John Tenniel on 29 March 1890, showing the Kaiser watching the old pilot leave the ship.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.6] In the following months the Reinsurance Treaty was not renewed and Germany signed the Heligoland-Zanzibar treaty with Britain (July 1890).
[hist-eur2-s1.8.b] Bismarck made the unification of Germany possible in the 1860s, and then governed it as Chancellor until 1890. This block covers the Chancellorship; his earlier career belongs to the notes on unification.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.0] Bismarck (1815–98) came from a Junker landowning family at Schönhausen in Prussia, and became Minister-President of Prussia in 1862.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.1] He was made Prince in 1871 and served as Chancellor under Wilhelm I, Frederick III and Wilhelm II. He controlled foreign policy personally, and most of the major policy decisions of the Empire were his.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.2] He saw himself as a conservative protecting the monarchy and Prussian power; he used nationalism, tariffs and social insurance to keep the new Reich stable.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.3] Contemporaries admired the 'Iron Chancellor' who had made Germany great: nationalists and conservatives adored him, while Catholics, Liberals and socialists suffered under his measures and called them persecution.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.4] Foreign observers respected him as a master diplomat. Punch's cartoon of 1890 and the many Bismarck monuments erected after his death show how large he loomed.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.5] After his dismissal he used the press to criticise Wilhelm II's governments, and published his memoirs, Thoughts and Recollections. He died on 30 July 1898 at Friedrichsruh.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.6] Historians are divided. A.J.P. Taylor and others praised his skill as a diplomat who kept the peace; Lothar Gall called him a 'white revolutionary' who changed Europe from above.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.7] Hans-Ulrich Wehler argued that Bismarck's rule was a kind of Bonapartist dictatorship that kept the old elites in power by 'negative integration', uniting the Reich against domestic 'enemies' such as Catholics and socialists; critics say it left Germany with a weak parliamentary tradition.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.0] Foreign success: Germany fought no war after 1871 and avoided a two-front war while Bismarck was in office.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.1] Foreign limit: the system was complicated and depended on Bismarck personally; the Reinsurance Treaty and the Dual Alliance pulled in opposite directions, and a successor could not keep all the balls in the air.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.2] Foreign limit: the Dual Alliance committed Germany to Austria-Hungary in a Balkan region where Habsburg and Russian interests clashed, and the treatment of Russia in 1878 and 1887 helped to push it towards France.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.3] Foreign success: the Congress of Berlin avoided a European war in 1878, and Bismarck was able to turn colonial rivalry to Germany's advantage.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.4] Domestic success: he created a unified state, a national market, a common currency (the mark, 1873), a central bank (the Reichsbank) and the first national state insurance system in Europe.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.5] Domestic failure: the Kulturkampf and the Anti-Socialist Law did not break the Catholic Church or the SPD; both emerged stronger, and the Reichstag remained weak.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.6] Contemporary view: Germans in 1890 saw a statesman who had made Germany one of the strongest states in Europe.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.7] Later view: historians stress the unfinished nature of his domestic settlement, which left his successors with an unreformed constitution and a divided society.
