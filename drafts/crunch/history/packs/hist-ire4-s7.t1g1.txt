[hist-ire4-s6.9.2] Church schools taught Catholic children their faith and gave the second generation a grounding in parish and community life.
[hist-ire4-s6.9.3] The second generation often moved towards American or Australian culture, so the church and GAA supported Irishness mainly for those who wanted it.
[hist-ire4-s6.9.4] Catholic and Orange rivalry in Canada and Australia shaped the public life of Irish communities, so sectarian tension was a constant feature of emigrant life.
[hist-ire4-s6.9.5] The Catholic Church encouraged loyalty to the new country as well as faith, so it was a vehicle of assimilation as well as of Irishness.
[hist-ire4-s6.10.b] Judging how far these institutions sustained Irishness means weighing what they achieved against who they missed. Contemporaries and later historians also disagreed about their effects.
[hist-ire4-s6.10.0] Achievement: the Catholic Church and the GAA gave Irish emigrants a community, a social life and an Irish identity that lasted for generations. The church was the stronger of the two because it reached nearly every Catholic, while the GAA was narrower, more male and sporting.
[hist-ire4-s6.10.1] Limit: many emigrants' children assimilated, so their Irishness weakened, and the institutions reached only part of the emigrant population.
[hist-ire4-s6.10.2] Contemporary view: Irish nationalists praised the church and the GAA as guardians of Irishness, while nativists and imperial loyalists regarded them as divisive.
[hist-ire4-s6.10.3] Later view: historians argue that the church and the GAA preserved identity but also helped Irish communities to organise and gain power in their new societies.
[hist-ire4-s6.10.4] Protestant and Orange identity achieved political influence in Canada and Australia, but it expressed loyalty to the British connection, not Irish nationalism.
[hist-ire4-s6.10.5] Sectarian rivalry was exported with the emigrants: Catholic against Orange in Canada, Australia, Britain and the USA.
