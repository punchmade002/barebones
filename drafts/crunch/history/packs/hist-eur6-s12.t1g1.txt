[hist-eur6-s1.0.b] The US Constitution (drafted 1787) divides power between the federal government in Washington and the fifty state governments. [hist-eur6-s1.0.0] The Bill of Rights (1791) added the Tenth Amendment, which reserves powers not given to the federal government to the states, so states controlled schools, policing, voting rules and marriage law.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.1] Before the 1960s most southern states enforced segregation (the separation of black and white people, often called Jim Crow) by state law, and federal action against it was met with the argument that such matters belonged to the states. This argument is known as states' rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.2] In 1948 southern Democrats angry at Truman's civil rights programme left the party and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party (the Dixiecrats) with Strom Thurmond as candidate. It carried four Deep South states: Alabama, Louisiana, Mississippi and South Carolina.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.3] The Civil Rights Act 1957 was the first federal civil rights law since Reconstruction (the years after the Civil War when the North tried to protect black rights in the South). It set up a Civil Rights Commission and let the Justice Department act over voting rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.4] In September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus of Arkansas used the National Guard to stop nine black students entering Little Rock Central High School. [hist-eur6-s1.0.5] The Civil Rights Act, signed on 2 July 1964, rested on the federal power to regulate interstate commerce (trade between states), which allowed Congress to ban segregation in hotels, restaurants and other public places even though they were privately owned.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.6] The Voting Rights Act, signed on 6 August 1965, suspended literacy tests used to keep black people off the voting lists and sent federal examiners to register voters. It put federal law above state practice, and it is a separate law from the 1957 and 1964 Acts.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.b] The Constitution gives law-making, executing and judging to three different bodies, so that no one can rule alone. Each can also limit the others, a system called checks and balances, and the period from 1945 to 1989 shows each check being used.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.0] Congress (the Senate and House of Representatives) makes laws, raises taxes and declares war; the President executes the laws, commands the armed forces as commander in chief and conducts foreign policy; the Supreme Court interprets the Constitution.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.1] The President can veto (refuse to sign) bills, but Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds majority in both houses. The Senate must also confirm senior appointments and ratify treaties by a two-thirds vote, which stops a President acting alone.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.2] Republicans won both houses of Congress in the November 1946 midterm elections (held halfway through a presidential term), using the slogan "Had enough?". It was the first Republican control since the 1928 elections, so Truman faced a hostile Congress until January 1949.

[hist-eur6-s1.5.3] As vice president from January 1945 he was not told of the atomic bomb project until he became president on 12 April 1945, so he relied heavily on advisers at first.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.4] In September 1945 he sent Congress a 21-point programme of reforms that continued the New Deal, but reconversion, inflation and strikes blocked most of it. In 1947 Congress overrode his veto of the Taft-Hartley Act.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.5] His civil rights programme began with the report To Secure These Rights (October 1947) from the President's Committee on Civil Rights. Executive Order 9981 (26 July 1948) then ordered the desegregation of the armed forces, which a president could do without Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.6] His loyalty programme (Executive Order 9835, March 1947) required checks on federal employees for disloyalty. It fed the wider Red Scare, the fear of communists in America, and is judged damaging to civil liberties.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.7] In January 1949 he outlined the Fair Deal: national health insurance, a wider Social Security system, a higher minimum wage and public housing. Congress blocked national health insurance and most other proposals; the Housing Act of 1949 and a rise in the minimum wage were among the main successes.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.b] Truman's own combative style won the 1948 election against the odds, but the Korean War, prices and scandal wore down his popularity. Contemporaries and historians have therefore judged him very differently.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.0] In 1948 he beat Thomas Dewey, 303 electoral votes to 189, even though the Democrats split three ways: Thurmond's Dixiecrats on the right and Henry Wallace's Progressives on the left.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.1] He ran a combative cross-country whistle-stop train campaign (speaking from the back of a train at small towns) attacking the Republican Congress as a do-nothing Congress, so the hostile Congress became his best argument.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.2] The Chicago Daily Tribune printed the headline "Dewey Defeats Truman" before the result was known, which is why the upset became famous.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.3] Contemporaries were often dismissive: the 1946 joke "To err is Truman" mocked his handling of strikes and prices, and his Gallup approval rating fell to about 22 per cent in early 1952.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.4] By 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply because of the Korean War, rising prices and corruption scandals in several federal agencies, and he announced in March 1952 that he would not seek re-election.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.5] Historians since the 1970s, and David McCullough's biography Truman (1992), have generally ranked him among the stronger presidents for his decisiveness and his civil rights initiatives.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.6] They also judge his domestic record more limited than he hoped and his loyalty programme damaging to civil liberties.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.b] After Truman, the Republican Eisenhower accepted the New Deal's main structures rather than undoing them, and Kennedy then promised a more active government. Both presidents faced the federal-state conflict over civil rights, and Kennedy's death left his bills to Johnson.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.0] Eisenhower, a Republican and former Allied commander in the Second World War, called his approach Modern Republicanism. He accepted most New Deal programmes rather than dismantling them, though he was not a New Dealer himself.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.1] He extended Social Security to about 10 million more workers in 1954 and created the Department of Health, Education and Welfare in 1953, so the New Deal's welfare structures grew under a Republican.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.2] The Federal-Aid Highway Act of June 1956 funded an Interstate Highway System of about 41,000 miles, the largest federal public works programme of the period, which shows a Republican using federal money for a national project.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.3] In his Farewell Address (January 1961) Eisenhower warned against the "military-industrial complex", the close link between the armed forces and the arms industry, which he feared could gain too much influence.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.4] Kennedy won the 1960 election narrowly over Richard Nixon, and his New Frontier, presented in his nomination speech of July 1960, promised a more active federal role at home and in space.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.5] Kennedy's tax cut was proposed in January 1963 and passed as the Revenue Act of February 1964 under Johnson, cutting the top income tax rate from 91 to 70 per cent. His civil rights bill was sent to Congress in June 1963 but only passed after his assassination.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.6] Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas on 22 November 1963; Lyndon Johnson was sworn in as president on Air Force One that afternoon, so the same day saw both the end of one presidency and the start of the next.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.b] Johnson's domestic programme was the largest federal social effort since the New Deal. He used Kennedy's death and his own legislative skill to pass a flood of laws in 1964 and 1965, until the Vietnam War began to drain money and attention from it.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.0] Johnson, Senate Majority Leader from 1955 to 1961, used his legislative skills (the persuasive "Johnson treatment") and the national mood after Kennedy's death to pass Kennedy's stalled bills.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.1] The Civil Rights Act of 2 July 1964 banned segregation in public places and discrimination in employment on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.2] In a speech at the University of Michigan on 22 May 1964, Johnson set out the Great Society, a programme to end poverty and racial injustice.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.3] The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 launched the War on Poverty, which included Job Corps (job training for young people) and community action programmes.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.4] In the 1964 election Johnson won about 61 per cent of the popular vote against Barry Goldwater, who carried only six states, giving him a huge majority in Congress to pass further laws.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.5] The Voting Rights Act of 6 August 1965 suspended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters in areas where discrimination was proven. Medicare for the elderly and Medicaid for the poor were signed into law on 30 July 1965.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.6] The Elementary and Secondary Education Act (April 1965) and the Higher Education Act (1965) provided federal aid to schools and colleges, and Head Start for poor pre-school children began in 1965. The Immigration and Nationality Act (1965) ended national-origin quotas.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.7] The Department of Housing and Urban Development was created in 1965, giving the federal government a cabinet department for cities and housing.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.8] The cost of the Vietnam War from 1965 drained money and attention from the Great Society, and Republican gains in the 1966 midterms slowed new legislation.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.b] Between 1969 and 1981 three presidents faced scandal, recession and inflation, and the public's trust in the presidency fell. Watergate was the turning point, because it forced a resignation and showed Congress and the Court could check a President.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.0] Richard Nixon won the 1968 election narrowly and was re-elected in 1972 by carrying 49 states, so his fall in 1974 came soon after a landslide.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.1] Nixon's domestic record included the Environmental Protection Agency (1970), wage and price controls (August 1971) to fight inflation, and the expansion of affirmative action (favouring minorities) in federal contracts.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.2] Five men were arrested for burgling the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate complex on 17 June 1972, and the Washington Post's reporting kept the scandal in the news. Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974, after the Supreme Court ordered him to hand over his tapes.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.3] Gerald Ford, who had become vice president in December 1973 after Spiro Agnew resigned, became president and pardoned Nixon on 8 September 1974. The pardon damaged his standing, and with inflation and recession in 1974 to 1975 he lost narrowly to Carter in 1976.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.4] Jimmy Carter, a Democrat from Georgia, won the 1976 election and negotiated the Panama Canal treaties of 1977, under which the canal passed to Panama at the end of 1999.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.5] The Camp David Accords of September 1978, brokered by Carter, led to a peace treaty between Egypt and Israel signed in March 1979. Carter also created the Departments of Energy (1977) and Education (1979).
[hist-eur6-s1.9.6] High inflation and the 1979 oil shock undermined Carter. His televised speech of July 1979 on a "crisis of confidence", nicknamed the malaise speech, was seen by critics as blaming the public.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.7] The Iran hostage crisis held 52 American diplomats and citizens in Iran from 4 November 1979 to 20 January 1981, 444 days. An attempted rescue in April 1980 failed, and the crisis was a major cause of Carter's defeat.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.b] Ronald Reagan won in 1980 promising smaller government and tax cuts, reversing the liberal direction of the New Deal and Great Society. His first term set the conservative agenda, and his second was clouded by Iran-Contra.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.0] Ronald Reagan defeated Carter in 1980 and was re-elected in 1984, carrying 49 states against Walter Mondale, which shows how popular he stayed.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.1] In his first inaugural address (20 January 1981) Reagan said that "government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem", which summed up his case against federal power.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.2] The Economic Recovery Tax Act of August 1981 cut income tax rates by about 25 per cent over three years and lowered the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.3] Reagan's first term combined cuts to many domestic social programmes with a large rise in defence spending, a pattern known as Reaganomics.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.4] When air traffic controllers in the PATCO union struck in August 1981, Reagan dismissed the strikers, signalling a hard line on public sector unions, because the strikers worked for the federal government.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.5] Iran-Contra was revealed in November 1986: the administration had sold arms to Iran and diverted the money to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua despite congressional restrictions, the Boland Amendments.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.6] The Tower Commission (February 1987) criticised Reagan's detached management style. National Security Adviser John Poindexter and his aide Oliver North were among those prosecuted.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.7] Reagan's popularity survived the scandal and he left office in January 1989 with high approval ratings, earning the nickname "the Teflon president" because scandals did not stick to him.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.b] Judging presidents means weighing what each achieved against where each failed, and contemporary verdicts often differ from later ones. A good answer gives a stated judgement with dated evidence on each side.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.0] Truman: his civil rights initiative, including desegregating the armed forces, and his leadership in the early Cold War (the Marshall Plan and NATO) are usually seen as successes.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.1] Truman's critics point to the failure to pass national health insurance, because a hostile Congress blocked it, and to the unpopularity of the Korean War.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.2] Johnson: the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts and Medicare are counted as major achievements, while his Vietnam policy is widely judged a failure that drained the Great Society.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.3] Nixon and Ford: Nixon's opening to China and his arms control agreements are credited as achievements, but Watergate and the secret bombing of Cambodia are judged serious failings, and Ford's pardon of Nixon cost him public trust.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.4] Yet the Court's ruling and the War Powers Resolution show that Congress and the Court acted as effective checks on the President.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.5] Carter: the Camp David Accords are judged a success, while the Iran hostage crisis, inflation and his 1980 defeat are the main charges against him.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.6] Reagan: supporters credit his tax cuts and conservative revival, and they claim he helped to end the Cold War, which is their view and not settled fact. Critics point to the rising deficit, the 1981 to 1982 recession and Iran-Contra.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.7] Public opinion polls made presidential popularity visible from the 1930s onwards, and the Watergate and Iran-Contra scandals led Congress to reassert its oversight of presidents.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.8] Contemporary verdicts often differed from later ones: Truman left office in 1953 deeply unpopular, yet his reputation rose sharply in later historians' rankings, as did Eisenhower's, while Johnson's soared in 1964 and collapsed by 1968.
