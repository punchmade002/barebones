[hist-ire3-s8.4.4] The Irish government complained when Britain restricted supplies of coal, wheat and shipping to Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s8.4.5] David Gray, the American Minister in Dublin, was strongly critical of neutrality, and the United States pressed the government after it entered the war in December 1941.
[hist-ire3-s8.4.6] In February 1944 the United States and Britain presented the American Note, asking the government to expel the German and Japanese diplomats before the D-Day landings.
[hist-ire3-s8.4.7] De Valera refused, and Britain restricted travel to Ireland in the following months.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.b] In practice neutrality was benevolent towards the Allies. At the same time, the state suffered from German bombing.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.0] Irish military intelligence (G2) shared information with British intelligence and the Garda watched German agents; the spy Hermann Görtz was caught and interned.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.1] Allied aircraft were allowed to fly over Donegal to the Atlantic, and Allied airmen who crashed in Ireland were quietly released, while Germans were interned in the Curragh.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.2] Weather reports from Blacksod Point lighthouse, Co. Mayo, in June 1944 contributed to the decision on the date of D-Day.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.3] About 50,000 to 70,000 people from the Free State served in the British forces; soldiers who deserted from the Irish army to join them were barred from public employment in 1945.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.4] German aircraft bombed Wexford (Campile creamery, August 1940, three killed) and other places.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.5] On the night of 31 May 1941 German bombers dropped bombs on the North Strand in Dublin, killing about thirty people (estimates range from 28 to 34), and destroying or damaging many houses.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.6] The government protested to Germany; Germany said it was an error, and West Germany paid compensation after the war.
[hist-ire3-s8.5.7] Dublin and other fire brigades went north to help Belfast in April 1941, with the approval of de Valera.
[hist-ire3-s8.6.b] De Valera's insistence on diplomatic correctness to the end brought a public attack from Churchill.
[hist-ire3-s8.6.0] On 2 May 1945, after Hitler's death was announced, de Valera called on the German minister Eduard Hempel to express condolences.
[hist-ire3-s8.6.1] He had paid a similar visit to the American Minister on the death of Roosevelt in April 1945.
[hist-ire3-s8.6.2] De Valera defended the visit as diplomatic protocol that a neutral state had followed throughout the war, but many Irish and Allied commentators criticised it.
[hist-ire3-s8.6.3] In his victory broadcast of 13 May 1945 Churchill attacked Irish neutrality and referred to the ports.
[hist-ire3-s8.6.4] De Valera replied on Radio Éireann on 16 May, saying that Churchill's speech had shown how a great power might justify infringing a small state's rights, and that Ireland had stood alone.
[hist-ire3-s8.6.5] The broadcast was widely praised in Ireland and strengthened de Valera's reputation, though it did not improve relations with Britain.
[hist-ire3-s8.7.b] The war closed Irish ports and imposed austerity, but opened the British labour market.
[hist-ire3-s8.7.0] Tens of thousands of workers went to Britain each year to work in factories and construction, and sent money home.
[hist-ire3-s8.7.1] The Irish economy suffered from shortages, and unemployment remained high for those who stayed.
[hist-ire3-s8.7.2] Prices rose faster than wages, and the poor in Dublin and other towns suffered most.
[hist-ire3-s8.7.3] The government used its emergency powers to maintain control of the press and labour, with little organised protest.
[hist-ire3-s8.7.4] The Children's Allowances Act of 1944 and the creation of a separate Department of Health (1947) showed the first steps in post-war social policy.
[hist-ire3-s8.7.5] Many Irish women and men served in the British forces, and the Irish government gave them little recognition after the war.
[hist-ire3-s8.8.b] Judgements depend on whether the priority is sovereignty and survival or the moral and economic costs.
[hist-ire3-s8.8.0] Achievement: the state avoided invasion and bombing on the scale of Belfast and kept its independence.
[hist-ire3-s8.8.1] Achievement: neutrality was popular and strengthened national unity after the Civil War.
[hist-ire3-s8.8.2] Limit: shortages, wage controls and censorship reduced living standards and freedom of expression.
[hist-ire3-s8.8.3] Limit: the policy isolated Ireland from the Allies and left it without a voice at the end of the war.
[hist-ire3-s8.8.4] Contemporary view: supporters called it a principled defence of independence; critics said it was selfish, as Churchill suggested.
[hist-ire3-s8.8.5] Later view: historians such as Joe Lee and Brian Girvin stress the practical advantages of neutrality and its pro-Allied bias, but also question the moral cost.
