TARGET hist-eur3-s8: 8. Case study: the Jarrow March, October 1936
[hist-eur3-s8.0.b] Jarrow was a one-industry town on the south bank of the Tyne in County Durham, and the loss of its shipyard in the early 1930s left most of its working men without work.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.0] Palmers Shipbuilding and Iron Company was founded in Jarrow in 1851 and grew into a yard and steelworks that dominated the town; it launched its thousandth ship in July 1930.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.1] Almost the whole local economy depended on the yard, so when orders collapsed in the Depression, shops, trades and families that depended on its wages collapsed with it.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.2] World shipbuilding was in slump: orders fell with world trade, and British yards had far more capacity than orders, which was the thinking behind the National Shipbuilders Security scheme.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.3] National Shipbuilders Security Ltd (NSS) was formed in 1930, backed by the shipbuilders with the support of the Bank of England, to buy and close redundant yards, financed by a levy on the shipbuilding industry.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.4] Palmers launched its last ship, the destroyer HMS Duchess, on 19 July 1932; creditors appointed a receiver in June 1933.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.5] NSS bought the yard in 1934 and began to dismantle it, and sites sold by NSS were barred from shipbuilding for at least forty years, which ruled out any revival of the yard.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.6] In 1934 the American businessman T. Vosper Salt proposed a modern steelworks on the Palmers site, but the British Iron and Steel Federation, which feared competition and a precedent for other areas, diluted the plan until it was unworkable; Wilkinson later said that it had been strangled at birth.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.7] Unemployment in the town was far higher than the national average, with well over half of insured workers on the dole in the mid-1930s and figures of around two-thirds often quoted, compared with about 17 per cent in the north-east in 1936 and about 33 per cent in shipbuilding.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.8] Jarrow had one of the worst records for poor housing, hunger and ill health in Britain, and Wilkinson later called it the town that was murdered; the Special Areas Act of 1934 had given it little help.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.b] The march was run by the town council with cross-party support, and its public face was the town's Labour MP.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.0] Ellen Wilkinson, known as Red Ellen, was a former Communist, a trade union organiser and Labour MP for Middlesbrough East from 1924 to 1931; she was elected for Jarrow in November 1935.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.1] She backed the march as MP and did much of the publicity and parliamentary work, but she left it at Chester-le-Street to attend the Labour Party conference in Edinburgh and joined it again later, so she was not on foot for the whole way.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.2] Councillor David Riley, the Labour leader on the council, was the leader and organiser of the marchers on the road.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.3] Jarrow Borough Council backed the march, and Conservatives, Liberals and Labour on the council supported it, so it was presented as a cross-party appeal by the whole town, not a party or Communist protest.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.4] Local clergy blessed the march at a departure service, and churches and Co-operative societies along the route provided food and beds.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.b] The organisers called the march a crusade and prepared it carefully to give it a respectable character.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.0] Their aim was a petition asking the government to bring new industry to Jarrow, above all a steelworks, and to end the distress of the town.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.1] The marchers were chosen from more than a thousand volunteers; about 200 men set out, although records name fewer, and each was examined by a doctor for fitness.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.2] The men carried a petition of over 11,000 signatures in an oak box, and a banner reading Jarrow Crusade, and a mouth organ band set the pace.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.3] The marchers were disciplined, wore good boots donated by supporters, and were organised in a way to show that the unemployed were responsible citizens who wanted work.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.4] The Labour Party and the TUC did not back the march, in part because they feared that it would be taken over by Communists and in part because they preferred parliamentary pressure; Labour's conference in Edinburgh in October 1936 gave it little support.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.5] The Communist-led National Unemployed Workers' Movement had its own tradition of hunger marches, and the Jarrow organisers deliberately kept apart from it.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.b] The men walked from the north-east to London, relying on the hospitality of towns along the way.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.0] The marchers set out from Jarrow on 5 October 1936 after a service and a send-off by crowds.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.1] They walked by way of Chester-le-Street, Darlington, Ripon, Harrogate, Leeds, Barnsley, Sheffield, Chesterfield, Nottingham, Leicester, Market Harborough, Northampton, Bedford, Luton and St Albans to London, a distance of about 290 miles.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.2] They covered about 15 miles a day, slept in church halls, schools and workhouses, and ate at meals provided by supporters and local Co-operative societies.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.3] In many towns, mayors, councils and Labour parties welcomed them, and the Liberal and Conservative leaders in some places joined in, although some local Conservatives distanced themselves.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.4] They arrived at Marble Arch in London on 31 October 1936, after which the petition was handed to Parliament.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.5] The press and newsreel coverage, much of it sympathetic, made the march a national story.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.b] The government met the march with a refusal to act, but the petition itself was received.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.0] Stanley Baldwin, the Prime Minister, declined to meet a deputation from Jarrow on 29 October 1936, and cited a fall in Jarrow's unemployment.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.1] Wilkinson answered that the fall was due to combining the figures for Jarrow with those of South Shields, which hid the true numbers.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.2] Wilkinson presented the petition in the House of Commons on 4 November 1936, but it was not debated and the government made no new commitment.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.3] The Unemployment Assistance Board ruled that the marchers' benefit should be reduced on the grounds that they were not available for work while they marched.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.4] The marchers went home by train on 5 November 1936, when crowds welcomed them in Jarrow.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.5] The government's argument was that national recovery would restore jobs, and it questioned whether special help for one town was fair when many areas suffered.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.b] The march brought no immediate results; recovery came with rearmament later in the decade.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.0] Sir John Jarvis, a Conservative MP and businessman, helped to attract small firms to the Palmers site, and by 1939 about 100 men worked in a furniture factory and up to 500 in metal industries.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.1] The Special Areas (Amendment) Act of 1937 provided tax reliefs for firms setting up in the depressed areas, but it was already being prepared and historians do not claim that the march caused it.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.2] Rearmament and the Second World War brought real employment back to Tyneside, and Jarrow's recovery owed far more to war orders than to the march.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.3] Wilkinson became Minister of Education in the Labour government of 1945 and died in 1947.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.4] Many marchers felt they had failed; one called it a waste of time, and David Riley and some councillors briefly left the Labour Party after the march.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.b] The march is remembered more than it achieved.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.0] Contemporaries sympathetic to the march praised the discipline and dignity of the men, and Labour later adopted its banners as a symbol of the injustice of the 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.1] Critics in the government and the press argued that the march was political theatre and could not solve structural problems.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.2] The march helped to form the postwar belief that the 1930s were a time of government failure, which contributed to support for full employment policy and the welfare state after 1945.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.3] The march was re-enacted in 1986 and 2011, and it remains one of the best-known symbols of the Depression in Britain.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.4] As a documents-based topic, sources such as photographs of the marchers, newspaper reports, Wilkinson's speeches and government statements show how supporters and critics saw the same event.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.b] The march took place in a month of major events, which affected how it was reported and received.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.0] The National Government under Stanley Baldwin (Prime Minister since 1935) held a large majority, and ministers argued that general recovery, already well advanced in the south, would reach the distressed areas in time.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.1] The march began in the same week as the Battle of Cable Street in London on 4 October 1936, when anti-fascists blocked a march by Oswald Mosley's British Union of Fascists, and it coincided with the Spanish Civil War, which dominated foreign news.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.2] The abdication crisis over Edward VIII broke in December 1936, soon after the marchers had gone home, and pushed the Jarrow story out of the headlines.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.3] Unemployment nationally was falling, to under two million by late 1936, which strengthened the government's argument that special help was unnecessary.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.4] The Unemployment Assistance Board paid benefit by household means test, so the long-term unemployed of Jarrow depended on a rate set in London that took no account of local conditions.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.5] Wilkinson was an experienced campaigner, and she used the march to dramatise the plight of her constituents in the Commons, where she argued that private industry could not be left to decide the fate of towns.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.b] Sources on the march show differences of purpose and audience that are useful in a documents-based question.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.0] Wilkinson, the council and the Daily Herald presented the march as a respectable appeal by a whole town, and photographs of the disciplined column with its banner and mouth organ band supported that image.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.1] Government statements, including Baldwin's reference to falling unemployment, aimed to show that recovery was under way and that special action was not needed.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.2] Labour and TUC leaders were cautious because they wished to avoid association with Communist-led marches and preferred political action through Parliament.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.3] Conservative and some local newspapers questioned the march as a political stunt, while sympathetic papers described crowds and gifts along the route.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.4] Wilkinson's book of 1939 was written after rearmament had begun to revive the town, so it combines a campaigner's anger with hindsight.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.5] A cartoon or poster of the period usually shows the contrast between the needs of the distressed areas and the comfort of the south or of ministers, and the date helps to place whether it comes before or after the petition on 4 November.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.b] The judgement depends on whether the march is measured by its immediate aims or by its long-term effect on opinion.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.0] Failure: the government refused to meet the marchers, no steelworks or shipyard was secured, and the men lost benefit for the weeks they marched.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.1] Failure: the real cause of recovery in Jarrow was rearmament and war, not the march.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.2] Success: the march made Jarrow a national symbol, kept the problem of the distressed areas before the public, and showed that the unemployed were disciplined and honest.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.3] Success: it was one of the few protests that won wide sympathy across party lines, and its memory shaped postwar attitudes to unemployment.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.4] Contemporary view: supporters saw a moral victory; Conservatives and officials saw an exercise in publicity that offered no solution.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.5] Later view: most historians see it as a failure in its immediate aims but a powerful moment in the politics of memory of the 1930s.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s7: 7. Economic and social problems of inter-war Britain, and Keynes
[hist-eur3-s7.0.b] A brief post-war boom gave way to a sharp slump, and the government's cuts in public spending deepened unemployment in the early 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.0] Demand for goods surged in 1919 and 1920 as wartime controls were removed, and prices rose sharply before collapsing in 1920 and 1921.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.1] Unemployment rose above two million by mid-1921 as export markets were lost and industries shed workers.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.2] The Geddes Axe of 1922, named after Sir Eric Geddes and his committee of economy, cut public spending on education, housing and the armed services.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.3] Unemployment insurance, first introduced in 1911, was extended in 1920 to cover most workers, but benefits were limited and payments ended for many after a set period.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.4] The coal industry was returned to private ownership in 1921, and the owners' attempt to cut miners' wages led to a lock-out; on 'Black Friday', 15 April 1921, the railwaymen and transport workers of the Triple Alliance refused to strike in support, leaving the miners to be defeated.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.b] The decision to return to the gold standard at the pre-war rate made British exports more expensive, and its effect on the coal industry led to the General Strike of 1926.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.0] In April 1925 the Chancellor of the Exchequer, Winston Churchill, returned sterling to the gold standard at the pre-war parity of about 4.86 dollars to the pound.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.1] John Maynard Keynes criticised the decision in his 1925 pamphlet The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill, arguing that the overvalued pound would make British exports uncompetitive and force down wages.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.2] The coal owners announced wage cuts and longer hours, and the miners refused, leading to a lock-out in May 1926.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.3] The Trades Union Congress called a General Strike from 4 to 12 May 1926 in support of the miners, and it was the only nationwide strike in British history.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.4] The government mobilised volunteers and used the Organisation for the Maintenance of Supplies to keep essential services running, and the TUC called off the strike after nine days.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.5] The miners stayed out for several months and were forced back to work on worse terms later in 1926.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.b] Unemployment was concentrated in the older industrial regions of Wales, the north of England and central Scotland, while the south-east prospered.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.0] The staple industries of coal, cotton, shipbuilding, iron and steel had been the basis of the Victorian economy, and they declined sharply in the inter-war years.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.1] The coal industry lost jobs steadily from the mid-1920s, and South Wales, Durham and Northumberland were the most heavily affected coalfields.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.2] The cotton industry of Lancashire lost export markets to Japan and India and suffered a long decline, with many mills closed by the 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.3] Shipbuilding on the Tyne, Wear and Clyde was badly hit by the collapse of world trade and the lack of new orders, and many yards closed in the 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.4] Unemployment in the distressed areas could remain above 30 per cent in many towns in the early 1930s, and higher still in places such as Jarrow and Merthyr Tydfil, while the south-east and the Midlands had relatively low unemployment.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.5] Workers in the depressed regions often moved to the new industries of the south and Midlands, but many could not afford the cost of relocation or of housing.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.b] The Wall Street Crash and the collapse of exports forced the Labour government to confront a budget crisis, leading to the formation of a National Government and the abandonment of gold.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.0] Ramsay MacDonald led the second Labour government of 1929 to 1931, which lacked a majority and struggled with rising unemployment.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.1] The May Committee on National Expenditure, reporting in July 1931, forecast a budget deficit of about 120 million pounds and recommended cuts of 20 per cent in unemployment benefit and public sector pay.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.2] The Cabinet split over a 10 per cent cut in unemployment benefit, and MacDonald formed a National Government in August 1931 with Conservative and Liberal support; the Labour Party expelled him for doing so.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.3] On 21 September 1931 Britain abandoned the gold standard, and sterling fell sharply against other currencies, which helped exports and allowed interest rates to fall.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.4] The National Government won a landslide in the October 1931 general election, with Labour reduced to a small group of MPs.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.5] The Invergordon mutiny of 15 to 16 September 1931, a few days before Britain left gold, in which sailors of the Atlantic Fleet protested against pay cuts, showed how the economy measures were resented in the armed forces.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.b] The National Government moved away from free trade, introducing tariffs and imperial preference, and used cheap money to support recovery.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.0] The Import Duties Act of February 1932 introduced a general tariff of 10 per cent on most imports, ending Britain's long commitment to free trade.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.1] The Ottawa Agreements of 1932 gave preferential trade terms to the British Empire, and Britain kept a system of imperial preference until the 1970s.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.2] Bank Rate was cut to 2 per cent in June 1932 and stayed there until 1939, a policy of cheap money that reduced the cost of borrowing and encouraged house building.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.3] Protection helped industries such as steel and motor vehicles, but it raised the cost of food and materials for consumers.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.4] The Ottawa system strengthened trade with the Dominions and colonies, but it reduced trade with the United States and with Europe.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.b] The unemployed depended on insurance and then on means-tested assistance, and the most visible protest against the system came from hunger marches organised by local communities.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.0] The National Unemployed Workers' Movement (NUWM), founded in 1921 by Communist-linked activists, organised protests, marches and resistance to evictions.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.1] Hunger marches from the depressed regions to London, notably in 1922, 1932, 1934 and 1936, drew attention to the poverty of the unemployed; the National Hunger March of 1932 presented a petition with a million signatures to Parliament, which refused to receive it.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.2] A household means test was introduced in November 1931, when benefit after 26 weeks passed to local Public Assistance Committees, and the Unemployment Act of 1934 created the national Unemployment Assistance Board to pay long-term unemployed people according to the same test of household income and savings.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.3] The means test was unpopular because it looked at the income of all family members, and many families lost their assistance when a son or daughter earned money.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.4] Local councils that paid relief in the depressed areas sometimes refused to apply the means test, and when the Board's new scales cut payments in early 1935, protests in South Wales and elsewhere forced the government to suspend them under the Standstill Act.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.5] Before 1931 insured workers drew insurance benefit for limited periods and others relied on Poor Law relief, which the Local Government Act of 1929 transferred to local council Public Assistance Committees in 1930; both were blamed for stigma and hardship.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.b] The government's policies for the depressed regions were limited, but house building and new light industries in the south and Midlands created jobs and transformed the economy.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.0] The Special Areas (Development and Improvement) Act of 1934 designated the worst-hit areas and offered grants for new industries and public works there.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.1] The Special Areas included South Wales, Tyneside, West Cumberland and parts of Scotland such as Clydeside, and the Commissioners appointed under the Act had limited funds.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.2] About four million houses were built in Britain between the wars, roughly two-thirds of them privately built for owner-occupiers, with about a million council houses built by local authorities under acts such as Addison (1919) and Wheatley (1924), and the largest growth of suburban housing was in the south and Midlands.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.3] The Housing Act of 1930 gave local authorities money to clear slums and build council housing, and the Housing Act of 1935 extended subsidies for slum clearance.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.4] New light industries such as motor vehicles, electrical goods and consumer goods grew rapidly in the south-east and Midlands, with factories built on the outskirts of London and Birmingham.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.5] The regional policy of the 1930s was modest in scale, and the depressed regions did not recover fully until rearmament created new demand at the end of the decade.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.b] Keynes was an economist and government adviser whose ideas about public spending and demand became the basis of modern economic policy.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.0] Born in Cambridge in 1883, he was a Fellow of King's College, Cambridge, and worked in the Treasury during the First World War.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.1] He was a participant in the Versailles peace negotiations as a Treasury representative, resigned in protest at the reparations settlement, and wrote The Economic Consequences of the Peace in 1919, which condemned the settlement as unworkable.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.2] In 1929 he co-wrote with Hubert Henderson the Liberal Party pamphlet We Can Conquer Unemployment, which proposed large public works programmes to reduce unemployment.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.3] He argued in his General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money of 1936 that government spending could restore demand and employment when private investment was low.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.4] Contemporaries disagreed about his ideas: the Treasury and the Bank of England were sceptical of deficit spending, while many Labour and Liberal politicians welcomed his arguments.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.5] Historians judge his work as a turning point in economic thought, though they debate how far the 1930s recovery depended on his policies rather than on rearmament and cheap money.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.6] In How to Pay for the War (1940) he proposed compulsory saving to prevent wartime inflation, and he returned to the Treasury as an adviser.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.7] He led the British delegation to the Bretton Woods conference in 1944, where he argued for an international clearing union and a more flexible monetary system, and the agreement set up the International Monetary Fund and World Bank.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.8] Created Baron Keynes in 1942, he died in April 1946 after negotiating the American loan to Britain.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.b] The interwar years brought rising living standards for those in work and persistent poverty in the depressed regions.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.0] Real wages rose for those in work, prices fell in the early 1930s, and a growing number of families in the south-east and Midlands bought houses, cars, radios and electrical goods.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.1] Trade unions were weakened after 1926, and the Trade Disputes Act of 1927 banned sympathetic strikes and made union members opt in to the political levy.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.2] In the depressed areas, long unemployment brought malnutrition, overcrowded housing and high infant mortality, as shown by J.B. Priestley's English Journey (1934), George Orwell's The Road to Wigan Pier (1937) and the Pilgrim Trust's survey Men Without Work (1938).
[hist-eur3-s7.8.3] Registered unemployment peaked at about three million, over 20 per cent of insured workers, in 1932 and 1933, and fell with recovery and rearmament after 1936 without ever dropping below a million before 1940.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.4] Rearmament from 1936 increased demand for steel, shipbuilding and engineering and eased the problems of some depressed regions.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.5] Mass leisure grew: cinema going, the BBC (founded 1922), dance halls and cheap holidays gave a shared culture, although families on the dole could afford few of these.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.b] Judgements of the government's response weigh its success in restoring growth and stability against its failure to reduce unemployment in the depressed regions.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.0] Achievement: the abandonment of gold and cheap money helped recovery in the south-east, and industrial production grew after 1932.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.1] Limit: unemployment stayed above one million for most of the 1930s, and the depressed regions suffered long-term decline.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.2] Achievement: house building and social provision expanded, and the National Government kept the parliamentary system working during a severe economic crisis.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.3] Limit: the means test and the Special Areas policy were judged inadequate by critics on the left, who saw them as punishing the poor rather than creating jobs.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.4] Contemporary view: the National Government's supporters argued that its policies had protected the currency and prevented a worse slump.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.5] Later view: historians disagree about whether the 1930s recovery was caused mainly by monetary policy or by the expansion of consumer demand in the south-east.
