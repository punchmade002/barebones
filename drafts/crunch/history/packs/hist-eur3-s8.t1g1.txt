TARGET hist-eur3-s8: 8. Case study: the Jarrow March, October 1936
[hist-eur3-s8.0.b] Jarrow's story only makes sense against the national picture. After the world slump of 1929, Britain had mass unemployment that fell unevenly, and the government believed time would cure it.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.0] Registered unemployment in Britain peaked at nearly three million in 1932-33 and was under two million in 1936. The Depression (the world slump in trade and production after 1929) hit hardest in the old staple industries such as shipbuilding.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.1] Recovery was uneven: the north of England, South Wales and central Scotland, where the staple industries were concentrated, stayed depressed, while the south-east and Midlands recovered. This is why Jarrow could be in crisis while the nation's figures improved.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.2] Stanley Baldwin, the Conservative leader, was Prime Minister of the National Government from 1935, and it held a large majority. His government kept to orthodox finance and balanced budgets, believing general recovery would in time reach the depressed areas, so it saw no need for special action.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.3] The long-term unemployed were paid by the Unemployment Assistance Board, set up under the Unemployment Act 1934. It paid benefit, the dole, only after a household means test, which examined the whole family's income.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.4] The Special Areas Act 1934 designated depressed areas, including Durham and Tyneside, where Jarrow lay. Commissioners were appointed, but with limited funds, so the town got little help.
[hist-eur3-s8.0.5] Before the march the government's attitude was summed up by Walter Runciman, President of the Board of Trade, who told Jarrow it must "work out its own salvation".
[hist-eur3-s8.1.b] Jarrow was a one-industry town, so its fortunes rose and fell with a single shipyard. When the yard failed, almost everything else in the town failed with it.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.0] Jarrow lies on the south bank of the River Tyne in County Durham and had a population of about 35,000.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.1] Charles Mark Palmer founded the shipyard in 1851. The firm later became Palmers Shipbuilding and Iron Company, with its own blast furnaces and steelworks, so it grew into a combined shipyard and iron and steel works that dominated the town.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.2] Almost the whole local economy depended on the yard's wages, so when orders collapsed, the shops, trades and families that lived off those wages collapsed with it.
[hist-eur3-s8.1.3] Palmers' last ship, the destroyer HMS Duchess, was launched in 1932, and a receiver (an official appointed to take over a failed company's affairs) was appointed in 1933.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.b] Palmers did not simply fail by accident. A world slump in shipbuilding, then a deliberate scheme by the industry itself, closed the yard, and a second body then blocked the one plan to replace it.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.0] World trade fell and foreign competition grew, so British yards had far more capacity than orders. This is the thinking behind the scheme that closed Palmers.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.1] National Shipbuilders Security Ltd (NSS) was formed in 1930 by the shipbuilding firms, with Bank of England support. It bought and closed redundant yards, financed by a levy (a compulsory charge) on shipbuilders.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.2] NSS bought Palmers in 1934 and dismantled the yard. The site could not be used for shipbuilding for forty years, which ruled out any revival of shipbuilding in Jarrow.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.3] In 1934 T. Vosper Salt proposed a modern steelworks on the Palmers site. The British Iron and Steel Federation opposed it, because it wanted no new competitor, and the scheme was dropped.
[hist-eur3-s8.2.4] Wilkinson's later book blamed NSS, the steel interests and the government for what happened to the town.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.b] The closure left Jarrow with unemployment far above the national level and the poverty that goes with it.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.0] In the mid-1930s about two-thirds to over 70 per cent of Jarrow's insured workers were unemployed. Figures quoted vary, so give the range, not one exact figure.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.1] National insured unemployment in 1936 was about 13 per cent, so Jarrow's rate was several times the national one.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.2] Jarrow had one of the worst records in Britain for poor and overcrowded housing, poverty, hunger, ill health and high death rates.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.3] J.B. Priestley visited Jarrow for his book English Journey (1934) and described it as a town without work or hope, which makes his book a useful source on conditions.
[hist-eur3-s8.3.4] Wilkinson later called her book on Jarrow The Town That Was Murdered, because she held that the town had been killed by decisions made elsewhere.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.b] The march was run by the town council with cross-party support, and its public face was the town's Labour MP, Ellen Wilkinson.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.0] Ellen Wilkinson (1891-1947), known as Red Ellen for her politics and red hair, was an early member of the Communist Party of Great Britain, which she left in the 1920s. She was a trade union organiser and Labour MP for Middlesbrough East from 1924 to 1931.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.1] She was elected Labour MP for Jarrow in 1935, so she spoke for a constituency in the Commons.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.2] Wilkinson was an experienced campaigner, and she used the march to dramatise the plight of her constituents in the Commons, where she argued that private industry could not be left to decide the fate of towns.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.3] She backed the march and did much of the publicity, speeches and parliamentary work, including presenting the petition in the Commons. She was a participant, but not on foot for the whole way.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.4] She left the march early on to attend the Labour Party conference in Edinburgh and rejoined it later. She did not share the men's sleeping quarters, so her account is that of an organiser, not an ordinary marcher.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.5] Councillor David Riley was the marshal and leader of the marchers on the road, so Wilkinson led the campaign and Riley led the column.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.6] Jarrow Borough Council organised the march, and Labour, Conservatives and Liberals on the council supported it, so it was presented as an appeal by the whole town, not a party or Communist protest.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.7] Wilkinson wrote The Town That Was Murdered in 1939, partly blaming NSS, the steel interests and the government. After the war she became Minister of Education in the Labour government of 1945 and died in 1947.
[hist-eur3-s8.4.8] Historians treat her book as the account of a partisan participant, written in 1939 with hindsight, which gives it great value as a source but means it needs care.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.b] The organisers called the march a crusade, not a hunger march, and prepared it carefully so that it would look respectable and non-political.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.0] The aim was a petition asking the government to provide work for Jarrow, above all by bringing new industry such as a steelworks, and to make the town's distress known.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.1] About 200 men were chosen from many more volunteers and each was medically examined, so that only fit men set out.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.2] The men carried a petition of about 11,000 signatures in an oak box. Their banner read Jarrow Crusade, and a mouth-organ band kept up their spirits and set the pace.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.3] They were organised and disciplined to show that the unemployed were respectable men who wanted work. A public appeal raised funds, and supporters gave boots, clothing and food.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.4] The national Labour Party and the TUC (the Trades Union Congress, the federation of trade unions) gave no official support, because they feared Communist links and preferred action through Parliament.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.5] The Labour conference in Edinburgh in October 1936 criticised the march, although many local Labour parties and trades councils helped it.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.6] The Communist-led National Unemployed Workers' Movement (NUWM) had a tradition of hunger marches that the government and the Labour leadership distrusted. Jarrow kept apart from it, which is why the town called its march a crusade.
[hist-eur3-s8.5.7] The churches gave a service and the Bishop of Jarrow's blessing at the start, and churches along the route gave food and shelter. Herbert Hensley Henson, Bishop of Durham, condemned the march as revolutionary mob pressure.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.b] The men walked from the north-east to London, relying on the hospitality of the towns along the way, and the sympathetic press turned their walk into a national story.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.0] The marchers left Jarrow on 5 October 1936 after a church service and a send-off by crowds.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.1] They walked about 300 miles by way of Chester-le-Street, Darlington, Ripon, Harrogate, Leeds, Barnsley, Sheffield, Chesterfield, Nottingham, Leicester, Market Harborough, Northampton, Bedford, Luton and St Albans.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.2] They marched in stages with rests and Sundays off, and slept in drill halls, schools, church halls and sometimes workhouses, relying on supporters for meals.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.3] They were welcomed and fed by councils, Co-operative societies, churches and local parties of all sides. At Leicester, Co-operative boot workers repaired the marchers' boots, although some officials and Conservatives were cool.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.4] The march was widely covered by the press and by newsreels (short films shown in cinemas), mostly sympathetically, which made it a national story.
[hist-eur3-s8.6.5] They reached London (Marble Arch) on 31 October 1936, and a meeting was held in Hyde Park on 1 November 1936.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.b] The government met the march with a refusal to act. The petition was presented, but it brought no new commitment.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.0] In October 1936 the government stated that marches on London were undesirable and that grievances should be raised through MPs, because processions had no constitutional influence.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.1] Stanley Baldwin, the Prime Minister, refused to receive a deputation of the marchers.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.2] Ellen Wilkinson presented the petition in the House of Commons on 4 November 1936. It led only to a brief exchange and no action.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.3] The Unemployment Assistance Board reduced the marchers' benefit, the dole cut, because they had not been available for work while they were marching.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.4] The marchers returned to Jarrow by train on 5 November 1936. They were welcomed home, but there was bitter disappointment among the men.
[hist-eur3-s8.7.5] The government's argument was that national recovery would restore jobs in time, and it questioned whether special help for one town was fair when many areas suffered.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.b] The march brought no immediate results. What helped Jarrow later came from other causes, chiefly rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.0] In the short term there was no steelworks, no shipyard and no new commitment from the government, so unemployment in Jarrow stayed high.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.1] Sir John Jarvis, Conservative MP for Guildford, organised help through a fund raised in Surrey. He brought shipbreaking (including the liner Olympic, broken up partly at Jarrow from 1935) and small metal works to the Palmers site, but they employed only a fraction of the jobless.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.2] The Special Areas (Amendment) Act 1937 gave tax, rent and rate reliefs to firms in the special areas. There is no evidence that the march caused it.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.3] Rearmament from the later 1930s and the Second World War brought work back to Tyneside, and this, not the march, ended mass unemployment in Jarrow.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.4] Wilkinson published The Town That Was Murdered in 1939, and became Minister of Education in 1945 and died in 1947.
[hist-eur3-s8.8.5] Many marchers felt they had failed.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.b] The march is remembered far more than it achieved. It became a symbol of the Depression and of how a democracy handled a peaceful protest.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.0] The march became the best-known symbol of the hungry thirties and of government failure on unemployment.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.1] Memories of the 1930s fed the post-1945 commitment to full employment and the welfare state.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.2] The march was re-enacted in 1986, its 50th anniversary, and in 2011, its 75th anniversary, which shows its place in the memory of the labour movement.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.3] The Depression: Jarrow was the extreme case of structural unemployment, meaning lasting joblessness caused by the decline of a whole industry, here shipbuilding, not a temporary dip.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.4] Democracy: the march was a peaceful, lawful protest within a parliamentary democracy, in contrast to the extremism of the dictatorships and the violence of Cable Street in the same week.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.5] It also showed the limits of democracy, since a petition could be ignored.
[hist-eur3-s8.9.6] Some note that the NUWM's national marches were larger but are less remembered.
[hist-eur3-s8.10.b] The march took place in a month of major events, which helps date and judge any source about it.
[hist-eur3-s8.10.0] The march set out the day after the Battle of Cable Street in London on 4 October 1936, when anti-fascists stopped a march by Oswald Mosley's British Union of Fascists.
[hist-eur3-s8.10.1] The Spanish Civil War, which began in July 1936, was also in the news.
[hist-eur3-s8.10.2] The NUWM's own national hunger march reached London in November 1936, and the Jarrow organisers had deliberately kept apart from it.
[hist-eur3-s8.10.3] The abdication of Edward VIII in December 1936 pushed Jarrow from the headlines soon after the marchers had gone home.
[hist-eur3-s8.10.4] Unemployment nationally was falling to under two million by late 1936, which strengthened the government's argument that special help was unnecessary.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s7: 7. Economic and social problems of inter-war Britain, and Keynes
[hist-eur3-s7.0.b] Britain entered the inter-war years with an economy that the First World War had weakened, and with a run of governments that tried different answers to the same problems. Knowing who governed when lets you match each policy to the right Chancellor and party.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.0] Before 1914 Britain's wealth rested on exports from the staple industries (the long-established heavy industries: coal, cotton, shipbuilding, iron and steel), which sold to the whole world and gave Britain its lead.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.1] The First World War cost Britain export markets to the USA, Japan and others, whose industries grew while Britain's factories made weapons, and it left large war debts. This is why the staple industries struggled to recover afterwards.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.2] Governments were: Lloyd George's coalition to October 1922; Conservative governments under Stanley Baldwin in 1923 and 1924-29; minority Labour governments (without a majority in the Commons) under Ramsay MacDonald in 1924 and 1929-31.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.3] From August 1931 the National Government, a cross-party coalition, ruled until 1940. MacDonald was Prime Minister to 1935, Baldwin 1935-37 and Neville Chamberlain 1937-40.
[hist-eur3-s7.0.4] The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 began a world depression, in which world trade, prices and investment all collapsed. Britain, which lived by selling goods abroad, was hit hard.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.b] A short post-war boom gave way to a sharp slump from late 1920, and the government's cuts in public spending deepened unemployment in the early 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.0] The boom of 1919-20 came because demand held back during the war was released once wartime controls were removed; prices rose sharply and speculation spread. Lloyd George's 1918 promise of 'homes fit for heroes' raised hopes of a better Britain.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.1] The slump began in late 1920, when prices and exports collapsed. Registered unemployment passed two million by mid-1921 and, after 1921, did not fall below one million until the Second World War.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.2] The Geddes Axe of 1922 was named after Sir Eric Geddes, who chaired the Committee on National Expenditure. It recommended deep cuts in public spending on education, housing and the armed forces, which deepened the slump.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.3] Unemployment insurance began in 1911 for a few trades and was extended in 1920 to most manual workers, about 12 million people, though farm workers and domestic servants were left out.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.4] Benefit, known as 'the dole', ran only for limited periods, so long-term unemployed people fell back on extra payments or Poor Law relief (the old system of local charity for the destitute). In the 1920s claimants also had to prove they were 'genuinely seeking work'.
[hist-eur3-s7.1.5] Wartime government control of the coal mines ended on 31 March 1921. The mines had always been privately owned, so the owners cut wages and locked miners out (refused to let them work).
[hist-eur3-s7.1.6] On 'Black Friday', 15 April 1921, the railwaymen's and transport workers' unions of the Triple Alliance, an agreement of three unions to strike together, called off their support, leaving the miners to be defeated.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.b] The staple industries had been the basis of the Victorian economy, and between the wars they shrank. Because each was concentrated in particular regions, the decline produced a country split between struggling areas and prosperous ones.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.0] The staple industries declined for several reasons: lost export markets, foreign competition, old equipment and methods, many small firms, falling world demand (oil and electricity replaced coal) and, after 1925, a pound that was too high for exporters.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.1] Coal lost exports, and the worst-hit coalfields were South Wales, Durham and Northumberland, and Lanarkshire. Whole villages depended on a single pit, so when it closed there was no other work.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.2] Lancashire's cotton industry lost markets in India and the Far East to Indian and Japanese mills, and suffered a long decline with many mills closed by the 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.3] Shipbuilding yards on the Clyde, Tyne and Wear were short of orders after the post-war boom and again after 1929, and many closed in the 1930s. Iron and steel suffered with them.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.4] 'Rationalisation' means closing surplus capacity to save the rest. National Shipbuilders' Security Ltd, set up in 1930 with the Bank of England's backing, bought up and closed yards it judged surplus.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.5] The 'distressed' or 'depressed' areas were South Wales, the north-east, Lancashire, west Cumberland and central Scotland. In 1932 more than a third of insured workers in Wales were unemployed, against about one in seven in London and the south-east.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.6] In the worst towns, such as Merthyr Tydfil and Jarrow, over half the insured workers were unemployed in the early to mid 1930s, while the south-east and the Midlands stayed comparatively prosperous.
[hist-eur3-s7.2.7] The unemployed did not simply move to where work was because moving and housing cost money, family and community ties held them, and older skilled men were unsuited to new work. The Industrial Transference Board (1928) helped some to move.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.b] In 1925 the government fixed the pound to gold at the pre-war rate. This made life harder for exporters such as the coal industry, and it set the scene for the General Strike.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.0] Under the gold standard a currency is fixed to gold, so its exchange rate is fixed too. A high rate makes a country's exports dearer abroad and its imports cheaper.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.1] Churchill, Chancellor of the Exchequer from 1924 to 1929, announced the return to gold in his April 1925 budget, at the pre-war rate of 4.86 dollars to the pound.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.2] Keynes argued in The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill (1925) that the pound was overvalued, by about 10 per cent in his estimate, so exports would be dearer and wages, above all miners' wages, would be forced down.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.3] Owners tried to protect profits by cutting wages and lengthening hours. The miners' leader A.J. Cook gave them their slogan: 'Not a penny off the pay, not a minute on the day'.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.4] On 'Red Friday', 31 July 1925, the government agreed a nine-month subsidy to the coal industry, buying time, and set up a Royal Commission under Sir Herbert Samuel.
[hist-eur3-s7.3.5] The Samuel Commission reported in March 1926. It rejected nationalisation (state ownership of the mines) and recommended wage cuts, which the miners refused. The owners locked them out from 1 May 1926.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.b] When the coal dispute reached a lock-out, the Trades Union Congress (TUC), the central body of the trade unions, called out its members in support. The strike lasted nine days and ended in defeat for the unions.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.0] The TUC called the General Strike, which ran from 4 to 12 May 1926, in support of the locked-out miners. About one and a half to one and three-quarter million workers, including transport, printing, iron and steel and power workers, came out.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.1] The government was prepared. The Organisation for the Maintenance of Supplies (OMS), set up in 1925, recruited volunteers, and troops guarded convoys of food. The strike was largely peaceful.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.2] The government published the British Gazette, edited by Churchill, while the TUC produced the British Worker. The BBC, then new, was under the government's influence and gave little voice to the strikers.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.3] The TUC called the strike off on 12 May, after nine days, without any guarantees for the miners. It was Britain's only general strike, and it was not an attempt at revolution.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.4] The miners stayed out until late 1926 and then returned on lower wages and longer hours.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.5] Union membership fell, and the Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act of 1927 made general and sympathetic strikes illegal. It also made members 'contract in' (choose to join) to pay the political levy to Labour, a rule repealed in 1946.
[hist-eur3-s7.4.6] The strike showed that the labour movement worked through constitutional, not revolutionary, politics, which is one reason Britain's democracy held in the 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.b] The world depression struck a minority Labour government that held to orthodox finance. A budget crisis in 1931 split the party, brought in the National Government and ended in Britain leaving the gold standard.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.0] The second Labour government (from June 1929) was the largest party with 287 seats but had no majority. Philip Snowden, the Chancellor, kept to balanced budgets and free trade, the 'Treasury view' that state spending only crowds out private investment.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.1] As exports fell, registered unemployment rose from just over one million in 1929 to about two and a half million by the end of 1930, and the unemployment insurance fund ran into debt.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.2] In 1930 the Labour minister Sir Oswald Mosley proposed public works, protection and earlier retirement in the Mosley Memorandum. It was rejected, and he resigned in May 1930.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.3] The May Report (Sir George May's Committee on National Expenditure), published on 31 July 1931, forecast a budget deficit (spending above income) of about 120 million pounds and recommended cuts of about 96 million pounds, including 20 per cent off unemployment benefit. A run on the pound followed.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.4] The Cabinet split over a 10 per cent cut in benefit. On 24 August 1931 MacDonald formed a National Government with the Conservatives and some Liberals, and Labour went into opposition and expelled him, Snowden and the others who joined.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.5] The emergency budget of September 1931 cut benefit by 10 per cent, cut public-sector pay and raised taxes. On 15 and 16 September sailors of the Atlantic Fleet at Invergordon, Scotland, refused duty over pay cuts, the Invergordon mutiny.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.6] Britain left the gold standard on 21 September 1931 and the pound fell by about a quarter against the dollar. This made exports cheaper and allowed interest rates to fall.
[hist-eur3-s7.5.7] In the general election of 27 October 1931 National Government candidates won 554 seats and Labour fell to about 50.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.b] Once off gold, the National Government dropped free trade and kept money cheap, while Chamberlain held to balanced budgets. Recovery came, but chiefly from these policies, private building and later rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.0] The Import Duties Act (passed February 1932, in force 1 March) set a general tariff (a tax on imports) of 10 per cent on most imports, higher on some. Empire goods were exempt. This ended free trade and is Britain's example of protectionism.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.1] Protection helped industries such as steel and motor vehicles, but it raised the cost of food and materials for consumers.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.2] The Ottawa Agreements, made at the Imperial Economic Conference in July-August 1932, gave preference to trade within the Empire (imperial preference). This helped trade with the Empire but cost trade with the USA and Europe.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.3] Bank Rate, the central bank's basic interest rate, was cut to 2 per cent on 30 June 1932 and kept there until August 1939. This 'cheap money' made borrowing cheaper, so mortgages were cheap and housebuilding boomed.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.4] In 1932 about 2 billion pounds of 5 per cent War Loan was converted to 3.5 per cent, lowering the government's own interest bill.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.5] The Wheat Act of 1932 gave a subsidy to wheat growers, and marketing boards such as the Milk Marketing Board (1933) helped farmers.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.6] Neville Chamberlain, Chancellor from November 1931 to May 1937, kept to balanced budgets and no large public-works programme. The 1931 cuts in benefit were restored in 1934.
[hist-eur3-s7.6.7] Rearmament from the mid-1930s, speeded up from 1936, brought orders for steel, shipbuilding, engineering and aircraft. It helped some depressed areas more than any regional policy did.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.b] How the unemployed were paid mattered as much as how many there were. From 1931 the household means test, which judged claims by the whole family's resources, became the most hated feature of the system.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.0] The Local Government Act of 1929 abolished the Boards of Guardians (the local bodies that ran Poor Law relief) and passed relief to the Public Assistance Committees (PACs) of county and county borough councils from April 1930.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.1] In November 1931 the household means test began. After 26 weeks on insurance benefit, unemployed people received 'transitional payments' from the PACs only after a test of the whole household's income and savings.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.2] The test was hated because inspectors came into the home, the earnings of sons and daughters and savings were counted, furniture might have to be sold, and families split up as young earners left home. It brought humiliation and stigma.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.3] Some councils applied it leniently. Labour-controlled authorities in County Durham and Rotherham were among those whose committees were replaced by government commissioners in 1932.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.4] The Unemployment Act of 1934 restored insurance benefit and ran it separately. It created the national Unemployment Assistance Board (UAB) to pay the long-term unemployed from January 1935 on national scales.
[hist-eur3-s7.7.5] Many payments fell under the UAB's new scales, causing mass protest, largest in South Wales in early 1935. The government suspended the cuts by the Unemployment Assistance (Temporary Provisions) Act of 1935, known as the 'Standstill' Act.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.b] The unemployed did not only wait for help. The National Unemployed Workers' Movement organised protest, and hunger marches to London brought the issue to public attention.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.0] The National Unemployed Workers' Movement (NUWM), founded in 1921 and led by the Communist Wal Hannington, organised protests, helped people with benefit claims and resisted evictions. The Labour Party and the TUC distrusted it because of its Communist leadership.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.1] National hunger marches to London took place in 1922, 1929, 1930, 1932, 1934 and 1936. Marchers walked from the depressed regions to demand work and an end to the means test.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.2] The authorities often met the marches with police and government hostility. The 1932 march saw clashes with police in London, in Hyde Park, though the marchers also won public sympathy.
[hist-eur3-s7.8.3] The Jarrow March of 5 to 31 October 1936, with about 200 marchers, became the best-known protest. It was organised by the council, not the NUWM. There is more on this in the Jarrow March notes.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.b] The government's regional help was small, but housebuilding and the new light industries of the south-east and the Midlands changed the economy and created jobs. This is why the 1930s were prosperous for much of Britain even while the staple regions suffered.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.0] The Special Areas (Development and Improvement) Act of December 1934 named four Special Areas: South Wales, Tyneside and Durham, west Cumberland, and central Scotland (Clydeside and Lanarkshire). London and the other areas were excluded.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.1] The Act appointed two commissioners, one for England and Wales and one for Scotland, with only 2 million pounds at first, and the power to aid industry but not to compel it to move.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.2] The Special Areas (Amendment) Act of 1937 offered firms help with rent, rates and taxes. Trading estates (planned industrial sites) such as Team Valley, Gateshead, and Treforest, near Pontypridd in South Wales, followed, but results were small before rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.3] Council housing acts were: Addison (1919), subsidies for council houses; Chamberlain (1923), subsidies mainly for private builders; Wheatley (1924), subsidies for council houses to rent; Greenwood (1930), slum clearance; and the 1935 Act on overcrowding.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.4] About 4 million houses were built in Britain in 1919-39, about 1.1 million by councils and the rest privately. In the 1930s cheap money, building societies and suburban semi-detached houses, mostly in the south and Midlands, made building a source of jobs.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.5] New industries grew in the south-east and Midlands: motor cars (Morris at Cowley, Oxford; Austin at Longbridge, Birmingham; Ford at Dagenham from 1931), electrical goods, chemicals (ICI, formed 1926), rayon and aircraft.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.6] The Central Electricity Board (1926) and the National Grid, largely complete by 1933, let factories locate anywhere, so they chose sites near the London and Midlands markets, far from the depressed regions.
[hist-eur3-s7.9.7] Those in work saw rising living standards: prices fell, real wages rose and hours shortened. Families bought radios, vacuum cleaners and cars and enjoyed holidays and the cinema.
[hist-eur3-s7.10.b] Between the wars Britain was in effect two countries: a prosperous south-east and Midlands, and depressed regions where long unemployment destroyed health and morale. Contemporary writers and investigators recorded the contrast, and their works are sources you may meet in a documents question.
[hist-eur3-s7.10.0] The J.B. Priestley book English Journey (1934) described three Englands: the old rural England, nineteenth-century industrial England, and the new England of by-passes, factories and suburbs. His contrast of old and new England is the 'two Britains' idea.
[hist-eur3-s7.10.1] Long-term unemployment meant poverty, poor diet and health, bad and overcrowded housing and high infant mortality. It also meant loss of self-respect, and women bore the burden of managing the household on the dole.
[hist-eur3-s7.10.2] Contemporary witnesses included Walter Greenwood's novel Love on the Dole (1933) and John Boyd Orr's Food, Health and Income (1936), which showed that much of the population could not afford a healthy diet.
[hist-eur3-s7.10.3] George Orwell's The Road to Wigan Pier (1937) and the Pilgrim Trust's survey Men Without Work (1938) also recorded life on the dole. All were written at the time, so they are contemporary sources.
[hist-eur3-s7.10.4] Registered unemployment peaked at about three million, over a fifth of insured workers, in 1932-33. It fell with recovery but stayed above one million until 1939.
[hist-eur3-s7.10.5] Trade unions were weakened after the General Strike, but the employed majority gained: real wages rose, so many families in work enjoyed a rising standard of living.
[hist-eur3-s7.11.b] Britain's Depression was milder than in Germany or the USA, and Britain kept its parliamentary democracy. Extremist movements won little support, and the contrast with Germany explains much of the examiners' interest.
[hist-eur3-s7.11.0] Sir Oswald Mosley founded the New Party in 1931 and, in October 1932, the British Union of Fascists (BUF). He is evidence that British democracy held because his movement stayed marginal.
[hist-eur3-s7.11.1] On 4 October 1936 at the Battle of Cable Street in East London, anti-fascists blocked a BUF march. The Public Order Act of 1936 banned political uniforms and gave police the power to ban marches.
[hist-eur3-s7.11.2] The small Communist Party of Great Britain, founded 1920, had just one MP, Willie Gallacher, from 1935.
[hist-eur3-s7.11.3] Extremism failed for several reasons: a long parliamentary tradition, Labour and the TUC committed to constitutional methods, the broad support for the National Government, the dole (however mean) and real prosperity in much of the country.
[hist-eur3-s7.11.4] Compared with Germany, Britain's problems were long-term decline in particular regions and industries, with no hyperinflation and no collapse of democracy. The Depression was milder than in the USA or Germany.
[hist-eur3-s7.12.b] John Maynard Keynes was a Cambridge economist and government adviser who argued that governments should spend to create demand when jobs are scarce. Governments ignored him in the 1930s, but his ideas shaped policy after 1945.
[hist-eur3-s7.12.0] Keynes was born in Cambridge on 5 June 1883 and became a Fellow of King's College, Cambridge. He belonged to the Bloomsbury group and in 1925 married the Russian ballerina Lydia Lopokova.
[hist-eur3-s7.12.1] He worked at the Treasury from 1915 and was its chief representative at the Paris Peace Conference. He resigned in June 1919 over reparations (payments demanded from Germany), a participant who became a critic.
[hist-eur3-s7.12.2] His book The Economic Consequences of the Peace (December 1919) was a best-seller. It called the reparations unpayable and damaging to Europe, because a ruined Germany could not trade with its neighbours.
[hist-eur3-s7.12.3] In the 1920s he attacked orthodox policy: A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923), with the line 'In the long run we are all dead', and The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill (1925) against the return to gold.
[hist-eur3-s7.12.4] In 1929 Lloyd George's Liberal programme We Can Conquer Unemployment promised to cut unemployment through public works. Keynes backed it and with Hubert Henderson wrote Can Lloyd George Do It?. He sat on the Macmillan Committee and the Economic Advisory Council (1930) and wrote A Treatise on Money (1930).
[hist-eur3-s7.12.5] The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (February 1936) argued that unemployment comes from too little total demand, so an economy can stay stuck in a slump. The government should then borrow and spend on public works.
[hist-eur3-s7.12.6] The multiplier (each pound spent creates further spending) spreads the effect. This challenged the Treasury view and balanced budgets, so it was a direct attack on orthodox policy.
[hist-eur3-s7.12.7] He wrote an open letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt in the New York Times in December 1933. Harold Macmillan's The Middle Way (1938) and other planners drew on his ideas, though they had little effect on British policy before 1940.
[hist-eur3-s7.13.b] In the war Keynes moved from critic to insider, helping plan how Britain would pay for the conflict and the peace. His legacy is judged both by what he achieved and by how far his ideas could have worked earlier.
[hist-eur3-s7.13.0] How to Pay for the War (1940) proposed compulsory saving to curb inflation, because heavy spending with few goods to buy would push prices up. He became adviser to the Chancellor at the Treasury from 1940.
[hist-eur3-s7.13.1] His approach shaped the 1941 budget, which used national income figures to set taxation against inflation. He was made Baron Keynes in 1942. He led the negotiations for the American loan to Britain, agreed in December 1945 (about 3.75 billion dollars), and died on 21 April 1946.
[hist-eur3-s7.13.2] He led the British delegation at the Bretton Woods conference in New Hampshire in July 1944, which set up the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank (the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development).
[hist-eur3-s7.13.3] His own plan for an international clearing union was largely set aside for the American plan of Harry Dexter White, because the USA was the strongest creditor and could insist on its own plan.
[hist-eur3-s7.13.4] Contemporaries thought him brilliant and persuasive, but the Treasury, the Bank of England, Snowden and Chamberlain resisted his ideas of deficit spending. Lloyd George, Mosley and some in the Labour Party welcomed them.
[hist-eur3-s7.13.5] His ideas shaped the 1944 Employment Policy White Paper, in which the government accepted responsibility for 'a high and stable level of employment'. They also shaped the post-1945 'Keynesian' consensus of managed demand and full employment.
[hist-eur3-s7.13.6] Later critics blamed Keynesian policies for the inflation of the 1970s.
[hist-eur3-s7.13.7] Historians debate whether Keynesian spending could have cured unemployment in 1930s Britain, given its dependence on trade and the very large scale that would have been needed. Do not credit his ideas with the 1930s recovery or with Roosevelt's New Deal, which was not built on the General Theory.
[hist-eur3-s7.14.b] Judgements of the government's response weigh the recovery after 1932 and the survival of democracy against persistent unemployment in the depressed areas. A clear overall verdict, such as partial success, repeated in each paragraph, scores better than a narrative.
[hist-eur3-s7.14.0] Achievement: recovery came after 1932, with housebuilding, new industries and rising real wages for those in work, and democracy was preserved through a severe crisis.
[hist-eur3-s7.14.1] Limit: unemployment never fell below one million, the depressed areas were neglected, the means test was hated and the Special Areas budget was small.
[hist-eur3-s7.14.2] Limit: recovery owed more to cheap money, private building and rearmament than to any planned regional policy.
[hist-eur3-s7.14.3] Contemporary view: the left and the writers of the time painted the 'hungry thirties' or 'devil's decade', pointing to the means test and the depressed areas. Supporters of the National Government argued instead that its policies protected the currency and prevented a worse slump.
[hist-eur3-s7.14.4] Later view: since the 1970s historians such as John Stevenson and Chris Cook (The Slump, 1977) have stressed the prosperity of the employed majority, while agreeing that the depressed areas suffered badly.
[hist-eur3-s7.14.5] Britain against Germany: Britain's decline was slower and regional, while Germany suffered hyperinflation and collapse in the Depression. Britain's democracy survived, so it dealt with its problems better, although it faced lesser ones.
[hist-eur3-s7.14.6] Keynes against other responses: Keynes changed economic thinking in the long run but not 1930s policy, while the Jarrow March won sympathy but no immediate help.
