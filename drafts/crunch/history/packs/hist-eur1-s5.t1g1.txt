TARGET hist-eur1-s5: 5. Autocracy in the East: Balkan nationalism (Greece and Serbia) and serfdom in Russia
[hist-eur1-s5.0.b] In 1815 the Ottoman Empire still ruled south-eastern Europe, but its grip was weakening. The Eastern Question asked what would happen to Ottoman lands as its power declined, and which Great Power would gain from it; this question drives the whole story of Balkan nationalism.
[hist-eur1-s5.0.0] The Ottoman Empire, ruled by the Sultan from Constantinople, was a Muslim state governing a largely Orthodox Christian population in the Balkans, including Greece, Serbia and the Danubian principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia.
[hist-eur1-s5.0.1] The Sultan ruled Balkan Christians through local officials and the millet system, in which each religious community ran its own religious and civil affairs, so the Orthodox were led by the Ecumenical Patriarch in Constantinople.
[hist-eur1-s5.0.2] The millet system gave religious communities legal autonomy but no political rights, and non-Muslims paid a poll tax (an extra tax on each person), which fed resentment of Ottoman rule.
[hist-eur1-s5.0.3] Ottoman power was declining: central control was weak and local governors and the Janissaries (the Sultan's elite troops) often acted independently. Sultan Mahmud II (reigned 1808–39) tried to reform and destroyed the Janissary corps in 1826.
[hist-eur1-s5.0.4] Russia claimed to protect Orthodox Christians because they shared its religion, sought passage through the Straits (the Bosporus and Dardanelles), and gained influence over Moldavia and Wallachia after its wars with the Ottomans; until 1821 these were ruled by Greek (Phanariot) princes appointed by the Sultan.
[hist-eur1-s5.0.5] Austria feared Russian expansion into the Balkans and nationalism among its own Slavs, so it wanted the Ottoman Empire kept intact as a barrier. Britain wanted to protect its route to India, which ran through the eastern Mediterranean and the Straits, and to keep Russia out of the Mediterranean.
[hist-eur1-s5.0.6] France had interests in the Levant (the eastern Mediterranean coast) and acted as protector of Catholics in the Holy Places. Because each Power wanted a different outcome, every Ottoman crisis became a Great Power quarrel.
[hist-eur1-s5.1.b] Balkan Christians had lived under Ottoman rule for centuries without rebelling together. In the early nineteenth century several forces gave them a sense of national identity and the hope that revolt could succeed.
[hist-eur1-s5.1.0] Religion and language gave a shared identity: the Orthodox Church and Greek or Serbian speech marked Christians as a people distinct from their Muslim rulers.
[hist-eur1-s5.1.1] Ottoman misrule, such as the abuse of Serbs by the Janissary garrison, gave people a grievance that made revolt seem worth the risk.
[hist-eur1-s5.1.2] Merchants and the diaspora (communities living abroad) supplied money, education and contacts. The Philiki Etaireia, for example, was founded by Greek merchants.
[hist-eur1-s5.1.3] The example of the French Revolution showed that a people could claim rights and a nation, which encouraged educated Greeks and Serbs to think in national terms.
[hist-eur1-s5.1.4] Nationalists hoped for Russian help because Russia was Orthodox and often at war with the Ottomans, although Russia in practice helped only when it suited its own interests.
[hist-eur1-s5.1.5] Nationalism, as used here, means the belief that a people sharing religion, language and history should govern itself in its own state.
[hist-eur1-s5.2.b] Serbia's path to autonomy was two risings, then a slow negotiated autonomy that depended on Russian pressure on the Sultan.
[hist-eur1-s5.2.0] The First Serbian Uprising began in February 1804 under Karadjordje (Đorđe Petrović, 'Black George'). It was aimed against the dahije, Janissary chiefs who had seized power in the pashalik (province) of Belgrade and terrorised the Serbs.
[hist-eur1-s5.2.1] The rising was crushed in 1813 because Russia made peace with the Ottomans in 1812 (the Treaty of Bucharest) before Napoleon's invasion, so the Serbs lost their ally. The Ottomans reconquered Serbia and Karadjordje fled across the Danube to Austrian territory.
[hist-eur1-s5.2.2] The Second Serbian Uprising came in 1815 under Miloš Obrenović, who combined limited fighting with negotiation with the Ottoman government. This worked better than all-out war against a stronger enemy.
[hist-eur1-s5.2.3] Russian–Ottoman agreements then bound the Sultan to grant Serbian autonomy: the Akkerman Convention (October 1826) and the Treaty of Adrianople (September 1829), which also ended a Russo-Ottoman war.
[hist-eur1-s5.2.4] A decree (hatt-i sharif) of the Sultan in 1830 recognised Serbia as an autonomous principality with Miloš as hereditary prince, and Serbia's territory was enlarged in 1833.
[hist-eur1-s5.2.5] Autonomy had limits. Serbia stayed under Ottoman suzerainty (the Sultan remained overlord), paid an annual tribute that was the price of its autonomy, and Ottoman garrisons stayed in Belgrade and other fortresses.
[hist-eur1-s5.3.b] Autonomy did not bring calm. Two families, the Obrenovićs and the Karadjordjevićs, fought for the throne, and Serbia's rulers were still checked by the Ottomans and the Great Powers.
[hist-eur1-s5.3.0] Miloš ruled autocratically and rivals were removed. Karadjordje returned to Serbia in 1817 and was murdered, on Miloš's orders according to most accounts.
[hist-eur1-s5.3.1] The liberal Sretenje Constitution of February 1835 limited Miloš's power but was suspended within weeks under pressure from Russia, Austria and the Ottomans, who disliked a liberal Serbia.
[hist-eur1-s5.3.2] An Ottoman statute of 1838 gave power to a council of notables to limit the prince, and Miloš abdicated in 1839. His son Milan died within weeks, and his other son Mihailo ruled 1839–42 before being overthrown.
[hist-eur1-s5.3.3] Alexander Karadjordjević, son of Karadjordje, was prince 1842–58. Miloš was restored in 1858 and died in 1860, and Mihailo's second reign (1860–68) followed.
[hist-eur1-s5.3.4] In 1867 the Ottoman garrisons left the fortresses, a real gain in Serbian control; Mihailo was assassinated in 1868. Full independence came only in 1878, recognised at the Congress of Berlin.
[hist-eur1-s5.3.5] In 1844 Ilija Garašanin, a Serbian minister, drew up the secret Načertanije, a plan for a larger South Slav state under Serbian leadership, which shows Serbian nationalism growing more ambitious.
[hist-eur1-s5.3.6] Serbia's example of autonomy under a local prince influenced other Balkan nationalists, who saw that pressure plus Great Power support could win self-government.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.b] The Greek revolt began as a secret plot by Greek merchants. It failed in the north but caught fire in the Peloponnese, the southern peninsula of Greece, and soon declared itself a nation.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.0] The Philiki Etaireia (Friendly Society) was a secret society founded in Odessa in 1814 by Greek merchants. It planned an armed rising against the Ottomans, recruited among merchants and intellectuals, and hoped for Russian support.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.1] Alexander Ypsilantis, a Greek officer in the Russian army, crossed into Moldavia in March 1821 to launch a rising in the Danubian principalities. Tsar Alexander I disowned him, so he received no Russian help.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.2] The Ottomans defeated Ypsilantis at Dragashani in June 1821, so the rising in the principalities failed. Name only Alexander, not his brother Demetrios, who fought in the Peloponnese, so that the two are not confused.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.3] The rising in the Peloponnese began in March 1821 (25 March is celebrated as Greek Independence Day) and spread to central Greece and the islands.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.4] Atrocities occurred on both sides, for example at Tripolitsa. When the Greeks took Tripolitsa, the Ottoman centre in the Peloponnese, in September 1821 they massacred its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.5] The Ottomans took reprisals: Patriarch Gregory V was hanged in Constantinople on Easter Sunday in April 1821, and in 1822 the Chios massacre killed or enslaved many thousands of Greeks.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.6] The First National Assembly at Epidaurus declared independence in January 1822 and adopted a provisional constitution, so the rebels now claimed to be a state and not just rebels.
[hist-eur1-s5.4.7] Civil wars among the Greek leaders in 1823–25 weakened the rising and left it open to counter-attack.
[hist-eur1-s5.5.b] Atrocities such as Chios turned European opinion towards the Greeks, even though Europe's conservative governments disliked rebellion. This public sympathy made it harder for the Powers to leave the Greeks to the Sultan.
[hist-eur1-s5.5.0] Philhellenism, European enthusiasm for Greek independence, drew on classical education (Greece as the home of ancient culture), Christian sympathy for fellow Christians, and romanticism. It brought volunteers and money to the Greek cause.
[hist-eur1-s5.5.1] The English poet Lord Byron arrived in Greece in 1823 and joined the cause. He died of fever at Missolonghi on 19 April 1824, which made him a symbol of the cause across Europe.
[hist-eur1-s5.5.2] Eugène Delacroix's paintings, The Massacre at Chios (1824) and Greece on the Ruins of Missolonghi (1826), gave the Greek cause a lasting image and kept public sympathy alive.
[hist-eur1-s5.5.3] Metternich, the Austrian chancellor, opposed the Greeks because he saw them as rebels against a legitimate ruler. At the Congress of Verona in 1822 the Greek delegates were not received.
[hist-eur1-s5.5.4] The Egyptian ruler (pasha) Muhammad Ali sent his son Ibrahim Pasha, who landed in the Peloponnese in 1825 and brought a new wave of destruction that reduced much of the region.
[hist-eur1-s5.5.5] Missolonghi fell to the Ottomans in April 1826 after a long siege, and the Greek cause was close to defeat. The fall strengthened European sympathy instead of ending it.
[hist-eur1-s5.6.b] With the Greeks near defeat, Britain, France and Russia stepped in. Their intervention, not the rebels' own strength, decided the outcome, as the fleet action at Navarino shows.
[hist-eur1-s5.6.0] Nicholas I became Tsar in December 1825 and was ready to act for the Greeks, because Russia wished to protect Orthodox Christians. The St Petersburg Protocol (April 1826, Britain and Russia, after Wellington's mission) began a joint approach.
[hist-eur1-s5.6.1] The Treaty of London (July 1827), signed by Britain, France and Russia, aimed to impose an armistice on the Ottomans and Egypt and to secure Greek autonomy. Navarino was fought as a result.
[hist-eur1-s5.6.2] The battle of Navarino took place on 20 October 1827. British, French and Russian squadrons under the British admiral Codrington destroyed the Ottoman–Egyptian fleet in Navarino Bay, in the south-west Peloponnese.
[hist-eur1-s5.6.3] Navarino made Greek independence possible. Ibrahim's forces could no longer be supplied by sea, and a French expeditionary force landed in the Peloponnese in 1828 and Ibrahim's army withdrew.
[hist-eur1-s5.6.4] In the Russo-Turkish War (1828–29) Russia fought the Sultan, and in the Treaty of Adrianople (September 1829) the Sultan accepted the Powers' settlement on Greece.
[hist-eur1-s5.6.5] The Treaty of Adrianople also gave Russia the mouths of the Danube and confirmed its influence over Moldavia and Wallachia, so Russia gained as well as the Greeks.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.b] Independence was won on paper in 1830, but Greece was a small state whose rulers were chosen by the Great Powers. Its first governor was killed, its first king was a foreign teenager, and many Greeks lived outside its borders.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.0] Ioannis Kapodistrias, a former joint foreign minister of Russia (to 1822), was elected governor of Greece in 1827 and arrived in January 1828. He was the first governor of independent Greece.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.1] Kapodistrias was assassinated at Nafplio in October 1831 by members of the Mavromichalis family, whose leader he had imprisoned.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.2] The London Protocol of 3 February 1830 made Greece an independent state under the guarantee (protection) of Britain, France and Russia. This differs from the 1852 protocol of the same name on the Danish succession.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.3] By the Convention of London in May 1832, Otto, the teenage son of King Ludwig I of Bavaria, was chosen as king. He arrived at Nafplio in 1833, so both 1832 (chosen) and 1833 (arrived) are correct.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.4] The 1832 frontier ran from the Gulf of Arta to the Gulf of Volos, which left most Greeks outside the kingdom.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.5] Otto's rule used Bavarian regents and officials and was autocratic. The capital moved from Nafplio to Athens in 1834, and the Bavarian regency ended in 1835.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.6] A revolt in Athens on 3 September 1843 forced Otto to grant a constitution, adopted in 1844. In the same year Ioannis Kolettis voiced the Megali Idea ('Great Idea'), the aim of uniting all Greeks in one state, which stayed the goal of Greek nationalism.
[hist-eur1-s5.7.7] Otto was deposed in October 1862 and the Powers chose Prince William of Denmark as King George I in 1863. Britain ceded the Ionian Islands to Greece in 1864.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.b] The Eastern Question exploded into a Great Power war in the 1850s. Its settlement guaranteed the Ottoman Empire but also made possible the union of Moldavia and Wallachia.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.0] The war grew from a dispute over the Holy Places between Catholic monks (backed by France) and Orthodox monks (backed by Russia). In 1853 Prince Menshikov's mission to Constantinople demanded a Russian protectorate over the Sultan's Orthodox subjects.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.1] Russia occupied Moldavia and Wallachia in July 1853, the Ottomans declared war in October 1853, and the Russian navy destroyed an Ottoman squadron at Sinope in November 1853. Britain and France declared war on Russia in March 1854.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.2] Russia withdrew from the principalities in 1854 because of Austrian pressure. The Crimean War (1853–56) is named for the Crimea, where the siege of Sevastopol lasted 1854–55 and the city fell in September 1855; Nicholas I died in March 1855 during the war.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.3] The Treaty of Paris (March 1856) guaranteed the integrity of the Ottoman Empire, neutralised the Black Sea (no warships or naval arsenals), and made Russia cede southern Bessarabia to Moldavia.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.4] It also placed Moldavia and Wallachia under the collective guarantee of the Powers, ending Russia's protectorate. The Hatt-i Humayun (February 1856), a decree the Powers had pressed for, promised equality to non-Muslim subjects.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.5] The Black Sea clauses removed Russian naval power from the region for a time, so Russia was weakened, and the Russian defeat became the trigger for emancipation of the serfs.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.6] Alexandru Ioan Cuza was elected prince of Moldavia in January 1859 and of Wallachia in February 1859. The union as Romania was recognised by the Sultan in December 1861 and proclaimed in 1862.
[hist-eur1-s5.8.7] Cuza was deposed in 1866 and replaced by Carol of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen. Romania stayed under Ottoman suzerainty until its independence was recognised in 1878.
[hist-eur1-s5.9.b] Balkan nationalism threatened the peace of Europe because every rising on Ottoman land invited the Great Powers to intervene. Yet the Powers usually co-operated to contain these crises, so the threat was real but limited.
[hist-eur1-s5.9.0] Threat: each rising invited Great Power intervention and rivalry, mainly Russia against Austria and Britain, which turned the Balkans into a focus of tension.
[hist-eur1-s5.9.1] Threat: the Greek revolt split the Congress System, because Britain and Russia acted apart from Metternich, who wanted to uphold legitimate rulers.
[hist-eur1-s5.9.2] Threat: the Russo-Ottoman wars (1828–29 and 1853) and the Crimean War, the only war between Great Powers in 1815–1854, began over the Eastern Question. Balkan crises in the 1850s and 1860s kept the Powers in tension.
[hist-eur1-s5.9.3] Limit of the threat: the Powers usually acted together to contain crises, as in the Treaty of London (1827), the London Protocol (1830) and the Treaty of Paris (1856). They imposed small, guaranteed states with foreign kings.
[hist-eur1-s5.9.4] Limit of the achievement: by 1871 only a small Greece was independent, Serbia and Romania were autonomous but tributary, and most Balkan Christians (Bulgarians, most Greeks, Bosnians) still lived under Ottoman rule.
[hist-eur1-s5.9.5] The Eastern Question stayed open, and the Ottoman Empire later got the nickname the sick man of Europe, a phrase attributed to no one here.
[hist-eur1-s5.10.b] Russia was the great autocracy of the East. Its Tsar held unlimited power, and the early hopes of reform under Alexander I faded without touching serfdom.
[hist-eur1-s5.10.0] Autocracy means rule by one person with unlimited authority. In Russia the Tsar had no constitution or national parliament to limit him, the nobility served the state, and the Orthodox Church supported the throne.
[hist-eur1-s5.10.1] Alexander I (reigned 1801–25) showed early reforming promise but took little action on serfdom. Mikhail Speransky drafted reform plans in 1809 but was dismissed in 1812.
[hist-eur1-s5.10.2] His Law on Free Cultivators (1803) let landlords free serfs, with land, by agreement. Very few landlords used it, so serfdom stayed in place.
[hist-eur1-s5.10.3] He granted a constitution to the Kingdom of Poland in 1815 and helped found the Holy Alliance (September 1815), and after 1815 he was involved in the Congress System. He became more reactionary after 1820.
[hist-eur1-s5.11.b] The first open challenge to the Tsar came from inside the elite. Young officers influenced by western ideas rose when the succession was unclear, and its failure shaped the reign that followed.
[hist-eur1-s5.11.0] The Decembrists were secret societies of young officers and nobles, influenced by western ideas after the wars of 1812–15. The Northern Society met in St Petersburg and the Southern Society, in the Ukraine, was led by Pavel Pestel.
[hist-eur1-s5.11.1] Alexander I died in November 1825. His brother Constantine had renounced the throne, so Nicholas succeeded, and this confusion over the succession gave the plotters their chance.
[hist-eur1-s5.11.2] On 14 December 1825 the Decembrists rose in Senate Square in St Petersburg demanding a constitution and the end of serfdom.
[hist-eur1-s5.11.3] Nicholas I crushed the revolt by artillery the same day, so it was over within hours. Five leaders, including Pavel Pestel and Kondraty Ryleev, were hanged in July 1826, and more than a hundred were exiled to Siberia.
[hist-eur1-s5.11.4] The revolt shaped Nicholas's rule, which was built on repression and strong censorship.
[hist-eur1-s5.12.b] Nicholas I answered the Decembrists with a system of control and strengthened the autocracy. He tried some administrative reform but would not tackle private serfdom.
[hist-eur1-s5.12.0] Nicholas I (reigned 1825–55) founded the Third Section of His Majesty's Own Chancellery in 1826, a political police force to monitor opponents of the regime.
[hist-eur1-s5.12.1] In 1833 Sergei Uvarov, the Minister of Education, proclaimed the doctrine of Orthodoxy, Autocracy and Nationality (Official Nationality) to define Russian identity and bind subjects to the throne.
[hist-eur1-s5.12.2] The autocracy kept rigid control of the press, universities and education, and censorship was tight under Nicholas I.
[hist-eur1-s5.12.3] Speransky's codification of the laws appeared as the Complete Collection of the Laws (1830) and the Digest of Laws (1832–33).
[hist-eur1-s5.12.4] Pavel Kiselev, Minister of State Domains from 1837, reformed the administration of the state peasants (peasants on state land) in 1837–41, but Nicholas did not tackle private serfdom.
[hist-eur1-s5.12.5] Nicholas was the 'gendarme of Europe': he crushed the Polish rising of 1830–31, abolished the 1815 Polish constitution, and helped Austria crush the Hungarians in 1849.
[hist-eur1-s5.12.6] In 1849 the Petrashevsky circle was arrested and Fyodor Dostoevsky was sentenced to death, reprieved at the place of execution and sent to Siberia, which shows how far repression went.
[hist-eur1-s5.13.b] Serfdom was the foundation of Russian society and the economy. It had already vanished from western Europe, so Russia stood out as backward.
[hist-eur1-s5.13.0] Serfs were peasants bound to the land and to the authority of a noble owner. Landlords controlled their labour, movement and marriage, punished them, and could sell or mortgage them.
[hist-eur1-s5.13.1] Serfs were subject to corporal punishment and the lord's courts, and could be sent into the army for long service. The estate system kept their lives under their owners' control.
[hist-eur1-s5.13.2] In the late 1850s there were about 22–23 million privately owned serfs, about a third of the empire's population. State peasants were roughly as numerous, so most Russians were unfree.
[hist-eur1-s5.13.3] Serfdom had gone from western Europe by then (in France from 1789; Prussia began abolishing it in 1807), so Russia's system looked old-fashioned.
[hist-eur1-s5.14.b] Serfs paid their owners with labour or money, and the system damaged the economy. Landlords had little reason to improve estates, and the serfs had none either.
[hist-eur1-s5.14.0] Barshchina was labour dues, when serfs worked on the lord's land, usually about three days a week. It was most common in the fertile black-earth region of the south.
[hist-eur1-s5.14.1] Obrok was quitrent, a payment in money or produce, and was more common in the less fertile north and centre. Household serfs (dvorovye) worked as domestic servants.
[hist-eur1-s5.14.2] Serfdom held back agricultural productivity: landlords had little incentive to invest and serfs had little interest in improving land they did not own. Strip farming and the commune added to the problem.
[hist-eur1-s5.14.3] It created little demand for manufactures, since peasants had little money to spend, and tied labour to the land, so a free workforce could not grow.
[hist-eur1-s5.14.4] Possessional serfs, and peasants tied to their villages by passports, worked in Urals ironworks and other factories, which limited the growth of a free labour force.
[hist-eur1-s5.14.5] By 1859 about two-thirds of privately owned serfs were mortgaged by their owners to state credit institutions, so many noble estates were deeply in debt.
[hist-eur1-s5.14.6] Serf unrest brought many local disturbances under Nicholas I, and landlords feared a Pugachev-style revolt (the great rising of 1773–75).
[hist-eur1-s5.15.b] By the 1850s there were moral, economic and military arguments against serfdom. The Crimean War made the case impossible to ignore, because it exposed an army and economy held back by the system.
[hist-eur1-s5.15.0] Moral argument: literature such as Ivan Turgenev's A Sportsman's Sketches (1852) showed the cruelty of serf life to educated readers, which turned opinion against the system.
[hist-eur1-s5.15.1] Economic argument: free labour would be more productive than forced labour, and noble estates were heavily mortgaged.
[hist-eur1-s5.15.2] Military argument: the Crimean defeat showed a backward army and economy, and a serf army could not have a large trained reserve.
[hist-eur1-s5.15.3] Serfdom was debated in secret committees under Nicholas I, but the government took no decisive action before the Crimean War exposed the weaknesses of the system.
[hist-eur1-s5.15.4] Fear of revolt added urgency, since serf unrest was growing and landlords remembered Pugachev.
[hist-eur1-s5.16.b] Alexander II acted after the Crimean defeat and chose to free the serfs from above, on terms the nobles could accept. The edict gave legal freedom, but at a price for the peasants.
[hist-eur1-s5.16.0] Alexander II (reigned 1855–81) made emancipation a priority because of Russia's defeat in the Crimean War, which had shown the empire's backwardness. The fall of Sevastopol in September 1855 forced Russia to accept peace in 1856.
[hist-eur1-s5.16.1] In a speech to the Moscow nobility in March 1856 he said, in paraphrase, that it was better to abolish serfdom from 'above' than to wait until it began to abolish itself from 'below'.
[hist-eur1-s5.16.2] Preparation began with provincial noble committees from 1857, and the Editing Commissions of 1859, set up under Yakov Rostovtsev with Nikolai Milyutin a leading member, drafted the terms.
[hist-eur1-s5.16.3] The Manifesto and Statutes were issued on 19 February 1861 (3 March in the western calendar). This was one event and not two.
[hist-eur1-s5.16.4] The edict freed about 22–23 million privately owned serfs from personal bondage, with rights to marry, move, own property, trade and go to court.
[hist-eur1-s5.16.5] Peasants received allotments of land, for example, but landlords kept much of the land and often the best of it. In the black-earth provinces peasants often lost a large part of land they had worked (the otrezki, 'cut-offs').
[hist-eur1-s5.16.6] Household serfs were freed without land, so they had freedom but no way of earning a living from the land.
[hist-eur1-s5.17.b] Freedom was not free. Peasants had to pay for their land over decades, through a village commune that also controlled them, and until then they still owed their old lords.
[hist-eur1-s5.17.0] Until redemption (buying out the land) was agreed, peasants were temporarily obligated to their former lords, paying dues in labour or obrok for the land they used.
[hist-eur1-s5.17.1] There was a two-year transition before the statutes took full effect. Redemption became compulsory for all remaining temporarily obligated peasants from 1883.
[hist-eur1-s5.17.2] Redemption payments worked as a loan: the state advanced about three-quarters to four-fifths of the land's price to landlords in bonds, and peasants repaid the state over 49 years at 6 per cent interest, paying the remaining part to the landlord directly.
[hist-eur1-s5.17.3] Many historians judge the land prices too high, so peasants paid more than the land was worth and were left poor and indebted for decades.
[hist-eur1-s5.17.4] The mir (also called the obshchina, the village commune) received the land, redistributed strips in many areas, and was collectively responsible for taxes and redemption payments.
[hist-eur1-s5.17.5] Peasants needed the mir's permission to leave, so it kept them tied to the village and to strip farming.
[hist-eur1-s5.17.6] Redemption payments were halved for 1906 and abolished from 1 January 1907, after the 1905 revolution.
[hist-eur1-s5.18.b] Emancipation changed the legal position of the peasants but left them poor, and it left the Tsar's power untouched. Other reforms followed because a freed society needed new institutions.
[hist-eur1-s5.18.0] For peasants: personal freedom and civil rights, but land hunger, debt and poverty. Troops fired on peasants at Bezdna (Kazan province) in April 1861, who believed the real emancipation was being hidden from them.
[hist-eur1-s5.18.1] For the economy: slow agricultural improvement, because the mir kept strip farming and tied peasants to villages, but also a gradual move to wage labour and, later, a labour supply for industry.
[hist-eur1-s5.18.2] Landlords' debts were often deducted from their compensation, and many nobles sold land in later decades.
[hist-eur1-s5.18.3] Emancipation came from above and kept the Tsar's power intact, with no national parliament. Radical and revolutionary opposition grew in the 1860s and 1870s, and Alexander II was assassinated on 1 March 1881.
[hist-eur1-s5.18.4] The zemstva (elected district and provincial councils) were set up in 1864, and the judicial reform of 1864 brought jury trial, independent judges and public trials.
[hist-eur1-s5.18.5] Town dumas (town councils) followed in 1870, and a military reform under Dmitry Milyutin in 1874 shortened service.
[hist-eur1-s5.19.b] Both halves of this subsection show change that came from above or from outside, with real gains and real limits. Examiners reward paired judgements and a clear verdict.
[hist-eur1-s5.19.0] Achievement: Serbia became autonomous (1830), Greece independent (1830–32) and Romania united (1859–62), the first national states carved from the Ottoman Empire. Greece was the first state to win full independence.
[hist-eur1-s5.19.1] Limit: success depended on Great Power intervention and rivalry, not on the nationalists' own strength, and the new states were small with imposed rulers. Most Balkan Christians were still under Ottoman rule in 1871.
[hist-eur1-s5.19.2] Stability: a real threat in Navarino, the 1828–29 war and the Crimean War, against containment by Great Power co-operation in London (1827 and 1830) and Paris (1856).
[hist-eur1-s5.19.3] Achievement: emancipation ended personal bondage for about a third of the population, peacefully and from above, and opened the way to further reforms.
[hist-eur1-s5.19.4] Limit: land loss, redemption debt, the mir and continuing poverty, with the autocracy unchanged.
[hist-eur1-s5.19.5] Views: contemporaries were disappointed because peasants expected all the land free, whereas later historians see a major but flawed reform.
[hist-eur1-s5.19.6] Overall: Balkan nationalism created new states but fed Great Power rivalry, while emancipation was a major reform that left the peasants with real grievances.

CANDIDATES hist-eur1-s1: 1. The Vienna settlement and the system of Metternich under attack, 1815–1848
[hist-eur1-s1.0.b] To understand the settlement of 1815 you need to know what it was reacting to. Napoleon had spread revolutionary ideas across Europe, and the victors wanted to undo his work and make sure it could not happen again.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.0] The French Revolution and Napoleon spread liberal ideas (written constitutions, elected parliaments) and national ideas (that a people sharing a language and history should form a state), which unsettled the rulers who had fought him.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.1] Napoleon dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in 1806 and set up the Confederation of the Rhine the same year, so Germany was reorganised before 1815 and the old order could not simply be switched back on.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.2] Napoleon was beaten at the Battle of Leipzig (the Battle of the Nations) in October 1813. The first Treaty of Paris (30 May 1814) left France roughly its 1792 frontiers.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.3] Napoleon escaped from Elba in March 1815 (the Hundred Days) and was finally beaten at Waterloo on 18 June 1815. The second Treaty of Paris (20 November 1815) then reduced France to roughly its 1790 frontiers, imposed an indemnity of 700 million francs and left an allied army of occupation.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.4] The great powers of 1815 were Austria, Britain, Prussia and Russia, with defeated France soon readmitted to their company.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.5] The Habsburg (Austrian) Empire was a dynastic, multinational state ruled by one family. Its peoples included Germans, Hungarians (Magyars), Czechs, Slovaks, Poles, Croats, Serbs, Slovenes, Romanians and Italians, so nationalism threatened to break it apart.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.b] Four ideas run through the whole period. Metternich's system makes sense only as conservatism and autocracy defending themselves against liberalism and nationalism.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.0] Liberalism means constitutions, elected parliaments, civil liberties such as a free press and free speech, and limits on rulers. It was supported mainly by the educated middle class and students, which is why universities became centres of trouble.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.1] Nationalism means that a people sharing a language, culture and history should have its own state. For Germans and Italians it meant unity; for Greeks, Belgians and Poles it meant independence from foreign rule.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.2] Autocracy means rule by one person with unlimited power and no constitution or elected body. In practice Austria, Russia and Prussia were autocracies, so liberal demands for constitutions challenged them directly.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.3] Conservatism was Metternich's own belief that monarchy, aristocracy, the Church, tradition and hierarchy are the basis of order. He therefore saw liberals and nationalists as threats to society itself, not just to his government.
[hist-eur1-s1.2.b] The powers that defeated Napoleon met at Vienna to redraw the map of Europe. They wanted to contain France, restore the old order and prevent another bid for European domination.
[hist-eur1-s1.2.0] The Congress met from autumn 1814, as the delegations gathered between September and November 1814, and its Final Act was signed on 9 June 1815, nine days before Waterloo (18 June 1815).
[hist-eur1-s1.2.1] The leading delegates were Metternich for Austria, Castlereagh for Britain (Wellington replaced him in early 1815), Tsar Alexander I for Russia, Hardenberg for Prussia and Talleyrand for defeated France. The first four are the Big Four, with Talleyrand as the fifth delegate.
[hist-eur1-s1.2.2] Metternich was Austrian foreign minister (from 1809) at the Congress and hosted it at Vienna, so he dominated the talks. He became state chancellor only in 1821.
[hist-eur1-s1.2.3] The aims were to contain France, restore legitimate rulers, create a balance of power, reward the victors and prevent revolution. The last aim explains why the powers ignored the demands of liberals and nationalists.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.b] Two principles guided the settlement. Legitimacy meant putting back the rulers Napoleon had removed, and the balance of power meant that no single state should be strong enough to dominate Europe again.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.0] Legitimacy: rulers deposed by Napoleon were restored. Louis XVIII returned in France (1814), Ferdinand VII in Spain (1814) and Ferdinand IV (later Ferdinand I of the Two Sicilies) in Naples (1815). The Pope regained the Papal States.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.1] Balance of power: a ring of buffer states was built around France so that it could not expand again. This ring included the Kingdom of the Netherlands, enlarged with Belgium (the former Austrian Netherlands), and Piedmont-Sardinia, strengthened by gaining Genoa.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.2] Prussia was placed on the Rhine so it could guard against France, and Switzerland's neutrality was guaranteed in 1815, completing the barrier against France.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.3] Britain, represented by Castlereagh, favoured a moderate peace that did not humiliate France, because a resentful France would be a lasting danger to the balance.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.b] The victors were rewarded with territory, mostly in the places that would block France or balance each other. The gains of Austria, Prussia and Russia shaped central Europe for decades.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.0] Austria gave up the Austrian Netherlands but gained Lombardy-Venetia in northern Italy, and also the Tyrol, Salzburg and the Illyrian provinces and Dalmatia on the Adriatic coast.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.1] Habsburg relatives ruled Tuscany and Modena, and Parma went to Marie-Louise (Napoleon's Habsburg wife), so Austria dominated the Italian peninsula either directly or through relatives.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.2] Prussia gained the Rhineland, Westphalia, about two-fifths of Saxony and Swedish Pomerania. Holding the Rhineland made Prussia the guardian of the Rhine frontier against France, the 'watch on the Rhine'.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.3] Russia gained most of the Duchy of Warsaw as the Kingdom of Poland (Congress Poland), with the Tsar as its king.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.4] Britain kept colonies and bases taken during the wars: Malta, the Cape Colony, Heligoland and a protectorate over the Ionian Islands. These gave its navy bases and secured sea routes.
[hist-eur1-s1.5.b] Italy shows the settlement's priorities clearly. Old rulers and Austrian power mattered more to the diplomats than the wishes of the peoples being governed.
[hist-eur1-s1.5.0] Italy after 1815 was divided into separate states, not a nation. Austria dominated it through Lombardy-Venetia and its Habsburg relatives, the Papal States were restored to the Pope, and Piedmont was strengthened by Genoa as a barrier against France.
[hist-eur1-s1.5.1] Nationalist and liberal hopes awakened by the French Revolution and Napoleon were ignored. Germans, Italians, Poles and Belgians were placed under rulers chosen by the great powers.
[hist-eur1-s1.5.2] Judgement of the settlement: it kept the peace among the great powers, but it ignored liberal and national feeling, so it stored up trouble that later broke out in 1820, 1830 and 1848.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.b] Napoleon had destroyed the old German structure, and the Congress had to put something in its place. It chose a loose league of rulers, not a nation-state, which suited Austria.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.0] The Federal Act (June 1815, signed on 8 June) replaced the Holy Roman Empire, dissolved in 1806, with a German Confederation (Bund) of 39 sovereign states. Counts vary slightly with later changes, but 39 is the figure to use.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.1] Its only institution was a Diet (Bundestag) at Frankfurt, made up of delegates sent by the member governments, with Austria presiding. There was no elected national parliament, so ordinary Germans had no voice in it.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.2] Important decisions needed large majorities or unanimity, and the member states kept their own rulers and armies. This meant the Bund could act only when Austria and Prussia agreed (Austro-Prussian dualism).
[hist-eur1-s1.6.3] Its main purpose was the security of its members against France and against revolution, not German unity, which is why it gave Germans no national institutions.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.4] Article 13 of the Federal Act promised each state a constitution with assemblies of estates. Saxe-Weimar (1816), Bavaria (1818), Baden (1818) and Württemberg (1819) granted constitutions.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.5] Austria granted none, and Prussia's king, who had promised a constitution in 1815, did not grant one. This angered liberals, who felt the promise of Article 13 had been broken.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.6] Austria and Prussia were the two dominant members, and their rivalry shaped German politics until 1866.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.b] After the settlement the powers wanted a way to defend it. Two alliances were signed in 1815, and they are easily confused because they had very different purposes.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.0] The Holy Alliance (26 September 1815) was proposed by Alexander I and signed by Russia, Austria and Prussia. It pledged rulers to govern by Christian principles, and Britain refused to join.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.1] Castlereagh called the Holy Alliance 'a piece of sublime mysticism and nonsense' and Metternich called it 'a loud-sounding nothing'. Yet Metternich used it to bind the eastern monarchies together, so it came to stand for the eastern autocracies.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.2] The Quadruple Alliance (20 November 1815, the same day as the second Treaty of Paris) was the working treaty. Britain, Austria, Prussia and Russia bound themselves to uphold the settlement, keep France in check and meet periodically.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.3] Those periodic meetings gave birth to the Congress System, the series of congresses of 1818–1822 at which the great powers managed crises together.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.b] The congresses began as cooperation among the victors, but they turned into a tool for crushing revolutions. Britain would not follow that path, and the split ended the system.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.0] Aix-la-Chapelle (1818) ended the allied occupation of France and admitted France to the Concert of Europe, making the Quadruple Alliance a Quintuple Alliance.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.1] Troppau (1820): Austria, Prussia and Russia signed the Troppau Protocol, claiming the right to intervene against revolutions that threatened other states. Britain refused, and France did not sign. Castlereagh's State Paper of May 1820 rejected general intervention.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.2] Laibach (1821) applied the Troppau principle: Austria was authorised to restore Ferdinand in Naples, and Austrian troops crushed the Neapolitan revolution in 1821, and also the Piedmontese rising of the same year.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.3] Verona (1822): the eastern powers approved French intervention in Spain. France invaded in 1823 and restored Ferdinand VII as an absolute ruler. Wellington represented Britain at Verona and withdrew.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.4] Castlereagh died (suicide) in August 1822, and George Canning became British foreign secretary. Canning broke with the system and recognised the Latin American republics in the 1820s.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.5] After Britain's withdrawal, what remained was an alliance of the three eastern autocracies (Austria, Prussia and Russia).
[hist-eur1-s1.9.b] Metternich's aim was to preserve the 1815 settlement and the multinational Habsburg Empire, which liberalism and nationalism threatened to break apart. At home that meant autocracy backed by watchfulness.
[hist-eur1-s1.9.0] His aims were to preserve the 1815 settlement and the Habsburg Empire, to resist liberalism and nationalism, and to keep Austria's leadership in Germany and Italy.
[hist-eur1-s1.9.1] Austria was an autocracy under Emperor Francis I (ruled to 1835) and then Ferdinand I (1835–1848), who was mentally unfit. There was no constitution or parliament, and power rested with the emperor, his ministers, the army and the bureaucracy.
[hist-eur1-s1.9.2] Under Ferdinand I government was carried on by a State Conference in which Metternich's rival, Count Kolowrat, was powerful, so Metternich was not in sole control at home.
[hist-eur1-s1.9.3] Inside Austria, police, informers, the opening of letters and strict censorship of books, newspapers and the theatre watched and silenced critics, so liberal ideas could spread only in secret.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.b] Metternich needed a reason to extend his methods from Austria to the whole of Germany. The murder of a conservative writer in 1819 gave him one.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.0] In March 1819 the conservative dramatist August von Kotzebue was murdered by Karl Sand, a Burschenschaft (student fraternity) member. Metternich used the murder to argue that radical students were a danger to every German government.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.1] Ministers met at Carlsbad in August 1819 and the Diet adopted the Decrees in September 1819. Friedrich von Gentz helped Metternich draft them.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.2] The Decrees banned the Burschenschaften, put each university under a government curator (supervisor), and allowed liberal professors and students to be dismissed or expelled.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.3] They also imposed censorship on newspapers and shorter publications (under 20 printed sheets), so that new liberal ideas could not easily be printed.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.4] A Central Investigating Commission at Mainz was set up under the Decrees to hunt down revolutionary conspiracies across the German states.
[hist-eur1-s1.11.b] The Hambach Festival of May 1832 alarmed the princes. The Diet responded by tightening the controls of 1819 even further.
[hist-eur1-s1.11.0] The Six Articles (28 June 1832), passed by the Diet after the Hambach Festival, reasserted the princes' authority: state parliaments could not withhold taxes to force concessions, and the Diet could intervene in states where order broke down.
[hist-eur1-s1.11.1] Further measures in July 1832 (the Ten Articles) banned political associations, public meetings and the display of political colours, and tightened press controls. Banning colours was meant to stop displays such as the black, red and gold flags shown at Hambach.
[hist-eur1-s1.12.b] Metternich could not hold Austria's position by Austrian strength alone. He relied on the congresses to justify intervention, and on two other powers to make it work.
[hist-eur1-s1.12.0] Abroad, Metternich used the congresses and the alliance with Russia and Prussia to justify intervention, and Austrian troops crushed risings in Naples and Piedmont (1821) and in Modena, Parma and the Papal Legations (1831).
[hist-eur1-s1.12.1] The system relied on Prussia's cooperation inside the Bund and on Russia's support in Europe, so it was weakened when either went its own way.
[hist-eur1-s1.12.2] Seen together, so that the two sides explain each other, the system had an international side (the Congress System and intervention) and an internal side (censorship, police, the Carlsbad Decrees and the Bund).
[hist-eur1-s1.13.b] The first challenges to the settlement came from liberal and nationalist students and from army-led revolts in the south of Europe. Metternich could crush all of them, but they showed that the old order was not secure.
[hist-eur1-s1.13.0] The first Burschenschaft was founded at Jena in June 1815. These student fraternities spread liberal nationalism, and adopted black, red and gold, linked to the Lützow Free Corps of the war of liberation and later the German national colours.
[hist-eur1-s1.13.1] The Wartburg Festival (18 October 1817) gathered about 500 students to mark 300 years since Luther's Reformation (1517) and the fourth anniversary of Leipzig. The students called for German unity, and conservative books were burned.
[hist-eur1-s1.13.2] The revolutions of 1820–21 broke out in Spain (January 1820, an army rising that restored the 1812 constitution), Portugal (1820), Naples (July 1820) and Piedmont (March 1821). Kings were forced to grant constitutions.
[hist-eur1-s1.13.3] These revolutions were suppressed. Austrian troops restored absolutism in Naples and Piedmont in 1821, and French troops did so in Spain in 1823.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.b] The Greek revolt against the Ottoman Empire was different from the revolutions in southern Europe, because the great powers themselves ended up helping the rebels. This went directly against Metternich's wishes.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.0] The Greek rising began in 1821. Lord Byron died at Missolonghi in April 1824, and his death boosted philhellenism (enthusiasm for the Greek cause) across Europe.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.1] Britain, France and Russia intervened on the rebels' side, for example when their fleets destroyed the Turkish-Egyptian fleet at the Battle of Navarino on 20 October 1827.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.2] The London Protocol (February 1830) recognised Greek independence, and the Convention of London (1832) made Greece a monarchy under Otto of Bavaria.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.3] Metternich had opposed this support for rebels against a legitimate ruler. The case showed that the powers could no longer be held to a common line against revolution.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.b] The year 1830 was a larger blow than anything in the 1820s, because revolution succeeded in the west and rocked the settlement's borders. Only the eastern risings were crushed.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.0] The July Revolution in France (July 1830, the 'Three Glorious Days') overthrew Charles X after his July Ordinances. Louis-Philippe, Duke of Orléans, became 'King of the French'. Charles X was the last king of the senior Bourbon line.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.1] Revolt broke out in Brussels in August 1830, separating Belgium from the Netherlands and breaching the 1815 settlement, which had joined them. The London Conference (1830–31) recognised Belgian independence, and Leopold of Saxe-Coburg became Leopold I in July 1831.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.2] The Treaty of London (1839) guaranteed Belgian neutrality, so the great powers accepted the change.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.3] The November Uprising (November 1830) in Russian Poland was crushed in 1831, and Nicholas I replaced the Polish constitution with the Organic Statute in 1832.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.4] Risings in Modena, Parma and the Papal Legations in February 1831 were put down by Austrian troops, so Metternich's armies were again the means of repression in Italy.
[hist-eur1-s1.16.b] The unrest of 1830 reached Germany too. Some princes gave way, and in 1832 a mass festival made the demand for unity and liberty public.
[hist-eur1-s1.16.0] Several states gained constitutions after the 1830 unrest: Saxony and Hesse-Kassel (1831), Brunswick (1832) and Hanover (1833).
[hist-eur1-s1.16.1] In 1837 the new king of Hanover cancelled the constitution, and seven Göttingen professors who protested, among them the Grimm brothers, were dismissed (the Göttingen Seven). This showed the limits of liberal gains.
[hist-eur1-s1.16.2] The Hambach Festival (27 May 1832) took place at Hambach Castle in the Bavarian Palatinate and brought about 20,000–30,000 people together under black, red and gold flags.
[hist-eur1-s1.16.3] The crowds demanded German unity, a free press and popular sovereignty (the idea that power comes from the people, not the ruler). This was what prompted the Six Articles and the July 1832 measures.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.b] While Metternich policed ideas, Prussia was quietly uniting Germany's economy. This gave it an advantage that Austria could not match.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.0] Prussia's Customs Law of May 1818 abolished internal duties across its scattered territories and set a low external tariff.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.1] Neighbouring states joined Prussia's system. The treaties were agreed in 1833, and the Zollverein (German Customs Union) came into force on 1 January 1834 with 18 states. Baden joined in 1835.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.2] Members traded freely with each other behind a common external tariff and shared the customs revenue in proportion to population.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.3] Austria stayed outside, because its protected industries and separate tariff system could not fit a low-tariff union. Austria was never a member, and Metternich feared the union was building Prussian influence.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.4] Leadership of the Zollverein gave Prussia economic leadership of Germany and accustomed the smaller states to looking to Berlin rather than Vienna.
[hist-eur1-s1.18.b] The system survived the shocks of 1830, but the revolution of 1848 brought it down within days. Economic distress in the 1840s had left many people ready to revolt.
[hist-eur1-s1.18.0] News of the February 1848 revolution in Paris, together with the economic distress of the 1840s, sparked demonstrations in German and Austrian cities.
[hist-eur1-s1.18.1] On 13 March 1848 students and workers demonstrated in Vienna and troops fired on the crowd. The court made Metternich resign that evening, and he fled, reaching England.
[hist-eur1-s1.18.2] His fall removed the main defender of the 1815 settlement, and revolution spread across the Habsburg lands and the German states.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.b] Prince Clemens von Metternich, a Rhineland aristocrat, guided Habsburg foreign policy from 1809 to 1848 and gave his name to the conservative order of 1815–1848.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.0] Born in Koblenz on 15 May 1773, he studied at Strasbourg and Mainz and married Eleonore von Kaunitz (1795), granddaughter of the former chancellor Kaunitz. French revolutionary armies took his family's Rhineland estates, which shaped his lifelong hatred of revolution.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.1] He was ambassador to Saxony (Dresden, 1801), Prussia (Berlin, 1803) and France (Paris, 1806), and arranged the marriage of Marie-Louise to Napoleon (1810). He was Austrian foreign minister from October 1809, was made a prince in 1813 and became state chancellor in 1821.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.2] He was a direct participant: he hosted and dominated the Congress of Vienna, led the congresses, drafted the Carlsbad Decrees and the 1832 measures, and sent Austrian troops into Italy.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.3] He believed that liberalism and nationalism would destroy the multinational Habsburg Empire, so he defended monarchy, legitimacy and the balance of power. He denied that there was a Metternich system, seeing his policy as the defence of the natural order.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.4] His policy was shaped by events he could not control: Greek independence, the 1830 revolutions and the Zollverein all showed the limits of his power, and 1848 ended his career.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.5] In 1847 he dismissed Italy as 'a geographical expression', meaning it was not a nation.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.6] After exile in England (1848) and Brussels he returned to Vienna in 1851 and died there on 11 June 1859. His memoirs (papers edited by his son Richard) were published from 1880.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.7] Contemporaries: conservative rulers, including the Tsar, praised him as the guardian of peace, while liberals and nationalists saw him as the symbol of reaction, censorship and police rule.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.8] Historians: Heinrich von Srbik (1925) and Henry Kissinger (A World Restored, 1957) praised him for the stable peace he built. Paul Schroeder (1962) was critical, calling his policy mere repression,. German nationalist historians such as Treitschke saw him as the enemy of German unity, and Alan Sked (2008) defended him.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.b] A strong answer states a clear judgement at the start and then weighs success against limits with dates. A useful split is short term against long term, and international peace against domestic repression.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.0] Success: the great powers fought no general war among themselves between 1815 and the Crimean War (1853–56).
[hist-eur1-s1.20.1] Limit: the peace owed much to exhaustion after the long wars and to Britain's naval power, not only to Metternich.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.2] Success: revolutions in Italy (1821, 1831) and liberal movements in Germany (1819, 1832) were crushed, the Habsburg Empire survived intact until 1848, and Austria kept the presidency of the Bund.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.3] Limit: the Congress System broke up in the 1820s because Britain withdrew, and Greek and Belgian independence changed the 1815 map.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.4] Limit: Prussia's Zollverein built a German economic unit without Austria, laying a foundation for Prussian leadership.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.5] Limit: it was repression without reform, with no Austrian constitution and the nationalities unsatisfied, so the causes of unrest remained.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.6] Overall: the system delayed liberal and national change for over thirty years but did not remove its causes, and it collapsed within days of the Vienna rising of March 1848.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.7] Contemporary against later views: conservatives saw a guardian of peace and liberals a symbol of reaction, while later historians divide between Srbik, Kissinger and Sked in his defence and Schroeder against him.
