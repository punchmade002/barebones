TARGET hist-eur2-s4: 4. Serbia, the Second International and the outbreak of war in 1914
[hist-eur2-s4.0.b] To see why a quarrel with a small state could start a world war, you first need the setting. The Ottoman Empire was losing control of the Balkans, and two larger empires wanted to fill the gap.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.0] The Eastern Question was the problem of what would happen as Ottoman power in the Balkans declined. The decline left a vacuum, so Austria-Hungary and Russia both wanted to fill it and became rivals.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.1] Austria-Hungary was a multinational empire of Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, South Slavs and others. Any nationalist movement therefore threatened its survival, because each nationality could claim the right to leave.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.2] Pan-Slavism was the idea that Russia, the largest Slav and Orthodox Christian power, should protect and lead the Slavs of the Balkans. This gave Russia a reason to take Serbia's side.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.3] By 1907 Europe was split into two blocs: the Triple Alliance of 1882 (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the Triple Entente (Britain, France, Russia). A Balkan quarrel could therefore draw in several Great Powers.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.4] Russia was weakened by defeat in the Russo-Japanese War (1904-05) and by the revolution of 1905. This is why it was less able to stand up to Austria-Hungary and Germany in the crises that followed.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.b] Serbia was a young state whose independence was won through the Great Powers, and it stayed economically tied to its larger neighbour. Its nationalism soon pulled against that tie.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.0] The Congress of Berlin (1878, the Balkan settlement, not the African Berlin Conference of 1884-85) recognised Serbia's full independence from the Ottoman Empire and gave it extra territory.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.1] At the same Congress Austria-Hungary was given the right to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina, which stayed under nominal Ottoman rule. This set up the later crisis over Bosnia.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.2] Serbia became a kingdom in 1882 under Milan Obrenović. It was a client of Austria-Hungary (dependent on it and under its influence), whose market took Serbia's main exports of pigs and other livestock.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.3] Serbian nationalists, like other South Slav nationalists, dreamed of uniting the South Slavs (Serbs, Croats, Slovenes and the Slavs of Bosnia) in one state. This is why Serbia and Austria-Hungary were bound to clash.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.4] Millions of South Slavs lived inside Austria-Hungary, so a strong Serbia that attracted them threatened to break up the empire. Vienna saw Serbian nationalism as a danger to its survival, not just a foreign matter.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.b] A fulcrum is the pivot a lever turns on. After 1903 Serbia turned from Vienna to Russia, and from then on its disputes with Austria-Hungary pulled the bigger powers in behind it.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.0] In June 1903 army officers murdered King Alexander Obrenović and Queen Draga in Belgrade; Dragutin Dimitrijević (Apis) was one of the conspirators. Peter I of the Karadjordjević dynasty became king. Sources differ on the day, so give the month only.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.1] The new regime turned away from Vienna and towards Russia and France. The Obrenović dynasty had been pro-Austrian; the Karadjordjević dynasty was pro-Russian.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.2] Russia saw itself as protector of the Balkan Slavs and wanted to stop Austria-Hungary dominating the Balkans. It also wanted its warships to be able to pass through the Straits (the Bosporus and Dardanelles).
[hist-eur2-s4.2.3] The Pig War began in 1906, when Austria-Hungary closed its border to Serbian livestock. It wanted to punish Serbia for a customs agreement with Bulgaria (1905) and for ordering artillery from France rather than from Austria-Hungary.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.4] Serbia found other markets, so the Austrian economic hold weakened and Serbia became less dependent on Vienna. Sources disagree on when the Pig War ended, so give no end date.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.5] Franz Conrad von Hötzendorf, Chief of the Austro-Hungarian General Staff from 1906 (briefly removed 1911-12), repeatedly urged preventive war against Serbia. His fear was that a strong Serbia could encourage the empire's Slav nationalities to leave.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.6] Serbia was a fulcrum because a small state's quarrel with Austria-Hungary drew in Russia, its protector, and through the alliances Germany (behind Austria-Hungary) and France (behind Russia).
[hist-eur2-s4.3.b] In 1908 Austria-Hungary turned its occupation of Bosnia into outright annexation. This humiliated Russia and Serbia, who both resolved not to back down again.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.0] Austria-Hungary had occupied and administered Bosnia-Herzegovina since 1878, though it remained under nominal Ottoman rule. The Young Turk revolution of July 1908 in the Ottoman Empire raised the chance that Bosnia would be reclaimed, so Austria acted.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.1] In September 1908 the Austrian foreign minister, Count Aehrenthal, met the Russian foreign minister, Izvolsky, at Buchlau in Moravia. They discussed a swap: Austria would annex Bosnia, and Russia would gain support for opening the Straits to its warships.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.2] Austria announced the annexation on 6 October 1908, before Russia had won anything on the Straits, so Russia felt cheated. Bulgaria declared full independence at the same time.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.3] Serbia regarded Bosnia as having a large Serb population, so it was outraged. It protested, prepared for war and demanded compensation. The Narodna Odbrana (National Defence), a nationalist society, was founded in 1908 in reaction to the annexation.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.4] In March 1909 Germany demanded that Russia accept the annexation and drop its support for Serbia. Russia, still weak after the war with Japan and the revolution of 1905, gave way, and Serbia formally accepted it that month.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.5] The crisis humiliated Russia and Serbia and left both determined not to back down again. Russia accelerated its rearmament, and Austria-Hungary and Germany drew closer.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.6] Germany's backing of Austria-Hungary in the Balkans set a precedent, which is why it mattered again in July 1914.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.b] The Balkan states used the Ottomans' weakness to grab land, and then fought over the spoils. Serbia emerged much bigger, which alarmed Austria-Hungary.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.0] In 1912 the Balkan League was formed by Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro, with Russian encouragement. Italy's war for Libya (1911-12) had exposed Ottoman weakness, so the League struck.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.1] The First Balkan War began in October 1912 and was ended by the Treaty of London in May 1913. The League defeated the Ottomans, who lost most of their European lands.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.2] A conference of ambassadors met in London in 1912-13 under Sir Edward Grey, the British Foreign Secretary. It created an independent Albania, backed by Austria-Hungary and Italy, which denied Serbia the Adriatic port it wanted.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.3] The League then quarrelled over the spoils. In the Second Balkan War (June to August 1913) Bulgaria attacked Serbia and Greece and was beaten, with Romania and the Ottomans joining against it. The Treaty of Bucharest followed in August 1913.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.4] Over the two wars Serbia almost doubled its territory, gaining Kosovo (in the First Balkan War) and much of Macedonia (confirmed at Bucharest).
[hist-eur2-s4.4.5] In October 1913 an Austro-Hungarian ultimatum forced Serbia to withdraw its troops from Albania, and Serbia complied. A stronger Serbia, backed by Russia and unsatisfied over the Adriatic, alarmed Austria-Hungary further, and Conrad pressed for war.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.6] Conference diplomacy had contained the wars, but Austria-Hungary concluded that next time it must fight. Russia's prestige now depended on Serbia, so it too would find it hard to give way.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.b] Serbian nationalism now had a violent wing. Officers in Serbia armed young Bosnians, and one of them killed the heir to the Habsburg throne.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.0] The Black Hand (official name Unification or Death) was a secret society founded in May 1911 by Serbian army officers. Its leader was Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, head of Serbian military intelligence by 1914. It aimed at a Greater Serbia.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.1] Young Bosnia was a movement of young South Slav nationalists in Bosnia, mostly Bosnian Serbs. They were armed and trained with Black Hand help because both wanted to end Habsburg rule.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.2] Do not confuse the groups. The Black Hand was a secret society of officers (1911), the Narodna Odbrana was a public nationalist society (1908), and Young Bosnia was Princip's group of Bosnian youths.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.3] Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Habsburg throne, visited Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 after army manoeuvres. That day is Vidovdan (St Vitus's Day), the anniversary of the Serbian defeat by the Ottomans at Kosovo in 1389.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.4] In the morning Nedeljko Čabrinović threw a bomb that missed the Archduke's car. Later the car took a wrong turn and stopped near Gavrilo Princip, who shot Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie, Duchess of Hohenberg. Both died.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.5] The assassins were arrested. Princip, born in 1894, was 19, so he was under 20 and could not be executed. He was sentenced to 20 years and died of tuberculosis in prison in 1918.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.6] The conspirators' pistols and bombs came from Serbia via the Black Hand. How much the Serbian government, headed by Prime Minister Nikola Pašić, knew of the plot is disputed.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.7] Austria-Hungary decided to use the murder to crush Serbia, but first sought Germany's backing. Count Berchtold was the foreign minister, while Count Tisza, the Hungarian Prime Minister, at first opposed war. Count Hoyos was sent to Berlin on 5 July 1914.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.b] In the five weeks after Sarajevo each step made the next harder to avoid. First Austria-Hungary secured German support, then it set Serbia terms designed to be refused.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.0] On 5 July Wilhelm II promised Austria-Hungary support, and on 6 July Chancellor Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg confirmed it. This 'blank cheque' was unconditional support in whatever action it took against Serbia. Berlin hoped for a short, localised war.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.1] French President Raymond Poincaré and Prime Minister René Viviani visited St Petersburg from 20 to 23 July 1914. This reaffirmed French support for Russia, which encouraged Russia to stand by Serbia.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.2] On 23 July Austria-Hungary delivered an ultimatum (a final demand with a deadline) to Serbia: ten demands and a 48-hour deadline. They included the suppression of anti-Austrian propaganda and organisations, and Austrian officials to take part in the inquiry inside Serbia. It was meant to be refused.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.3] On 25 July Serbia accepted most demands but refused Austrian officials a part in the judicial inquiry on Serbian soil. Serbia began mobilising the same day, and Austria-Hungary broke off relations that day.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.4] On 26 July Grey proposed a conference, but Germany declined. The Tsar and Kaiser also exchanged the 'Willy-Nicky' telegrams in late July, but mediation failed. Sazonov was the Russian foreign minister.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.5] Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July 1914, three days after breaking off relations, and Belgrade was shelled from 29 July.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.b] Once Austria-Hungary was at war with Serbia, the alliances and military timetables turned a Balkan war into a European one within a week.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.0] Russia ordered partial mobilisation (against Austria-Hungary only) on 29 July and general mobilisation on 30 July. Germany demanded on 31 July that Russia stop, and treated general mobilisation as a cause of war.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.1] Germany and France ordered general mobilisation on 1 August. Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August, and Austria-Hungary declared war on Russia on 6 August.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.2] Alfred von Schlieffen, German Chief of the General Staff until 1906, drew up the Schlieffen Plan and Helmuth von Moltke the Younger modified it in 1914. It aimed to defeat France in about six weeks through Belgium before Russia was fully mobilised, then turn east.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.3] This is why Russian mobilisation meant war in the west. Germany had to strike France first and could not wait, so it would attack through Belgium whether or not France was the aggressor.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.4] Germany sent Belgium an ultimatum on 2 August and Belgium refused on 3 August. Germany declared war on France on 3 August and invaded Belgium on 4 August.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.5] Britain was a guarantor of Belgian neutrality under the Treaty of London of 1839 and used the invasion of Belgium as its reason. It was also concerned for the balance of power and had the Entente with France. Its ultimatum to Germany expired at 11 pm on 4 August 1914.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.6] Bethmann Hollweg called the 1839 treaty 'a scrap of paper' to the British ambassador, Sir Edward Goschen, on 4 August 1914. Britain's entry made it a European war and, through the empires, a world war.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.7] Italy declared neutrality in early August 1914 because the Triple Alliance was defensive and Austria-Hungary had not consulted it. It entered the war on the Entente side in 1915.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.b] The Second International was a federation of socialist and labour parties. Its members believed that workers had more in common across borders than with their own ruling classes, and it looked strong on paper before 1914.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.0] Marxism holds that society is divided by class struggle and that workers of all countries share interests. Its aim is to replace capitalism with socialism. The SPD (the Social Democratic Party of Germany) was Germany's Marxist party.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.1] The Second International was founded in Paris in July 1889, the centenary of the French Revolution, as a federation of socialist and labour parties. It had a permanent International Socialist Bureau in Brussels from 1900. It believed in workers' solidarity across borders.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.2] The German SPD was the largest and most respected party. In the 1912 Reichstag election it won 110 seats and became the biggest party. Other members were French socialists under Jean Jaurès, British Labour, and Russian and Polish Marxists such as Lenin and Luxemburg.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.3] Its parties were divided over reform or revolution. Eduard Bernstein, an SPD revisionist, wanted socialism by gradual reform, while revolutionary Marxists such as Luxemburg and Lenin rejected this. August Bebel led the SPD until his death in 1913.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.4] They also disagreed over how to stop a war. Jaurès and Keir Hardie supported a general strike against war, but the SPD leaders opposed making it binding.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.5] The Stuttgart Congress (1907) resolved that socialists should do everything to prevent war and, if it broke out, to work to end it quickly and use the crisis to hasten the fall of capitalism. The last part came from an amendment by Luxemburg, Lenin and Martov.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.6] An extraordinary Congress at Basel on 24-25 November 1912, held during the First Balkan War, issued a manifesto against war. It warned governments that war could bring revolution.
[hist-eur2-s4.9.b] The International's pledges to stop war were put to the test in the last days of July 1914. They failed, and the failure is the most examined part of its story.
[hist-eur2-s4.9.0] The International Socialist Bureau met in Brussels on 29-30 July 1914, and Jaurès spoke there. He was shot dead in a Paris café by a nationalist, Raoul Villain, on 31 July 1914, which removed the leading French advocate of a general strike.
[hist-eur2-s4.9.1] On 4 August 1914 the SPD Reichstag group voted for war credits (money for the war), in the party truce known as the Burgfrieden. Karl Liebknecht was the only Reichstag deputy to vote against war credits, in December 1914.
[hist-eur2-s4.9.2] In France the union sacrée (sacred union) brought socialists such as Jules Guesde and Marcel Sembat into the government in August 1914. In Britain Labour largely backed the war, and Ramsay MacDonald resigned as leader of the Labour MPs.
[hist-eur2-s4.9.3] It collapsed because nationalism proved stronger than class loyalty. German socialists feared repression and Tsarist Russia, French socialists feared German invasion, and ordinary workers were patriotic themselves. There had also been no agreed plan to stop a war.
[hist-eur2-s4.9.4] Socialists split into those who backed their governments, pacifists, and a revolutionary minority including Luxemburg, Liebknecht and Lenin. This minority opposed it as an imperialist war.
[hist-eur2-s4.9.5] Anti-war socialists met at Zimmerwald in Switzerland (September 1915) and at Kienthal (April 1916). The split helped produce the communist parties and the Third (Communist) International, the Comintern, founded in Moscow in March 1919.
[hist-eur2-s4.10.b] Luxemburg was a Marxist theorist, speaker and journalist on the left of the SPD, a participant in the International's debates and a leading opponent of the war. She was a witness to the SPD's vote of 1914 and to the Russian Revolution.
[hist-eur2-s4.10.0] She was born on 5 March 1871 in Zamość, Russian-ruled Poland, into a Jewish family. A childhood hip condition left a limp that did not stop her speaking in public. She studied at the University of Zurich (doctorate 1897).
[hist-eur2-s4.10.1] In 1893 she co-founded the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland (later the SDKPiL), opposing Polish independence as a goal because she put class before nation. In 1898 she moved to Germany, gained citizenship by a marriage of convenience, and joined the SPD on its left.
[hist-eur2-s4.10.2] In Reform or Revolution (1899) she attacked Bernstein's revisionism. She went to Warsaw in December 1905 to join the Russian revolution and was arrested in March 1906. Her pamphlet The Mass Strike, the Party and the Trade Unions (1906) argued for the mass strike as a revolutionary weapon.
[hist-eur2-s4.10.3] She taught at the SPD party school in Berlin (1907-14) and wrote The Accumulation of Capital (1913), an economic theory of imperialism. At the Stuttgart Congress in 1907 she helped amend the anti-war resolution with Lenin and Martov.
[hist-eur2-s4.10.4] For anti-war speeches in 1913 urging workers not to take up arms against other nations she was tried and sentenced to a year in prison in February 1914; she began the sentence in February 1915. The SPD's vote for war credits on 4 August 1914 horrified her.
[hist-eur2-s4.10.5] With Karl Liebknecht, Franz Mehring and Clara Zetkin she formed the International group (1915), which became the Spartacus group (1916). In prison from February 1915 to February 1916, and again from July 1916 in 'protective custody' until November 1918, she wrote the Junius pamphlet (1915, published 1916), condemning the war.
[hist-eur2-s4.10.6] She welcomed the Russian Revolution of 1917 but criticised Bolshevik dictatorship and the closing the Constituent Assembly: 'Freedom is always the freedom of the one who thinks differently.' She co-founded the KPD (30 December 1918-1 January 1919), thought the January 1919 rising premature but backed it once begun.
[hist-eur2-s4.10.7] Freikorps soldiers murdered her and Liebknecht in Berlin on 15 January 1919 and threw her body in the Landwehr Canal. To the SPD leaders and conservatives she was a dangerous agitator, and enemies called her 'Red Rosa'; supporters saw a courageous internationalist, Lenin's praise was mixed. Historians admire a major Marxist thinker who opposed both militarism and dictatorship, but debate her practicality.
[hist-eur2-s4.11.b] The war had no single cause. Long-term pressures made Europe dangerous, and a chain of Balkan events from 1908 to 1914 set them off.
[hist-eur2-s4.11.0] Alliances: Europe was divided into the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente, so a local quarrel could bring in all the Great Powers.
[hist-eur2-s4.11.1] Militarism and war plans: the powers built large conscript armies and had mobilisation timetables that left little time for diplomacy. The Schlieffen Plan required a swift attack on France.
[hist-eur2-s4.11.2] Naval and colonial rivalry: the Anglo-German naval race and the Moroccan crises poisoned relations between the powers.
[hist-eur2-s4.11.3] Nationalism: South Slav nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary, French revanche (revenge) aimed to recover Alsace-Lorraine, which Germany annexed in 1871, and pan-Slavism and pan-Germanism pushed Russia and Germany towards confrontation.
[hist-eur2-s4.11.4] Short-term causes were the Bosnian crisis (1908-09), the Balkan Wars, the assassination at Sarajevo, the blank cheque, the ultimatum, the mobilisations and Belgium. Each made the next crisis more dangerous.
[hist-eur2-s4.11.5] The Balkans were where Austrian and Russian interests met. Neither could back down without losing Great Power status, which is why a regional quarrel could not be left alone.
[hist-eur2-s4.12.b] Each government acted out of a fear as well as an aim. Pairing what each did with what it feared shows why none backed down.
[hist-eur2-s4.12.0] Austria-Hungary issued the ultimatum and declared war on Serbia because it feared that Slav nationalism would break up the empire and cost it Great Power status.
[hist-eur2-s4.12.1] Germany gave the blank cheque and hoped for a short, localised war. It feared encirclement, and the Schlieffen Plan meant it could not wait once Russia mobilised.
[hist-eur2-s4.12.2] Russia backed Serbia and mobilised, because it feared losing prestige and status after the humiliation of 1909.
[hist-eur2-s4.12.3] France reaffirmed support for Russia in the visit of 20-23 July and mobilised, because it feared a German attack and wanted to keep its ally.
[hist-eur2-s4.12.4] Britain declared war on 4 August after the invasion of Belgium, because it was concerned for the balance of power and had the Entente with France.
[hist-eur2-s4.12.5] Serbia accepted most of the ultimatum but refused Austrian officials on its soil, because it feared losing its sovereignty. It had begun mobilising on 25 July.
[hist-eur2-s4.13.b] Who started the First World War has been argued over since 1914. Examiners reward paired views and a reasoned conclusion, not a one-sided verdict.
[hist-eur2-s4.13.0] Contemporary German view: Germany was encircled and forced into a defensive war by Russian mobilisation. Entente view: Germany and Austria-Hungary were aggressors (blank cheque, ultimatum, Belgium). This fed the war-guilt clause, Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles (1919).
[hist-eur2-s4.13.1] Interwar view: David Lloyd George, in his War Memoirs, said the nations 'slithered over the brink' into war. This suggests shared responsibility rather than one guilty power.
[hist-eur2-s4.13.2] Fritz Fischer, a German historian, argued in Germany's Aims in the First World War (German edition 1961) that German leaders deliberately risked or sought war for world-power aims. His view raised an enduring debate about German war aims.
[hist-eur2-s4.13.3] Christopher Clark, in The Sleepwalkers: How Europe Went to War in 1914 (2012), argues that all the powers, Serbia included, share responsibility. He stresses contingency and Balkan politics.
[hist-eur2-s4.13.4] Austria-Hungary's decision to crush Serbia, Serbian nationalism, Russian backing of Serbia, German backing of Austria and military planning were all links in the chain. Historians differ on the weight of each, so weigh them.
[hist-eur2-s4.13.5] Could organised workers have stopped the war? On paper the International was strong, as Stuttgart (1907) and Basel (1912) showed. It failed because nationalism was stronger, there was no agreed plan and workers feared repression.
[hist-eur2-s4.13.6] Luxemburg's achievements were her theory, her consistent opposition to war and the founding of the KPD. Her limits were a small following, the failed rising and her murder before she could shape events.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s1: 1. The Second Reich, Bismarck's foreign policy and the changing balance of power, 1871–1890
[hist-eur2-s1.0.b] To judge Bismarck's foreign policy you first need the starting point: a Europe run by five Great Powers, which the new German Empire suddenly disturbed. The key idea is the balance of power.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.0] Balance of power means that no single state, or bloc of allied states, is strong enough to dominate Europe, because the others would combine against it. A united Germany upset the old balance, and Bismarck tried to manage the new one in Germany's favour.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.1] Germany was unified by Prussia under Bismarck through three wars: against Denmark (1864), against Austria (1866, the Austro-Prussian or Seven Weeks' War) and against France (1870-71, the Franco-Prussian War, with French defeat at Sedan in September 1870 and the siege of Paris).
[hist-eur2-s1.0.2] Before 1871 the centre of Europe was weak and divided into many German states. This is why the five Great Powers, Britain, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary and Prussia/Germany, could balance one another.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.3] The Eastern Question was the problem of what would happen as the Ottoman Empire (Turkey) declined in the Balkans. Russia and Austria-Hungary were rivals for influence there.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.4] Britain wanted to keep Russia away from the Straits (the sea passage from the Black Sea to the Mediterranean) and the eastern Mediterranean, because that route guarded its own interests.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.b] The German Empire (the Second Reich) was proclaimed in 1871 and combined a strong monarchy with an elected parliament. Its constitution put real power in the hands of the Kaiser and the Chancellor, not the Reichstag.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.0] The Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, while Paris was still under siege in the Franco-Prussian War. King Wilhelm I of Prussia became German Emperor (Kaiser).
[hist-eur2-s1.1.1] It was a federation of 25 states: four kingdoms (Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg), grand duchies, duchies and principalities, and three free cities (Hamburg, Bremen, Lübeck). The population was about 41 million in 1871. Because the states stayed in the federation, local rulers kept their thrones.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.2] Prussia dominated the federation, with about two-thirds of the territory and about three-fifths of the population. Alsace-Lorraine was a Reichsland (imperial territory), ruled directly from Berlin and not a state.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.3] The Kaiser was hereditary King of Prussia. He commanded the armed forces, appointed and dismissed the Chancellor and ministers and conducted foreign policy, and he could declare war with the consent of the Bundesrat (needed unless Germany was attacked).
[hist-eur2-s1.1.4] The Chancellor was responsible to the Kaiser, not to the Reichstag, and ministers (state secretaries) answered to the Chancellor. Bismarck was Chancellor from 1871 to 1890 and, separately, Prussian Minister-President for almost all of that time, apart from a short break in 1873.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.5] The Bundesrat was the federal council of delegates from the state governments, who were not elected. Prussia held 17 of its 58 votes in 1871, and 14 votes could block a constitutional amendment, so Prussia alone could do so.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.6] The Reichstag was elected by universal male suffrage (men aged 25 and over) by secret ballot. It voted on laws and the budget, but it could not appoint or dismiss the Chancellor, which is why it was weak. This is why real power lay with the Kaiser and the Chancellor.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.b] The peace that ended the Franco-Prussian War created a lasting French grievance. Together with the birth of a powerful Germany, it changed how the Great Powers related to one another.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.0] The Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871) made France pay an indemnity (a payment of compensation) of 5 billion francs and cede Alsace and part of Lorraine, including Strasbourg and Metz.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.1] German troops occupied part of France until the indemnity was paid, which France achieved by 1873.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.2] A new Great Power in the centre of Europe, with a growing population and industry, changed the balance of power. Germany now outweighed its neighbours in a way the old divided German lands never had.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.3] France was weakened and embittered by defeat and the loss of the provinces, so it became hostile to Germany. Britain, Russia and Austria-Hungary had to adjust to a united Germany.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.b] Bismarck governed by managing the Reichstag, working with one party after another and naming the enemies of the Reich in turn. His domestic record matters because examiners ask you to compare it with his foreign policy.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.0] His method was to work with Reichstag parties in turn and to label groups as Reichsfeinde (enemies of the Reich): Catholics (the Centre Party), socialists, and national minorities such as the Poles, the Danes and the people of Alsace-Lorraine. The National Liberals were his main partners from 1871 to 1878.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.1] The Kulturkampf was his struggle with the Catholic Church, begun in 1871 and wound down after Leo XIII became pope in 1878 and largely ended by 1887. It failed to break the Church, and it matters here because the assessment names it.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.2] After attempts on the life of Wilhelm I in May 1878 (Hödel) and June 1878 (Nobiling, who badly wounded the Kaiser), Bismarck used the fear of revolution as a pretext, although neither attacker acted for the SPD. He dissolved the Reichstag, and a new election followed in July 1878.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.3] The Anti-Socialist Law of October 1878 banned socialist associations, meetings and publications, but socialists could still stand for and sit in the Reichstag. It was renewed several times and lapsed on 30 September 1890. This means the law attacked socialist organisation, but not the party's place in parliament.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.4] The law failed to stop the SPD (the Social Democratic Party). At the February 1890 election it won about 1.4 million votes, the largest share of any party, and 35 seats.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.5] The tariff law of 1879 protected imported iron, steel and grain, an alliance of Junker landowners and heavy industry called 'rye and iron'. It split the National Liberals (their left wing seceded in 1880), so Bismarck turned to the Conservatives and, for the tariff, the Centre Party.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.b] Bismarck combined repression of socialists with benefits for workers, and he fought the Reichstag over the army. At the same time he built the shared institutions of a single nation state.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.0] To win workers away from socialism he introduced state insurance: sickness insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old age and invalidity insurance (1889). Germany was the first state to create a national compulsory scheme of this kind.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.1] This mix of repression and welfare is called 'carrot and stick' (in German, Zuckerbrot und Peitsche, 'sugar-bread and whip'). The schemes had limits: pensions were small and paid only from age 70, and the insurance failed to reduce support for the SPD.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.2] The Septennate was the system of voting the army budget for seven years at a time (first in 1874), which kept the army outside yearly Reichstag control.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.3] In January 1887 the Reichstag refused the new army bill, so Bismarck dissolved it. In the February 1887 election he fought on a war scare with France, and the Kartell (Conservatives, Free Conservatives and National Liberals) won a majority and passed the bill.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.4] He also created national institutions: the mark became the single currency in 1873, and the Reichsbank was founded in 1875 (it opened on 1 January 1876). These count as domestic achievements because they made a national market possible.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.b] After 1871 Bismarck declared Germany a satiated power, one with no wish for more European territory. His aim was to protect the new Reich from a hostile coalition, above all one that included France.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.0] Bismarck described Germany after 1871 as a 'satiated' (saturated) power: it had what it wanted, so more expansion in Europe would only provoke the other powers. This is why he wanted peace rather than conquest.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.1] French revanche, the desire for revenge and recovery of Alsace-Lorraine, followed the loss of the provinces. Bismarck assumed that France was irreconcilable and would never become a reliable friend.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.2] His core aim was to keep France isolated and prevent a hostile coalition, above all a two-front war (war against France in the west and Russia in the east at once). He called this fear his 'nightmare of coalitions', his own phrase.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.3] In the Kissingen Dictation (June 1877) he set out, as reported, that he wanted all the powers except France to need Germany and to be kept from coalitions against it by their own relations with one another.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.4] He also wanted to keep Russia and Austria-Hungary from war with each other over the Balkans, to keep Britain friendly, and to divert rivalries to the edges of Europe, such as the Balkans and Africa, away from Germany's borders.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.5] A later summary of this logic is that, in a Europe of five Great Powers, the aim was to be one of three, so that Germany was never the odd one out. He is reported to have said this to the Russian diplomat Saburov in 1880.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.b] French hostility never disappeared, and several crises showed how fragile peace between Germany and France was. Bismarck also tried to ease French bitterness by encouraging French empire-building.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.0] In the War-in-Sight crisis of spring 1875, France passed an army law (March 1875) expanding its army. An article headed 'Is War in Sight?' in the Berlin newspaper Die Post (April 1875) suggested Germany might strike first.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.1] Britain and Russia warned Berlin, and Tsar Alexander II and his chancellor Gorchakov visited Berlin in May 1875. Historians disagree on whether Bismarck was bluffing or testing the other powers.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.2] Either way, the lesson for Bismarck was that Britain and Russia would not allow France to be crushed a second time, which fed his fear of a hostile coalition.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.3] In 1886-87 General Georges Boulanger, French war minister and a popular figure of revanche, stirred up feeling against Germany.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.4] In the Schnaebelé affair (April 1887) the Germans arrested a French frontier police official on the border, and he was released about a week later. It showed how a small incident could threaten war. Such incidents mattered because French feeling was already hostile.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.5] Bismarck encouraged France to take Tunisia, which it made a protectorate in 1881, hoping to turn French attention from Alsace-Lorraine. In 1884-85 he cooperated with the French premier Jules Ferry against Britain on African questions.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.b] Bismarck's first attempt to keep Russia and Austria-Hungary friendly with Germany was a conservative understanding among three monarchies. It came under strain when the Balkans erupted.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.0] The Three Emperors' League (Dreikaiserbund), completed in October 1873, linked Wilhelm I, Franz Joseph of Austria-Hungary and Alexander II of Russia.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.1] It was a conservative understanding to consult each other and resist revolution, not a military alliance. This is why it could not bind the three emperors when their interests clashed.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.2] The Balkan crisis of 1875-78 began with risings against Ottoman rule in Bosnia-Herzegovina (1875) and Bulgaria (1876).
[hist-eur2-s1.7.3] Serbia and Montenegro fought Turkey in 1876, and Russia fought the Russo-Turkish War from April 1877 to early 1878.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.4] Bismarck did not want Germany dragged in. He told the Reichstag in December 1876 that the Balkans were not worth 'the healthy bones of a single Pomeranian musketeer'.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.5] The Treaty of San Stefano (3 March 1878, between Russia and Turkey) created a large autonomous Bulgaria under Russian influence. Austria-Hungary and Britain refused to accept it, and Britain sent its fleet to the Straits.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.b] To prevent a European war over San Stefano, the powers met in Berlin under Bismarck's chairmanship. The settlement avoided war, but it left Russia angry with Germany.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.0] In a Reichstag speech of February 1878 Bismarck promised that Germany would act as an 'honest broker' (ehrlicher Makler), meaning an impartial mediator. The Congress of Berlin met from 13 June to 13 July 1878 with Bismarck in the chair.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.1] Britain was represented by Disraeli (Lord Beaconsfield) and Salisbury, Austria-Hungary by Andrássy and Russia by Gorchakov and Shuvalov.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.2] The large Bulgaria was split into three: a smaller autonomous Principality of Bulgaria, Eastern Rumelia (autonomous under the Sultan) and Macedonia, which returned to Ottoman rule.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.3] Serbia, Montenegro and Romania became fully independent. Austria-Hungary was allowed to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina (and garrison the Sanjak of Novi Pazar).
[hist-eur2-s1.8.4] Russia kept southern Bessarabia and gained Kars and Batum. Britain gained Cyprus at the time of the Congress, by a separate convention with Turkey in June 1878. France was told it could take Tunisia.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.5] War was avoided, but Russia felt cheated of the fruits of its victory over Turkey and blamed Bismarck, and the Russian press attacked Germany.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.6] The first League lapsed, and Alexander II sent an angry letter to Wilhelm I in August 1879, the 'box on the ears' letter. This is why the Congress is judged a success for peace but a failure for Russo-German relations.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.b] After 1878, with Russia resentful, Bismarck secured Germany by tying it to Austria-Hungary and then to Italy. These treaties became the core of his alliance system.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.0] The Dual Alliance of 7 October 1879 was a secret defensive treaty between Germany and Austria-Hungary. If either was attacked by Russia, the other would help with all its forces; if attacked by another power, the other would stay at least neutral, unless Russia joined the attacker.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.1] Bismarck chose Austria-Hungary to guard against a Russian attack after Russia's anger in 1878, and to bind the Habsburg state to Germany. Andrássy negotiated for Austria-Hungary. This is why the Dual Alliance became the base of his system.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.2] Wilhelm I disliked a treaty aimed at his nephew, Tsar Alexander II, and agreed only after Bismarck threatened to resign.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.3] The Dual Alliance was renewed regularly and lasted to the end of the war in 1918, making it the permanent core of German policy. Its danger was that it committed Germany to Austria-Hungary in the Balkans.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.4] The Triple Alliance (20 May 1882) was a secret treaty, first for five years and renewed until 1914. Italy joined because it was angry at France's seizure of Tunisia in 1881 and wanted Great Power standing.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.5] Its terms were that Germany and Austria-Hungary would help Italy if attacked by France, Italy would help Germany if attacked by France, and all three would fight if any member was attacked by two or more Great Powers.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.6] It had weaknesses. Italy stated (the Mancini declaration, 1882) that the alliance could not be directed against Britain, and relations with Austria-Hungary were poor because Italian-speaking Trentino and Trieste remained under Habsburg rule.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.7] Italy stayed neutral in August 1914 and entered the war on the Allied side in May 1915.
[hist-eur2-s1.10.b] Bismarck repaired relations with Russia in 1881, but Balkan rivalry between Russia and Austria-Hungary wrecked the arrangement. His answer was a secret treaty with Russia alone.
[hist-eur2-s1.10.0] The second Three Emperors' League was signed on 18 June 1881, secretly and for three years, and renewed in 1884. Each member would stay neutral if another was at war with a fourth Great Power.
[hist-eur2-s1.10.1] Balkan changes were to be made only by agreement, which was meant to stop Russia and Austria-Hungary clashing. Austria-Hungary could annex Bosnia-Herzegovina when it chose.
[hist-eur2-s1.10.2] In the Bulgarian crisis, Bulgaria united with Eastern Rumelia in 1885, and Serbia attacked Bulgaria and was defeated. Prince Alexander of Battenberg (not a Tsar) was forced to abdicate in 1886 under Russian pressure.
[hist-eur2-s1.10.3] Russia and Austria-Hungary came close to war, and Russia refused to renew the League in 1887, so it collapsed. This kept Russia friendly to Germany without breaking the Dual Alliance.
[hist-eur2-s1.10.4] Bismarck replaced it with the Reinsurance Treaty of 18 June 1887, a secret treaty between Germany and Russia for three years. Each would stay benevolently neutral if the other was at war with a third Great Power, except if Germany attacked France or Russia attacked Austria-Hungary.
[hist-eur2-s1.10.5] A secret protocol recognised Russia's predominant influence in Bulgaria and promised German moral and diplomatic support if Russia had to defend the Straits.
[hist-eur2-s1.10.6] Austria-Hungary and Italy were not told. It can be argued that the treaty conflicted in spirit with the Dual Alliance, since Germany was promising friendship to Austria-Hungary's rival.
[hist-eur2-s1.11.b] In late 1887 Bismarck made two moves that pulled in different directions. One pushed Russia towards France, while the other built a loose Mediterranean barrier against Russia with British help.
[hist-eur2-s1.11.0] In November 1887 Bismarck barred the Reichsbank from accepting Russian bonds as security for loans (the Lombardverbot). Russia raised its loans in Paris instead from 1888, which helped to bring Russia and France together. This is why Russia, for example, turned elsewhere for money.
[hist-eur2-s1.11.1] The Mediterranean Agreements of 1887 were made by Britain, Italy and Austria-Hungary, and Germany was not a signatory. The first (February-March 1887) linked Britain and Italy, then Austria-Hungary, with Spain associated.
[hist-eur2-s1.11.2] The second (December 1887, Britain, Austria-Hungary and Italy) kept the status quo in the Mediterranean, the Black Sea and the Near East, in effect against Russia and France. Bismarck encouraged them, which tied Britain loosely to the Triple Alliance powers.
[hist-eur2-s1.11.3] The Reinsurance Treaty (Germany and Russia only) must not be confused with the Mediterranean Agreements. Bismarck was in effect supporting both Russia and its opponents, which only he could keep up.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.b] Bismarck long thought colonies not worth the trouble, then changed course in 1884-85. Historians disagree about why, and the colonies strained relations with Britain.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.0] Bismarck long regarded colonies as costly and as a source of quarrels with Britain, and Germany lacked a fleet to protect them.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.1] In 1884 Germany took South West Africa (Lüderitz's land at Angra Pequena, April 1884), Togoland and the Cameroons (July 1884), and north-east New Guinea (Kaiser-Wilhelmsland) and the Bismarck Archipelago. In 1885 it added German East Africa (Carl Peters' German East Africa Company) and the Marshall Islands.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.2] One explanation is pressure from merchants (such as Lüderitz of Bremen, and Hamburg trading firms) and the German Colonial Society (Kolonialverein, 1882). Colonies were also popular in the Reichstag election of October 1884, and they gave Bismarck leverage against Britain and a gesture towards France.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.3] A.J.P. Taylor argued that the colonies were taken to quarrel with Britain and so prevent an Anglo-German liberal friendship under the future Frederick III. Hans-Ulrich Wehler argued they were 'social imperialism', a way to divert domestic discontent. Treat both as interpretations.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.4] Bismarck preferred protectorates (Schutzgebiete) run at first by chartered companies at their own expense, rather than state-run colonies, but the state soon had to take over administration.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.5] Gladstone was British prime minister from 1880 to 1885, and London protested about Angra Pequena and East Africa. The Berlin Conference on West Africa (15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885), called by Bismarck with France, set the rules for claiming African territory.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.6] Relations improved with Salisbury, prime minister from June 1885 (briefly) and again from 1886, and the Mediterranean Agreements helped. In December 1888 Bismarck told the explorer Eugen Wolf, 'my map of Africa lies in Europe', with France on one side, Russia on the other and Germany in the middle.
[hist-eur2-s1.12.7] Crown Prince Frederick had married Victoria, Queen Victoria's eldest daughter, in 1858. Both were liberal, and Bismarck distrusted their influence.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.b] Bismarck fell because a young Kaiser who wanted to rule himself clashed with a Chancellor used to controlling everything. The quarrel centred on workers, the constitution and access to the Kaiser.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.0] 1888 is called the year of the three emperors. Wilhelm I died on 9 March 1888 aged 90, and his son Frederick III, already gravely ill with cancer of the throat, reigned 99 days and died on 15 June 1888.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.1] Wilhelm II, born in 1859, became Kaiser aged 29. He wanted to rule personally, so he was ready to clash with Bismarck.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.2] They quarrelled over the workers. During a miners' strike in the Ruhr in May 1889 Wilhelm II favoured concessions, and he wanted to ease the Anti-Socialist Law, which Bismarck wanted to make permanent.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.3] In January 1890 the Reichstag rejected Bismarck's bill to make the law permanent. In the February 1890 election the Kartell lost its majority and the SPD vote rose, and Bismarck spoke of a Staatsstreich (coup) to change the constitution or suffrage.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.4] He invoked the Prussian Cabinet Order of 1852 (ministers could approach the King only through the Minister-President), which would limit the ministers' access to the Kaiser. He also met the Centre leader Ludwig Windthorst in March 1890 without the Kaiser's consent.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.5] Wilhelm II demanded his resignation. Bismarck sent his resignation letter on 18 March 1890, it was accepted on 20 March, and General Leo von Caprivi became Chancellor.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.6] The Punch cartoon 'Dropping the Pilot' by John Tenniel (29 March 1890) shows Wilhelm II on deck watching Bismarck, the pilot, leave the ship.
[hist-eur2-s1.13.7] The immediate results were that the Reinsurance Treaty was not renewed in 1890 and the Heligoland-Zanzibar Treaty was signed with Britain on 1 July 1890. Both were Caprivi's, not Bismarck's, and Russia moved towards France; the Franco-Russian military convention followed in 1892.
[hist-eur2-s1.14.b] Bismarck made German unification possible in the 1860s and then governed the new Empire as Chancellor until 1890. As a participant who shaped events, and was in turn shaped by them, he is judged very differently by contemporaries and historians.
[hist-eur2-s1.14.0] He was born on 1 April 1815 and lived until 1898 (1815-98) at Schönhausen (Brandenburg) into a Junker family (Prussian landed nobility), and became Prussian Minister-President in September 1862. He made unification possible, and his phrase 'blood and iron' belongs to those earlier years, not to the Chancellorship.
[hist-eur2-s1.14.1] He was made a prince (Fürst) in 1871 and was Chancellor under Wilhelm I (1871-88), Frederick III (1888) and Wilhelm II (1888-90). As Chancellor he shaped the constitution, the domestic campaigns, the alliances of 1873 to 1887, the Congress of Berlin and the colonies of 1884-85.
[hist-eur2-s1.14.2] He controlled foreign policy personally, and most of the major policy decisions of the Empire were his.
[hist-eur2-s1.14.3] He was shaped by events as well. The War-in-Sight crisis taught him that Britain and Russia would not let France be crushed, Russian resentment after 1878 and the Bulgarian crisis forced his alliance changes, and in the end a new Kaiser ended his career.
[hist-eur2-s1.14.4] He was a conservative monarchist defending Prussian power and the Junker order, using nationalism and the Reichstag as tools. In diplomacy he practised realpolitik, politics based on power and interests rather than principles.
[hist-eur2-s1.14.5] Contemporaries saw the 'Iron Chancellor'. Nationalists and conservatives admired him, Catholics, socialists and many liberals hated him, and foreign observers respected him as a master diplomat.
[hist-eur2-s1.15.b] After 1890 Bismarck became a critic of his successors, and his reputation was fought over by historians. Their verdicts divide into favourable and critical views.
[hist-eur2-s1.15.0] He retired to Friedrichsruh, near Hamburg, and attacked the 'new course' of Wilhelm II's governments in the press, especially the Hamburger Nachrichten. He died there on 30 July 1898.
[hist-eur2-s1.15.1] His memoirs, Thoughts and Recollections, appeared just after his death (two volumes in 1898, the third in 1921). Hundreds of Bismarck towers and monuments were built in Germany, which shows the cult that grew around him.
[hist-eur2-s1.15.2] Favourable historians stress his diplomacy. A.J.P. Taylor (Bismarck: The Man and the Statesman, 1955) held that it was peaceful after 1871, though he also stressed his opportunism.
[hist-eur2-s1.15.3] Lothar Gall (Bismarck: The White Revolutionary) called him a 'white revolutionary': a conservative who used revolutionary means, accepting nationalism and universal suffrage, to preserve the monarchy and the old elites.
[hist-eur2-s1.15.4] Critics stress the flaws. Hans-Ulrich Wehler argued that his rule was a 'Bonapartist' dictatorship that kept the old elites in power through 'negative integration', uniting the majority against Reichsfeinde, and through social imperialism.
[hist-eur2-s1.15.5] On this view he left a weak parliamentary tradition, and his system of secret, contradictory treaties needed his own skill to work, so it could not outlast him.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.b] The usual judgement is that Bismarck was more successful abroad than at home, but both records have successes and limits. Contemporaries and later historians also judged him differently.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.0] Foreign success: Germany fought no war between 1871 and 1890 and avoided a two-front war, while France stayed isolated. The Russo-Turkish War of 1877-78 did not become a general European war.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.1] Foreign success: the Congress of Berlin prevented a general war in 1878 and put Germany at the centre of European diplomacy, and colonies were gained in 1884-85 without war.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.2] Foreign limit: the system was complex, secret and partly contradictory, because the Reinsurance Treaty and the Dual Alliance pulled in opposite directions. It depended on Bismarck personally, so a successor could not keep all the balls in the air.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.3] Foreign limit: the Dual Alliance tied Germany to Austria-Hungary in the Balkans, where Habsburg and Russian interests clashed. The treatment of Russia in 1878 and the Lombardverbot of 1887 prepared the Franco-Russian link, and French hostility never ended.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.4] Domestic success: he created national unity, a common currency (the mark, 1873), a central bank (the Reichsbank) and the first national social insurance scheme.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.5] Domestic limit: the Kulturkampf and the Anti-Socialist Law failed, and both the Catholic Centre and the SPD grew stronger. The Reichstag stayed weak, and he left his successors an unreformed constitution.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.6] Contemporary view: in 1890 Germans saw a great statesman who had made Germany one of the strongest states in Europe, and foreign observers respected him as a master diplomat; the British magazine Punch's 'Dropping the Pilot' cartoon (1890) shows how large he loomed.
[hist-eur2-s1.16.7] Later view: historians stress the flaws he left behind, a divided society and a fragile alliance system that depended on him.
