[hist-eur3-s1.0.b] Fascism grew where parliamentary government seemed weak and where war, economic crisis and fear of revolution had left people frightened and angry.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.0] Italy's war effort cost heavy casualties and debt, and the peace settlement gave Italy less than the Treaty of London (1915) had promised, feeding the sense of a 'mutilated victory'.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.1] Italy had five prime ministers between 1919 and 1922 (Orlando, Nitti, Giolitti, Bonomi and Facta), and proportional representation, introduced in 1919, produced weak coalitions dependent on the Socialists and the Catholic Popolari.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.2] The Biennio Rosso (1919-1920) brought strikes, peasant land seizures and factory occupations in the north, leading landowners, industrialists and the middle classes to fear a socialist revolution.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.3] Inflation, unemployment and food shortages added to the anger of ex-servicemen, who believed the state had failed to reward them.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.4] Fascism drew support from ex-servicemen, the lower middle class, large landowners in the Po valley and industrialists who wanted an end to strikes and socialist organisation.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.5] The poet Gabriele D'Annunzio's seizure of Fiume with volunteers (September 1919 to December 1920) showed the weakness of the state and gave Fascism its style: black shirts, balcony speeches, the Roman salute and mass ritual.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.6] Characteristics of fascism: extreme nationalism that places the nation above the individual, with power concentrated in one leader who embodies the nation (the leader principle) and is promoted by a personality cult.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.7] A single party and a totalitarian state: Mussolini's own formula, from a 1925 speech, was 'everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state'.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.8] Fascists were hostile to communism, socialism and liberal democracy, which they blamed for class conflict and national weakness.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.9] Fascism glorified violence, war and youth through uniformed squads and mass rallies, and promised corporatism, in which employers and workers would co-operate in state-controlled corporations instead of class conflict.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.10] Comparison only: other interwar authoritarian regimes included Horthy's Hungary (1920), Primo de Rivera's Spain (1923-30), Piłsudski's Poland (1926), Salazar's Portugal (1932) and Metaxas's Greece (1936); most were conservative military or clerical dictatorships rather than mass fascist movements.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.b] The Fascist movement gained power through organised violence against its opponents, often tolerated by local police and army commanders.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.0] Benito Mussolini, a former editor of the socialist newspaper Avanti!, founded the Fasci di Combattimento in Milan in March 1919 with a programme that mixed radical and nationalist ideas.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.1] The squadristi were armed squads, often led by ex-servicemen, who attacked socialist offices, trade union halls and Catholic People's Party premises, especially in the Po valley and Tuscany.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.2] Fascist candidates won 35 seats in the May 1921 election, when Mussolini himself was elected, and Mussolini founded the National Fascist Party (PNF) that November.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.3] Mussolini signed a Pacification Pact with socialist leaders in August 1921 to end the violence, but local squads ignored it and the pact collapsed.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.4] Liberal leader Giovanni Giolitti included Fascists in his 1921 electoral bloc, underestimating their strength and believing he could control them.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.5] The squads' violence was directed against the organisations that had led the 1919-1920 unrest, so Fascism presented itself as the defender of order against socialism.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.6] By 1922 the Fascists had taken control of many local councils in northern and central Italy, giving them local bases of power independent of Rome.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.b] The March on Rome was a show of force that persuaded the King to hand power to Mussolini rather than declare a state of siege.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.0] Between 27 and 30 October 1922, about 30,000 Fascist squads converged on Rome under four leaders, the quadrumvirs (Balbo, De Bono, De Vecchi and Bianchi), while Mussolini waited in Milan; Prime Minister Luigi Facta asked for a state of siege.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.1] King Victor Emmanuel III refused to sign the state of siege decree, fearing civil war and doubting the army's loyalty; he invited Mussolini to form a government on 29 October and Mussolini took office on 30 October 1922.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.2] Mussolini's first government was a coalition in which Fascists held only a minority of posts alongside Liberals, Popolari and nationalists, and parliament granted it emergency powers for a year in November 1922.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.3] The Acerbo Law of 1923 gave two-thirds of the seats in the Chamber of Deputies to any list winning at least 25 per cent of the vote.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.4] In the April 1924 election the Fascist-led national list won about 65 per cent of the vote, in a campaign marked by intimidation and violence against opponents.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.5] The Fascist Grand Council (December 1922) became the party's governing body and the Fascist militia (MVSN, January 1923) turned the squads into a state-paid force loyal to Mussolini rather than the King.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.b] The murder of a socialist deputy in 1924 threatened Mussolini's government and was followed by the removal of parliamentary and press freedom.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.0] On 30 May 1924 Giacomo Matteotti, a Socialist deputy, denounced electoral fraud and violence in the Chamber of Deputies.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.1] Matteotti was abducted in Rome on 10 June 1924 by men connected to the Fascist squads and was murdered; his body was found in August.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.2] Opposition deputies walked out of the Chamber in what became known as the Aventine Secession, hoping the King would dismiss Mussolini; the King did not act.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.3] On 3 January 1925 Mussolini told the Chamber that he accepted political responsibility for the violence, which marked the start of his open dictatorship.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.4] Between 1925 and 1926 the government banned opposition parties, censored the press, and replaced elected mayors with appointed podesta.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.5] The Leggi Fascistissime of 1925-26 made Mussolini responsible only to the King, let him rule by decree, set up a Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State (1926) and created the OVRA secret police; political prisoners were sent to internal exile (confino) on islands such as Lipari.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.6] Violet Gibson, an Englishwoman, shot Mussolini in Rome on 7 April 1926 and wounded his nose; the attack was used to justify further repression.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.7] A 1928 electoral law let the Grand Council draw up a single list of 400 candidates for voters to accept or reject, and the 1929 plebiscite approved it with about 98 per cent voting yes.

[hist-eur3-s1.8.b] Mussolini led Italy from 1922 to 1943 and was the founder and leader of the Fascist movement.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.0] Born in 1883 at Predappio in the Romagna region, he trained as a teacher, became a socialist journalist, and edited Avanti! from 1912.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.1] He was expelled from the Socialist Party in 1914 for supporting Italian entry into the First World War, and he served in the army and was wounded in 1917.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.2] As founder of the Fasci di Combattimento (1919) and the National Fascist Party (1921), he led the movement that took power in 1922 and ruled as dictator from 1925.

[hist-eur3-s1.9.3] Limit: the Battle for Grain did not make Italy self-sufficient in food, and the deflationary policy of Quota 90 damaged industry and jobs.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.4] Achievement: Italy gained territory in Abyssinia and Fiume, which the regime presented as the fulfilment of national destiny.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.5] Limit: the Abyssinian war was costly, the alliance with Germany drew Italy into a war it was ill-prepared to fight, and the regime collapsed in 1943 after defeats in North Africa and Sicily.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.6] Contemporary view: many Italians and foreign conservatives saw Mussolini as the saviour of order who had defeated Bolshevism; later view: his regime is judged by its violence, its alliance with Hitler, the 1938 racial laws and the defeat it brought on Italy.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s3: 3. The Nazi state in peace, 1933-1939, and Hitler's foreign policy
[hist-eur3-s3.0.b] Within months of his appointment as Chancellor, Hitler removed legal and political limits on his power, using emergency decrees and intimidation.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.0] The Reichstag fire of 27 February 1933 was blamed on a Communist plot (the Dutch communist Marinus van der Lubbe was found in the building) and used to justify the Decree for the Protection of People and State of 28 February, which suspended civil liberties such as freedom of the press and assembly.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.1] Communists were arrested in large numbers after the fire, and the Communist Party was effectively destroyed in early 1933.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.2] The Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 allowed the cabinet to make laws without the Reichstag for four years; only the Social Democrats voted against it.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.3] Trade unions were abolished on 2 May 1933 and replaced by the German Labour Front, led by Robert Ley.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.4] Gleichschaltung, the coordination of institutions, brought state governments, the civil service, the courts, universities and cultural bodies under Nazi control.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.5] A law of 14 July 1933 made the NSDAP the only legal party in Germany.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.b] The purge of the SA leadership in 1934 removed the last rival power bloc, and Hitler combined the offices of Chancellor and President after Hindenburg's death.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.0] The SA under Ernst Röhm had grown to millions of members and wanted a second revolution and a larger role in the army.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.1] Between 30 June and 2 July 1934, Hitler ordered the murder of Röhm and other SA leaders, and of political figures such as Gregor Strasser and former Chancellor Kurt von Schleicher.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.2] The cabinet legalised the killings retrospectively on 3 July as acts of state self-defence, and the army leadership accepted the purge in return for the removal of the SA as a rival force.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.3] President Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934, and Hitler combined the presidency and the chancellorship in the title of Führer and Reich Chancellor.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.4] Soldiers swore a personal oath of loyalty to Hitler as Führer rather than to the constitution.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.5] Hitler's authority rested on the Führer principle, in which decisions flowed down from the leader, and on the cult of his personal leadership in propaganda.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.6] Heinrich Himmler, Reichsführer-SS from 1929, built up the SS and took control of the political police in most German states.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.7] Reinhard Heydrich led the Security Service (SD) and, with Himmler, controlled the Gestapo, which had wide powers of arrest without legal challenge.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.8] Dachau, opened near Munich in March 1933, was the first concentration camp, and others such as Sachsenhausen (1936) and Buchenwald (1937) followed.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.9] Political opponents, trade unionists, Social Democrats and members of religious groups were the first people held in the camps.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.10] Courts were restricted by the Special Courts and the people's court, which handed down political verdicts with little legal protection for defendants.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.11] The Gestapo and the SS were given powers to detain people without trial, which the regime described as protective custody.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.b] Nazi economic policy reduced unemployment through public works and rearmament, and prepared the economy for war.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.0] Hjalmar Schacht, appointed president of the Reichsbank in 1933 and Economics Minister in 1934, financed rearmament through Mefo bills, which concealed military spending.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.1] Public works, including autobahns (the first section opened in 1935), housing and military construction, reduced unemployment, which fell sharply between 1933 and 1936.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.2] The Reich Labour Service became compulsory for young men in June 1935, and conscription was reintroduced in March 1935.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.3] The New Plan of 1934 controlled foreign trade through bilateral agreements and exchange controls, which gave priority to imports needed for rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.4] The Four Year Plan of September 1936, under Hermann Göring, aimed to make Germany ready for war within four years and to achieve economic self-sufficiency (autarky).
[hist-eur3-s3.2.5] Schacht resigned as Economics Minister in 1937 after disagreements with Göring over the pace and cost of rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.b] Hitler's first foreign moves rejected the Versailles restrictions and gained international acceptance of rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.0] On 14 October 1933 Germany left the League of Nations and the Geneva Disarmament Conference, citing unequal treatment.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.1] The Saar territory, governed by the League since 1920, returned to Germany after a plebiscite in January 1935 in which over 90 per cent voted for reunion.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.2] On 16 March 1935 Hitler announced the reintroduction of conscription and an air force, in breach of the Treaty of Versailles.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.3] The Stresa Front of Britain, France and Italy condemned the move in April 1935, but took no effective action against Germany.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.4] The Anglo-German Naval Agreement of June 1935 allowed Germany a navy about 35 per cent the size of the British navy, and it was accepted by Britain without consulting France.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.5] The agreement gave Hitler the diplomatic recognition that he had sought, and it encouraged him to test the western powers further.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.b] Between 1936 and 1939 Hitler took territory by threat and by diplomacy, and each success made the western powers more reluctant to resist him.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.0] On 7 March 1936 German troops reoccupied the demilitarised Rhineland, and the Locarno Treaties were breached without military resistance from Britain or France.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.1] The Anschluss, the annexation of Austria, took place on 12 March 1938 after German pressure forced Austrian Chancellor Kurt Schuschnigg to cancel a plebiscite on independence and resign, and German troops entered Austria.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.2] Hitler's demands for the German-speaking Sudetenland in Czechoslovakia led to the Munich Agreement of 29 to 30 September 1938, which Britain and France signed with Germany and Italy.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.3] Czechoslovakia was not consulted at Munich, and Britain's Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain described the agreement as securing "peace for our time".
[hist-eur3-s3.4.4] German troops entered Prague on 15 March 1939, and Bohemia and Moravia became a German protectorate while Slovakia became a nominally independent client state.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.5] Britain gave a guarantee to Poland on 31 March 1939, followed by France, but Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939, with a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe between them.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.6] Konrad Henlein's Sudeten German Party, funded from Berlin, demanded autonomy for the three million Sudeten Germans, giving Hitler a pretext to threaten Czechoslovakia in 1938.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.7] Chamberlain flew to meet Hitler at Berchtesgaden on 15 September and again at Bad Godesberg on 22 September, where Hitler raised his demands; the Munich conference followed on 29 September.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.8] The Munich Agreement required Czechoslovakia to hand over the Sudetenland to Germany between 1 and 10 October 1938; the Soviet Union, which had a treaty with Czechoslovakia, was not invited.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.9] Hitler's occupation of Prague in March 1939 broke the Munich promises and ended the belief that his claims were limited to Germans, which led Britain to abandon appeasement and guarantee Poland.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.10] The Nazi-Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939 removed the threat of a war on two fronts; Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and Britain and France declared war on 3 September.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.11] Earlier steps included the German-Polish non-aggression pact of January 1934, a failed Austrian Nazi coup in July 1934 in which Chancellor Dollfuss was murdered, and the German Condor Legion's intervention for Franco in the Spanish Civil War from 1936.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.b] Hitler's foreign policy was driven by racial ideology and the goal of Lebensraum in the east, which the intentionalist school of historians sees as a plan for war from the start.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.0] In Mein Kampf Hitler argued that Germany needed Lebensraum (living space), to be won in Eastern Europe and settled by a racially pure population, with the Slavs to be subjugated.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.1] The idea of a herrenvolk, a master race of Germanic people, underpinned both domestic policy and plans for the conquest and resettlement of Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.2] At a meeting of 5 November 1937, later recorded in the Hossbach Memorandum, Hitler told his military and foreign policy chiefs that Germany's space problem could be solved only by force, probably around 1943 to 1945.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.3] Appeasement was British and French policy to satisfy Germany's grievances through concessions, based on the belief that Versailles had been unfair and that war must be avoided.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.4] Historians disagree about whether Hitler planned a general war from 1933 (the intentionalist view) or responded to opportunities and his own followers' initiatives (the functionalist view).
[hist-eur3-s3.5.5] A.J.P. Taylor's 1961 book argued that Hitler's aims were opportunist and similar to those of earlier German leaders, a view that was widely challenged by later historians.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.b] Hitler's rule from 1933 to 1939 combined a consolidated dictatorship at home with a foreign policy of rearmament, expansion and war.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.0] As Chancellor and then Führer, he set the direction of policy, while rival agencies competed for influence, a structure historians call polycracy.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.1] He was a participant in the foreign policy decisions of 1933 to 1939, making the key choices about withdrawal from the League, rearmament, the Anschluss and Czechoslovakia.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.2] Public opinion in Germany responded enthusiastically to his foreign successes, especially the Saar, the Rhineland and the Anschluss, which made his popularity high by 1938.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.3] Foreign contemporaries were divided: some British politicians saw him as a revisionist who could be satisfied, while others, such as Winston Churchill, warned from the 1930s that he was a threat.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.4] Later historians discuss how far he controlled events and how far his policies were shaped by pressure from the military, the party and the economy.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.5] His foreign policy and the deaths of Jews, opponents and others in the camps are judged together by historians as the central crimes of his regime, a matter taken up in the notes on the Holocaust.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.b] The regime aimed to unite Germans of 'pure' race in a national community under party control, and it reshaped work, education and family life to do so.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.0] The German Labour Front under Robert Ley replaced the free unions; its Strength Through Joy (Kraft durch Freude) section organised cheap holidays, cruises and theatre trips, and its Beauty of Labour section improved factory conditions.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.1] The Volkswagen savings scheme, in which workers paid weekly instalments towards a Strength Through Joy car, never delivered cars to the savers, because the factory was turned over to military production.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.2] The Hitler Youth, led by Baldur von Schirach, became the only legal youth organisation under the Hitler Youth Law of December 1936, with boys in the Jungvolk and Hitler Youth and girls in the League of German Girls (BDM).
[hist-eur3-s3.7.3] Schools taught racial science and nationalist history, textbooks were rewritten, and teachers were pressed to join the National Socialist Teachers' League.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.4] Nazi ideology assigned women the roles of wife and mother (often summed up as Kinder, Küche, Kirche); marriage loans from 1933 were reduced by a quarter for each child born, and the Mother's Cross was awarded from 1938 to women with large families.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.5] Women were pushed out of the civil service and professions such as law and medicine in 1933, although rearmament and labour shortages brought many back into work by the late 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.6] Opponents, 'asocials' and people judged racially or biologically inferior were excluded from the community: Jews by the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, and disabled people by forced sterilisation under the law of July 1933.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.7] Unemployment, officially about six million in early 1933, fell below one million by 1937 and gave way to labour shortages, although the figures excluded Jews and many women, and wages were held down.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.b] The war gave the regime radical new powers; the course of the fighting is covered in the notes on the Second World War.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.0] Albert Speer, Hitler's architect, became Minister for Armaments in February 1942 and raised output of weapons by rationalising production, even as Allied bombing increased.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.1] After the defeat at Stalingrad, Goebbels called for total war in his Sportpalast speech in Berlin on 18 February 1943, leading to the closure of non-essential businesses and the conscription of women up to the age of 45 into war work.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.2] Millions of forced labourers from occupied Europe, including prisoners of war and concentration camp inmates, worked in German factories and farms.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.3] On 20 July 1944 Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg planted a bomb at Hitler's headquarters at Rastenburg (the Wolf's Lair), but Hitler survived and the plot, which involved army officers and conservative civilians, was crushed; thousands were arrested and several hundred executed.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.4] Hitler remained in command to the end, committing suicide in Berlin on 30 April 1945.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.b] Judgements of Hitler's foreign policy divide between seeing him as the driving force of a deliberate war and seeing the war as the product of wider forces and of appeasement.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.0] Achievement: by 1939 Hitler had reversed the main Versailles restrictions, rebuilt the army and expanded German territory without a major war.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.1] Limit: the expansion depended on the restraint of the western powers, which many German generals feared would fight, and the economy was strained by rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.2] Intentionalist view: Hitler's Mein Kampf aims, the Hossbach Memorandum and the invasion of Poland show a deliberate plan for war.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.3] Functionalist view: the policies of the Nazi state, the competition between agencies and the choices of Britain and France shaped how and when the war began.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.4] Contemporary view: appeasement was supported by many British people who wanted to avoid another war, and Munich was welcomed at first as a rescue of peace.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.5] Later view: Munich is generally judged a failure, because it removed Czechoslovakia's defences and left Hitler convinced that the western powers would not fight.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s9: 9. The Second World War: blitzkrieg, wartime alliances, the technology of warfare and Churchill
[hist-eur3-s9.0.b] Blitzkrieg, or lightning war, combined fast armoured thrusts with air power and was first used on a large scale against Poland in September 1939.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.0] Blitzkrieg relied on concentrated armoured divisions, close air support from dive-bombers such as the Junkers Ju 87 Stuka, and rapid breakthroughs that encircled enemy forces.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.1] Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and the Polish army was defeated within about five weeks, with Warsaw surrendering at the end of September.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.2] The Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on 17 September 1939 under the terms of the Nazi-Soviet Pact, and the two powers divided the country.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.3] Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939, but their armies made no major attack on the Western Front during the Phoney War.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.4] The Phoney War lasted from September 1939 until the German offensive in the west in May 1940.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.5] Germany invaded Denmark and Norway on 9 April 1940; Denmark fell within hours, and Norway was conquered by June despite a failed Anglo-French landing, giving Germany Atlantic naval bases and Swedish iron-ore supplies.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.b] In May and June 1940 German armoured forces defeated France in six weeks, and the Battle of Britain that followed was the first major campaign fought entirely in the air.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.0] The German offensive in the west began on 10 May 1940, with an advance through the Netherlands, Belgium and the Ardennes, which bypassed the Maginot Line.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.1] German armoured divisions reached the Channel coast at Abbeville by 20 May, cutting off the British and French forces in the north.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.2] The Battle of Britain was fought from July to October 1940, as the Luftwaffe attacked RAF airfields and aircraft factories in an attempt to gain air superiority.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.3] The RAF's Fighter Command, using Spitfire and Hurricane fighters and the radar early-warning Chain Home system, defeated the attempt to win air superiority.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.4] Germany then switched to bombing London and other cities in the Blitz, which began on 7 September 1940 and continued into 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.5] Germany's invasion plan for Britain, Operation Sea Lion, was postponed in September 1940 and then abandoned.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.b] The German invasion of the Soviet Union and the Japanese attack on the United States in December 1941 turned the war into a global conflict.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.0] Operation Barbarossa, the German invasion of the Soviet Union, began on 22 June 1941 along a front of about 3,000 kilometres from the Baltic to the Black Sea.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.1] German forces advanced rapidly in the first months and reached the outskirts of Moscow and Leningrad by late 1941, but were halted by the winter and Soviet counter-attacks in December.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.2] The Japanese attack on the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 brought the United States into the war, and Germany and Italy declared war on the United States on 11 December.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.3] The Lend-Lease Act of March 1941 allowed the United States to supply Britain, and later the Soviet Union, with weapons and equipment without immediate payment.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.4] Japan's attacks also took the Philippines, Malaya and Singapore, forcing Britain to surrender Singapore in February 1942.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.5] Japan had been at war with China since 1937 and occupied French Indochina in 1940 and 1941; the United States responded with an oil embargo, which pushed Japan towards the attack of December 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.6] Within months Japan captured Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore, the Dutch East Indies, the Philippines and Burma.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.7] The American victory at Midway in June 1942, in which four Japanese carriers were sunk, ended Japan's advance, and the Guadalcanal campaign began the American counter-offensive.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.8] The United States used an island-hopping strategy, backed by submarines and carrier air power, to approach Japan, and later bombed Japanese cities with B-29 bombers.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.9] Japan surrendered after the atomic bombs and the Soviet declaration of war on 8 August 1945; the formal surrender was signed on 2 September 1945.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.b] The Axis powers of Germany, Italy and Japan were linked by formal treaties, but their war aims were not coordinated and they often acted separately.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.0] The Pact of Steel of 22 May 1939 bound Germany and Italy into a military alliance, although Italy was not ready for war when it entered in June 1940.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.1] The Tripartite Pact of 27 September 1940 added Japan to the Axis, recognising the leadership of Germany and Italy in Europe and of Japan in East Asia.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.2] Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Slovakia later joined the Axis, and Finland fought alongside Germany against the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.3] Italy's defeats in Greece and North Africa in 1940 and 1941 forced Germany to intervene in the Balkans and in North Africa, drawing forces away from Barbarossa.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.4] The Axis powers did not share a joint strategy, and Germany and Japan did not coordinate their attacks on the Soviet Union and the United States.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.b] Britain, the Soviet Union and the United States formed a wartime coalition in which cooperation over strategy and the future of Europe was often strained.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.0] Churchill and President Franklin Roosevelt issued the Atlantic Charter in August 1941, setting out shared principles including self-determination and the restoration of sovereignty to those deprived of it.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.1] The Soviet Union joined the Allied coalition after Barbarossa, and Stalin repeatedly called for a second front in Western Europe to relieve pressure on the Red Army.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.2] The Tehran Conference of November and December 1943 brought Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin together for the first time, and it set the plan for the invasion of France.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.3] The Yalta Conference of February 1945 agreed the occupation zones of Germany, the principle of free elections in liberated Eastern Europe and the entry of the Soviet Union into the war against Japan.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.4] Disputes over the timing of the second front, the future of Poland and the Soviet occupation of Eastern Europe foreshadowed the tensions of the Cold War.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.b] The Allied victories of 1942 and 1943 reversed the Axis advance, and the Normandy landings of June 1944 opened the way to the defeat of Germany.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.0] The Second Battle of El Alamein, from October to November 1942, defeated the Axis armies in North Africa under Erwin Rommel, and the Allies landed in Morocco and Algeria the same month.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.1] The Battle of Stalingrad ended in February 1943 with the surrender of the German Sixth Army, and the Battle of Kursk in July 1943 became the last major German offensive in the east.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.2] The Allies invaded Sicily in July 1943 and Italy's government surrendered in September 1943, but Germany occupied most of northern and central Italy.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.3] The Normandy landings (D-Day) began on 6 June 1944, and Allied forces broke out of the beachheads and liberated Paris on 25 August 1944.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.4] The Soviet Red Army advanced into Poland and the Balkans in 1944, and the Soviet offensive on Berlin began in April 1945.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.5] Hitler killed himself in Berlin on 30 April 1945, and German forces surrendered in May 1945, with the surrender at Reims on 7 May and in Berlin on 8 May.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.b] Technological developments shaped the war on land, at sea and in the air, and the decisive use of the atomic bomb ended the war against Japan.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.0] Tanks: the German Panzer divisions used radio communication and combined armour with motorised infantry, while the Soviet T-34 and the American Sherman were produced in large numbers.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.1] Aircraft: strategic bombing by RAF Bomber Command and the US Army Air Forces attacked German cities, causing fires and civilian deaths, with the bombing of Hamburg in July 1943 and Dresden in February 1945 among the most devastating raids.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.2] Radar: the British Chain Home system and later airborne and ship-borne radar gave early warning of attacks and helped locate U-boats and aircraft.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.3] Codebreaking: at Bletchley Park the British decrypted German Enigma messages, and the resulting intelligence, known as Ultra, was used in the Battle of the Atlantic and the North African campaign.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.4] Submarines and convoys: German U-boats sank large numbers of Allied merchant ships in the Atlantic, but the convoy system and escort groups reduced losses from 1943.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.5] Rockets and jets: Germany's V-1 flying bombs were launched against London from June 1944 and its V-2 rockets from September 1944, and later against Antwerp, and the Messerschmitt Me 262 jet fighter entered service in 1944, too late to change the outcome.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.6] Atomic weapons: the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, and Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945; the bombs had been developed in the Manhattan Project, and the first test took place in New Mexico on 16 July 1945.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.b] Churchill served as Prime Minister from May 1940 and led Britain through the war and the negotiations of the Grand Alliance.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.0] Born in 1874 at Blenheim Palace into an aristocratic family, he entered Parliament in 1900 as a Conservative, joined the Liberals in 1904, was First Lord of the Admiralty from 1911 to 1915, and was Chancellor of the Exchequer from 1924 to 1929, then spent the 1930s out of office warning against Hitler.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.1] He became First Lord of the Admiralty again in September 1939 and succeeded Neville Chamberlain as Prime Minister on 10 May 1940.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.2] His speech of 4 June 1940 included the phrase 'we shall fight on the beaches', and his leadership in the Battle of Britain made him a symbol of national resistance.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.3] He was a participant in the Allied conferences at Tehran and Yalta and negotiated closely with Roosevelt and Stalin, and he lost office in July 1945 after the general election.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.4] Contemporaries, including some of his own party, criticised his part in the Gallipoli campaign in 1915 and his return to the gold standard in 1925, but his wartime leadership won wide admiration.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.5] Historians have praised his resolve in 1940 and his strategic leadership, while criticising the bombing of German cities such as Dresden, the diversion of forces to the Mediterranean and Greece in 1941, and his attitude to colonial peoples, as in the Bengal famine of 1943.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.6] He became Prime Minister at the head of a coalition including Labour leaders Clement Attlee and Ernest Bevin, and he also acted as Minister of Defence.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.7] His speeches on the radio and in Parliament, including 'blood, toil, tears and sweat' (13 May 1940) and 'their finest hour' (18 June 1940), sustained morale after Dunkirk and during the Blitz.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.8] Between 26 May and 4 June 1940, Operation Dynamo evacuated over 330,000 Allied troops from Dunkirk.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.9] He built a close relationship with Roosevelt, meeting him at Placentia Bay, Washington, Casablanca (January 1943, where unconditional surrender was announced) and Quebec.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.10] In October 1944 he met Stalin in Moscow and proposed the 'percentages agreement' on spheres of influence in the Balkans, which has been seen as a sign of wartime realism or of cynicism.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.11] He attended the Potsdam Conference in July 1945 until the Labour election victory replaced him with Attlee.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.b] The 1920s and 1930s produced the ideas and machines that were used in the Second World War.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.0] Armoured warfare theory by British writers such as J.F.C. Fuller and Basil Liddell Hart, and by the German general Heinz Guderian in Achtung - Panzer! (1937), influenced the formation of German Panzer divisions from 1935.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.1] Air power theorists such as Giulio Douhet argued that bombers would decide wars; the bombing of Guernica in April 1937 by the German Condor Legion in the Spanish Civil War, which tested aircraft and tactics, shocked world opinion.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.2] Britain developed radar from 1935, when Robert Watson-Watt showed that radio waves could detect aircraft, and Chain Home stations were built along the east and south coasts from 1936.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.3] Fighter design improved with the Hawker Hurricane (first flown 1935) and the Supermarine Spitfire (1936), and the Messerschmitt Bf 109 entered service in Germany.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.4] Polish mathematicians broke the early Enigma machine in the early 1930s and passed their methods to Britain and France in July 1939.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.5] Frank Whittle patented the jet engine in 1930, and the first German jet aircraft, the Heinkel He 178, flew in August 1939.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.6] Rocket research by Wernher von Braun for the German army began in the early 1930s and led to the V-2 at Peenemünde.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.b] Judgements of Churchill and the alliances weigh the British war effort and the coalition's success against its divisions and its costs.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.0] Achievement: Britain's refusal to negotiate a peace in 1940 kept open a base from which the Allies could later invade Europe.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.1] Limit: British strategy relied heavily on American resources after 1941, and the British Empire's position in Asia was lost in 1942.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.2] Achievement: the Grand Alliance defeated the Axis powers and produced the United Nations as a post-war organisation.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.3] Limit: the alliance concealed deep divisions over Eastern Europe, and these became the Cold War once Germany was defeated.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.4] Contemporary view: many British people saw Churchill as the embodiment of national resistance, while critics on the left and in his own party questioned his judgement.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.5] Later view: historians still debate how far Churchill's leadership was essential, with some stressing his role in 1940 and others emphasising the decisive contribution of Soviet and American forces.
