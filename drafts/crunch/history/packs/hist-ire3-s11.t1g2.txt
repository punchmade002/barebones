TARGET hist-ire3-s11: 11. Case study: the Eucharistic Congress, 1932
[hist-ire3-s11.0.b] The Eucharistic Congress was an international Catholic gathering held in Dublin in 1932. Its date was chosen to tie Catholicism to Irish identity, and it came ten years after independence, in a country where the Church was very strong.
[hist-ire3-s11.0.0] An International Eucharistic Congress is a gathering of Catholics from many countries to honour the Eucharist, the sacrament in which Catholics believe bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ. The first was held in 1881, and each is held in a different city.
[hist-ire3-s11.0.1] Dublin's was the 31st Congress. 1932 was chosen because it was the 1,500th anniversary of the traditional date of St Patrick's arrival in Ireland in 432, which linked the event to Irish Catholic identity.
[hist-ire3-s11.0.2] The invitation and the early planning were made while Cosgrave's government (Cumann na nGaedheal, in office 1922-32) was in power, so the Congress was planned under Cosgrave. The Church and the state treated it as a national occasion.
[hist-ire3-s11.0.3] Archbishop Edward Byrne of Dublin headed the organising effort, and the Pope sent a legate (a personal representative), Cardinal Lorenzo Lauri.
[hist-ire3-s11.0.4] The Free State was ten years old in 1932, after the Treaty and the Civil War, so the Congress was an opportunity for the new state to show itself to the world.
[hist-ire3-s11.0.5] The Catholic Church had a strong position in Irish life because the vast majority of the population was Catholic and the Church ran most schools and many hospitals. This is why a religious event could become a national one.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.b] The Congress was planned under Cosgrave but held under de Valera, whose Fianna Fáil party took office three months before it opened. Both governments backed it, and the preparations were large.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.0] Fianna Fáil won the February 1932 election and de Valera became President of the Executive Council (head of government) on 9 March 1932, three months before the Congress.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.1] De Valera was known for his personal piety, so he took a leading part and his government gave the planning full support. He did not issue the invitation, which had come from Cosgrave's government.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.2] Dublin was prepared with altars, decorations, flags and illuminations, and the streets of poorer districts (the slums) were decorated too, so that the whole city took part.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.3] A large altar was built in the Phoenix Park for the final Mass, and the arrangements for transport, accommodation and crowd control were extensive because very large crowds were expected.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.4] A radio station at Athlone was brought into use in 1932, so that the ceremonies and the Pope's message could reach listeners around the country. A loudspeaker system let the huge crowds hear the services.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.5] Visitors came from the United States, Britain and Europe, and thousands of pilgrims came from Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.6] A national committee raised money by public subscription (donations from the public), and parishes organised local groups for the processions.
[hist-ire3-s11.1.7] Thousands of volunteers acted as stewards, and special first-aid and catering services were arranged for the Phoenix Park.
[hist-ire3-s11.2.b] The Congress showed close co-operation between the Catholic hierarchy (the bishops) and the new Fianna Fáil government. It also exposed the unresolved question of the Crown's representative in the Free State.
[hist-ire3-s11.2.0] Cardinal Lauri arrived at Dún Laoghaire on 21 June 1932, the day before the Congress opened. De Valera and Archbishop Byrne welcomed him, and he was escorted through crowds to Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s11.2.1] The Governor-General, James McNeill, who represented the Crown in the Free State, was not invited to the legate's reception at Dublin Castle. The Crown and the oath were live issues for the new government, so this was a deliberate snub.
[hist-ire3-s11.2.2] The affair was part of the conflict that ended in McNeill's dismissal on 1 November 1932, after the government advised the King to remove him.
[hist-ire3-s11.2.3] De Valera used the occasion to present his government as both Catholic and independent of the Crown. In this way the dispute showed sovereignty, the state's independent standing, being asserted.
[hist-ire3-s11.2.4] The Congress also gave de Valera's new government a chance to show that it could work with the Church.
[hist-ire3-s11.3.b] Five days of Masses, processions and services culminated in an open-air Mass in the Phoenix Park and a procession to O'Connell Bridge. Remember the dates, since examiners reward dated detail.
[hist-ire3-s11.3.0] The Congress ran from 22 to 26 June 1932 and opened at the Pro-Cathedral in Dublin, with Masses and services in city churches, including a procession of the Blessed Sacrament (the consecrated bread of the Eucharist).
[hist-ire3-s11.3.1] On 25 June a children's Mass was held in the Phoenix Park, attended by about 100,000 children.
[hist-ire3-s11.3.2] On Sunday 26 June the closing Mass was celebrated at the altar in the Phoenix Park before a crowd often estimated at about a million. This may be exaggerated, but it is described as about a quarter of the population of the state.
[hist-ire3-s11.3.3] John McCormack, the famous Irish tenor, sang César Franck's Panis Angelicus at a Mass of the Congress.
[hist-ire3-s11.3.4] A message from Pope Pius XI was broadcast live by radio from the Vatican to the crowd, so the Pope could address the people of Ireland without being present.
[hist-ire3-s11.3.5] After the Mass a procession went from the park to O'Connell Bridge, where Cardinal Lauri gave the closing benediction (a final blessing) to a very large crowd, up to several hundred thousand people.
[hist-ire3-s11.3.6] Cardinal Lauri addressed the crowds and presided as the Pope's representative throughout the Congress.
[hist-ire3-s11.4.b] The size of the event tested the state's administration and brought international attention. Its success helped present the Free State as a stable and devout country.
[hist-ire3-s11.4.0] Special trains, buses and sailings brought crowds from all over Ireland and abroad, and the Garda and the Army managed the crowds.
[hist-ire3-s11.4.1] Loudspeakers allowed huge crowds to hear the ceremonies, and radio carried the broadcast to the country, so people who could not be in the Phoenix Park could still take part.
[hist-ire3-s11.4.2] Foreign newspapers reported the event, and it gave the Free State international publicity as a devout, stable Catholic country.
[hist-ire3-s11.4.3] The Congress passed off without major disorder, which was a strong demonstration of public order only ten years after the Civil War.
[hist-ire3-s11.4.4] The event was a success for de Valera, whose prestige grew at home and abroad.
[hist-ire3-s11.4.5] Archbishop Byrne and the organising committee had to coordinate bishops from around the world, and papal flags and Irish flags were flown together.
[hist-ire3-s11.4.6] The state radio service, which grew from the Athlone station brought into use for the Congress, was named Radio Éireann in 1938.
[hist-ire3-s11.5.b] In the North the Congress was seen very differently. It highlighted the religious divide on the island, since unionists (who wanted Northern Ireland to remain in the United Kingdom) saw it as a threat.
[hist-ire3-s11.5.0] Catholic pilgrims travelling by train and bus to and from Dublin were attacked in Northern Ireland, and trains were stoned at places such as Portadown, Lisburn and Belfast.
[hist-ire3-s11.5.1] Police made baton charges at some stations to protect pilgrims.
[hist-ire3-s11.5.2] Unionists saw the Congress as proof that the Free State was a Catholic state, which reinforced their support for partition (the division of the island) because they feared Rome rule.
[hist-ire3-s11.5.3] Protestant newspapers and Orange leaders criticised the scale of Catholic display, and the attacks on pilgrims added to sectarian tension.
[hist-ire3-s11.5.4] For Northern nationalists, the Congress was a source of pride and reinforced their sense of belonging to an Irish Catholic nation.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.b] The Congress is usually assessed through four themes: Church-state relations, Catholic identity, Ireland's image and standing abroad, and unionist perceptions. Naming each and giving evidence earns credit, whereas narrative alone does not.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.0] Church and state: it showed the alliance between the hierarchy and the political leadership, the same alignment that is seen in the tone of the 1937 constitution and in social legislation. The Congress passed no laws and did not cause the constitution.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.1] Catholic identity: it presented Ireland as a Catholic nation, in line with the Gaelic and Catholic ideal of the 1930s. This is an example of conformity, the pressure to share the dominant Catholic values, as censorship laws of the period were too.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.2] International image: it presented the Free State as stable, devout and independent, and made de Valera well known abroad.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.3] Sovereignty: the Governor-General dispute showed the government's desire to downgrade the Crown's representative and assert the state's independent standing.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.4] Unionist view: it confirmed fears of a state dominated by the Catholic Church.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.5] Contemporary view: the Catholic press and most of the public regarded it as a great national triumph.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.6] Later view: historians describe it as the high point of the Church's public influence, and a sign of the conformity that marked the 1930s. Some stress it as a political success for de Valera.
[hist-ire3-s11.6.7] De Valera's critics in Cumann na nGaedheal noted that the Congress had been planned by Cosgrave's government but that Fianna Fáil gained the credit.
[hist-ire3-s11.7.b] The Congress was a great public success, but its effect was largely symbolic. Examiners reward judgements set out as paired points.
[hist-ire3-s11.7.0] Achievement: it showed the state could organise a major international event soon after the Civil War.
[hist-ire3-s11.7.1] Achievement: it united people across party lines at a time of political change.
[hist-ire3-s11.7.2] Limit: it did not change any laws or institutions, so its influence was cultural and political rather than legal.
[hist-ire3-s11.7.3] Limit: it deepened the distrust between North and South, and Catholic and Protestant, on the island.
[hist-ire3-s11.7.4] Contemporary view: a triumph for the Church and the nation.
[hist-ire3-s11.7.5] Later view: a moment when church, state and nation appeared identical, which later generations judged more critically.

CANDIDATES hist-ire3-s10: 10. State and culture, North and South: language, religion, education and cultural identity, 1920–1949
[hist-ire3-s10.0.b] Both parts of Ireland were brand new in the early 1920s, and each government used language, religion, schools and culture to build or defend a sense of who its people were. The South promoted a Gaelic and Catholic identity, the North a Protestant and British one.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.0] Northern Ireland was created in 1920-21 under the Government of Ireland Act 1920, and the Free State came into being in December 1922, so both states had to decide what kind of society they wanted.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.1] In the South the cultural programme came from the Gaelic revival before 1914, led by the Gaelic League (which promoted the Irish language) and the GAA (the Gaelic Athletic Association, which promoted Irish games). The Church was the dominant institution, and after 1922 the Protestant minority was small.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.2] In the North the state was run by Unionists, who wanted to keep the link with Britain, and Catholics were about a third of the population, so they were a large minority and not a tiny one.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.3] Conformity means pressure to follow the accepted values, here Catholic and Gaelic ones, enforced through law, schools and public opinion. This is why those who did not fit, such as Protestants or writers, could feel left out.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.4] Censorship is state control of what may be shown or read, carried out by film and publications censors who had the power to ban. Discrimination is unequal treatment of one group; in the North it was aimed at Catholics, including in school funding and control.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.b] The Free State made reviving Irish (which had become a minority language) a central aim of cultural policy. It used schools and the civil service to spread it, and tied knowing Irish to getting a job.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.0] Irish became a compulsory subject in national schools (primary schools) in 1922, and infant classes were taught wholly through Irish. This was a change in national schools only; second-level requirements came later.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.1] A pass in Irish became necessary for State examinations at second level and for entry to the civil service and the Garda, so that knowing the language was tied to careers. No exact year is given for these rules, which belong to the 1920s and 1930s.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.2] Ernest Blythe, Minister for Finance from 1923 to 1932, was a leading advocate of Irish, and the civil service was required to learn it.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.3] The Gaeltacht Commission (the Gaeltacht is the Irish-speaking districts) was set up in 1925 and reported in 1926. It defined those districts and recommended economic and educational support for them.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.4] An Gúm was founded in 1926 to publish books in Irish, including translations of foreign works, so that schools had texts and readers in the language.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.5] Taibhdhearc na Gaillimhe, an Irish-language theatre, opened in Galway in 1928. Preparatory colleges trained teachers who could teach through Irish, because the policy depended on teachers able to do so.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.6] The 1937 constitution recognised Irish as the first official language, with English as the second.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.b] The policy raised the number of people who could say they knew Irish, but few used it in daily life. Judging it means weighing the census figures against what happened in everyday speech.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.0] Census returns showed that about 18 per cent of people said they could speak Irish in 1926, rising to nearly a quarter by 1936. The rise came mainly through schooling, so it measured what pupils had learned and not what they spoke at home.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.1] The Gaeltacht, the real Irish-speaking districts, continued to shrink because of emigration and poverty, so the policy failed to protect the places where Irish was a living language.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.2] Many parents and teachers resented the compulsion, and some pupils learned little, which weakened support for the policy.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.3] Irish remained a symbol of national identity, so the state kept it as a school subject even where it failed as a spoken language.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.b] In the Free State the Church ran most schools and the state accepted its role. The state paid for education but left control largely to the clergy.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.0] National schools were managed by clergy of the local denomination, and the state paid teachers' salaries and set the curriculum, so the Church controlled the running of schools while the state supplied the money.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.1] The School Attendance Act of 1926 made school attendance compulsory for children from 6 to 14.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.2] Secondary schools were mostly fee-paying and run by religious orders, so few working-class children reached them and access for the poor was limited.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.3] The Vocational Education Act of 1930 set up vocational education committees to provide technical education, a route outside the fee-paying secondary schools.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.4] The Church influenced the content of teaching, and the 1937 constitution recognised the family and the Church's role.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.5] John Charles McQuaid, Archbishop of Dublin from 1940, was an influential voice on social and educational issues.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.b] Laws in the Free State reflected Catholic teaching on morality, and critics saw the result as an enforced conformity. Each law below can be dated and linked to that ethos.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.0] The Censorship of Films Act of 1923 set up a film censor who could cut or ban films, so what people saw at the cinema was controlled from the start of the state.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.1] In 1925 the Dáil changed its standing orders (its own rules) so that private bills for divorce could no longer be introduced. W.B. Yeats made a famous speech in the Seanad defending the Protestant minority, who did not share the Catholic view of divorce.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.2] The Censorship of Publications Act of 1929 set up a board with the power to ban books and periodicals considered indecent or immoral.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.3] Writers were banned, among them Samuel Beckett (More Pricks Than Kicks, 1934) and, later, Kate O'Brien and Seán Ó Faoláin. A banned writer is good evidence of censorship.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.4] The Public Dance Halls Act of 1935 required dance halls to be licensed, after clerical concern about dances.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.5] The Criminal Law Amendment Act of 1935 banned the sale and import of contraceptives, in line with Catholic teaching.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.6] Article 41 of the 1937 constitution, on the family, banned divorce, and Article 44 recognised the special position of the Catholic Church (it also named other churches and Jewish congregations).
[hist-ire3-s10.4.7] The Protestant population of the Free State fell in the 1920s and 1930s, partly through emigration, the Ne Temere rule (the Church rule on the religion of children of mixed marriages) and the violence of the revolution and Civil War. No figure is given.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.b] The state promoted a national culture through games, theatre, radio and the collection of folklore, while the GAA enforced a cultural boundary. Behind all of this lay the ideal of a Gaelic and Catholic Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.0] The Tailteann Games were held in Dublin in 1924, 1928 and 1932, a festival of sport and the arts modelled on the ancient Irish games and open to people of Irish descent abroad.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.1] The Abbey Theatre received a state subsidy from 1925, regarded as the first state subsidy for a national theatre in the English-speaking world. The Gate Theatre was founded in 1928 by Hilton Edwards and Micheál Mac Liammóir to stage international plays.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.2] The state radio station 2RN began broadcasting from Dublin on 1 January 1926, a high-power transmitter at Athlone opened in 1932, and the station was renamed Radio Éireann in 1938.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.3] The Irish Folklore Commission was established in 1935 under Séamus Ó Duilearga to record traditional stories and customs. In the Schools' Collection of 1937-38, primary pupils collected local folklore.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.4] The GAA's Rule 27, in the rulebook from 1905, banned members from playing foreign games such as soccer, rugby, hockey and cricket, and was later extended to attending them. It was repealed in 1971.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.5] In 1938 the GAA removed Douglas Hyde, President of Ireland, as its patron after he attended an international soccer match in Dublin. Hyde had founded the Gaelic League in 1893.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.6] Culture was linked to nationalism by the idea of a Gaelic and Catholic Ireland, which excluded many Protestants and urban people, because it measured Irishness by language, religion and rural tradition.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.b] Education was contested between the Unionist government, which wanted a non-denominational state system, and the churches. The outcome was a school system split by religion.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.0] The Lynn Committee was appointed in 1921 to review education under the first Minister of Education, Lord Londonderry, and reported in 1922-23.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.1] The Education Act (Northern Ireland) 1923, known as the Londonderry Act, created education committees run by local authorities, with public elementary schools to be non-denominational, that is, free of church control.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.2] Protestant churches and the Orange Order objected to the lack of religious instruction, so the Act was amended in 1925 to allow Bible instruction, and the Orange Order and clergy gained places on school committees.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.3] The Catholic Church refused to transfer its schools to state control and kept them as voluntary schools, outside the state system, with only partial public funding.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.4] The 1930 Amendment Act gave committees of transferred schools four representatives of the transferors (the churches that handed over their schools) and two of the local authority, which Catholics saw as favouring Protestant interests.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.5] The result was a divided school system, which remained a source of segregation and a grievance for Catholics, an example of discrimination.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.b] In the North the unionist and nationalist communities expressed separate identities in a state that favoured the first. This was the Northern counterpart of the Southern Gaelic and Catholic ideal.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.0] Orange Order parades on 12 July and loyalist bands were central to unionist culture and enjoyed official protection.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.1] The Protestant ethos of the state was symbolised by the flag, the Union Jack, and by the royal visits and ceremonies at Stormont.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.2] Nationalists supported the GAA, Irish-language classes and Catholic schools, but the Irish language received little state support.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.3] Under the Special Powers Act (1922) the authorities could ban processions and emblems, and nationalist parades were more likely to be banned, so the law fell unevenly on the two communities.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.4] James Craig (Lord Craigavon), the prime minister, said in 1934 that it was "a Protestant Parliament and a Protestant State". This was matched in the South by the Catholic emphasis of the 1937 constitution.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.b] Hone was a leading modernist painter who turned to stained glass and became the best-known Irish stained-glass artist of her time. Her career shows how art could express an independent Ireland's identity.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.0] Evie Hone (1894-1955) was born in Dublin and studied art in London and later in Paris under André Lhote and Albert Gleizes.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.1] With Mainie Jellett she was one of the first Irish painters to work in a cubist style, which breaks a subject into geometric shapes.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.2] From the 1930s she worked in stained glass with An Túr Gloine (the Tower of Glass), the Dublin studio founded by Sarah Purser in 1903 to revive Irish stained glass.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.3] She converted to Catholicism in 1937, and much of her work was for churches, so church patronage shaped her career.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.4] My Four Green Fields, a window symbolising the four provinces, was made for the Irish pavilion at the 1939 New York World's Fair.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.5] The Crucifixion east window of Eton College Chapel was commissioned in 1949 to replace glass destroyed by a bomb in 1940, and was installed in 1952.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.6] Contemporaries praised her work as modern Irish art of international standing.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.7] Later writers on Irish art place her in the effort to give independent Ireland a modern cultural identity.
[hist-ire3-s10.9.b] Cultural policy gave each state a distinct identity, but it also narrowed the range of what was acceptable. Each state promoted the identity of its own majority community.
[hist-ire3-s10.9.0] Achievement: institutions such as An Gúm, the Folklore Commission, the Abbey and Radio Éireann preserved and promoted Irish culture, giving the new state a distinct identity.
[hist-ire3-s10.9.1] Limit: compulsory Irish did not make it the daily language of most people, and the Gaeltacht continued to decline.
[hist-ire3-s10.9.2] Achievement: arts and crafts, including the work of Hone, flourished with state and church patronage.
[hist-ire3-s10.9.3] Limit: censorship and moral legislation restricted writers and individual choice, and minorities felt excluded, which produced conformity.
[hist-ire3-s10.9.4] In the North the divided education system and separate cultural traditions deepened the communal division, in contrast with the South, where the state and the majority shared one cultural ideal.
[hist-ire3-s10.9.5] Contemporary view: supporters saw cultural policy as defending Irish identity; critics, including writers such as Ó Faoláin, saw a narrow and conformist vision.
[hist-ire3-s10.9.6] Later view: historians stress the tension between revival and restriction, and the influence of the Church.
