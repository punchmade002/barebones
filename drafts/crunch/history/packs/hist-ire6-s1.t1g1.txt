TARGET hist-ire6-s1: 1. Alternating governments and their economic and social policies, 1948–1959
[hist-ire6-s1.0.b] To understand why governments changed so often in the 1950s, you first need to know what Ireland inherited from the 1930s and the war years. The country was poor, politically divided and closely tied to Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.0] Fianna Fáil under de Valera governed from 1932 to February 1948 and built a protected, self-sufficiency economy, using tariffs (taxes on imports) and the Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934, which required Irish citizens to hold a majority of shares in most firms.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.1] The party system was Fianna Fáil against Fine Gael and Labour, and the Civil War still shaped loyalties. Proportional representation (voters rank candidates, so smaller parties win seats) made coalitions possible when no party had a majority.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.2] Ireland was neutral in the Second World War, which it called 'the Emergency'. It came out of the war poor, isolated and tied to the British market and to sterling (the British pound).
[hist-ire6-s1.0.3] The Catholic Church had strong influence on social policy, and its position was recognised in the Constitution of 1937. This is why a clash between a minister and the bishops, as in 1951, could decide government policy.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.b] Two economic terms run through the whole decade. Between them they explain why governments of every party kept cutting spending, even when it made life harder.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.0] The balance of payments is the record of a country's payments to and from the rest of the world. If more money goes out (mainly to buy imports) than comes in (mainly from exports), the country has a deficit.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.1] A deficit drained Ireland's reserves of foreign currency, because the money to pay for imports had to come from those reserves. Since the Irish pound was linked to sterling, the government could not simply let its currency fall.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.2] The usual cure was deflation: raising taxes and cutting government spending or credit to reduce demand, so that people bought fewer imports. The cost was jobs, because falling demand led firms to cut staff.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.3] This is why MacEntee's 1952 budget was unpopular, and why deflation (including the measures of 1955–56) helped drive both unemployment and emigration.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.b] After sixteen years of Fianna Fáil rule, a coalition of the opposition parties took office in 1948. An Inter-Party Government is simply a coalition of several parties that agreed to govern together; there were two, in 1948–51 and 1954–57.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.0] In the February 1948 general election Fianna Fáil, in power since 1932, remained the largest party but lost its majority, so it could no longer govern alone.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.1] Clann na Poblachta, a republican party founded in 1946 by Seán MacBride, won only 10 seats in 1948 but held the balance of power in the Dáil, meaning the coalition needed its votes.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.2] The government that took office on 18 February 1948 united Fine Gael, Labour, the National Labour Party, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan and some independents.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.3] Richard Mulcahy, the Fine Gael leader, stood aside because of his Civil War record, so John A. Costello became Taoiseach. William Norton of Labour was Tánaiste (deputy prime minister).
[hist-ire6-s1.2.4] Seán MacBride was Minister for External Affairs, and Noël Browne of Clann na Poblachta became Minister for Health in 1948.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.5] The coalition formed to end Fianna Fáil's long hold on office and had no common economic programme. It contained both conservative and radical members, which made bold policy hard.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.b] The government's best-known act was constitutional, but it also made economic and health decisions that mattered for the rest of the decade.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.0] Costello announced in Ottawa, Canada, in September 1948 that the government would repeal the External Relations Act of 1936 and declare a republic.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.1] The Republic of Ireland Act, passed in 1948, came into force on 18 April 1949 (Easter Monday). It ended the state's last formal link with the Crown and its Commonwealth membership.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.2] Britain replied with the Ireland Act 1949, which guaranteed that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of its parliament.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.3] The Ireland Act also said the Republic was not a foreign country and its citizens were not aliens in Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.4] Ireland declined an invitation to join NATO (the Western military alliance) in 1949, with MacBride citing partition as the obstacle.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.5] The Industrial Development Authority (IDA) was set up in 1949 to encourage new industry, including investment from abroad, though in the 1950s it had little money or power.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.6] Ireland took part in Marshall Aid (the European Recovery Programme, American aid for rebuilding after the war) and received about 146 million dollars, mostly as loans. Much went on land reclamation and agriculture rather than industry, which later critics saw as a missed chance.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.7] Noël Browne led a campaign against tuberculosis (TB), a major killer. New sanatoria (hospitals for TB patients) were paid for partly from the Irish Hospitals' Sweepstake, and a BCG vaccination programme began. TB deaths fell steeply during the 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.8] The government also expanded house building.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.b] Browne's plan for free health care for mothers and children became the clearest example of the Catholic hierarchy's power over social policy in the 1950s. Its defeat also helped end the government.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.0] Browne's scheme of 1950 used the Health Act 1947 to offer free care for mothers before and after childbirth and for children up to the age of 16, available to all without a means test (a check on income).
[hist-ire6-s1.4.1] The Irish Medical Association opposed it as a threat to doctors' private practice and independence.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.2] The Catholic hierarchy (the bishops) objected that the scheme gave the state too much power over family life, that it might lead to state-provided education in sexual matters, and that it undermined the rights of parents.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.3] The hierarchy wrote to Costello in late 1950 and again in April 1951, saying the scheme was contrary to Catholic social teaching.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.4] MacBride, who wanted to protect his government from a clash with the church, asked Browne to resign. Browne resigned in April 1951 and then published the correspondence, which made the church's role public.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.5] Costello and the cabinet accepted the bishops' view, so the scheme was dropped.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.6] The affair helped bring down the government. Costello called a general election for 30 May 1951, in which Clann na Poblachta fell from 10 seats to 2.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.7] The Fianna Fáil government that took office replaced the scheme with the Health Act 1953, which offered means-tested maternity, child and hospital services, so many people were left without free care.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.b] McQuaid was Archbishop of Dublin from 1940 to 1972 and the most influential churchman in the state in the 1950s. Here the focus is only his part in the crisis of 1950–51.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.0] John Charles McQuaid was born in 1895 in Cootehill, Co. Cavan, joined the Holy Ghost Fathers and was president of Blackrock College before he became Archbishop of Dublin in 1940.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.1] His influence rested on close personal contact with ministers, notably de Valera, whom he had advised on the religious article of the Constitution of 1937.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.2] In 1950–51 he was a participant: he advised Browne and the government on the church's objections and was the most influential voice in arguing that the scheme conflicted with Catholic social teaching. The letters themselves were the bishops' collective act, headed by the primate, Cardinal D'Alton.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.3] His principle was that the family and the church, not the state, should direct the care of mothers and children. He objected to the principle, not only to medical detail.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.4] Contemporaries who agreed with him saw a defender of Catholic social teaching. Browne and his supporters saw an interfering prelate who used his power to defeat a good scheme.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.5] John Whyte, in Church and State in Modern Ireland, 1923–1979 (1971), concluded that the government deferred to the hierarchy. Later work using archives stresses Browne's weak political position and the doctors' opposition, while still treating the bishops' stance as decisive.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.b] De Valera returned as Taoiseach in June 1951, but without a majority and with an economy short of foreign currency. The government's answer to the balance of payments problem was deflation.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.0] Fianna Fáil formed a minority government with de Valera as Taoiseach, Seán Lemass as Tánaiste and Minister for Industry and Commerce, and Seán MacEntee as Minister for Finance.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.1] The minority government depended on the votes of independent deputies, which limited how far it could go with unpopular measures.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.2] MacEntee's 1952 budget was deflationary: it cut subsidies on basic foods and raised duties, which pushed prices up, in order to reduce imports and the balance of payments deficit. It was unpopular.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.3] The Underdeveloped Areas Act 1952 gave grants for new industry in the poorer western counties.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.4] The Health Act 1953 provided a framework of means-tested maternity, child and hospital care in place of the Mother and Child Scheme.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.5] Emigration continued at a high level and unemployment stayed high. In the 1954 general election Fianna Fáil lost seats.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.b] Costello returned as Taoiseach in 1954 at the head of a coalition that again had no programme for growth. It then faced a balance of payments crisis.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.0] The second coalition contained Fine Gael, Labour and Clann na Talmhan, with Clann na Poblachta supporting it from outside government, meaning it voted for the government but held no ministries.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.1] Gerard Sweetman, the Minister for Finance, was a strong believer in private enterprise and low state spending.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.2] A balance of payments crisis built up in 1955 and 1956 because imports ran well ahead of exports and foreign currency reserves fell.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.3] The government responded with higher taxes, credit restrictions and cuts in current spending. This reduced demand and pushed up unemployment.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.4] The Capital Investment Advisory Committee, set up in 1956, reviewed proposals for public capital spending. It was an early step towards planning public investment.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.5] In December 1956 the IRA began its Border Campaign, and the government faced pressure from Clann na Poblachta over its handling of the North and its response to the campaign.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.6] Clann na Poblachta withdrew its support in January 1957, which left Costello without a majority, and he called an election.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.b] While most of western Europe boomed, the Irish economy grew only slowly and people left in large numbers. Unemployment stayed high throughout the decade.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.0] The census showed a population of 2,960,593 in 1951 and 2,818,341 in 1961, a fall of about 142,000 despite a high birth rate, because so many people emigrated.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.1] Net emigration (people leaving minus people arriving) in the 1950s was about 400,000, roughly 200,000 in each half of the decade. Most of those who left were young people heading for Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.2] Britain attracted them because it offered work in construction, factories, the health service and transport, and its economy was growing faster than the Irish.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.3] Agriculture still employed about two-fifths of the workforce, mostly on small farms of low productivity, and cattle exports to Britain were the main earner.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.4] Industry sheltered behind protective tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts, which required that most shares in new firms be held by Irish citizens and so discouraged foreign investment.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.5] Irish growth was a fraction of the growth in western Europe, so the gap with other countries widened.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.6] The Irish pound was linked to sterling, so Irish interest rates and trade followed British conditions, including the British government's alternating cycles of expansion and restriction, known as 'stop-go'.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.b] The 1957 election ended the second coalition. Historians use the phrase 'wasted years' for the 1950s, a decade they see as one of missed opportunity, and they disagree about who or what was to blame.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.0] In the March 1957 general election Fianna Fáil won 78 seats and an overall majority, and de Valera became Taoiseach for the last time (to 1959).
[hist-ire6-s1.9.1] Lemass returned to Industry and Commerce and also served as Tánaiste, and James Ryan became Minister for Finance.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.2] T.K. Whitaker had become Secretary of the Department of Finance in 1956 and began the work that led to Economic Development.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.3] View 1: historians such as J.J. Lee (Ireland 1912–1985) and Tom Garvin (Preventing the Future, 2004) criticise the leaders of the 1950s for lacking ambition and for preferring stability to change, which gave rise to the term 'wasted years'.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.4] View 2: others point to constraints, such as a small economy that depended on the British market and the sterling link, and weak foreign currency reserves.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.5] View 3: the Inter-Party governments did lay groundwork, through the IDA, the Underdeveloped Areas Act and public investment, that Fianna Fáil later built on. Planning tools were developed only late in the decade, so policy-makers earlier had little to work with.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.b] Examiners reward a clear judgement that pairs successes with failures and explains why governments kept changing. Instability and economic failure were linked: poor results brought defeat, and short-lived governments could not plan.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.0] Achievements: the Republic of Ireland Act completed the move to a republic, while the IDA, the Underdeveloped Areas Act and Marshall Aid laid foundations for later industrial policy. The anti-tuberculosis campaign saved lives.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.1] Limits: no government drew up a long-term plan, and each balance of payments crisis led to deflation, which made unemployment and emigration worse.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.2] Limits: the Mother and Child affair showed the Church's power over social policy, since the state could not act when the church and the medical profession opposed it.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.3] Contemporary view: voters punished each government in turn in 1951, 1954 and 1957 for high taxes and emigration, and the sharp swings between coalition and Fianna Fáil governments reflected that dissatisfaction.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.4] Later view: historians generally accept that the decade was one of slow growth and missed opportunities, but they differ about how far this was caused by policy mistakes rather than by Ireland's dependence on Britain.
