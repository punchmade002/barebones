TARGET hist-ire3-s4: 4. Case study: the Treaty negotiations, October–December 1921
[hist-ire3-s4.0.b] The War of Independence (1919–21) ended in a Truce, but a truce only stops the fighting. Months of talk and letters were needed before a formal conference could meet, and the central question was already clear: the Crown, meaning the British monarchy and Ireland's link to it.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.0] The First Dáil (the Irish assembly set up by Sinn Féin) had declared a republic in January 1919, and the IRA owed its loyalty to the Dáil. This is why anything short of a republic was bound to be controversial.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.1] Partition was already in place: the Government of Ireland Act 1920 had created Northern Ireland, with its own parliament and a unionist government under James Craig, and the talks had to deal with this existing border, which is why Ulster became one of the hardest issues.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.2] George V called for reconciliation in his Belfast speech on 22 June 1921, and Lloyd George then invited de Valera to talks in a letter of 24 June. The Truce came into force at noon on 11 July 1921.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.3] De Valera met Lloyd George in London four times in July 1921 (14, 15, 18 and 21 July), and neither side could shift the other on principle, so the talks produced no agreement.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.4] The British proposals, handed over on 20 July, offered dominion status (self-government within the Empire, like Canada) with restrictions: British control of defence and the ports, free trade, and no change to the position of the six counties, which republicans saw as too restrictive for independence.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.5] In August 1921 Ireland's government rejected the offer. De Valera's counter-proposal was ‘external association’: Ireland would be a separate state linked to the British Commonwealth, with the Crown recognised only as head of the association, not as ruler of Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.b] After the offer was refused, both sides kept talking by letter. Each also had practical reasons to want a settlement, which is why the conference finally met.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.0] Through the late summer Lloyd George insisted that Ireland accept the Crown and remain within the Empire, while de Valera insisted on the right of self-determination, that is, the Irish people's right to choose their own government.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.1] Lloyd George's invitation of 29 September proposed a conference to see how the association of Ireland with the Empire might be reconciled with Irish national aspirations. The Irish accepted, and the conference opened at 10 Downing Street on 11 October 1921.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.2] Britain wanted a settlement because the war was costing money and reputation, the dominion prime ministers urged compromise, and reconquest would have needed a very large army and martial law over the whole island.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.3] The Irish side wanted a settlement because the IRA was short of arms and ammunition, and because civilians in the war zones were paying a heavy cost.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.b] Who went to London, and with what powers, shaped the outcome and the argument about it afterwards. De Valera chose to stay in Dublin, and the delegates carried two different sets of authority.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.0] The Irish delegation was agreed in September 1921: Arthur Griffith (chairman), Michael Collins, Robert Barton, George Gavan Duffy and Éamonn Duggan.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.1] Erskine Childers was the delegation's secretary, with Diarmuid O'Hegarty, John Chartres and others as assistant secretaries.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.2] The delegation lived at 22 Hans Place in Knightsbridge, while Collins stayed separately at 15 Cadogan Gardens. Keep the two addresses apart: it matters when placing a document.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.3] De Valera stayed in Dublin, saying that as head of state he should be held in reserve and that he had to keep republican opinion on side. Cathal Brugha and Austin Stack also stayed at home.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.4] Critics, including Collins, argued that this was a mistake, because the man with most authority and most experience of Lloyd George was absent, and he later disowned the result, which is why the criticism was so sharp.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.5] The delegates' credentials described them as plenipotentiaries, which meant they had full power to negotiate and sign a treaty without further approval.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.6] Their written instructions were different: they were to submit the draft treaty to Dublin before signing and await a reply. How binding this was is still disputed, and it is the heart of the plenipotentiary issue, because full powers and a duty to refer back pulled in opposite directions.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.7] The British team was Lloyd George (Prime Minister), Austen Chamberlain (Lord Privy Seal), Lord Birkenhead (Lord Chancellor), Winston Churchill (Colonial Secretary), Sir Laming Worthington-Evans (War Secretary), Sir Hamar Greenwood (Chief Secretary for Ireland) and Gordon Hewart (Attorney General).
[hist-ire3-s4.2.8] Lionel Curtis and the cabinet secretary Tom Jones acted as advisers and go-betweens, carrying messages between the two sides.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.b] The conference began with full meetings of both sides but soon moved into small private groups, where Griffith and Collins argued with the British over four problems: the Crown, Ulster, defence and finance.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.0] The plenary sessions (full meetings of both delegations) came early in the conference and produced no agreement. After that small sub-conferences met, with Griffith and Collins facing Lloyd George, Chamberlain and Birkenhead, so most of the bargaining was done privately, away from the full delegations.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.1] The Crown and the oath: the British demanded an oath of allegiance (a promise of loyalty) to the Crown and membership of the Commonwealth, and the Irish tried to substitute external association and an oath to the constitution.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.2] Ulster: the Irish argued that Ireland was one nation and that the six counties should not be allowed to opt out, because partition would divide the nation. Lloyd George said he could not coerce them.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.3] The Boundary Commission idea came from the British side. Griffith and Collins took it as a way of removing large nationalist areas from the North, reducing it so much that it could not survive.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.4] Defence: Britain insisted on naval facilities in Irish ports and coastal defence, which the Irish saw as a limit on their sovereignty (supreme power over their own affairs).
[hist-ire3-s4.3.5] Trade and finance: the British wanted free trade between the two islands and a share of the British public debt, for example, while the Irish claimed offsetting counter-claims for overtaxation.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.6] Craig and the Northern government refused to join the talks and refused to accept any all-Ireland arrangement, and Lloyd George, who had difficulty with the unionists, tried to extract concessions from Craig on the basis of a united Ireland within the Empire.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.7] The delegates travelled to Dublin to consult the cabinet in November and December, so the process was not entirely independent of de Valera and the cabinet.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.8] Throughout, Tom Jones and Lionel Curtis passed messages between Lloyd George and Griffith, and Collins formed a good relationship with Birkenhead.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.b] The final week was a negotiation under deadline pressure. The decision to sign without sending the final text back to Dublin is the central controversy of the whole case study.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.0] On 2 December Griffith privately assured Lloyd George that he would not let the conference break on Ulster, provided a Boundary Commission was set up. Lloyd George later treated this as a commitment, and republicans saw it as a concession made without the cabinet's knowledge.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.1] On 3 December the delegates went to Dublin and met the cabinet. There was no agreement on the oath wording, and the delegates returned to London having been instructed not to accept the oath as drafted. So the delegates did consult Dublin, but not on a final text.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.2] On 4 and 5 December the British pressed for signature. On 5 December Lloyd George said that refusal meant war, that he wanted a signature that night, and that his couriers were ready to carry letters to Craig either way.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.3] Barton later recalled that Lloyd George warned of ‘immediate and terrible war’. Whether the threat was a bluff is still disputed.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.4] The delegates returned to Hans Place, where Collins and Griffith argued that the alternative to signing was war. Barton, who had at first refused, gave way because he accepted that refusal meant war.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.5] The Treaty (officially the Articles of Agreement for a Treaty between Great Britain and Ireland) was signed at 10 Downing Street at about 2.10 am on 6 December 1921 by all five Irish delegates, with the British team.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.6] Collins afterwards wrote, in a letter of December 1921, that he had signed his own death warrant.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.7] De Valera was not told in advance. He learned of the signing on 6 December and was angry that the delegation had not sent the final text to Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.b] The Treaty created the Irish Free State as a self-governing dominion in the Empire. It kept the Crown, an oath, British naval bases and partition, qualified by a Boundary Commission.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.0] Article 1: the Irish Free State was to have the same constitutional status as Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and Newfoundland. This is dominion status, which falls short of a republic because the Crown remained, so republicans objected to it.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.1] The Crown was represented in Ireland by a Governor-General, and the Free State's ministers were to be responsible to the Irish parliament, following Canadian practice, so real power rested with elected ministers and not the Crown.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.2] The oath: members of the Free State parliament had to swear allegiance to the Constitution of the Free State and to be faithful to the King, ‘in virtue of the common citizenship of Ireland with Great Britain’.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.3] Defence: Britain would defend the coasts and kept the use of the treaty ports (the naval harbours) of Berehaven, Queenstown (Cobh) and Lough Swilly, and of Belfast Lough, with the right to use other Irish ports in time of war.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.4] The Free State army was to be limited in proportion to population.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.5] Finance: the Free State was to bear a share of the British public debt, subject to counter-claims, so Irish claims such as those for overtaxation could reduce the final sum.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.6] Ulster: Northern Ireland could opt out of the Free State within one month of the Treaty's ratification, and a Boundary Commission would then settle the border ‘in accordance with the wishes of the inhabitants, so far as may be compatible with economic and geographical conditions’.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.7] A Provisional Government would take over from the British administration until the Free State was established.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.8] The Treaty had to be approved by the Dáil and by the British Parliament, and it was also to be ratified by a meeting of the House of Parliament of Southern Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s4.6.b] The Treaty still needed the Dáil's approval, and the debate that followed split the cabinet, the army and families, and led towards the Civil War.
[hist-ire3-s4.6.0] On 8 December 1921 the cabinet divided four to three in favour of the Treaty. De Valera, Brugha and Stack were against.
[hist-ire3-s4.6.1] De Valera issued a statement to the press saying that he could not recommend the Treaty to the Dáil.
[hist-ire3-s4.6.2] The Dáil debated in private from 14 to 17 December. De Valera put forward his own alternative text, Document No. 2, based on external association. It was never presented to the British, so it was never a Treaty draft.
[hist-ire3-s4.6.3] The public debate began on 19 December, was adjourned for Christmas and resumed in January 1922. Speakers included Griffith, Collins, Brugha, Mary MacSwiney and Childers.
[hist-ire3-s4.6.4] Collins described the Treaty as giving ‘the freedom to achieve freedom’, a stepping stone to full independence, which is why he defended it, while Brugha attacked Collins's reputation.
[hist-ire3-s4.6.5] Opponents argued that the oath and the Crown betrayed the republic proclaimed in 1916 and declared in 1919. Supporters argued that the people did not want more war.
[hist-ire3-s4.6.6] All six women deputies voted against the Treaty.
[hist-ire3-s4.7.b] The vote decided the Treaty's fate, and the changes of government that followed put pro-Treaty leaders in power in Dublin and in the new Provisional Government.
[hist-ire3-s4.7.0] The Dáil approved the Treaty on 7 January 1922 by 64 votes to 57. Do not confuse this with the cabinet vote of four to three.
[hist-ire3-s4.7.1] De Valera resigned as President on 9 January and lost a vote to be re-elected, and Griffith was elected President of Dáil Éireann on 10 January.
[hist-ire3-s4.7.2] A Provisional Government under Collins was set up on 14 January and took over from the British administration when Dublin Castle was handed over on 16 January.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.b] Griffith was chairman of the delegation and its senior negotiator. After the Treaty he led the pro-Treaty side in the Dáil until his early death.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.0] Griffith (born 1871) was a Dublin printer and journalist who founded Sinn Féin in 1905. He advocated a dual monarchy, on the Hungarian model (two kingdoms sharing one king), and economic self-reliance.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.1] He was arrested after the Rising, although he had not taken part in it, and in 1917 he stood aside for de Valera as Sinn Féin president.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.2] He was acting head of the Dáil during de Valera's American tour in 1919–20 and was Minister for Foreign Affairs in 1921.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.3] In London he led the negotiations on the Crown and Ulster, and he was prepared to accept dominion status within the Empire with the Boundary Commission. His letter of assurance of 2 December committed him on Ulster.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.4] He was a shy, scholarly man, in poor health after months in prison, so the strain of the negotiations told heavily on him.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.5] After his election as President of Dáil Éireann in January 1922 he led the pro-Treaty side until his death from a cerebral haemorrhage on 12 August 1922.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.6] Contemporaries: supporters of the Treaty praised his courage and integrity, which is a judgement rather than a fact, while republicans condemned him as having abandoned the republic.
[hist-ire3-s4.8.7] Historians stress his role in creating the political movement that won independence, through Sinn Féin, and his realism in 1921.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.b] Collins was reluctant to go to London, but his reputation as the man who had beaten the British gave the delegation influence and made it hard for the Dáil to reject the result.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.0] Collins was Minister for Finance, president of the IRB (the Irish Republican Brotherhood, a secret republican organisation) and director of intelligence in the War of Independence.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.1] He warned the cabinet that he would be a poor negotiator. He believed that his name was needed for the military side but that the politicians should lead, which explains why he went reluctantly.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.2] In London he handled the military and financial issues and built relationships with Birkenhead and Churchill.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.3] He accepted the Boundary Commission clause in the expectation that Tyrone, Fermanagh and parts of Armagh and Down would be transferred, and he believed Northern Ireland could not survive after that.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.4] He argued on the night of 5 December that the IRA could not win another round and that the Treaty gave the means to build up the army and the state.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.5] He saw the Treaty as giving ‘the freedom to achieve freedom’, so he signed it as a first step and not as the end.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.6] He became chairman of the Provisional Government on 14 January 1922.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.7] Contemporaries: anti-Treaty republicans accused him of using his IRB position to override the cabinet, while his supporters said he was honest about the Treaty's limits.
[hist-ire3-s4.9.8] Historians: Tim Pat Coogan sees him as a pragmatic realist, while Peter Hart draws attention to his ruthlessness.
[hist-ire3-s4.10.b] De Valera was President of Dáil Éireann, and his title was changed to President of the Republic in August 1921. He set the strategy and then rejected the outcome, and historians still argue about whether his decision to stay at home was shrewd or a mistake.
[hist-ire3-s4.10.0] His talks with Lloyd George in July 1921 showed that the British would not allow an Irish republic, yet he remained committed to external association.
[hist-ire3-s4.10.1] He chose the delegation and wrote its instructions, but did not join it. His reasons were to keep a power in reserve and to avoid committing the head of state.
[hist-ire3-s4.10.2] He sent messages to London advising the delegates to hold out on the Crown and oath, and kept in touch with cabinet colleagues in Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s4.10.3] After the signing he opposed the Treaty on the grounds that the delegates had signed without the cabinet's approval of the final text. He resigned as President on 9 January 1922 after the Treaty vote.
[hist-ire3-s4.10.4] His Document No. 2 would have accepted the Crown as head of an association with the Commonwealth, with an oath to the constitution only. The Dáil did not adopt it.
[hist-ire3-s4.10.5] Contemporaries: supporters of the Treaty said that he had set a trap for Collins and Griffith, and republicans said that he was upholding the Republic.
[hist-ire3-s4.10.6] Historians: the Earl of Longford's Peace by Ordeal (1935) defended the delegates, while later writers such as Joseph Lee and Diarmaid Ferriter see him as a politician who used ambiguity to hold the movement together.
[hist-ire3-s4.11.b] Judgements depend on whether the Treaty is measured against the republic of 1916 and 1919 or against the realistic alternatives of December 1921. Weigh each claim against the other side's.
[hist-ire3-s4.11.0] Achievement: Britain withdrew its army and administration from 26 counties, and the Free State got its own parliament, police, army and civil service.
[hist-ire3-s4.11.1] Limit: the Treaty kept the Crown, the oath, British bases and partition, which republicans said betrayed the declared republic.
[hist-ire3-s4.11.2] Pro-Treaty view: it gave the freedom to achieve freedom, and a refusal would have meant war in 1922 with the IRA short of arms.
[hist-ire3-s4.11.3] Anti-Treaty view: the delegates were bullied into signing, and the Dáil had no right to give away the republic.
[hist-ire3-s4.11.4] Public opinion: most newspapers, the Catholic bishops, farmers and business supported the Treaty, and pro-Treaty candidates won a clear majority in the June 1922 election.
[hist-ire3-s4.11.5] Unionists saw the Treaty as removing the threat to the North, but the Boundary Commission disappointed nationalists in 1925.
[hist-ire3-s4.11.6] Later view: historians debate whether the delegates were out-manoeuvred, whether the ultimatum was a bluff, and whether de Valera's absence made a bad outcome more likely or was a shrewd move.

CANDIDATES hist-ire3-s2: 2. The second Sinn Féin, the 1918 election and the War of Independence, 1917–1921
[hist-ire3-s2.0.b] The second Sinn Féin grew out of the 1916 Rising and the collapse of the older nationalist politics. A few pieces of background explain why Irish opinion moved so fast after 1916.
[hist-ire3-s2.0.0] The Rising began on Easter Monday, 24 April 1916, and its leaders were executed in May 1916. The executions turned opinion towards the rebels.
[hist-ire3-s2.0.1] The British called the Rising the 'Sinn Féin rebellion', and survivors and the public took up the name, so 'Sinn Féin' became a label for the Rising and everyone who supported it, even though the party had played little part.
[hist-ire3-s2.0.2] Before 1916 the Irish Parliamentary Party (IPP), led by John Redmond, dominated nationalist politics and sought Home Rule (self-government within the United Kingdom) by constitutional means. It lost ground after 1916.
[hist-ire3-s2.0.3] The Irish Volunteers (founded 1913) and the secret Irish Republican Brotherhood (IRB) were the physical-force organisations behind the Rising, and the Volunteers later became the IRA. This is why the IRA later drew on the same men.
[hist-ire3-s2.0.4] Britain governed Ireland from Dublin Castle through the Lord Lieutenant (the Viceroy, the crown's representative), the Royal Irish Constabulary (RIC, an armed police force) and the Dublin Metropolitan Police (DMP). This is why attacks on the police and the Castle were attacks on British rule itself.
[hist-ire3-s2.0.5] World War I ended with the Armistice on 11 November 1918. Britain's desperate need for soldiers in the last year of the war is what caused the conscription crisis of 1918.
[hist-ire3-s2.1.b] Four ideas run through this period, and examiners expect you to use them precisely. Each one is a demand Sinn Féin made or a method it used.
[hist-ire3-s2.1.0] A republic is a state with no monarch as head of state. Sinn Féin demanded one from 1917 and the Dáil declared one in 1919.
[hist-ire3-s2.1.1] Sovereignty is supreme authority over a state's own affairs, with no other power able to override it. The Dáil claimed sovereignty over Ireland in the Declaration of Independence, which is why it set up its own government and courts.
[hist-ire3-s2.1.2] Abstention means refusing to attend. Sinn Féin MPs refused to take their seats at Westminster, the British parliament, and sat in their own assembly, the Dáil, instead.
[hist-ire3-s2.1.3] The IRB (Irish Republican Brotherhood) was a secret society. The IRA (Irish Republican Army) was the Volunteers' fighting force, owing allegiance to the Dáil, so it claimed to be the army of an elected government.
[hist-ire3-s2.2.b] Sinn Féin was a small party before 1916. In 1917 a run of by-elections (elections held to fill a single vacant seat) showed that separatists, who wanted a complete break with Britain, could beat the IPP.
[hist-ire3-s2.2.0] Arthur Griffith founded the first Sinn Féin in 1905. It stood for economic self-reliance, abstention from Westminster and a dual monarchy of Britain and Ireland on the Austro-Hungarian model, which is very different from the republic the party wanted by 1917.
[hist-ire3-s2.2.1] Count Plunkett, father of the executed Joseph Plunkett, won the North Roscommon by-election in February 1917 as an independent separatist and then abstained from Westminster. His win showed that the rebels' cause had public support.
[hist-ire3-s2.2.2] Joseph McGuinness, a prisoner in Lewes, won South Longford in May 1917 by 37 votes, helped by the slogan 'Put him in to get him out'. This showed that a prisoner could be a successful candidate.
[hist-ire3-s2.2.3] De Valera won East Clare in July 1917 by a large majority, and W.T. Cosgrave won Kilkenny City in August 1917, so the successes were repeated in different parts of the country.
[hist-ire3-s2.2.4] Together the four results showed that separatists could win seats from the IPP and that voters would back men who were in jail or had fought in 1916.
[hist-ire3-s2.3.b] A martyr and a new constitution turned a set of election victories into a movement. The Ard Fheis (the party's annual conference) of October 1917 gave it a leader and a united aim.
[hist-ire3-s2.3.0] Thomas Ashe, one of the 1916 leaders, died on 25 September 1917 after being forcibly fed during a hunger strike in Mountjoy prison. His funeral drew huge crowds and became a rallying point for the movement.
[hist-ire3-s2.3.1] Michael Collins spoke at the graveside, which marked his rise among the republicans.
[hist-ire3-s2.3.2] At the Sinn Féin Ard Fheis of 25-26 October 1917 de Valera was elected president, with Griffith standing aside. De Valera also became president of the Volunteers that month, so political and military leadership was joined in one man.
[hist-ire3-s2.3.3] The new constitution was a compromise: the aim was an independent Irish republic, and once that was achieved the people could choose their own form of government. This allowed republicans and Griffith's followers, who wanted a monarchy, to stay in one party.
[hist-ire3-s2.3.4] Sinn Féin now drew in ex-Volunteers, the IRB, Gaelic Leaguers (supporters of the Irish-language revival) and many younger priests, and its clubs spread through the country. The IPP, by contrast, seemed discredited.
[hist-ire3-s2.4.b] Conscription (compulsory military service) united nearly all Irish nationalist opinion against the government and gave Sinn Féin credit for leading the resistance.
[hist-ire3-s2.4.0] The German spring offensive began in March 1918 and Britain was desperately short of men, so the Military Service Act of 18 April 1918 allowed conscription in Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s2.4.1] An all-party anti-conscription conference met at the Mansion House in Dublin on 18 April, with Dillon, de Valera, Griffith, William O'Brien, Tim Healy and Labour representatives. The Catholic bishops, meeting at Maynooth, backed resistance, which gave it moral weight.
[hist-ire3-s2.4.2] A national pledge to resist conscription was signed at church doors on 21 April, and on 23 April a one-day general strike closed most of the country outside Belfast.
[hist-ire3-s2.4.3] The government never applied conscription, but it replaced the Viceroy with Lord French in May 1918. It also arrested about 70 Sinn Féin leaders on the claim of a 'German Plot' (a supposed plot with Germany), citing the landing of Joseph Dowling from a German submarine as its pretext.
[hist-ire3-s2.4.4] De Valera, Griffith, Count Plunkett and Markievicz were among those held in Britain, while Collins and Harry Boland avoided arrest.
[hist-ire3-s2.4.5] The arrests removed the older leaders and strengthened the radicals. Sinn Féin won by-elections in the summer, and Redmond's party, weakened by Redmond's death on 6 March 1918, lost ground.
[hist-ire3-s2.5.b] The first election after the end of the war was fought on a franchise (the right to vote) that roughly tripled the electorate, and it transformed the Irish party system.
[hist-ire3-s2.5.0] The Representation of the People Act 1918 gave the vote to all men over 21 and women over 30, and the Irish electorate rose from about 700,000 to about 1.9 million.
[hist-ire3-s2.5.1] The election was held on 14 December 1918 and counted on 28 December. Sinn Féin won 73 of Ireland's 105 seats, the Unionists won 26 and the Irish Parliamentary Party won only six.
[hist-ire3-s2.5.2] Sinn Féin took about 47 per cent of the votes, and 25 of its seats were won without a contest (uncontested, because no rival stood).
[hist-ire3-s2.5.3] The IPP was almost wiped out, but Joe Devlin held a Belfast seat.
[hist-ire3-s2.5.4] Unionists won most of the seats in the north-east, so Belfast and Ulster supplied the evidence for partition.
[hist-ire3-s2.5.5] Constance Markievicz won St Patrick's, Dublin, while in Holloway prison, and became the first woman elected to the House of Commons. She did not take her seat, in line with the policy of abstention.
[hist-ire3-s2.5.6] De Valera was elected for East Clare and East Mayo, and many successful Sinn Féin candidates were in prison. This meant the Sinn Féin benches in the Dáil would be thin, so few could attend its first meeting.
[hist-ire3-s2.5.7] Sinn Féin had promised to abstain and set up an Irish assembly, so the result gave it a mandate for that. It showed a vote for a republic and against the IPP, but with only about 47 per cent of votes, not every voter backed Sinn Féin.
[hist-ire3-s2.6.b] Sinn Féin kept its election promise to set up its own parliament instead of going to Westminster. The Dáil (the Irish assembly) then began to behave like a government.
[hist-ire3-s2.6.0] The First Dáil met at the Mansion House, Dublin, on 21 January 1919, but only 27 of the elected deputies attended because many were in jail.
[hist-ire3-s2.6.1] It adopted three documents: a Declaration of Independence, a Message to the Free Nations of the World and the Democratic Programme. The Programme put the nation's claim to its resources and the right of every citizen to a living above private property.
[hist-ire3-s2.6.2] De Valera escaped from Lincoln Gaol on 3 February 1919 with help from Collins and Boland, and was elected Príomh Aire (head of the Dáil ministry) on 1 April 1919.
[hist-ire3-s2.6.3] The ministry included Griffith (Home Affairs), Collins (Finance), Cathal Brugha (Defence), Count Plunkett (Foreign Affairs) and Markievicz (Labour).
[hist-ire3-s2.6.4] Remember that the First Dáil and the shooting at Soloheadbeg both took place on 21 January 1919, but they were separate events.
[hist-ire3-s2.7.b] A counter-state is a rival government that takes over the work of the official one. Between 1919 and 1921 the Dáil built one alongside Dublin Castle, which is what made its claim to sovereignty real.
[hist-ire3-s2.7.0] The Dáil Loan (the National Loan), organised by Collins from 1919, raised over £350,000 in Ireland and more than 5 million dollars in the United States. These are two distinct sums, so do not add them up or confuse them.
[hist-ire3-s2.7.1] Sinn Féin won most of the councils in the 1920 local elections, and local authorities then pledged loyalty to the Dáil, which gave the Dáil a base of local power.
[hist-ire3-s2.7.2] The Dáil set up arbitration courts in 1919, which developed into Dáil courts from mid-1920, and republican police replaced the RIC in many areas.
[hist-ire3-s2.7.3] The British banned the Dáil in September 1919, and in 1920 many of the republican ministers worked in hiding.
[hist-ire3-s2.7.4] From August 1920 the Volunteers swore an oath of allegiance to the Dáil, which tied the army to the elected government. The name IRA was in general use during the war.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.b] The IRA waged a guerrilla war (small-scale surprise attacks rather than open battles) on the RIC and the army. A few thousand active men tied down many times their number.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.0] The first shots came at Soloheadbeg, Co. Tipperary, on 21 January 1919, when Seán Treacy, Dan Breen and others killed two RIC constables guarding gelignite (an explosive). The Dáil had not ordered the attack. It is often seen as the start of the war.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.1] The RIC withdrew from many isolated barracks in 1919-20, because they were hard to defend, and the IRA burned the empty buildings and attacked others. The IRA burned the empty buildings and attacked others.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.2] Active service units, known as flying columns, ambushed patrols and barracks and then dispersed into the countryside. Tom Barry's West Cork column was the best known.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.b] Collins understood that in a war against a much larger force, information mattered more than numbers. Three deaths in 1920 also moved opinion against the British.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.0] Collins was Director of Intelligence and used moles (informers inside the enemy's own offices) in Dublin Castle and the police, such as Ned Broy, a Dublin Castle clerk, to get information.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.1] His Squad, formed in 1919, killed detectives of the DMP's G Division, the political branch of the Dublin police, and other agents.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.2] Tomás Mac Curtain, Lord Mayor of Cork, was murdered by police in March 1920, and Terence MacSwiney, his successor, died on hunger strike in Brixton prison on 25 October 1920.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.3] Kevin Barry, an 18-year-old medical student, was hanged on 1 November 1920 for his part in an ambush that killed three soldiers. His death, like the deaths of MacSwiney and Mac Curtain, affected opinion.
[hist-ire3-s2.10.b] The war reached its most violent point in the winter of 1920-21. Then in May 1921 the IRA suffered a heavy loss.
[hist-ire3-s2.10.0] On Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920, the Squad killed 14 men, mostly British officers and intelligence agents, in Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s2.10.1] That afternoon Auxiliaries and RIC opened fire on the crowd at a football match at Croke Park, killing 14 civilians, including a player, Michael Hogan.
[hist-ire3-s2.10.2] At Kilmichael, Co. Cork, on 28 November 1920, Tom Barry's column killed 17 Auxiliaries and lost three men. This was the same week as Croke Park, but it was an IRA ambush of Auxiliaries, not a British killing of civilians.
[hist-ire3-s2.10.3] In May 1921 the IRA burned the Custom House in Dublin but lost five men killed and about 80 captured, a heavy blow to the Dublin Brigade.
[hist-ire3-s2.10.4] About 2,000 people were killed in the war, including soldiers, police, IRA men and civilians, though estimates vary.
[hist-ire3-s2.11.b] Britain reinforced the police and the army, but its harsher methods turned opinion against it instead of beating the IRA.
[hist-ire3-s2.11.0] The Black and Tans were ex-soldiers recruited as temporary RIC constables. Because of a shortage of police uniforms they wore a mix of khaki and dark green, which gave them their nickname; they began arriving in March 1920 and about 10,000 served.
[hist-ire3-s2.11.1] The Auxiliary Division of the RIC (ADRIC), about 2,000 ex-officers, was formed in July 1920. Unlike the Tans, it operated in companies with considerable independence. This is why they were a separate force from the Tans.
[hist-ire3-s2.11.2] The Restoration of Order in Ireland Act (August 1920) allowed courts-martial (military trials) for civilians, and martial law (rule by the army) was declared in several southern counties from December 1920.
[hist-ire3-s2.11.3] Reprisals (revenge attacks on civilians) included the sacking of Balbriggan in September 1920 and the burning of Cork city centre on the night of 11-12 December 1920, after an IRA ambush at Dillon's Cross. Auxiliaries and Black and Tans were blamed.
[hist-ire3-s2.11.4] The Labour Party, the press and many Church leaders in Britain criticised the reprisals, and the Liberal and Labour leaders called for talks. International opinion also turned against them.
[hist-ire3-s2.12.b] By mid-1921 politics, not just fighting, was pushing both sides towards talks. The Truce followed within weeks of the first formal invitation.
[hist-ire3-s2.12.0] The Government of Ireland Act of 1920 set up a Northern parliament (and a Southern one). The elections of May 1921 confirmed Sinn Féin's hold on the south and unionist control in the north-east.
[hist-ire3-s2.12.1] In June 1921 George V opened the Northern parliament and called for reconciliation, and Lloyd George, the British prime minister, invited de Valera to talks on 24 June.
[hist-ire3-s2.12.2] The Truce (a halt to the fighting) began on 11 July 1921.
[hist-ire3-s2.12.3] Britain went to the negotiating table for several reasons. Reprisals damaged its reputation, the IRA could not be crushed without a much larger force, dominion premiers (leaders of self-governing countries of the Empire) and American opinion urged a settlement, and the cost of the war was rising.
[hist-ire3-s2.12.4] De Valera spent June 1919 to December 1920 in the United States raising money and seeking recognition for the republic.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.b] Collins was the organiser of the IRA's intelligence war and of the republican finances. He was a participant in the Rising and the war and then a negotiator of the Treaty.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.0] Collins (1890-1922) was born near Clonakilty, Co. Cork. He worked in London in the post office and then in a financial firm from 1906 to 1916, and joined the IRB there.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.1] He fought in the GPO in 1916 as aide to Joseph Plunkett and was interned at Frongoch, where he built a network of future activists.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.2] After his release he rose in the Volunteers, the IRB and Sinn Féin. He organised the National Aid funds (support for the families of those killed or jailed) and spoke at Ashe's funeral.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.3] He was Minister for Finance from April 1919, organised the Dáil Loan, and as Director of Intelligence ran the Squad and the spy network. This is why money, as well as guns, was central to his role.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.4] He escaped capture throughout the war and was the most wanted man in Ireland. This made him a symbol of the IRA's survival.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.5] After the Truce he went on to negotiate the Treaty, so he was both a soldier and a negotiator.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.6] Contemporaries: republicans admired him as the man who won the war, while British officials regarded him as a ruthless organiser of killing. Churchill and others came to respect him in London.
[hist-ire3-s2.13.7] Historians: Tim Pat Coogan (1990) portrayed a hero of the revolution, while Peter Hart (2005) gave a more critical account of the killings in which he was involved.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.b] De Valera held the movement together as the leader of Sinn Féin and the Dáil before the Treaty talks. This part covers only the years 1917-1921.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.0] De Valera (1882-1975) was born in New York and raised in Bruree, Co. Limerick. He was a maths teacher who joined the Volunteers in 1913.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.1] He commanded the garrison at Boland's Mill in 1916, and his death sentence was commuted, partly because he was born in America, and most of the other leaders were shot.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.2] He was released in June 1917 under the amnesty, won East Clare in July, and became president of Sinn Féin and of the Volunteers in October 1917.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.3] He was arrested in the German Plot in May 1918, escaped from Lincoln on 3 February 1919, and was elected head of the Dáil as Príomh Aire in April 1919.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.4] He was in America from June 1919 to December 1920, where his disputes with the Irish-American leaders John Devoy and Judge Cohalan limited his success.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.5] After his return he took the lead in the talks with Lloyd George in the summer of 1921.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.6] Contemporaries: supporters saw him as the leader who held the movement together, while critics said he was rigid and out of touch with the IRA's campaign.
[hist-ire3-s2.14.7] Historians: Tim Pat Coogan (1993) and Diarmaid Ferriter (Judging Dev, 2007) debate whether he was a pragmatist (willing to compromise) or an ideologue (bound by principle).
[hist-ire3-s2.15.b] This part covers Markievicz after her release in 1917, as a Sinn Féin politician and Dáil minister. Prison and hiding shaped much of her career in these years.
[hist-ire3-s2.15.0] After her release in June 1917 under the amnesty she joined Sinn Féin and the Volunteers' public campaign, and she was elected to the executive.
[hist-ire3-s2.15.1] She was arrested in the German Plot of May 1918 and held in Holloway, where she was elected in December 1918, and she was released in March 1919.
[hist-ire3-s2.15.2] She was Minister for Labour in the Dáil ministry from April 1919 to January 1922, the only woman in the cabinet and one of the first women in Europe to hold cabinet rank.
[hist-ire3-s2.15.3] In September 1920 she was arrested again and sentenced to two years' hard labour; she was released in July 1921, after the Truce.
[hist-ire3-s2.15.4] Her work as minister was limited because she was in jail or on the run for much of the time.
[hist-ire3-s2.15.5] She later opposed the Treaty in the Dáil vote in January 1922.
[hist-ire3-s2.15.6] Contemporaries admired her bravery but some Sinn Féin colleagues distrusted her socialism and her flamboyance.
[hist-ire3-s2.15.7] Historians now emphasise her importance as a pioneer of women's participation in revolutionary politics.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.b] Judgements depend on whether the 1918 election, the Dáil and the IRA are credited with winning the settlement or blamed for its costs. Examiners reward answers that weigh both sides.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.0] Achievement: the 1918 result gave Sinn Féin a democratic mandate, and the counter-state showed that Irish people could run their own affairs.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.1] Achievement: the IRA campaign made Ireland costly and hard to govern and, with Dáil publicity abroad, brought Britain to talks.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.2] Limit: the IRA never controlled much territory, and it was small, short of arms and under strain by mid-1921.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.3] Limit: the election was won on about 47 per cent of the votes, with many seats uncontested. It was a vote for a republic or against the IPP, not an approval of every IRA act, and many voters wanted an end to Home Rule rather than war.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.4] Limit: the war also involved violence and the intimidation of informers.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.5] Contemporary view: nationalists saw a legitimate government defending itself, while the British government at first treated the IRA as a criminal conspiracy before changing course.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.6] Later view: historians debate how far the war was supported by the majority and how far it involved intimidation of informers and loyalists, as in Peter Hart's work on Cork.
[hist-ire3-s2.16.7] The dual strategy of Dáil and IRA left the movement with two sources of authority, which left it open to the split over the Treaty in 1922.

CANDIDATES hist-ire3-s3: 3. Partition and Northern Ireland under the Unionist Party, 1912–1949
[hist-ire3-s3.0.b] Partition (the division of Ireland into Northern Ireland and Southern Ireland) came about because unionists would not accept Home Rule (self-government for Ireland within the United Kingdom), British governments failed to find an all-Ireland settlement, and the 1918 vote showed a divided island.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.0] Unionist resistance came first: the Ulster Covenant (signed 28 September 1912) and the Ulster Volunteer Force, or UVF (an armed unionist militia formed in January 1913), showed that Home Rule for all Ireland could be imposed only by force.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.1] Exclusion debates followed, all assuming that some Ulster counties would be left out of Home Rule. These were the county option (Asquith's proposal of March 1914, letting Ulster counties opt out for six years), the failed Buckingham Palace Conference (July 1914) and Lloyd George's 1916 proposals.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.2] The 1916 plan collapsed over whether exclusion would be permanent or temporary: nationalists were told it would be temporary, while unionists were told it would be permanent. Unionist and Conservative members of the cabinet, including Lansdowne and Long, then insisted on permanence.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.3] The First World War and the 1916 Rising changed nationalist politics, and the Irish Convention of 1917–18 failed to produce agreement. Sinn Féin, the rising republican party, boycotted it.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.4] The 1918 general election (December) confirmed the divide, because Sinn Féin won 73 seats, the Irish Parliamentary Party 6, and unionists 26, mostly in the north-east.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.5] The six counties were chosen, not all nine of Ulster. Only Antrim, Down, Armagh and Londonderry had unionist majorities, Tyrone and Fermanagh had narrow nationalist majorities, and Donegal, Cavan and Monaghan were left out. Six counties gave a safer unionist majority than nine would have.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.6] Northern Ireland had about 1.25 million people (1926 census), of whom about two-thirds were Protestant and about one-third Catholic. This one-third minority is why the new state's treatment of Catholics mattered so much later.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.7] British policy made partition the practical answer. Lloyd George's coalition, with a large Conservative element, was unwilling to coerce Ulster, so the Government of Ireland Act of 1920 gave the unionists the offer of their own parliament.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.b] The Act of 1920 created two Home Rule parliaments, but only the northern one worked. The Treaty then let Northern Ireland stay out of the Free State, and the Boundary Commission failed to move the border.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.0] The Government of Ireland Act (royal assent December 1920) set up a parliament for the six counties of Northern Ireland and one for the 26 counties of Southern Ireland. A Council of Ireland was meant to link the two, but it never worked.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.1] In the Northern election of 24 May 1921 the Unionists won 40 of 52 seats. Sinn Féin and the Nationalists won six each but refused to take part, an abstention (staying away from the parliament).
[hist-ire3-s3.1.2] Craig became Prime Minister on 7 June 1921, and George V opened the Northern parliament in Belfast on 22 June 1921, which gave the new state royal approval.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.3] Southern Ireland's parliament never worked, because Sinn Féin won 124 of 128 seats in the same election (the other four went to Dublin University unionists) and treated it as an election to the Dáil, the republican assembly.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.4] Under the Treaty (the 1921 agreement creating the Irish Free State) Northern Ireland could opt out of the Free State. It did so on 7 December 1922, the day after the Free State came into being on 6 December.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.5] The Treaty also provided for a Boundary Commission (set up 1924) to review the border. Richard Feetham, a South African judge, was chair, with Eoin MacNeill for the Free State and J.R. Fisher for Northern Ireland, appointed by Britain because Craig refused to nominate.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.6] The commission's expected report was leaked to the Morning Post in November 1925, and MacNeill resigned. On 3 December 1925 the three governments (British, Free State and Northern Ireland) agreed in the Tripartite Agreement that the border would stay as it was.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.7] The Tripartite Agreement also released the Free State from its share of the imperial (UK) debt and abolished the powers of the Council of Ireland. So the border was fixed, and the Council had no further role.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.b] The new state was born amid sectarian rioting and an IRA campaign. The government answered with a Protestant-recruited police force and exceptional powers that stayed in place long after the crisis.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.0] Expulsions of Catholic workers, and of some Protestant labour supporters, from the Belfast shipyards began in July 1920 amid IRA violence and unionist anger. Thousands of people were affected.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.1] Riots in Derry in June 1920 and in Belfast through 1920–22 killed about 500 people, most of them Catholics, and drove many families from their homes.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.2] The Dáil organised a boycott of Belfast goods in 1920. It hurt Catholic workers and shopkeepers in Belfast more than it hurt unionist business.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.3] The Ulster Special Constabulary, formed in late 1920, had full-time A Specials, part-time B Specials and reserve C Specials. It was recruited almost entirely from Protestants.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.4] The Royal Ulster Constabulary (RUC) replaced the older Royal Irish Constabulary (RIC) in June 1922. A third of its places were notionally reserved for Catholics, but Catholics never filled them.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.5] The Craig–Collins pacts of January and March 1922 failed. After the IRA offensive on the border in May 1922 the government interned republicans (imprisoned them without trial).
[hist-ire3-s3.2.6] The Civil Authorities (Special Powers) Act of April 1922 allowed internment, curfews, the banning of meetings and organisations, and arrest without warrant. It was renewed each year and made permanent in 1933.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.7] Some of the interned men were held on the prison ship Argenta in 1922.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.b] The Ulster Unionist Party governed without interruption from 1921. It also changed the electoral rules in ways that kept it in power, while nationalists took little part in the system.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.0] Nationalists abstained from Stormont (the Northern Ireland parliament, named after its later building) at first. The Nationalist Party under Joe Devlin took its seats in 1925, but Sinn Féin remained abstentionist.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.1] In 1922 Tyrone and Fermanagh county councils, which refused to recognise the Belfast government, were dissolved and replaced by commissioners (officials appointed by the government), as were other nationalist-controlled councils.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.2] The Local Government Act of 1922 abolished proportional representation, or PR (a voting system that gives parties seats in proportion to their votes), in local elections, and boundaries were redrawn.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.3] PR for Stormont elections was abolished in 1929 and replaced by single-member constituencies (one winner per area). This helped the Unionists and hurt Labour and independents.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.4] Derry Corporation stayed under unionist control from 1923 to 1969 although Catholics were a majority of the city's population. This is the best-known case of gerrymandering, drawing electoral boundaries to favour one side.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.5] The Unionist Party won every Stormont election from 1921 to 1969 by large margins. A bloc vote on the border issue kept Protestant workers within the party.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.6] Stormont Parliament Buildings, on the outskirts of Belfast, were opened by the Prince of Wales in 1932. Before then the parliament had sat in the Presbyterian Assembly's College in Belfast, so the new building was a symbol of the permanence of the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.b] Discrimination means unequal treatment of Catholics in jobs, housing, policing and local government. Nationalists said that the state practised it, while unionists denied it and argued that Catholics refused to take part in the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.0] Catholics were about a third of the population but held a small share of senior posts, for example in the civil service, the judiciary and local government.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.1] The shipyards and heavy industry employed few Catholics. Protestant workers' networks and the Orange Order (a Protestant fraternal society) helped to secure jobs for their own members, which kept the pattern in place.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.2] Local councils controlled by unionists allocated public housing, so nationalists argued that houses went to Protestant families first.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.3] In speeches in the 1930s (in 1933 and 1934) Basil Brooke urged unionists not to employ Catholics. His remarks are widely quoted as evidence of the attitudes of leading unionists.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.4] On 24 April 1934 Craig told the Stormont parliament, in reply to claims that the South was a Catholic state, that he boasted of “a Protestant Parliament and a Protestant State”. It is the best-known statement of the attitudes of the unionist government.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.5] Historians disagree. Michael Farrell, in The Orange State (1976), sees systematic discrimination, while others accept discrimination in some fields but stress that it was uneven and not centrally planned.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.6] Unionists defended the system by pointing to nationalist non-participation. Abstention in the early years and the Catholic Church's separate schools left the minority with a limited voice in the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.b] Northern Ireland began with high-value industries that depended on export markets. The post-war slump and then the Depression hit them hard, so poverty and unemployment shaped the state's first two decades.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.0] Shipbuilding at Harland and Wolff and linen were the two main industries. Both fell back after the post-war boom ended in 1920–21, because they depended on export markets.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.1] Unemployment was about 20 per cent for much of the 1920s and above 25 per cent in the early 1930s, among the highest in the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.2] Agriculture remained important because small farms could still sell goods, such as cattle and eggs, to Britain.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.3] The government followed a policy of ‘step by step’ with Britain, keeping welfare benefits such as pensions and unemployment pay in line with British levels. This was financed by the Imperial Contribution (Northern Ireland's payment towards UK-wide costs).
[hist-ire3-s3.5.4] Housing in the slums of Belfast was poor and overcrowded because the government built little until the late 1930s.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.5] In October 1932 unemployed Catholic and Protestant workers joined together in the Outdoor Relief strike and riots in Belfast against the harsh relief rules (the payments to the unemployed). Police shot dead two people, and relief rates were increased.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.b] Partition broke up an economy that had been built on all-Ireland trade, and it weakened both parts. It also left a lasting political issue in the border.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.0] Customs controls on the border from 1923 interrupted the flow of goods and cattle. The Economic War (1932–38) between the Free State and Britain then made smuggling common.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.1] Belfast's linen, shipbuilding and engineering had sold to a UK and world market, so they lost the southern market they had counted on.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.2] The South lost most of the island's large-scale industry, which was in the north-east, so it was left with an economy dependent on agriculture.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.3] Railways were also split by the border. The Great Northern Railway, for example, crossed it.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.4] Society divided as well. Catholics in the North were cut off from the southern state's institutions, while the Southern Protestant minority declined and had a smaller place in public life.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.5] Politically the border became the main theme of Northern nationalism and of southern foreign policy. Articles 2 and 3 of the 1937 Constitution claimed the whole island.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.b] Craig organised unionist resistance to Home Rule and became the first Prime Minister of Northern Ireland. He is judged both as the builder of a stable state and as the leader of a sectarian one.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.0] Craig (1871–1940) was born in Belfast, the son of a wealthy whiskey distiller, and worked as a stockbroker. He served in the Boer War and became MP for East Down in 1906.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.1] Before partition he organised the UVF and the Larne gun-running of April 1914 with Carson (the unionist leader). From 1916 to 1921 he held junior ministerial posts in the British government, the last as Parliamentary and Financial Secretary to the Admiralty (1920–21).
[hist-ire3-s3.7.2] He succeeded Carson as Unionist leader in February 1921 and became Prime Minister of Northern Ireland in June 1921, serving until his death on 24 November 1940. He was made Viscount Craigavon in 1927.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.3] He met Collins in January and March 1922 (the pacts) and negotiated with Lloyd George, but insisted that the border was settled. He also refused to nominate a member to the Boundary Commission.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.4] His government created the RUC and the Special Powers Act and abolished PR, and he told Stormont in 1934 that it was a Protestant parliament and state. Critics therefore see him as a sectarian figure. His government also kept welfare in line with Britain's step by step.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.5] Events shaped him: the violence of 1920–22, the Boundary Commission and the Depression all shaped him. His defenders say he held the state together against IRA violence and economic crisis.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.6] Contemporaries: unionists revered him as the founder of their state, while nationalists considered him responsible for their exclusion and for sectarian rule.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.7] Historians: Patrick Buckland's biography James Craig (1980) portrays a cautious, conservative leader who made no attempt to win Catholic consent. Others likewise criticise the lack of any attempt to win minority consent.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.b] Bates was the minister who ran the machinery of control for more than 20 years. His name is tied to the security system and the electoral changes.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.0] Richard Dawson Bates (1876–1949) was a Belfast solicitor. He was secretary of the Ulster Unionist Council (the governing body of the unionist movement) from 1906 to 1921 and helped to organise the UVF.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.1] He was Minister of Home Affairs from 1921 to 1943, which made him responsible for the RUC, the Special Constabulary, the Special Powers Act, local government and elections.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.2] He introduced the Special Powers Act and the Local Government Act of 1922, which abolished PR for local elections, and he was the minister responsible for the 1929 change for Stormont elections.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.3] He used his powers to ban republican parades and organisations, to arrest and intern suspects and to censor some publications.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.4] Events shaped him: the security crisis of 1921–22 created the powers that he then used for over twenty years. He left office in 1943 in ill health.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.5] Contemporaries: nationalists blamed his department for the system of control, and many unionists praised his efficiency.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.6] Historians: Patrick Buckland, in The Factory of Grievances: Devolved Government in Northern Ireland, 1921–39 (1979), describes how his department's decisions, even if cautious, left a legacy of grievances.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.b] Judgements depend on whether the state is measured by survival and stability or by its treatment of the minority. Good answers set paired points against each other and reach a verdict.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.0] Achievement: it survived the violence of 1920–22 and the Depression, and secured the position of Northern Ireland within the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.1] Achievement: through its step-by-step parity policy it maintained British standards in education, health and welfare.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.2] Limit: it relied on emergency powers, a largely Protestant police and manipulated electoral rules, so the minority never accepted the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.3] Limit: a one-party government with a permanent majority did not develop real opposition, and its economic and housing policy was timid.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.4] Contemporary view: unionists regarded the state as a bulwark against a Catholic all-Ireland state, while nationalists saw a Protestant state.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.5] Later view: historians disagree on how far the system was planned discrimination, but accept that its legacy shaped the Troubles.
