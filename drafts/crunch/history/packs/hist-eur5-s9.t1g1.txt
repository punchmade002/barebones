TARGET hist-eur5-s9: 9. Case study: race relations in France in the 1980s
[hist-eur5-s9.0.b] By the early 1980s the children of North African and West African migrants lived in the banlieues, where unemployment and policing were the main grievances.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.0] The banlieues were the working-class suburbs and high-rise estates around French cities, many built in the 1960s to house migrant families and urban workers.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.1] Les Minguettes, an estate in Vénissieux near Lyon, was one of the best-known banlieues, and it became the scene of unrest in 1981.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.2] The second generation, known as ‘beurs’ (a verlan term for Arabs), had been born or raised in France and had French citizenship or a right to it.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.3] Youth unemployment in the banlieues was far above the national average, and many young people were in low-skilled work or outside the labour market.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.4] Relations between young people and the police were poor, and identity checks and stop-and-search practices were a frequent complaint in the banlieues.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.5] The oil shock of 1973 ended the post-war economic boom, and migrant workers in the factories were among the first to lose their jobs.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.6] Many banlieue families lived in HLM estates, public housing built from the 1950s and 1960s that had become rundown by the 1980s.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.7] By the 1982 census about 800,000 Algerians lived in France, making them the largest foreign nationality, ahead of Portuguese and Moroccans.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.0] In the summer of 1981 young men in the Minguettes estate stole and burned cars in what the press called 'rodéos' and clashed with police, in an episode that had no single cause and drew national television coverage.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.1] The unrest spread to other banlieues in Lyon and elsewhere, and it drew national attention to the living conditions of second-generation migrants.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.2] The Socialist government of François Mitterrand, elected in May 1981, responded with social programmes for the banlieues and promised reforms to immigration law.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.3] The government created Priority Education Zones (ZEP) in schools in 1981 and the 'Banlieues 89' regeneration project in 1983, which were criticised as too limited to address the causes of disadvantage.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.4] The Dubedout report of 1983 recommended local regeneration and greater involvement of residents in their neighbourhoods, and it led to local development programmes.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.5] Mitterrand's government regularised the status of about 130,000 undocumented migrants in 1981-82, and a law of 1984 created a ten-year residence card.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.6] Mitterrand had promised local voting rights for foreign residents and spoke of a 'right to be different', but the voting-rights promise was never carried out.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.0] The March for Equality and against Racism, which the press called the Marche des Beurs, set out from Marseille on 15 October 1983 with about 30 people, after Toumi Djaïdja, a young man from the Minguettes, was shot and wounded by a police officer in June 1983; the priest Christian Delorme and the pastor Jean Costil helped organise it.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.1] The march grew as it crossed France, reached Paris on 3 December 1983 with tens of thousands of supporters, and a delegation was received by President Mitterrand, who promised the ten-year residence card.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.2] The march's demands included equal treatment in jobs, housing and policing, and the right of second-generation migrants to be treated as French citizens.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.3] The march was organised with local associations and churches, and it showed that second-generation migrants could organise a national political campaign.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.0] SOS Racisme was founded in Paris in 1984, launched in October, by young activists including Harlem Désir and Julien Dray, and it quickly grew into a national organisation with local branches.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.1] Its slogan, ‘Touche pas à mon pote’ (Hands off my pal), was printed on badges worn widely by young people, and it expressed a message of solidarity across ethnic lines.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.2] SOS Racisme was supported by the Socialist government and by celebrities and trade unions, and it organised concerts and campaigns against racism in schools and workplaces.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.3] Critics on the left argued that SOS Racisme focused on individual racism and did not address the social causes of disadvantage in the banlieues.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.4] A free concert of SOS Racisme in the Place de la Concorde on 15 June 1985 drew hundreds of thousands of young people.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.5] The Left's critics, including some of the 1983 marchers, accused the Socialists of using SOS Racisme to take over the anti-racist movement.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.0] The Front National (FN) was founded in October 1972 by Jean-Marie Le Pen, a former paratrooper who had served in Algeria, with the backing of far-right groups including Ordre Nouveau, and it drew on supporters of the French Algeria cause.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.1] Its policies included the restriction of immigration, the expulsion of illegal migrants and the preference for French citizens in jobs and housing, known as ‘national preference’.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.2] The FN's breakthrough came in the Dreux municipal by-election of September 1983, where its list led by Jean-Pierre Stirbois won about 17 per cent in the first round and then joined a right-wing list that took the town, ending the taboo against allying with the FN.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.3] In the 1984 European elections the FN won about 11 per cent of the vote and sent 10 deputies to the European Parliament.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.4] In the 1986 parliamentary elections, held under a proportional system, the FN won 35 seats in the National Assembly.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.5] The FN's support was strongest in the south and east of France, in areas with high unemployment and large migrant communities, such as Marseille and Nice.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.6] In the 1988 presidential election Jean-Marie Le Pen won about 14.4 per cent of the first-round vote, over four million votes, while under the return to two-round constituency voting the FN won only one seat in the 1988 parliamentary election.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.7] Le Pen was elected as the youngest deputy in 1956 as a Poujadist, and in September 1987 he called the Nazi gas chambers a 'detail' of the history of the Second World War, a remark that brought him a conviction and wide condemnation.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.0] The Chirac government of 1986–88, with Charles Pasqua as interior minister, passed laws that tightened immigration controls, permitted identity checks and made deportation easier.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.1] In 1986 the Chirac government proposed ending the automatic acquisition of French nationality at 18 by young people born in France to foreign parents, who would have to make a formal declaration; after mass protests, including from SOS Racisme, it dropped the plan in 1987 and set up the Long commission, and the Méhaignerie law of 1993 later introduced the declaration.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.2] The Pasqua law of September 1986 made expulsion of illegal immigrants easier and required visas for most foreigners, and in October 1986 a charter flight deported about 100 Malians.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.3] The Socialist government returned to power in 1988 and the Joxe law of 1989 eased some of the 1986 restrictions; the Rocard government created the Haut Conseil à l'intégration in December 1989 and favoured the word integration over assimilation.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.4] In December 1986 the death of a student, Malik Oussekine, beaten by riot police during protests in Paris, caused outrage and led Chirac to drop an education bill.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.0] In October 1989 the head of a collège in Creil, north of Paris, excluded three Muslim girls for wearing headscarves in class, which became a national controversy.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.1] The Minister of Education, Lionel Jospin, referred the question to the Conseil d'État, which ruled on 27 November 1989 that headscarves were allowed unless they were ostentatious or used for propaganda.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.2] The affair showed the tension between laïcité and Muslim identity in schools; it was debated in the press and by politicians of all parties, and the Conseil d'État's approach lasted until the law of 2004 banned conspicuous religious symbols in state schools.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.3] The Jospin circular of December 1989 told schools to accept headscarves unless they were ostentatious or used for proselytising.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.0] Algerian-origin families formed the largest group among the second generation, and the Algerian war, which ended in 1962, shaped how many French people saw North African migrants.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.1] Many Algerian migrants and their children were linked by family and history to the war, while the harkis and their families remained a sensitive issue in French politics.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.2] The FN drew support from some former settlers and army veterans of the Algerian war, and its rhetoric echoed arguments about French identity from the colonial period.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.3] The government's policies on immigration in the 1980s were shaped by the memory of decolonisation, including the Évian Accords and the departure of between 800,000 and a million settlers in 1962.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.4] Pieds-noirs settled in large numbers in southern France, a region where the FN later won much of its support, though the party's voters were not drawn only from former settlers.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.5] Under the 'double jus soli' rule of the nationality code, children born in France to parents born in Algeria before 1962 were French from birth, which was one target of the right's proposed reforms.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.0] Like Britain, France had racial violence in urban areas in the early 1980s and a far-right party that gained votes from anti-immigration feelings.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.1] Unlike Britain, France had no race relations act and did not record ethnic statistics, which made the measurement of discrimination more difficult.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.2] France's assimilation model, which treated citizens as individuals, meant that anti-discrimination arguments were framed in terms of universal rights rather than ethnic groups.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.3] Britain's Scarman Report of 1981 and the Race Relations Acts offered a framework for responding to racial disadvantage that France lacked in the 1980s.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.4] Britain's Commonwealth migrants had the vote from arrival, whereas in France non-citizen residents did not, which made the unfulfilled promise of local voting rights a live grievance.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.5] Both countries saw confrontations between police and young people in the early 1980s, but Britain answered with a public inquiry, the Scarman Report, while France's response was through urban regeneration programmes.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.0] Achievement: the second generation organised national movements such as the 1983 march and SOS Racisme, and they gained a public voice.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.1] Limit: the government's responses in the 1980s focused on policing and immigration control, and social disadvantage in the banlieues remained a source of unrest.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.2] Achievement: the Conseil d'État's 1989 headscarf ruling set a legal framework for religious expression in schools that lasted until the 2004 law.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.3] Limit: the FN's electoral success showed that anti-immigrant politics could gain mainstream attention, and mainstream parties adopted some of its themes.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.4] Contemporary view: supporters of assimilation saw the banlieue unrest as a failure of integration, while activists saw it as the result of racism and social exclusion.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.5] Later view: historians stress that the banlieues were shaped by housing policy, deindustrialisation and the colonial past, not by immigration alone.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.6] Contemporary government view: Socialist ministers presented integration, rather than assimilation, as the aim in 1989, while Le Pen's supporters said immigration was a threat to French identity.

CANDIDATES hist-eur5-s8: 8. Immigration patterns and policies in Britain and France; multi-racial Britain
[hist-eur5-s8.0.b] Britain's post-war labour shortage and the Commonwealth citizenship laws brought large numbers of migrants from the Caribbean and South Asia.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.0] The British Nationality Act of 1948 gave citizens of Commonwealth countries the right to enter and settle in Britain as British subjects.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.1] The SS Empire Windrush docked at Tilbury on 22 June 1948 with about 1,000 passengers, most of them from Jamaica and other Caribbean islands, and it became a symbol of this migration.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.2] Post-war labour shortages led Britain to recruit workers for the National Health Service, London Transport and the postal service, many of them from the Caribbean.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.3] Migration from India and Pakistan grew in the 1950s, with many migrants from Punjab and Gujarat settling in Birmingham, Leicester, Bradford and London.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.4] Migration peaked in 1961–62 as migrants rushed to enter before new restrictions took effect; the migrant population was concentrated in a few cities, especially London, the West Midlands and Yorkshire.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.5] The Commonwealth citizenship status of most migrants meant they had the right to vote and to live in Britain, but they faced discrimination in housing and jobs.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.6] Caribbean migrants met a colour bar in lodgings; in Notting Hill the landlord Peter Rachman became notorious for overcrowded, exploitative lettings to newcomers.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.0] In August and September 1958 white youths attacked West Indian residents in the Notting Hill and Nottingham areas, in the worst racial violence in post-war Britain up to that time.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.1] The disturbances were caused by overcrowded housing, competition for jobs and housing, and the activities of far-right groups in the area, such as Oswald Mosley's Union Movement and the White Defence League.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.2] The Trinidadian journalist Claudia Jones organised an indoor carnival in London in January 1959, which became a precursor of the Notting Hill Carnival.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.3] Kelso Cochrane, an Antiguan-born carpenter, was murdered in a racist attack in Notting Hill in May 1959, and his death increased pressure for a legal response.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.0] The Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962 introduced employment vouchers, which restricted the entry of Commonwealth citizens without jobs in Britain.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.1] In 1968 a new Commonwealth Immigrants Act was rushed through Parliament in a few days to limit the entry of Kenyan Asians holding British passports, who faced discrimination and expulsion in Kenya.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.2] Enoch Powell, a Conservative MP, warned in a speech to a Conservative meeting in Birmingham on 20 April 1968, known as the 'rivers of blood' speech, that continued immigration would lead to violence, and Edward Heath dismissed him from the Shadow Cabinet; London dockers marched in his support.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.3] The Immigration Act of 1971 introduced the concept of patriality, which gave the right of abode to those with a parent or grandparent born in Britain.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.4] The National Front, founded in 1967, campaigned for an end to immigration, stood candidates in the general elections of 1974 and 1979 and never won a parliamentary seat; the Battle of Lewisham (August 1977) and the death of the teacher Blair Peach at an anti-NF protest in Southall in April 1979 marked its confrontations with opponents.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.5] President Idi Amin gave Asians in Uganda 90 days to leave in August 1972; about 27,000 of them, many holding British passports, settled in Britain, helped by a government resettlement board.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.6] The Anti-Nazi League and Rock Against Racism, founded in 1976-77, organised counter-campaigns against the National Front.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.0] The Race Relations Act of 1965 made discrimination on grounds of colour, race or ethnic origin illegal in public places, and it created a Race Relations Board.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.1] The Race Relations Act of 1968 extended the ban on discrimination to housing and employment, and it created the Community Relations Commission.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.2] The Race Relations Act of 1976 extended the law to indirect discrimination and created the Commission for Racial Equality.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.3] The laws were enforced through complaints and conciliation, and critics argued that their effectiveness was limited by the difficulty of proving discrimination in practice.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.4] Home Secretary Roy Jenkins said in 1966 that integration should not mean assimilation but equal opportunity together with cultural diversity, which set the direction of British policy.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.0] Police stop-and-search powers under the ‘sus’ laws (the Vagrancy Act of 1824) were used heavily against young black men, which many black residents saw as harassment.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.1] The Brixton riots of 10-12 April 1981 followed Operation Swamp 81, in which police stopped and searched nearly a thousand people, mostly young black men, in a week; the riots caused widespread damage and injured hundreds.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.2] Riots broke out in Toxteth in Liverpool and Moss Side in Manchester in July 1981, as well as in other cities; police used CS gas on the British mainland for the first time in Toxteth.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.3] The Scarman Report of November 1981 identified racial disadvantage, unemployment and poor relations between police and the community as causes of the riots; it found no institutional racism in the police but accepted that individual officers had acted improperly.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.4] The Scarman Report recommended more accountable policing and community consultation, and it led to the Police and Criminal Evidence Act of 1984; the 'sus' law had already been repealed in 1981.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.b] By the 1980s Britain had large Caribbean, Indian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi communities whose culture changed British life in many areas.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.0] Caribbean communities shaped British music, including reggae, ska and calypso, and Caribbean food and festivals became part of urban life.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.1] Notting Hill Carnival began as a street festival in 1966 and became one of the largest street festivals in Europe.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.2] Muslims from South Asia built mosques in the 1960s and 1970s, and Sikh and Hindu communities built gurdwaras and temples in Birmingham, Leicester and London.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.3] Britain's policy of multiculturalism, supported by local councils and the Race Relations Acts, encouraged the recognition of distinct cultures within a common framework of laws.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.4] The Rushdie affair of 1988-89 showed the tensions between religious identity and freedom of expression: copies of The Satanic Verses were burned in Bradford in January 1989, and Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa against Salman Rushdie in February 1989.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.5] Black and Asian candidates entered Parliament: Diane Abbott, Paul Boateng, Bernie Grant and Keith Vaz were elected in 1987, and the Rampton Report (1981) and the Swann Report (1985) argued for schools to recognise the needs of ethnic minority children.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.0] Algerians held French nationality until Algerian independence in 1962, and they could move freely between Algeria and France, so Algerian labour migration to mainland France was not legally immigration before that date.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.1] Post-war reconstruction created labour shortages, and migrants from Portugal, Spain, Italy, Morocco, Tunisia and West Africa filled jobs in factories, mines and construction.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.2] Many migrants lived in bidonvilles, makeshift shanty settlements on the edges of French cities, such as Nanterre near Paris, where living conditions were poor.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.3] Algerian migration continued after 1962, and it brought the Algerian war's legacy into French politics; the harkis who came to France faced hostility and poor housing.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.4] In July 1974 the French government suspended labour immigration, and in 1976 it allowed family reunification for migrants who had been living in France.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.5] Migrants from North and West Africa formed the second generation, the ‘beurs’, who grew up in the banlieues and by the 1980s were a focus of French debate about integration.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.6] The Franco-Algerian accord of 27 December 1968 gave Algerian nationals preferential rights to enter and work in France.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.7] The harkis and their families were housed in camps known as hameaux de forestage in France, and some camps were still in use in the mid-1970s.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.8] The French government set up the National Immigration Office in 1945, and Sonacotra, founded in 1956, built hostels for Algerian workers.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.9] On 17 October 1961 Paris police under Maurice Papon attacked a demonstration by Algerians against a curfew, killing dozens or more; the number remains disputed.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.10] After 1977 the government offered payments to migrants who agreed to go home, with little effect.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.0] The French model of integration was based on universal citizenship: all citizens were equal before the law, and the state did not recognise ethnic or religious communities as such.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.1] Assimilation meant that migrants were expected to adopt the French language, values and civic practices, and to keep their ethnic identities in private.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.2] The law on the separation of church and state of 1905 established laïcité, a principle of state secularism that shaped how public religious expression was regulated.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.3] France did not collect statistics on ethnic origin, which meant that discrimination was harder to measure and debate than in Britain.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.4] The Pleven law of 1972 was France's main anti-racist legislation, and it made incitement to racial hatred a criminal offence.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.0] Similarity: both states restricted immigration in the 1970s, both had far-right parties that gained support by the late 1970s and 1980s, and both had racial violence in urban areas.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.1] Difference: most migrants to Britain were Commonwealth citizens with a legal right of entry, while migrants to France came from southern Europe as well as North and West Africa, and North African migrants had a complex legal status.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.2] Difference: Britain developed a policy of multiculturalism that recognised ethnic groups, while France used the assimilation model that recognised individuals as citizens.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.3] Difference: the colonial histories differed, since Britain's migrants came largely from its former colonies in the Caribbean and South Asia, and France's came from its former North African and West African colonies.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.0] Achievement: Britain's anti-discrimination laws gave migrants a legal basis for challenging discrimination in jobs, housing and public services.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.1] Limit: the laws did not end disadvantage, and the 1981 riots showed that unemployment and police practices remained sources of conflict.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.2] Achievement: France's universal citizenship gave second-generation migrants a legal claim to French nationality, and many took part fully in French public life.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.3] Limit: the assimilation model made it difficult to recognise ethnic discrimination, and the headscarf question and the banlieues showed the tensions it created.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.4] Contemporary view: many politicians in both countries saw migration mainly as an economic and security problem, while migrant communities often saw discrimination as the main issue.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.5] Later view: scholars contrast Britain's race-relations model, which counted ethnic groups and tackled discrimination through the law, with France's republican model, which counted no one by origin; each has been criticised for failing to end disadvantage.

CANDIDATES hist-eur5-s4: 4. Africa and the “winds of change”: Algeria and de Gaulle
[hist-eur5-s4.0.b] From 1957 British and French policy in Africa shifted towards rapid independence, and Algeria became the hardest case.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.0] Ghana (the former Gold Coast) became independent on 6 March 1957, the first sub-Saharan African colony to do so.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.1] Guinea voted against joining the French Community in the referendum of September 1958 and became independent on 2 October 1958, the first French colony in Africa to leave the community.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.2] Harold Macmillan told the South African Parliament in Cape Town on 3 February 1960 that a ‘wind of change’ was blowing through Africa.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.3] Seventeen African states gained independence in 1960, a year that became known as the Year of Africa.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.4] France's neighbours Tunisia and Morocco became independent in March 1956, and Britain chose negotiated transfers of power in most of its colonies, while France's policy in Algeria was contested by about a million settlers and an armed nationalist movement.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.5] The Loi-cadre of June 1956, introduced by the minister Gaston Defferre, gave the African territories elected assemblies and governments with wider powers, as an early step towards self-government.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.6] De Gaulle toured Africa in August 1958 and at Brazzaville on 24 August offered the territories a choice between the Community and independence; the Brazzaville Conference of 1944 had earlier promised reforms but ruled out self-government.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.7] In 1960 the Community broke up in practice: most French West and Equatorial African territories, along with Madagascar, became independent by negotiation and signed co-operation accords with France on defence, currency and aid.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.0] France conquered Algeria from 1830, and by the 1848 constitution Algeria was divided into three départements that were treated as part of metropolitan France.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.1] The settler population, known as pieds-noirs, numbered about one million by 1954 out of about nine million inhabitants, and dominated the economy, land and local administration.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.2] Most Muslim Algerians were subjects rather than full citizens; the Statute of 1947 created an Algerian Assembly with two colleges of equal size, one elected by about a million Europeans and one by about nine million Muslims, which favoured the settlers.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.3] Messali Hadj's Movement for the Triumph of Democratic Liberties (MTLD), founded in 1946, campaigned for independence, and it split in 1954 between moderates and the armed nationalists.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.4] The French government's policy was assimilation: the French state aimed to make Algerians French in language, law and culture, a goal few Muslim Algerians accepted in practice.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.5] The Sétif and Guelma uprising of 8 May 1945 killed about a hundred Europeans and was met by French repression that killed many thousands of Algerians; official French figures say about 1,500 and Algerian nationalists claim 45,000, and historians generally accept a figure in between.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.6] Muslim Algerians who wanted full French citizenship had to give up their Islamic personal status, a condition that few chose to meet before 1954.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.0] The National Liberation Front (FLN) launched a rising on 1 November 1954 with attacks on police and military targets across Algeria.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.1] The French government sent conscripts in large numbers, reaching about 400,000 troops at the peak, and the army was given wide powers to restore order in the cities and countryside.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.2] The FLN held the Soummam Valley Congress in August 1956, which created its political leadership and confirmed the primacy of the armed struggle.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.3] The Morice Line, a barrier of wire, mines and electrified fencing along the Tunisian frontier from 1957, was built to stop arms and fighters crossing the border.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.4] The Battle of Algiers of 1957 saw French paratroopers under General Jacques Massu break the FLN's urban network in the city through arrests, interrogation and torture.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.5] The use of torture by French forces was reported in Henri Alleg's book La Question, which was banned in France in 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.6] The FLN used terrorism against civilians, including bombings in Algiers, and it killed Algerians who opposed it; the French forces also killed civilians in reprisal operations.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.7] The French army moved large numbers of rural Algerians, probably more than a million, into fenced regroupement camps from 1955 and especially after 1957, as part of its counter-insurgency strategy.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.8] French military deaths numbered about 25,000; estimates of Algerian deaths range from about 300,000 to the Algerian official figure of 1.5 million, and historians still debate the figures.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.9] Support for the FLN abroad grew: Tunisia and Morocco sheltered its fighters, Egypt supplied arms, and the French bombing of the Tunisian village of Sakiet in February 1958 damaged France's reputation with the US and UN.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.b] A revolt by settlers and army officers in Algiers brought Charles de Gaulle back to power.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.0] On 13 May 1958 settlers and army officers seized government buildings in Algiers and demanded that Algeria stay French, and they formed a Committee of Public Safety.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.1] The Fourth Republic's government under Pierre Pflimlin lost the confidence of the army, President Coty asked de Gaulle to form a government, and the National Assembly invested him on 1 June 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.2] A new constitution was approved by referendum on 28 September 1958, establishing the Fifth Republic with a strong presidency.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.3] The constitution set up the French Community, which gave the colonies internal self-government, but kept foreign affairs, defence and currency with France.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.4] De Gaulle was elected president in December 1958 by an electoral college, and he took office in January 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.5] Between 1946 and 1958 the Fourth Republic had some two dozen governments, which made it hard to sustain a consistent policy on Algeria.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.6] Speaking to the crowd in Algiers on 4 June 1958 de Gaulle said 'Je vous ai compris' (I have understood you), and at Mostaganem on 6 June he cried 'Vive l'Algérie française', which led the settlers to believe he would keep Algeria French.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.0] On 16 September 1959 de Gaulle announced that Algerians would be offered self-determination, a choice between independence, integration with France and a form of association.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.1] In January 1960 settler militants staged the Barricades Week in Algiers, a revolt that de Gaulle put down with the army.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.2] The referendum of 8 January 1961 approved self-determination by about 75 per cent of votes cast overall, and by about 70 per cent in Algeria itself.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.3] In April 1961 a group of generals who opposed Algerian independence seized Algiers in the Algiers putsch, which failed after four days when conscripts and the army stayed loyal to de Gaulle.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.4] The Secret Army Organisation (OAS), formed in early 1961, carried out bombings and assassinations in Algeria and in France to stop independence, and it attacked de Gaulle himself.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.5] In a speech of 4 November 1960 de Gaulle spoke of an 'Algérie algérienne' and the prospect of an Algerian Republic, and an earlier set of talks with the FLN at Melun in June 1960 broke down.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.0] The Évian Accords were signed on 18 March 1962 after negotiations between the French government and the FLN, and a ceasefire took effect the next day.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.1] The Accords guaranteed the rights of the French minority, gave France continued use of military bases including Mers-el-Kébir for a time, and provided for French access to Saharan oil and gas.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.2] The Évian Accords were approved in a referendum in France on 8 April 1962 and in Algeria on 1 July 1962, with an overwhelming majority in favour.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.3] Algeria became independent on 5 July 1962, and Ahmed Ben Bella, after a struggle with rival nationalist leaders in the summer of 1962, became prime minister in September 1962 and president in 1963.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.4] The OAS was defeated in Algiers and in France, and its leaders were tried; General Salan, arrested in April 1962, received a life sentence, and a general amnesty in 1968 freed those still in prison.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.5] The OAS ambush of de Gaulle's car at Petit-Clamart on 22 August 1962 failed, and its organiser Jean Bastien-Thiry was executed in March 1963, the last person executed by firing squad in France for a political offence.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.6] On 26 March 1962 French troops fired on pro-French demonstrators in the rue d'Isly in Algiers, killing about 50 people, deepening the settlers' sense of betrayal.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.7] After independence Ben Bella was overthrown by his defence minister Houari Boumediene in June 1965; Algeria nationalised French oil and gas holdings in 1971, and France handed over the naval base of Mers-el-Kébir in 1968.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.0] About 800,000 settlers left Algeria in 1962, many within a few months and most for France, which had to absorb them into an already strained economy; nearly a million had gone by the mid-1960s.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.1] The harkis were Algerians who had served as auxiliaries in the French forces; the French government gave orders to prevent their organised transfer, and while about 90,000 reached France with their families, tens of thousands were killed in reprisals, with estimates ranging widely from about 30,000 to 150,000.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.2] Most harkis who reached France were placed in camps and forest hamlets; the question of recognition and compensation remained a political issue, and President Chirac paid national tribute to them in 2001 and President Macron asked their forgiveness in 2021.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.3] The departure of the settlers ended the French presence in Algerian agriculture and administration, and many farms were taken over by the new state.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.4] On 5 July 1962, the day of independence, violence against Europeans broke out in Oran, where hundreds were killed or went missing, and many settlers fled the city.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.b] De Gaulle led the Free French in the Second World War and then ended the Algerian war as president of the Fifth Republic.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.0] Born 22 November 1890 in Lille, he was a career army officer who became undersecretary for war in June 1940 and then, with his appeal of 18 June 1940 from London, leader of the Free French.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.1] He headed the provisional government from 1944 to January 1946, resigned, founded the Rally of the French People (RPF) in 1947 and withdrew from politics in the 1950s, returning to power in 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.2] As a participant in the 1958 crisis he was welcomed by the settlers, who believed he would keep Algeria French, but his 1959 policy changed course.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.3] Contemporaries split sharply: the settlers and the OAS saw him as a traitor, while many in France and Algeria saw him as the leader who ended a costly war.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.4] Some historians argue that de Gaulle always intended to end French rule in Algeria and moved slowly to avoid civil war, while others argue that he drifted from integration towards independence as the war's costs, the army's attitude and international pressure became clear; he moved from 'Je vous ai compris' in 1958 to self-determination in 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.5] He resigned as president in April 1969 and died on 9 November 1970.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.6] He used the Community and, after 1960, bilateral co-operation accords to keep French influence in sub-Saharan Africa, working through Jacques Foccart, his adviser on African affairs, and the Brazzaville Conference of 1944 that he had convened had promised only reform within the empire.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.0] Achievement: de Gaulle ended a war that the Fourth Republic had failed to win, and he gave Algerians the right of self-determination.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.1] Limit: the war cost many lives, caused the exodus of about a million people and divided the French army and political class.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.2] Achievement: the Fifth Republic's constitution gave France a stable government that could take the decision to withdraw.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.3] Limit: the French Community did not hold the colonies together, and most independent states of West and Equatorial Africa kept close ties with France through co-operation accords, with French troops stationed in several of them.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.4] Explanations for withdrawal: the cost and unpopularity of the Algerian war, the independence of Tunisia and Morocco in 1956, UN General Assembly debates on Algeria from 1955, American pressure and the growth of African nationalist parties.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.5] The British and French cases differed: Britain's transfers were mostly negotiated, while France fought a war in Algeria before accepting independence.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.6] Later view: the war divided the army, the political class and French society, and many judge that de Gaulle's greatest achievement was to end it without a collapse of the Republic.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.7] Contemporary view: settlers, the OAS and the generals of the 1961 putsch saw withdrawal as betrayal, while about 91 per cent of French voters approved the Évian Accords in April 1962.
