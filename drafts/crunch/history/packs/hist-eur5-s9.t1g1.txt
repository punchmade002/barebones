TARGET hist-eur5-s9: 9. Case study: race relations in France in the 1980s
[hist-eur5-s9.0.b] To follow race relations in 1980s France you need the ideas and the politics behind them. France expected everyone to become French in the same way, and the party in power changed twice in the decade.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.0] The republican ideal says citizens are individuals, equal before the law, so the state does not recognise ethnic or religious groups. This is why France expected migrants and their children to assimilate (adopt French language, values and civic culture) rather than keep a separate group identity.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.1] Laïcité (state secularism, based on the 1905 law separating Church and State) keeps religion out of public institutions such as state schools. It was at stake in the headscarf affair of 1989.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.2] Jus soli means nationality by birth in the territory. It matters here because French-born children of foreign parents could become French, and the Right tried to change how this worked in 1986-87.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.3] Racism means ranking people by race and treating them differently, shown in violence, discrimination and exclusion.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.4] Identity, here, is the question of how the second generation (French citizens of North African heritage) saw themselves and were seen. Cultural diversity means many cultures and religions in one society, which the banlieues and the second generation made visible.
[hist-eur5-s9.0.5] Politically, the Socialist François Mitterrand became President in May 1981 and was re-elected in 1988. The Right won the 1986 parliamentary elections, giving a Chirac government (1986-88) that shared power with a Socialist president, known as cohabitation, and Michel Rocard led the Left's government from 1988.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.b] By the early 1980s the children of North African and West African migrants lived in the banlieues, where unemployment and policing were the main grievances. Knowing who lived there, and why they were angry, explains the events that follow.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.0] The banlieues were the working-class suburbs and high-rise estates around French cities. Many were built from the 1950s and 1960s to house migrant families and urban workers, and by the 1980s they had become rundown.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.1] Many families lived in HLM estates (habitation à loyer modéré, public housing), so poor housing and the condition of the estates became part of the grievance.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.2] Les Minguettes, an estate in Vénissieux near Lyon, was one of the best-known banlieues, and it became the scene of unrest in 1981.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.3] The second generation, known as 'beurs' (verlan, a French slang that reverses syllables, from 'arabe'), had been born or raised in France and had French citizenship or a right to it. This is why their demand was to be treated as French.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.4] The oil shock of 1973 ended the post-war economic boom, and migrant workers in the factories were among the first to lose their jobs. Youth unemployment in the banlieues was therefore far above the national average, and many young people were in low-skilled work or outside the labour market.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.5] Relations between young people and the police were poor, and identity checks and stop-and-search practices were a constant complaint in the banlieues.
[hist-eur5-s9.1.6] At the 1982 census about 800,000 Algerians lived in France, making them the largest foreign nationality, ahead of Portuguese and then Moroccans. Post-war labour migration, including from the Maghreb and West Africa, and the halt to labour migration in 1974 explain how these communities came to settle.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.b] In summer 1981 anger in the banlieues erupted at Les Minguettes. The new Socialist government had to respond, and its measures helped some people but left the main grievances untouched.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.0] In summer 1981 young men at Les Minguettes stole and burned cars, in what the press called 'rodéos', and clashed with police, drawing national television coverage.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.1] The unrest spread to other banlieues in Lyon and elsewhere, and it drew national attention to the living conditions of second-generation migrants.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.2] The Socialist government of François Mitterrand, elected in May 1981, responded with social programmes for the banlieues and promised reforms to immigration law.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.3] The government created Priority Education Zones (ZEP, giving extra help to schools in deprived areas) in 1981 and launched the 'Banlieues 89' regeneration project in 1983. Both were limited in scale, so they did not remove the causes of disadvantage.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.4] The Dubedout report of 1983 (by Hubert Dubedout, 'Ensemble, refaire la ville') recommended local regeneration and more involvement of residents in their neighbourhoods. It led to the 'développement social des quartiers' programme, so residents' views began to shape local projects.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.5] The government regularised the status of about 130,000 undocumented migrants in 1981-82, so they could stay legally. A law of July 1984 created a ten-year residence card, which gave long-term security.
[hist-eur5-s9.2.6] Mitterrand's 1981 manifesto promised local voting rights for foreign residents, and he spoke of a 'right to be different' (droit à la différence). The voting-rights promise was never carried out, which left a lasting grievance.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.b] The second generation organised a national protest in 1983. A police shooting set it off, and the march showed that beurs could run a national campaign.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.0] The cause was the shooting and wounding of Toumi Djaïdja, a young man from Les Minguettes, by a police officer in June 1983. The priest Christian Delorme and the Protestant pastor Jean Costil helped organise the march.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.1] The march set out from Marseille on 15 October 1983 with about 30 people. It grew as it crossed France and reached Paris on 3 December 1983 with tens of thousands of supporters.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.2] A delegation was received at the Élysée (the presidential palace) by President Mitterrand, who promised the ten-year residence card. It was enacted in 1984.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.3] The demands were equal treatment in jobs, housing and policing, and the right of second-generation migrants to be treated as French citizens.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.4] The press called it the 'Marche des Beurs' because its core was second-generation young people, but its official title was the March for Equality and against Racism.
[hist-eur5-s9.3.5] It was organised with local associations and churches, and it showed that second-generation migrants could run a national political campaign.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.b] After the march, a new organisation turned anti-racism into a mass youth movement. It was popular and well backed, but critics doubted how much it changed.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.0] SOS Racisme was founded in Paris in 1984, launched in October, by young activists including Harlem Désir and Julien Dray. It quickly grew into a national organisation with local branches.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.1] Its slogan, 'Touche pas à mon pote' (Hands off my pal), appeared on a badge in the shape of a yellow open hand worn widely by young people. It expressed solidarity across ethnic lines.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.2] It was supported by the Socialist government, by celebrities and by trade unions, and it ran concerts and campaigns against racism in schools and workplaces.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.3] A free concert in the Place de la Concorde, Paris, on 15 June 1985 drew hundreds of thousands of young people, which shows how popular the movement had become.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.4] Critics on the left argued that SOS Racisme focused on individual racism and did not address the social causes of disadvantage in the banlieues, such as unemployment and housing.
[hist-eur5-s9.4.5] Some of the 1983 marchers accused the Socialists of using SOS Racisme to take over the anti-racist movement.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.b] The Front National (FN) was a far-right party that won votes by campaigning against immigration. Its breakthrough in 1983 made it a political force.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.0] The FN was founded on 5 October 1972 by Jean-Marie Le Pen, born in 1928, a former paratrooper who had served in Algeria. It had the backing of far-right groups including Ordre Nouveau, and it drew on supporters of Algérie française (keeping Algeria French).
[hist-eur5-s9.5.1] Its policies were to restrict immigration, expel illegal migrants and give 'national preference' (priority for French citizens) in jobs and housing.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.2] Its breakthrough came in the municipal election at Dreux (Eure-et-Loir) in September 1983, not a by-election. Jean-Pierre Stirbois's list won about 17 per cent in the first round on 4 September.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.3] In the second round on 11 September the FN joined a right-wing list that took the town. This ended the taboo on the mainstream Right allying with the FN.
[hist-eur5-s9.5.4] In the 1984 European elections the FN won about 11 per cent of the vote and 10 seats, which confirmed that Dreux was not a one-off.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.b] The FN's national success followed. How many seats it won depended on the voting system as much as on its share of the vote, which is a point examiners like.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.0] In the 1986 legislative elections, held under proportional representation (seats shared out according to each party's share of the vote), the FN won 35 seats in the National Assembly, with about 10 per cent of the vote.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.1] The FN's support was strongest in the south and east of France, in areas with high unemployment and large migrant communities, such as Marseille and Nice.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.2] In the April 1988 presidential election Le Pen won about 14.4 per cent of the first-round vote, about 4.4 million votes.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.3] In June 1988 France had returned to single-member two-round constituency voting, which rewards parties that can win a majority locally, so the FN won only one seat.
[hist-eur5-s9.6.4] Le Pen had been elected the youngest deputy in 1956, aged 27, for the Poujadist movement (a protest movement of small shopkeepers).
[hist-eur5-s9.6.5] In September 1987 Le Pen said on radio that the Nazi gas chambers were 'a detail' of the history of the Second World War. The remark was widely condemned.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.b] When the Right won in 1986, policy turned towards control. Protests forced the government to retreat on nationality and a student's death forced it to drop a bill, and the Left then changed course in 1988.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.0] The Chirac government of 1986-88, with Charles Pasqua as interior minister, passed laws that tightened immigration controls, permitted identity checks and made deportation easier.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.1] The Pasqua law of September 1986 made expulsion of illegal immigrants easier and required visas for most foreigners, so entering France became harder.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.2] On 25 October 1986 a charter flight deported about 100 Malians, an example of the tougher policy.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.3] In 1986 the government planned to end the automatic acquisition of French nationality at 18 by French-born children of foreign parents, who would instead have to make a formal declaration. This was a change to jus soli in practice.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.4] After mass protests, including from SOS Racisme, the plan was dropped in 1987 and a commission headed by Marceau Long was set up (1987-88). The Méhaignerie law of 1993 later introduced the declaration of will to acquire nationality.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.5] Malik Oussekine died on 6 December 1986 after being beaten by riot police (the 'voltigeurs') during student protests against the Devaquet university reform bill. The outrage led the government to withdraw the bill.
[hist-eur5-s9.7.6] When the Left returned in 1988, the Joxe law of 1989 (Pierre Joxe, interior minister) eased some of the 1986 restrictions. The Haut Conseil à l'intégration was created in December 1989, and the Rocard government preferred the word integration to assimilation.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.b] A dispute in one school became a national argument about what laïcité meant for Muslim pupils. The 1989 ruling and the later 2004 law show how the issue developed.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.0] In autumn 1989 the headmaster, Ernest Chenière, of the Collège Gabriel-Havez in Creil (Oise), north of Paris, excluded three Muslim girls for wearing headscarves in class. It became a national controversy.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.1] Education Minister Lionel Jospin asked the Conseil d'État (France's highest administrative court) for its opinion. On 27 November 1989 it ruled that headscarves were allowed unless ostentatious or used for pressure or propaganda.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.2] Jospin's circular of 12 December 1989 told schools to apply that ruling and to accept headscarves unless they were ostentatious or used for proselytising.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.3] The approach was case-by-case, not a ban, because each school judged whether a headscarf was ostentatious, and it lasted until the law of 15 March 2004 banned conspicuous religious symbols in state schools.
[hist-eur5-s9.8.4] The affair showed the tension between laïcité and Muslim identity in schools, and it was debated in the press and by politicians of all parties.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.b] The 1980s tensions were not only about the 1980s. Decolonisation and the Algerian war (1954-62), which ended in Algerian independence, shaped who the migrants were, how others saw them, and which groups supported the FN.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.0] Algerians had been French nationals before independence in 1962, and Algerian-origin families formed the largest group among the second generation. The Algerian war shaped how many French people saw North African migrants.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.1] Many Algerian migrants and their children were linked by family and history to the war, and the harkis (Algerians who served France in the war) and their families remained a sensitive issue.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.2] The Évian Accords (March 1962) led to independence, and between 800,000 and a million pieds-noirs (European settlers) left Algeria, mostly in 1962. Many settled in southern France, where the FN later won much of its support, though its voters were not only former settlers.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.3] The FN drew support from Algerian war veterans and supporters of Algérie française, and its rhetoric echoed arguments about French identity from the colonial period.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.4] Under the 'double jus soli' rule, a child born in France to a parent born in Algeria before independence was French at birth. This shows how the colonial past was written into the nationality rules.
[hist-eur5-s9.9.5] The government's immigration policies in the 1980s were shaped by the memory of decolonisation, so debates about migrants were never only about the present.
[hist-eur5-s9.10.b] Britain faced similar problems in the same years but answered them differently. The contrast comes from France's assimilationist model against Britain's multicultural framework.
[hist-eur5-s9.10.0] Similarities: both countries had urban unrest and confrontations between police and young people in the early 1980s, and both had a far-right party that gained votes from anti-immigrant feeling.
[hist-eur5-s9.10.1] France lacked the British framework of a series of Race Relations Acts (1965, 1968, 1976) and a public body like the Commission for Racial Equality. It did have the loi Pleven of 1972, which penalised racial incitement and discrimination.
[hist-eur5-s9.10.2] France did not record ethnic statistics, which made discrimination harder to measure, for example in jobs or housing.
[hist-eur5-s9.10.3] France's assimilation model treated citizens as individuals, so anti-discrimination arguments were framed in terms of universal rights rather than ethnic groups.
[hist-eur5-s9.10.4] Britain answered its unrest of 1981 (the Brixton riots) with a public inquiry, the Scarman Report of 1981, while France's response was urban regeneration programmes such as Banlieues 89.
[hist-eur5-s9.10.5] Britain's Commonwealth citizens had the vote, whereas non-citizen residents in France did not, which made the unfulfilled promise of local voting rights a live grievance.
[hist-eur5-s9.11.b] A good judgement weighs what the second generation achieved against what stayed unresolved. State your view early and keep to it.
[hist-eur5-s9.11.0] Achievement: the second generation found a national voice through the 1983 march and SOS Racisme, which made racism a public issue.
[hist-eur5-s9.11.1] Limit: government responses centred on policing, immigration control and limited regeneration, so disadvantage in the banlieues persisted and remained a source of unrest.
[hist-eur5-s9.11.2] Achievement: the Conseil d'État's 1989 ruling gave schools a legal framework for religious expression that lasted until 2004.
[hist-eur5-s9.11.3] Limit: the FN's success showed that anti-immigrant politics could win mainstream attention, and mainstream parties were pulled towards some of its themes.
[hist-eur5-s9.11.4] Contemporary views: assimilationists saw banlieue unrest as a failure of integration, while activists saw it as the result of racism and exclusion. Le Pen's supporters said immigration threatened French identity.
[hist-eur5-s9.11.5] Contemporary government view: Socialist ministers presented integration, rather than assimilation, as the aim in 1989.
[hist-eur5-s9.11.6] Later view: historians stress that the banlieues were shaped by housing policy, deindustrialisation and the colonial past, not by immigration alone.

CANDIDATES hist-eur5-s8: 8. Immigration patterns and policies in Britain and France; multi-racial Britain
[hist-eur5-s8.0.b] After 1945 both Britain and France needed workers and both ruled or had ruled overseas peoples, so people from the colonies moved to the old imperial centres. Four ideas run through this topic and are used in the same sense throughout.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.0] Britain after 1945 had labour shortages in reconstruction, the new National Health Service, transport and industry, and its empire was turning into a Commonwealth (a group of independent states linked to Britain), so many colonial subjects held British status.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.1] France also needed labour for reconstruction and the post-war boom. It had colonies and territories in North and West Africa, and Algeria was treated as part of France until independence in 1962.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.2] The Second World War had discredited racist ideas, because the Nazis had shown where they led. Even so, colour prejudice persisted in both societies, and this is the setting for the conflicts that follow.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.3] Racism means treating people as inferior or different because of their race or colour. It shows itself in prejudice (hostile attitudes), discrimination (unfair treatment in jobs or housing) and violence.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.4] Cultural diversity means many cultures, religions and languages living within one society, which is what migration produced in both countries.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.5] Identity is how migrants and their children saw themselves, often caught between the culture of their family's origin and the host society they lived in.
[hist-eur5-s8.0.6] Assimilation is the expectation that migrants adopt the majority culture, which was the French approach. Multiculturalism, or integration with diversity, accepts that distinct cultures can continue within a common framework, which became the British approach.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.b] Britain's post-war labour shortage and the Commonwealth citizenship laws brought large numbers of migrants from the Caribbean and South Asia. They came because there was work in Britain and because the law let them in.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.0] The British Nationality Act of 1948 made citizens of the UK and Colonies and of Commonwealth countries British subjects, with the right to enter and settle in Britain.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.1] The SS Empire Windrush docked at Tilbury on 22 June 1948 with about 1,000 passengers, most of them from Jamaica and other Caribbean islands, and it became the symbol of the start of Caribbean migration.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.2] Post-war labour shortages led Britain to recruit workers in the Caribbean in the 1950s, for example for the National Health Service (founded in 1948) and London Transport, and poor prospects at home made the offer attractive.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.3] Migration from India and Pakistan grew in the 1950s and later included Bangladesh, with many migrants from Punjab and Gujarat settling in Birmingham, Leicester, Bradford and London.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.4] Migrants were concentrated in a few big cities and industrial areas, especially London, the West Midlands and Yorkshire, where the jobs were.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.5] Most migrants held Commonwealth citizenship, so they had the right to vote and to live in Britain, but they still faced discrimination in housing and jobs.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.6] Caribbean migrants met a colour bar (a refusal to let rooms to black people) in lodgings. In Notting Hill the landlord Peter Rachman became notorious in the 1950s for overcrowded, exploitative lettings to newcomers.
[hist-eur5-s8.1.7] Migration rose sharply in 1961–62 because migrants rushed to enter Britain before the 1962 Act took effect and restricted entry.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.b] As migrants settled, hostility from some of the white population turned to violence in 1958. These were the first major riots about race in post-war Britain, and they pushed the question of racism into politics.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.0] In August 1958 disturbances broke out in Nottingham, and in late August and September 1958 white youths attacked West Indian residents in Notting Hill, in serious racial violence.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.1] The disturbances were caused by overcrowded housing and competition for jobs and housing, and by far-right agitation in the area, such as Oswald Mosley's Union Movement and the White Defence League.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.2] The Trinidad-born journalist Claudia Jones organised an indoor Caribbean carnival in London in January 1959, a response that celebrated Caribbean culture and became a precursor of the Notting Hill Carnival.
[hist-eur5-s8.2.3] Kelso Cochrane, an Antiguan-born carpenter, was murdered in a racist attack in Notting Hill in May 1959, and his death increased pressure for a legal response to racism.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.b] From 1962 governments began to restrict entry, and public debate treated migration as a problem. Extremist groups grew, and so did campaigns against them.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.0] The Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962 introduced employment vouchers, which restricted the entry of Commonwealth citizens who had no job arranged in Britain. The Irish were not covered because of the Common Travel Area.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.1] In February 1968 a new Commonwealth Immigrants Act was passed through Parliament in a few days to restrict the entry of Kenyan Asians holding British passports, who faced discrimination in Kenya.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.2] Enoch Powell, a Conservative MP, spoke at a Conservative meeting in Birmingham on 20 April 1968, warning that continued immigration would lead to violence. Edward Heath dismissed him from the Shadow Cabinet the next day, but some London dockers marched in his support.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.3] The speech is known as the 'rivers of blood' speech because it referred to the Tiber 'foaming with much blood', though Powell did not use the phrase itself.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.4] The Immigration Act of 1971 introduced patriality, the right of abode (the right to live in Britain freely) for those with a parent or grandparent born in Britain. It came into force in 1973.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.5] President Idi Amin ordered Asians out of Uganda in August 1972, giving them 90 days to leave. About 27,000 settled in Britain, many holding British passports, helped by the Ugandan Asian Resettlement Board.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.6] The National Front, founded in 1967, campaigned for an end to immigration but stood candidates in the general elections of 1974 and 1979 but never won a parliamentary seat. Its clashes with opponents included the Battle of Lewisham in August 1977 and the death of the teacher Blair Peach at an anti-NF protest in Southall in April 1979.
[hist-eur5-s8.3.7] Rock Against Racism (1976) and the Anti-Nazi League (1977) organised counter-campaigns against the National Front. Overall, migration was framed as a problem and entry was restricted, while the rights of those already settled were protected by law.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.b] While entry was being restricted, Parliament also made discrimination against people already living in Britain illegal. Each Act widened the ban and set up a body to promote it.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.0] The Race Relations Act of 1965 banned discrimination on grounds of colour, race or ethnic or national origins in public places, and it created the Race Relations Board.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.1] The Race Relations Act of 1968 extended the ban to housing, employment and services, which were the areas where migrants had faced the colour bar, and it created the Community Relations Commission.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.2] The Race Relations Act of 1976 extended the law to indirect discrimination (rules that look neutral but disadvantage one group) and created the Commission for Racial Equality.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.3] The laws were enforced through complaints and conciliation, and critics argued that they were limited because discrimination was difficult to prove in practice.
[hist-eur5-s8.4.4] Home Secretary Roy Jenkins said in 1966 that integration should mean equal opportunity accompanied by cultural diversity, not assimilation, and this set the direction of British policy.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.b] By the early 1980s many young black people faced unemployment, discrimination in jobs and housing, and tense relations with the police. In 1981 these tensions exploded in riots.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.0] Young black people suffered high unemployment in the early 1980s as well as discrimination in jobs and housing, which left many with little stake in society and fed resentment.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.1] Stop and search under the 'sus' laws (the Vagrancy Act of 1824, which let police stop people on suspicion) was used heavily against young black men, and many black residents saw it as harassment. The law was repealed in 1981.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.2] The Brixton riots of 10–12 April 1981 followed Operation Swamp 81, in which police stopped and searched nearly a thousand people in about a week, mostly young black men. Hundreds were injured.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.3] Riots broke out in Toxteth in Liverpool and Moss Side in Manchester in July 1981, as well as in other cities. Police used CS gas on the British mainland for the first time in Toxteth.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.4] The Scarman Report of November 1981 (by Lord Scarman) named racial disadvantage, unemployment and poor relations between police and community as the causes of the riots.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.5] Scarman rejected the idea that the police were institutionally racist (racist in their rules and culture), but accepted that individual officers had acted improperly.
[hist-eur5-s8.5.6] Scarman recommended community policing and consultation, so that police would work with local people. The Police and Criminal Evidence Act of 1984 reformed police powers, and the report was one influence on it among others.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.b] By the 1980s Britain had large Caribbean, Indian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi communities, and their culture changed British life in music, food, religion and politics. Their presence also caused tension, as the Rushdie affair showed.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.0] Caribbean communities shaped British music, including reggae, ska and calypso, and Caribbean food and festivals became part of urban life.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.1] Notting Hill Carnival began as a street festival in 1966 and grew into one of the largest street festivals in Europe.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.2] Muslims from South Asia built mosques in the 1960s and 1970s, and Sikh and Hindu communities built gurdwaras (Sikh places of worship) and temples in Birmingham, Leicester and London.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.3] Multiculturalism became British policy, supported by local councils and the Race Relations Acts, which encouraged recognition of distinct cultures within a common framework of laws.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.4] The Rampton Report (1981) and the Swann Report (1985) on the education of ethnic minority children argued that schools should recognise those children's needs.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.5] Black and Asian candidates entered Parliament: Diane Abbott, Paul Boateng, Bernie Grant and Keith Vaz were elected in 1987.
[hist-eur5-s8.6.6] The Rushdie affair of 1988–89 showed the tension between religious identity and freedom of expression. Copies of The Satanic Verses (1988) were burned in Bradford in January 1989, and Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa (a religious ruling) against Salman Rushdie in February 1989.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.b] France also recruited workers after 1945, but its migrants came from southern Europe and from its former colonies in North and West Africa. Many of them lived in very poor conditions, and the Algerian war cast a long shadow over them.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.0] Post-war reconstruction created labour shortages, and the state set up the National Immigration Office (ONI) in 1945 to recruit. Migrants came from Italy, Spain, Portugal, Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria and West Africa and worked in factories, mines and construction.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.1] Algerians had French status until independence in 1962 and could move freely between Algeria and France, so their movement to the mainland was not immigration in the legal sense before then.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.2] Many migrants lived in bidonvilles, shanty towns on the edges of French cities, such as Nanterre near Paris, in the 1950s and 1960s. Later migrants were housed on housing estates and in state-backed hostels for migrant workers.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.3] On 17 October 1961 Paris police under Maurice Papon attacked an Algerian demonstration against a curfew. Dozens at least were killed, and the number remains disputed.
[hist-eur5-s8.7.4] Algerian migration continued after 1962 and brought the legacy of the Algerian war into French politics. The harkis, Algerians who had served in the French army, came to France as refugees rather than as labour migrants and faced hostility and poor housing.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.b] Labour immigration was suspended in 1974, but migration did not end, because families joined those already in France. This produced a new generation that was born and raised in France.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.0] In July 1974 the French government suspended labour immigration, which stopped new recruitment of workers but not the arrival of families.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.1] In 1976 the government allowed family reunification for migrants who had been living in France, so wives and children joined them and migrants settled permanently.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.2] After 1977 the government offered payments to migrants who agreed to go home, but few accepted, with little effect.
[hist-eur5-s8.8.3] Migrants from North and West Africa raised a second generation, the 'beurs' (the French-born children of North African migrants), who grew up in the banlieues (the suburbs around big cities) and by the 1980s were a focus of French debate about integration.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.b] France treated migrants very differently from Britain. Its model rested on the idea that the state deals with individual citizens, not with ethnic or religious groups.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.0] The French model of integration was based on universal citizenship: all citizens were equal before the law, and the state did not recognise ethnic or religious communities as such.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.1] Assimilation meant that migrants were expected to adopt the French language, values and civic practices, and to keep their ethnic identities in private.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.2] The law separating church and state in 1905 established laïcité, state secularism, which shaped how public religious expression was regulated. Laïcité is secularism, not assimilation itself.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.3] France collected no statistics by ethnic origin, which meant that discrimination was harder to measure and debate than in Britain.
[hist-eur5-s8.9.4] The Pleven law of 1972 was France's main anti-racist legislation, and it made incitement to racial hatred a criminal offence.
[hist-eur5-s8.10.b] The two countries faced similar pressures but responded in different ways. A good comparison pairs a similarity or difference in one country with the matching fact from the other.
[hist-eur5-s8.10.0] Similarity: both states restricted immigration in the 1970s, Britain with the Acts of 1962, 1968 and 1971 and France with the halt of 1974.
[hist-eur5-s8.10.1] Similarity: both had far-right parties that gained support by the late 1970s and 1980s, both had racial violence in urban areas, and both saw discrimination and the concentration of migrants in poor housing.
[hist-eur5-s8.10.2] Difference: most migrants to Britain were Commonwealth citizens with a legal right of entry, while migrants to France came from southern Europe as well as North and West Africa, and Algerians had a distinct legal status until 1962.
[hist-eur5-s8.10.3] Difference: Britain developed multiculturalism, which recognised ethnic groups, while France used assimilation, which recognised individuals as citizens.
[hist-eur5-s8.10.4] Difference: Britain monitored ethnic groups, which let it measure discrimination, while France collected no ethnic statistics. Britain also passed three Race Relations Acts, while France's main anti-racist law was the Pleven law of 1972.
[hist-eur5-s8.10.5] Difference: Britain's migrants came largely from former colonies in the Caribbean and South Asia, and France's from its former North African and West African colonies.
[hist-eur5-s8.11.b] Both countries tried to integrate migrants, with real achievements and real limits. Historians judge each model by how far it ended disadvantage.
[hist-eur5-s8.11.0] Achievement: Britain's anti-discrimination laws gave migrants a legal basis for challenging discrimination in jobs, housing and public services.
[hist-eur5-s8.11.1] Limit: the laws did not end disadvantage, and the 1981 riots showed that unemployment and police practices remained sources of conflict.
[hist-eur5-s8.11.2] Achievement: France's universal citizenship gave second-generation migrants a legal claim to French nationality, and many took part fully in French public life.
[hist-eur5-s8.11.3] Limit: the assimilation model made it difficult to recognise ethnic discrimination, and the banlieues showed the tensions it created.
[hist-eur5-s8.11.4] Contemporary view: many politicians in both countries saw migration mainly as an economic and security problem, while migrant communities often saw discrimination as the main issue.
[hist-eur5-s8.11.5] Later view: scholars contrast Britain's race-relations model, which counted ethnic groups and tackled discrimination through the law, with France's republican model, which counted no one by origin. Each has been criticised for failing to end disadvantage.

CANDIDATES hist-eur5-s4: 4. Africa and the “winds of change”: Algeria and de Gaulle
[hist-eur5-s4.0.b] After the Second World War the old empires were harder to hold than before. Britain and France had to decide whether to fight for their colonies or let them go, and France's own recent history shaped its choices.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.0] Empires lost both the power and the will to continue: war-weariness at home, the weakness of Britain and France after the war, and hostility to empire from both the United States and the Soviet Union all made colonial rule harder to defend.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.1] The new United Nations gave colonial peoples a public place to raise their grievances, and nationalist movements (organised groups demanding self-rule) grew stronger, so every colony became a potential problem for its ruler.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.2] France was defeated in 1940 and the Vichy regime governed France afterwards, which damaged French prestige. De Gaulle's appeal of 18 June 1940, made from London, began the Free French movement that fought on.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.3] The Fourth Republic (1946–58) was unstable, with weak governments that rarely lasted long, so France found it hard to settle on a firm colonial policy.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.4] Britain's withdrawal from India in 1947 and France's defeat in Indochina in 1954 had already shown that empires could be ended, so nationalists in Africa could point to real examples.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.5] The French Union, created by the 1946 constitution, kept the empire under Paris. It was replaced in the 1958 constitution by the French Community, which gave territories internal self-government but left foreign affairs, defence and currency with France.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.b] Before the Algerian war ended, change had already begun elsewhere. Reform in French Africa and independence for Ghana and Guinea showed that independence was becoming possible.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.0] The Brazzaville Conference of January–February 1944, a Free French gathering, promised reforms for the empire but ruled out self-government, so it admitted that change was needed while keeping French rule intact.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.1] Tunisia and Morocco, France's neighbours to Algeria's east and west, became independent in March 1956. Their independence mattered to the Algerian war because both later sheltered FLN forces.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.2] The Loi-cadre (framework law) of June 1956, introduced by the minister Gaston Defferre, gave the French African territories elected assemblies and governments with wider powers, an early step towards self-government.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.3] Ghana (the former Gold Coast, a British colony) became independent on 6 March 1957 under Kwame Nkrumah, the first sub-Saharan colony to do so, which gave other African nationalists an example to point to.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.4] During an African tour de Gaulle spoke at Brazzaville on 24 August 1958 and offered the territories a choice between the Community and independence.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.5] Guinea, under Sékou Touré, rejected the 1958 constitution (and so the Community) in the referendum of 28 September 1958 and became independent on 2 October 1958, the first French colony in sub-Saharan Africa to leave. Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, Tunisia and Morocco had already left French rule.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.b] In 1960 independence came in a rush. A British prime minister gave the movement its best-known phrase, and France's Community broke up in practice.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.0] Harold Macmillan, the British prime minister, told the South African Parliament in Cape Town on 3 February 1960 that 'the wind of change is blowing through this continent'.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.1] Seventeen African states became independent in 1960, so it is known as the Year of Africa. Examples include the Ivory Coast, Nigeria and the Congo.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.2] Most French West and Equatorial African territories, along with Madagascar, became independent by negotiation in 1960 and signed co-operation accords with France on defence, currency and aid, so independence did not mean a clean break.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.3] In practice the French Community therefore broke up within two years of its creation, because its members chose independence instead of internal self-government.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.4] Britain chose negotiated transfers of power in most of its colonies. France's policy in Algeria was contested by about a million settlers and an armed nationalist movement, which made a negotiated settlement far harder.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.b] Algeria was not an ordinary colony in French eyes. It had been conquered long before, was treated as part of France itself, and was home to a large settler community, which is why giving it up was so painful.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.0] France began its conquest of Algeria in 1830. By the 1848 constitution Algeria was divided into three départements (administrative districts like those of mainland France) that were treated as part of metropolitan France.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.1] The settlers, known as pieds-noirs, numbered about one million out of about nine million people by 1954. They dominated the economy, land and local administration, so they had much to lose from any change.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.2] The French aim was assimilation: making colonial subjects French in language, law and culture. Few Muslim Algerians accepted this in practice, and most never won full equality.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.3] Most Muslim Algerians were subjects rather than full citizens. Those who wanted full French citizenship had to give up their Islamic personal status (Muslim family law), a condition few chose to meet before 1954.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.4] The Sétif and Guelma uprising of 8 May 1945 killed about 100 Europeans. French repression killed thousands of Algerians: the official French figure is about 1,500, Algerian nationalists claim 45,000, and historians generally accept a figure in between.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.5] Messali Hadj founded the Movement for the Triumph of Democratic Liberties (MTLD) in 1946 to campaign for independence. It split in 1954 between moderates and the armed nationalists.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.6] The Statute of 1947 created an Algerian Assembly with two colleges (voting groups) of equal size. Europeans, with a small number of Muslims, elected the first and the Muslim majority the second, which favoured the settlers because far fewer people elected as many members.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.b] On 1 November 1954 a new nationalist movement chose armed force. France answered with its army, and by 1957 the war was being fought in the cities, the countryside and along the borders.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.0] The National Liberation Front (FLN) launched a rising on 1 November 1954 with attacks on police and military targets across Algeria, which began the Algerian war.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.1] The French government sent conscripts (young men called up for compulsory service), reaching about 400,000 troops at the peak. Under Guy Mollet's government in 1956 the Special Powers law gave the army wide powers.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.2] The FLN held the Soummam Valley Congress in August 1956, which set up its political leadership, so the movement had an organised direction beyond its fighting units.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.3] The Morice Line, built from 1957, was a barrier of wire, mines and fencing along the Tunisian border, intended to stop arms and fighters crossing from Tunisia, where the FLN found shelter.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.4] In the Battle of Algiers (January to October 1957) French paratroopers under General Jacques Massu broke the FLN's network in the city through arrests, interrogation and torture. It was a military success won by methods that damaged France's reputation.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.b] As the war grew, both sides used methods that shocked observers. How the war was fought turned it into a political problem for France at home and abroad.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.0] French forces used torture. Henri Alleg's La Question, an account of his own torture, was banned in France in 1958, but as a first-hand account it brought the issue before French opinion.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.1] Terrorism means violence against civilians to spread fear and win a political aim. The FLN used it, with bombings in Algiers cafés from September 1956 onward, and it also killed Algerian rivals and Muslim opponents.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.2] French forces also killed civilians in reprisal operations (revenge attacks), so civilians suffered at the hands of both sides.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.3] From 1955, and especially after 1957, the French army moved more than a million rural Algerians into fenced regroupement camps as part of its counter-insurgency strategy (the effort to defeat a guerrilla rising).
[hist-eur5-s4.5.4] French military deaths numbered about 25,000. Estimates of Algerian deaths range from about 300,000 to the Algerian official figure of 1.5 million, so historians still debate the figures.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.5] Support for the FLN abroad grew. Tunisia and Morocco sheltered its fighters, Egypt supplied arms, and the UN General Assembly debated Algeria from 1955, so the war was never only a French matter.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.6] On 8 February 1958 French aircraft raided the Tunisian village of Sakiet, which damaged France's reputation with the US and the UN.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.b] By 1958 the Fourth Republic could neither win the war nor settle on a policy. A revolt by settlers and army officers in Algiers gave Charles de Gaulle his chance to return to power.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.0] Between 1946 and 1958 the Fourth Republic had more than twenty governments, which made it hard to sustain a consistent policy on Algeria and helped bring about its fall.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.1] On 13 May 1958 settlers and army officers seized government buildings in Algiers and demanded that Algeria stay French. They formed a Committee of Public Safety.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.2] The government of Pierre Pflimlin, the last prime minister of the Fourth Republic, lost the confidence of the army, and President René Coty then called on de Gaulle to form a government.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.3] The National Assembly invested de Gaulle on 1 June 1958 as prime minister, not president, because he became president only in January 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.4] Speaking to the crowd in Algiers on 4 June 1958 de Gaulle said 'Je vous ai compris' (I have understood you). At Mostaganem on 6 June he cried 'Vive l'Algérie française'.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.5] These words led the settlers to believe he would keep Algeria French, which is why they later felt betrayed when he changed course.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.b] De Gaulle's first task was to rebuild the state. The new constitution gave France the strong government the Fourth Republic had lacked, and it set out a new relationship with the colonies.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.0] A new constitution was approved by referendum on 28 September 1958, establishing the Fifth Republic with a strong presidency, which gave France stable government after the Fourth Republic's constant changes of government.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.1] The constitution also set up the French Community, which gave the colonies internal self-government but kept foreign affairs, defence and currency with France.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.2] De Gaulle was elected president in December 1958 (on 21 December) by an electoral college, a limited group of electors rather than a popular vote, and he took office on 8 January 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.b] Once in office de Gaulle moved step by step away from the settlers' hopes. Each step provoked a violent reaction from those who wanted Algeria to stay French.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.0] On 16 September 1959 de Gaulle announced that Algerians would be offered self-determination (the right to decide their own future), with a choice among independence, close association with France and full integration.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.1] Settler militants staged Barricades Week in Algiers from 24 January to 1 February 1960, a revolt against this policy that de Gaulle put down with the army.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.2] Talks with the FLN at Melun in June 1960 failed, so the war went on.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.3] On 4 November 1960 de Gaulle spoke of an Algerian Republic in the future, a clear sign that independence was now a real prospect.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.4] In the referendum of 8 January 1961 about 75 per cent of votes cast overall approved self-determination, and about 70 per cent in Algeria itself, which gave de Gaulle a clear mandate (authority from the voters).
[hist-eur5-s4.8.5] In the Algiers putsch (a seizure of power by force) of 21–26 April 1961, four generals who opposed independence, Challe, Salan, Jouhaud and Zeller, took Algiers. It collapsed in about four days because conscripts and most of the army stayed loyal to de Gaulle.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.6] The Secret Army Organisation (OAS), formed in early 1961 by settlers set on stopping independence, carried out bombings and assassinations in Algeria and in France, and it attacked de Gaulle himself.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.b] After years of war France and the FLN finally negotiated a settlement. Independence came in stages, which is why several dates are given for it.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.0] The Évian Accords were signed on 18 March 1962 after negotiations between the French government and the FLN, and a ceasefire took effect on 19 March.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.1] The Accords guaranteed the rights of the French minority (the settlers), kept the Mers-el-Kébir naval base for France for a time, and gave France access to Saharan oil and gas.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.2] The Accords were approved in a referendum in France on 8 April 1962 (about 91 per cent yes) and in Algeria on 1 July 1962 (an overwhelming yes), so signing them was not the end of the process.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.3] Algeria became independent after the 1 July vote: France recognised it on 3 July, and 5 July is the date celebrated as independence.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.4] After a struggle with rival nationalist leaders in the summer of 1962, Ahmed Ben Bella became prime minister in September 1962 and president in 1963.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.b] The OAS tried to wreck the settlement by violence, and the settlers' anger peaked in the weeks after Évian. Its defeat ended the settlers' armed resistance.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.0] On 26 March 1962 French troops fired on pro-French demonstrators in the rue d'Isly in Algiers, killing about 50 people, which deepened the settlers' sense of betrayal because the shots came from French soldiers.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.1] The OAS was defeated in Algiers and in France, and its leaders were tried. General Raoul Salan, a leader of the 1961 putsch, was arrested in April 1962 and received a life sentence.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.2] The OAS ambush of de Gaulle's car at Petit-Clamart on 22 August 1962 failed. Its organiser, Jean Bastien-Thiry, was executed in March 1963, the last person executed by firing squad in France for a political offence.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.3] A general amnesty in 1968 freed those OAS members still in prison.
[hist-eur5-s4.11.b] Independence did not end the links between the two countries, but the new state steadily took control of what France had kept.
[hist-eur5-s4.11.0] Ben Bella was overthrown by his defence minister Houari Boumediene in June 1965.
[hist-eur5-s4.11.1] France handed over the Mers-el-Kébir naval base in 1968, ending the military presence that the Évian Accords had allowed it to keep for a time.
[hist-eur5-s4.11.2] In 1971 Algeria nationalised (took into state ownership) French oil and gas holdings, reversing the access to Saharan oil and gas that the Accords had given France.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.b] Independence turned into flight for the European settlers and tragedy for many of the Algerians who had served France.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.0] On 5 July 1962, the day celebrated as independence, violence against Europeans broke out in Oran. Estimates vary, but hundreds were killed or went missing, and many settlers fled the city.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.1] About 800,000 settlers left Algeria in 1962, many within a few months and most for France, which had to absorb them into an already strained economy. Nearly a million had gone by the mid-1960s.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.2] The departure of the settlers ended the French presence in Algerian agriculture and administration, and many farms were taken over by the new state.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.3] The harkis were Algerians who had served as auxiliaries (helpers) in the French forces.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.4] The French government refused or obstructed organised rescue of the harkis, and officers who helped them were punished. About 90,000 reached France with their families.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.5] Tens of thousands of harkis were killed in reprisals (revenge killings) after independence, with estimates ranging widely from about 30,000 to 150,000.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.6] Most harkis who reached France were placed in camps and forest hamlets. Recognition and compensation remained a political issue: President Chirac paid national tribute to them in 2001 and President Macron asked their forgiveness in 2021.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.b] De Gaulle led the Free French in the Second World War and then ended the Algerian war as president of the Fifth Republic. His career shows both a leader shaping events and one pushed by them.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.0] Born on 22 November 1890 in Lille, he was a career army officer who became undersecretary for war in June 1940. His appeal of 18 June 1940 from London made him leader of the Free French.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.1] He headed the provisional government from 1944 to January 1946, resigned, founded the Rally of the French People (RPF) in 1947, and withdrew from politics in the 1950s before returning to power in 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.2] As the key decision-maker he took the major steps himself: the return in 1958, the Fifth Republic, the 1959 offer of self-determination, putting down Barricades Week and the putsch, and the path to Évian.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.3] In 1958 the settlers welcomed him because they believed he would keep Algeria French, but his 1959 turn showed events shaping him too. He moved from 'Je vous ai compris' in 1958 to self-determination in 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.4] Contemporaries split sharply: the settlers, the army and the OAS called him a traitor, while many in France and Algeria saw him as the leader who ended a costly war.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.5] Some historians argue that he planned withdrawal from early on and moved slowly to avoid civil war. Others argue that he moved step by step as the war's costs, the army's attitude and international pressure became clear.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.6] He used the Community and, after 1960, bilateral co-operation accords (agreements between two countries) to keep French influence in sub-Saharan Africa, working through Jacques Foccart, his adviser on African affairs.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.7] He resigned as president in April 1969 after losing a referendum, and died on 9 November 1970.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.b] De Gaulle's record and the wider retreat from empire can be judged by pairing achievements against limits, and contemporary views against later ones.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.0] Achievement: de Gaulle ended a war that the Fourth Republic had failed to win, and he gave Algerians the right of self-determination.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.1] Limit: the war cost many lives, caused the exodus of about a million people and divided the French army and political class. His policy was also delayed and ambiguous, which left the settlers feeling deceived.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.2] Achievement: the Fifth Republic's constitution gave France a stable government that could take the decision to withdraw.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.3] Limit: the French Community did not hold the colonies together, yet most independent states of West and Equatorial Africa kept close ties with France through co-operation accords, with French troops stationed in several of them.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.4] Explanations for withdrawal: the cost and unpopularity of the Algerian war, the independence of Tunisia and Morocco in 1956, UN General Assembly debates on Algeria from 1955, American pressure, world opinion and the growth of African nationalist parties.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.5] The British and French cases differed: Britain's transfers were mostly negotiated, while France fought a war in Algeria before accepting independence.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.6] Later view: the war divided the army, the political class and French society, and many judge that de Gaulle's greatest achievement was to end it without a collapse of the Republic.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.7] Contemporary view: settlers, the OAS and the generals of the 1961 putsch saw withdrawal as betrayal, while about 91 per cent of French voters approved the Évian Accords in April 1962.
