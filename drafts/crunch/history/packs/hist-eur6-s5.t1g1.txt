[hist-eur6-s2.0.b] After 1945 the United States abandoned its pre-war isolation and committed itself to resisting Soviet expansion, a policy known as containment.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.0] The United States emerged from the Second World War as the richest industrial power and, from 1945, the only country with atomic weapons.

[hist-eur6-s2.0.4] The Truman Doctrine of 12 March 1947 asked Congress for about 400 million dollars in aid to Greece and Turkey, and it committed the United States to support free peoples resisting armed minorities or outside pressure.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.5] The Communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 convinced many in Washington and Western Europe that the Soviet threat was immediate.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.6] Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949, and Republicans later used the question "Who lost China?" to attack Truman's Asia policy.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.7] In April 1950 the National Security Council paper NSC-68 called for a large rise in defence spending to meet the Soviet threat, and it was adopted after the Korean War began.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.b] Economic aid and a military alliance were the two pillars of containment in Western Europe.

[hist-eur6-s2.1.2] The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and pressured its satellite states in Eastern Europe to refuse it too, so the division of Europe became more fixed.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.3] Most aid was given as grants of food, fuel and machinery rather than loans, and the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC) was set up in April 1948 to allocate it; Britain, France and West Germany were the largest recipients.

[hist-eur6-s2.1.5] Article 5 of the NATO treaty stated that an armed attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, the basis of collective defence.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.6] Germany was divided: the Federal Republic of Germany was founded in May 1949 in the Western zones, and the German Democratic Republic followed in October 1949 in the Soviet zone.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.b] Berlin, divided into four sectors, was the most dangerous point of Cold War contact in Europe.

[hist-eur6-s2.2.3] The Berlin Wall was built from 13 August 1961, closing the border between East and West Berlin and stopping the flow of East German refugees.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.4] In October 1961 American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, the crossing point for foreigners, in a tense standoff that ended without shots.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.5] Well over two million East Germans had fled to the West between 1949 and 1961, most of them by crossing from East to West Berlin, which drained the East's skilled workforce.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.6] Kennedy visited West Berlin on 26 June 1963 and declared in German "Ich bin ein Berliner" (I am a Berliner), a statement of solidarity that was widely praised in the West.

[hist-eur6-s2.3.5] UN forces advanced north of the 38th parallel in October 1950, and Chinese forces entered the war in large numbers from late October and November 1950, driving them back.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.6] Truman dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 after the general publicly argued for attacking China, a decision that caused a major domestic row.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.7] An armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953; the frontier stayed close to the 38th parallel, and no peace treaty was ever signed.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.8] About 36,000 American servicemen died in the war, and US troops remained in South Korea after the armistice.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.b] Cuba lay ninety miles from Florida, and the United States had long treated the island as within its sphere of influence.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.0] Under the Platt Amendment of 1901 the United States had the right to intervene in Cuba, a clause repealed in 1934 but leaving a legacy of US economic dominance.

[hist-eur6-s2.4.2] Eisenhower cut the Cuban sugar quota in July 1960, imposed a partial trade embargo in October 1960 and broke off diplomatic relations in January 1961; Cuba turned to the Soviet Union for trade and oil.

[hist-eur6-s2.4.4] Operation Mongoose, a covert campaign against Castro's government, was authorised in November 1961.

[hist-eur6-s2.4.9] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed on Moscow radio to remove the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.10] Kennedy also secretly agreed to withdraw US Jupiter missiles from Turkey within months, a deal kept private for years; a hotline between Washington and Moscow was installed in 1963.

[hist-eur6-s2.6.2] Kennedy replaced massive retaliation with flexible response, building up conventional and special forces (such as the Green Berets) so the United States could fight limited wars.

[hist-eur6-s1.0.b] The Constitution divides power between the federal government and the states, and that division shaped the civil rights and social policy fights of the period.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.0] Federalism: powers not given to the federal government are reserved to the states under the Tenth Amendment, so states controlled schools, policing, voting rules and marriage law.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.1] Before the 1960s most southern states enforced segregation by state law, and federal action against it was met with the argument that such matters belonged to the states.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.2] In 1948 southern Democrats angry at Truman's civil rights programme left the party and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party (the Dixiecrats) with Strom Thurmond as candidate; it carried four Deep South states.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.3] The Civil Rights Act 1957 was the first civil rights law since Reconstruction; it set up a Civil Rights Commission and gave federal authorities power to act over voting rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.4] The Civil Rights Act 1964 rested on federal power to regulate interstate commerce, which allowed Congress to ban segregation in hotels, restaurants and other public places.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.5] In September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus of Arkansas used the National Guard to stop nine black students entering Little Rock Central High School; Eisenhower sent federal troops, including the 101st Airborne Division, to enforce integration.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.b] The Constitution gives Congress, the President and the Supreme Court separate powers, and each can limit the others.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.0] The Constitution (1787) gives Congress the power to make laws, raise taxes and declare war; the President executes the laws, commands the armed forces and conducts foreign policy; the Supreme Court interprets the Constitution.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.1] The President can veto bills, but Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds majority in both houses; the Senate must confirm senior appointments and ratify treaties by a two-thirds vote.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.2] Congress passed the Taft-Hartley Act in 1947, restricting trade union activity such as the closed shop, and overrode Truman's veto in June 1947.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.3] Republicans won control of both houses of Congress in the 1946 midterm elections (slogan: "Had enough?") for the first time since 1928, leaving Truman to govern with a hostile Congress until 1948.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.4] Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (June 1952): the Supreme Court ruled that Truman had exceeded his powers by seizing the steel mills during a strike in April 1952 without congressional authority.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.5] The Warren Court (Chief Justice Earl Warren, appointed by Eisenhower in 1953) used judicial review to extend civil rights and liberties, e.g. Brown v. Board of Education (1954) and Miranda v. Arizona (1966) on the rights of arrested suspects.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.6] The Twenty-Second Amendment, passed by Congress in March 1947 and ratified in 1951, limits a president to two elected terms, a direct response to Roosevelt winning four elections.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.7] United States v Nixon (July 1974): the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Nixon must hand over the White House tapes, and his resignation followed in August 1974.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.8] Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto in November 1973 to limit the President's power to commit troops without congressional approval.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.9] The Boland Amendments (1982 to 1984) restricted US aid to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua; officials in the Reagan administration kept the Contras supplied in breach of them, the core of the Iran-Contra affair.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.b] The President's staff and agencies grew steadily from the 1930s, so that more policy was shaped inside the White House and its agencies.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.0] The Executive Office of the President was created in 1939 under Roosevelt, giving the President a personal staff separate from the government departments.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.1] The National Security Act of July 1947, signed by Truman, created the National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency and the National Military Establishment, later renamed the Department of Defense.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.2] The National Security Council gave the President a forum of advisers on foreign and defence policy, often bypassing the State Department and Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.3] The Council of Economic Advisers was created by the Employment Act of 1946, which committed the federal government to promote maximum employment, production and purchasing power.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.4] Kennedy held live televised press conferences from January 1961, using the press conference as a direct channel to public opinion.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.5] Arthur Schlesinger Jr. described the expanded executive in The Imperial Presidency (1973), a phrase that became the standard label for concern over presidential power after Watergate.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.b] Roosevelt's coalition and the wartime economy shaped the politics that Truman inherited in 1945.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.0] Roosevelt won four presidential elections (1932, 1936, 1940 and 1944) and served from March 1933 until his death.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.1] The New Deal coalition combined urban workers, trade unions, Catholics and Jews, farmers, southern whites and black voters who moved to the Democrats from the 1930s.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.2] Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945, and Harry Truman, vice president since January 1945, became president.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.3] Truman had chaired a Senate committee (the Truman Committee, 1941 to 1944) that investigated waste and fraud in wartime production, which gave him a reputation for careful oversight.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.4] Truman inherited a wave of strikes in 1945 and 1946 as the wartime economy was converted to peacetime production.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.5] Truman's party was divided between northern liberals and conservative southern Democrats, who often voted with Republicans in Congress (the conservative coalition) to block reform.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.b] Truman was a participant in the domestic politics of his presidency and a figure whose popularity swung widely. His foreign policy record is covered in the notes on US foreign policy.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.0] Born in Lamar, Missouri, in 1884, he worked as a farmer, a haberdasher and a county court official before being elected to the Senate in 1934 and taking his seat in 1935.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.1] He served as an artillery captain in France in the First World War and owed his early political career to the Kansas City Democratic machine of Tom Pendergast.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.2] As vice president from January 1945 he was not told of the atomic bomb project until he became president, a fact that shaped his early reliance on advisers.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.3] In September 1945 he sent Congress a 21-point programme of reforms that continued the New Deal, but reconversion, inflation and strikes blocked most of it.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.4] His domestic programme, the Fair Deal, was outlined in January 1949 and included national health insurance, a wider Social Security system, a higher minimum wage and public housing.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.5] Congress blocked national health insurance and most other Fair Deal proposals; the Housing Act of 1949 and a rise in the minimum wage were among the main successes.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.6] His civil rights programme began with the report To Secure These Rights (October 1947) from the President's Committee on Civil Rights, and Executive Order 9981 (26 July 1948) ordered the desegregation of the armed forces.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.7] His loyalty programme (Executive Order 9835, March 1947) required checks on federal employees for disloyalty, and it fed the wider Red Scare.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.8] Contemporaries were often dismissive: the 1946 joke "To err is Truman" mocked his handling of strikes and prices, and his Gallup approval rating fell to about 22 per cent in early 1952.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.9] In 1948 he won an upset victory over Thomas Dewey after a campaign attacking the Republican Congress; the Chicago Daily Tribune had printed the headline "Dewey Defeats Truman" before the result was known.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.10] In 1948 the Democrats split three ways (Thurmond's Dixiecrats on the right, Henry Wallace's Progressives on the left), yet Truman won 303 electoral votes to Dewey's 189 after a cross-country whistle-stop train campaign.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.11] By 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply because of the Korean War, rising prices and corruption scandals in several federal agencies, and he announced in March 1952 that he would not seek re-election.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.12] Historians since the 1970s, and David McCullough's biography Truman (1992), have generally ranked him among the stronger presidents for his decisiveness and his civil rights initiatives, while judging his domestic record more limited than he hoped and his loyalty programme damaging to civil liberties.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.0] Eisenhower, a Republican and former Allied commander in the Second World War, kept most New Deal programmes and called his approach Modern Republicanism.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.1] Eisenhower extended Social Security to about 10 million more workers in 1954 and created the Department of Health, Education and Welfare in 1953, accepting the New Deal's main structures.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.2] Eisenhower's Farewell Address (January 1961) warned against the "military-industrial complex" (see the notes on the economic boom).
[hist-eur6-s1.5.3] The Federal-Aid Highway Act of June 1956 funded an Interstate Highway System of about 41,000 miles, the largest federal public works programme of the period.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.4] Kennedy won the 1960 election narrowly over Richard Nixon, and his New Frontier, presented in his nomination speech of July 1960, promised a more active federal role at home and in space.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.5] Kennedy's civil rights bill was sent to Congress in June 1963, and it was only passed after his assassination.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.6] Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas on 22 November 1963; Lyndon Johnson was sworn in as president on Air Force One that afternoon.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.7] Kennedy's tax cut was proposed in January 1963 and passed as the Revenue Act of February 1964 under Johnson, cutting the top income tax rate from 91 to 70 per cent.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.b] Johnson's domestic programme was the largest federal social effort since the New Deal. His Vietnam policy is covered in the notes on the case study.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.0] Johnson, Senate Majority Leader from 1955 to 1961, used his legislative skills (the persuasive "Johnson treatment") and the national mood after Kennedy's death to pass Kennedy's stalled bills.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.1] The Civil Rights Act of 2 July 1964 banned segregation in public places and discrimination in employment on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.2] In a speech at the University of Michigan on 22 May 1964, Johnson set out the Great Society, a programme to end poverty and racial injustice.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.3] The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 launched the War on Poverty, which included Job Corps and community action programmes.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.4] The Voting Rights Act of 6 August 1965 suspended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters in areas where discrimination was proven.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.5] Medicare for the elderly and Medicaid for the poor were signed into law on 30 July 1965.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.6] The Elementary and Secondary Education Act (April 1965) and the Higher Education Act (1965) provided federal aid to schools and colleges, and Head Start for poor pre-school children began in 1965.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.7] In the 1964 election Johnson won about 61 per cent of the popular vote against Barry Goldwater, who carried only six states.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.8] The Great Society also included the Immigration and Nationality Act (1965), which ended national-origin quotas, and the creation of the Department of Housing and Urban Development (1965).
[hist-eur6-s1.6.9] The cost of the Vietnam War from 1965 drained money and attention from the Great Society, and Republican gains in the 1966 midterms slowed new legislation.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.0] Richard Nixon won the 1968 election narrowly and was re-elected in 1972 by carrying 49 states.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.1] Nixon's domestic record included the Environmental Protection Agency (1970), wage and price controls (August 1971) and the expansion of affirmative action in federal contracts.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.2] Five men were arrested for burgling the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate complex on 17 June 1972, and the Washington Post's reporting kept the scandal in the news.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.3] Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974; Gerald Ford, who had become vice president in December 1973 after Spiro Agnew resigned, pardoned him on 8 September 1974.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.4] Ford's pardon of Nixon damaged his standing; he faced inflation and recession in 1974 to 1975 and lost narrowly to Carter in 1976.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.5] Jimmy Carter, a Democrat from Georgia, won the 1976 election and negotiated the Panama Canal treaties of 1977, under which the canal passed to Panama at the end of 1999.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.6] The Camp David Accords of September 1978, brokered by Carter, led to a peace treaty between Egypt and Israel signed in March 1979.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.7] Carter created the Departments of Energy (1977) and Education (1979), but high inflation and the 1979 oil shock undermined him; his televised speech of July 1979 on a "crisis of confidence", nicknamed the malaise speech, was seen by critics as blaming the public.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.8] The Iran hostage crisis held 52 American diplomats and citizens from 4 November 1979 to 20 January 1981, 444 days; an attempted rescue in April 1980 failed, and it was a major cause of Carter's defeat.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.0] Ronald Reagan defeated Carter in 1980 and was re-elected in 1984, carrying 49 states against Walter Mondale.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.1] In his first inaugural address (20 January 1981) Reagan said that "government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem".
[hist-eur6-s1.8.2] The Economic Recovery Tax Act of August 1981 cut income tax rates by about 25 per cent over three years and lowered the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.3] Reagan's first term combined cuts to many domestic social programmes with a large rise in defence spending, a pattern known as Reaganomics.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.4] When air traffic controllers in the PATCO union struck in August 1981, Reagan dismissed the strikers, signalling a hard line on public sector unions.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.5] Iran-Contra was revealed in November 1986: the administration had sold arms to Iran and diverted the money to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua despite congressional restrictions.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.6] The Tower Commission (February 1987) criticised Reagan's detached management style; National Security Adviser John Poindexter and his aide Oliver North were among those prosecuted.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.7] Reagan's popularity survived the scandal and he left office in January 1989 with high approval ratings, earning the nickname "the Teflon president" because scandals did not stick to him.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.0] Truman's Marshall Plan and NATO are usually seen as successes of US leadership; his failure to pass national health insurance and the unpopularity of the Korean War are the main criticisms.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.1] Johnson's Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts are counted as major achievements, while his Vietnam policy is widely judged a failure.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.2] Nixon's opening to China and his arms control agreements are credited as achievements, while Watergate and the secret bombing of Cambodia are judged serious failings.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.3] Carter's Camp David Accords are judged a success, while the Iran hostage crisis and his 1980 defeat are the main charges against him.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.4] Reagan's supporters credit his tax cuts and his role in ending the Cold War; critics point to the rising deficit, the 1981 to 1982 recession and Iran-Contra.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.5] Public opinion polls made presidential popularity visible from the 1930s onwards, and the Watergate and Iran-Contra scandals led Congress to reassert its oversight of presidents.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.6] Contemporary verdicts often differed from later ones: Truman left office in 1953 deeply unpopular, yet his reputation rose sharply in later historians' rankings, as did Eisenhower's, while Johnson's soared in 1964 and collapsed by 1968.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s3: 3. Case study: Lyndon Johnson and Vietnam, 1963–1968
[hist-eur6-s3.0.b] Johnson became president in November 1963 with an American commitment in South Vietnam that had already grown from advisers into a costly war against the Viet Cong.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.0] Lyndon Baines Johnson was born in 1908 near Stonewall, Texas, worked in the New Deal's National Youth Administration in Texas, entered the House in 1937 and won his Senate seat in 1948 by 87 votes, a result that earned him the nickname Landslide Lyndon.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.1] He became Democratic leader in the Senate in 1953 and Majority Leader in 1955, and in 1960 he accepted the vice presidency under Kennedy after losing the nomination.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.2] He was a master of Senate politics and persuasion, using personal pressure on colleagues (the Johnson treatment) to pass legislation, but he could not apply those methods to a war against an enemy who would not bargain on his terms.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.3] He was vice president under Kennedy and sworn in as president on 22 November 1963, after Kennedy's assassination in Dallas.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.4] Kennedy had committed the United States to the survival of South Vietnam but had avoided combat troops, and the South Vietnamese government was in disarray after Diem's overthrow and killing on 2 November 1963.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.5] Within days of taking office Johnson told his ambassador in Saigon that he would not lose Vietnam, and National Security Action Memorandum 273 (26 November 1963) confirmed the policy of supporting South Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.6] Johnson kept Kennedy's advisers, including Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara, Secretary of State Dean Rusk and National Security Adviser McGeorge Bundy, so there was continuity of thinking about the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.7] Johnson feared that losing Vietnam would repeat the political damage of the loss of China, when Truman's party had been attacked as soft on communism, and he feared a right-wing backlash that would wreck his domestic programme.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.8] When Johnson took office there were about 16,000 American military advisers in South Vietnam, and the government of Diem had been overthrown in November 1963.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.9] Johnson inherited a South Vietnamese government that was unstable and a war in which the Viet Cong controlled large parts of the countryside.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.10] He was a firm anti-communist who was also aware of the domestic cost of war, and he believed that losing South Vietnam would damage US credibility worldwide.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.b] The Gulf of Tonkin incidents gave Johnson congressional authority for a larger war, and the 1964 election confirmed his mandate.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.0] On 2 August 1964 North Vietnamese torpedo boats attacked the US destroyer USS Maddox in the Gulf of Tonkin, which was on an intelligence-gathering patrol near the North Vietnamese coast, days after South Vietnamese commando raids on the coast (Operation 34A) that Hanoi linked to the patrol.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.1] A second attack reported on 4 August 1964, on the Maddox and the destroyer USS Turner Joy, probably never happened: sonar and radar readings in bad weather were misread, and later documents showed that officials doubted the report even as it was used.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.2] On the evening of 4 August 1964 Johnson announced retaliatory air strikes on North Vietnamese naval bases, which were carried out on 5 August.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.3] The Tonkin Gulf Resolution, passed on 7 August 1964 by 416 votes to 0 in the House and 88 to 2 in the Senate, authorised the President to take all necessary measures to protect US forces and to prevent further aggression.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.4] Critics later argued that the resolution was prepared before the incidents were fully understood, and Senator Wayne Morse and Senator Ernest Gruening voted against it.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.5] In the 1964 election Johnson won about 61 per cent of the popular vote against Barry Goldwater, who had called for stronger action in Vietnam, and he presented himself as the candidate of restraint.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.6] The campaign's restraint was a political strategy, and Johnson privately prepared for a larger war while publicly promising not to send American boys to fight an Asian war.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.b] The decision to bomb North Vietnam and to commit ground combat forces in 1965 turned an advisers' war into an American war.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.0] In February 1965 a Viet Cong attack on the American base at Pleiku killed eight Americans, and Johnson ordered reprisal raids (Flaming Dart) that led on to the sustained bombing.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.1] Operation Rolling Thunder, a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam, began on 2 March 1965, and it continued in stages until 1968.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.2] The first US Marine combat units landed at Da Nang on 8 March 1965, the first American ground combat troops in Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.3] General William Westmoreland, commander of US forces from June 1964, argued that a war of attrition with large forces would wear down the North Vietnamese.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.4] On 28 July 1965 Johnson announced at a press conference that US forces in Vietnam would rise from 75,000 to 125,000 at once and that more would be sent as commanders requested, without asking Congress for a declaration of war or calling up the reserves.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.5] US troop numbers reached about 184,000 by the end of 1965, about 385,000 by the end of 1966 and over 500,000 in 1968, peaking at about 540,000 in 1969 before falling.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.6] Johnson's strategy was to hold the South while signalling willingness to negotiate, and his April 1965 Johns Hopkins speech offered unconditional talks and a development plan for the Mekong Delta.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.7] North Vietnam refused to negotiate while the bombing continued, and the Viet Cong kept fighting in the South, supplied through the Ho Chi Minh Trail in Laos and Cambodia.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.8] Johnson's advisers were not united: Under Secretary of State George Ball argued in memos in 1965 that the United States would be dragged into an unwinnable land war, but Johnson, McNamara, Rusk and Bundy chose escalation.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.9] Johnson avoided a formal declaration of war and wanted to keep the escalation low-key in order not to endanger his Great Society legislation, which was passing through Congress in 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.0] Westmoreland's strategy was search and destroy: large units hunted communist forces in the countryside, using helicopters, artillery and air power, and success was measured by the body count.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.1] The communists could choose when to fight, and they replaced losses from a large population and from supplies carried down the Ho Chi Minh Trail, so attrition did not break their will.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.2] Heavy firepower, including napalm and the defoliant Agent Orange, killed large numbers of civilians and drove peasants into government-controlled camps or towards the Viet Cong.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.3] The pacification of the countryside and the building of a stable South Vietnamese government lagged behind: the Saigon regime was led by generals, and Nguyen Van Thieu became president in 1967 after elections criticised as flawed.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.4] In November 1967 Westmoreland told Washington that the end was in view, which set up the shock of Tet.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.5] In March 1968 American soldiers killed several hundred unarmed villagers at My Lai, an atrocity covered up until it was exposed in 1969.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.6] American casualties passed 30,000 dead by the end of 1968, and the draft, which took young men who lacked college deferments, spread the war's costs through American families.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.b] Johnson's public statements on Vietnam were increasingly doubted, and his critics in Congress and the media challenged the war from 1966.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.0] The term credibility gap was used by journalists from the mid-1960s to describe the difference between official statements on the war and the reality reported from Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.1] Senator J. William Fulbright, chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, held televised hearings on Vietnam in early 1966 that questioned the war's basis.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.2] Fulbright's book The Arrogance of Power (1966) argued that the United States was acting as a great power imposing its will on a small nation.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.3] The teach-in movement began at the University of Michigan in March 1965, and student and church opposition grew steadily through 1966 and 1967.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.4] Martin Luther King Jr. spoke against the war in his Beyond Vietnam speech at Riverside Church, New York, on 4 April 1967, linking the war to poverty and racism.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.5] Muhammad Ali refused induction into the army on 28 April 1967, a decision that cost him his heavyweight title and was widely discussed in the press.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.6] Johnson's critics said the war was consuming money and attention needed for the Great Society, while his supporters argued that withdrawal would damage America's position abroad.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.7] Opposition spread beyond students: on 21 October 1967 about 50,000 people marched on the Pentagon, and in April 1965 the Students for a Democratic Society had led a march of about 20,000 in Washington.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.b] Johnson tried to fund both the war and his domestic programme, and the cost of the war weakened his reform agenda.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.0] Johnson tried to pay for the war and the Great Society together, a policy critics called guns and butter, and he delayed asking Congress for a tax increase until 1967.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.1] Federal deficits grew and inflation rose after 1965, and Congress passed a 10 per cent income tax surtax in June 1968 to restrain spending.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.2] Funding for the War on Poverty was cut or diverted as military spending grew, and many programmes were underfunded as they expanded.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.3] The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 were passed before the war's costs became acute, and they stand as Johnson's most secure achievements.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.4] By 1967 the rising cost of the war and the urban riots had turned public opinion against new domestic spending, weakening the Great Society's momentum.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.b] The Tet Offensive of January 1968 did not defeat the Americans militarily, but it shattered their optimism and ended Johnson's chance of re-election.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.0] On 30 January 1968, during the Tet holiday, Viet Cong and North Vietnamese forces attacked more than a hundred towns and cities across South Vietnam, including Saigon and the city of Hue.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.1] A Viet Cong squad broke into the grounds of the US embassy in Saigon and held out for about six hours before being killed, and television pictures of it shocked American opinion, even though the offensive was militarily a failure for the communists.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.2] General Westmoreland had said that the enemy could no longer mount major offensives, and Tet was taken by many Americans as proof that official optimism was false.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.3] Tet was a heavy military defeat for the communists, who lost many of their best Viet Cong fighters, but American commentators judged it by Westmoreland's earlier promises.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.4] On 27 February 1968 the television newsman Walter Cronkite broadcast a report calling the war a stalemate, and his views were widely reported as a sign of changing opinion.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.5] In February 1968 Westmoreland and the Joint Chiefs asked for about 206,000 more troops; Johnson's new Secretary of Defense Clark Clifford studied the request and advised against it, and a group of senior outside advisers known as the Wise Men told Johnson in March that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.6] Eugene McCarthy's strong showing in the New Hampshire primary in March 1968 and Robert Kennedy's entry into the race weakened Johnson's position within his own party.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.7] On 31 March 1968 Johnson announced a partial halt to the bombing of North Vietnam above the 20th parallel, offered peace talks and declared that he would not seek re-election.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.8] Peace talks began in Paris in May 1968, but the war continued, and the Republican Richard Nixon won the presidency in November 1968 after promising an honourable end to the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.9] 1968 turned into a year of crisis: Martin Luther King was assassinated on 4 April and Robert Kennedy on 5 June, and the Democratic convention in Chicago in August was marked by rioting between police and protesters, and Johnson's vice president Hubert Humphrey lost narrowly to Nixon.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.b] Historians and contemporaries have offered several reasons why an enormous American military effort failed to defeat a determined enemy.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.0] The war was a limited one: the US avoided invading North Vietnam, mining Haiphong harbour, or using nuclear weapons, and this limited the pressure on Hanoi.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.1] North Vietnam and the Viet Cong could use bases in Laos and Cambodia that the US was reluctant to attack, so they could withdraw and rebuild.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.2] The South Vietnamese government was weak and corrupt, and the Army of the Republic of Vietnam could not hold the territory cleared by American forces.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.3] The strategy of attrition and the body count measure of success were criticised within the US army and in the press as misleading about the progress of the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.4] Many historians argue that Johnson's gradual escalation, and his refusal to consider a clear withdrawal, kept options open while the costs and divisions at home grew.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.5] Johnson played down the war in public to protect the Great Society, so he never explained clear aims to the public, and the later gap between his optimism and events hurt his standing.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.b] Johnson was the president who made Vietnam an American war and also the president of the Great Society. He was a decision-maker at the centre of events rather than a witness.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.0] His career: congressman 1937, senator 1949, Senate Majority Leader 1955 to 1961, vice president 1961 to 1963, president from 22 November 1963 until January 1969.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.1] His domestic record was one of the greatest legislative achievements of any president: the Civil Rights Act (1964), Voting Rights Act (1965), Medicare, Medicaid and federal aid to education.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.2] In Vietnam he took decisions in 1964 and 1965 to bomb North Vietnam and send combat troops, always in steps, and he hoped to avoid a wider war with China or the Soviet Union, as in Korea in 1950.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.3] Contemporaries saw him as a forceful, domineering leader who was crude, devious and driven, and the credibility gap made many doubt his word; his supporters pointed to his achievements for the poor and for black Americans.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.4] Protesters chanted at him outside the White House, and the demonstrations helped to persuade him not to stand again in 1968.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.5] Historians in the 1970s, such as the journalist David Halberstam in The Best and the Brightest (1972), blamed the arrogance of the advisers he inherited; later historians, such as Robert Dallek and Fredrik Logevall, stress that he had alternatives in 1964 and 1965 and chose to escalate partly to protect his domestic reputation.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.6] Johnson's reputation has risen since the 1990s for civil rights and fallen over Vietnam, so most historians now regard him as a tragic figure.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.b] Examiners reward evaluation that weighs Johnson's domestic achievements against his failure in Vietnam, and contemporary against later judgements.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.0] Success at home: the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act, Medicare and Medicaid are judged as major achievements of the twentieth century's social reform.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.1] Failure abroad: the war ended without victory in 1973 and cost about 58,000 American lives, and it damaged the Great Society and the trust of the public in government.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.2] Contemporaries were divided: in 1964 and 1965 most Americans admired him as a strong, effective leader, but the war cut his approval below 40 per cent by late 1967, and he was jeered by protesters chanting about how many children he had killed that day.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.3] Later historians disagree about whether Johnson was trapped by the assumptions of the Cold War or whether he made avoidable choices on escalation.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.4] The phrase credibility gap summed up the lasting problem of trust between a president and the public, a theme that shaped politics after Vietnam and Watergate.
