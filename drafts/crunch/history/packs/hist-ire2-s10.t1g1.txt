TARGET hist-ire2-s10: 10. Cultural revivals and the consolidation of Catholic identity
[hist-ire2-s10.0.b] By the 1880s many Irish people felt that politics alone had not made Ireland distinct, and that the country's own culture was fading. Below are the background events and the vocabulary that the rest of the subsection depends on.
[hist-ire2-s10.0.0] The Great Famine (1845–52) killed about one million people and led to the emigration of about one million more. Irish as a spoken language fell sharply after the Famine.
[hist-ire2-s10.0.1] English was the language of the state, law and commerce, and the national school system (begun in 1831) taught in English. Revivalists later set out to restore Irish.
[hist-ire2-s10.0.2] Anglicisation means adopting English language, dress, games, names and manners. De-anglicisation is Douglas Hyde's programme to reverse this by reviving the Irish language, games, music and customs.
[hist-ire2-s10.0.3] The Anglo-Irish were the mainly Protestant landowning and professional class with English ties, many of them unionist (supporters of the union with Britain). In this subsection the term also labels the writers of the Literary Revival, and is the label their critics used for them.
[hist-ire2-s10.0.4] Cultural nationalism defines the nation by its language, culture and tradition rather than by parliaments and votes. The Gaelic League, the GAA and the Literary Revival were all cultural nationalist movements.
[hist-ire2-s10.0.5] Irish Ireland is D.P. Moran's idea of a Gaelic and Catholic nation built on the Irish language, Irish industry and Irish games. It was narrower than Hyde's approach.
[hist-ire2-s10.0.6] The Catholic Church under Paul Cullen (archbishop of Armagh from 1849 and of Dublin from 1852, with the Synod of Thurles in 1850) set about reforming Irish Catholic practice. The Catholic majority also gave nationalist politics (the Home Rule movement and Parnell) its weight.
[hist-ire2-s10.0.7] The GAA, founded at Thurles on 1 November 1884, was the first of the cultural bodies.
[hist-ire2-s10.1.b] Hyde's lecture of 1892 gave the cultural revival its programme: Irish people should stop imitating England and revive their own language and customs.
[hist-ire2-s10.1.0] Douglas Hyde delivered The Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland to the National Literary Society in Dublin on 25 November 1892. The Society had been founded in Dublin that year, with Hyde, Yeats and John O'Leary among those involved.
[hist-ire2-s10.1.1] Hyde argued that Ireland had been anglicised in language, names, dress, music, games and ideas. The Irish, he said, were neither fully English nor fully Irish, because they had abandoned their own ways without becoming truly English.
[hist-ire2-s10.1.2] He urged the revival of Irish and of native customs, so that Ireland could preserve what was distinctive about it rather than copy England.
[hist-ire2-s10.1.3] The lecture was presented as non-political: its aim was cultural, and it appealed to all creeds and parties. This is why it could later support a League that welcomed Catholics, Protestants, nationalists and unionists.
[hist-ire2-s10.1.4] Hyde pointed to the decline of Irish as a spoken language, which had fallen sharply since the Famine, and asked for it to be cultivated and used in schools.
[hist-ire2-s10.1.5] The GAA (1884) had already shown the appeal of native games. The lecture gave a wider programme, covering language and customs, and led within a year to the founding of the Gaelic League.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.b] The Gaelic League, founded in 1893, turned Hyde's argument into a mass movement to revive the Irish language. Its story is one of real successes, then growing political pressure.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.0] The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge) was founded in Dublin on 31 July 1893 by Douglas Hyde, Eoin MacNeill and others, with Hyde as president.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.1] It was officially non-political and non-sectarian, so it welcomed Catholics, Protestants, nationalists and unionists. This broad stance helped it recruit across the divisions of Irish society.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.2] Branches ran Irish classes, so that adults could learn the language, and timirí (travelling teachers of Irish) were sent to rural areas. There were several hundred branches by the mid-1900s.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.3] The League organised feiseanna (festivals of music, dance and language competitions), so that people could enjoy Irish culture and not only study it, and, from 1897, the annual festival called the Oireachtas.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.4] Its weekly paper An Claidheamh Soluis began in 1899, and Patrick Pearse edited it from 1903 to 1909. This gave the League a way to spread its ideas.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.5] Success in schools: Irish was admitted to primary schools as an ordinary subject by the Revised Programme of 1900, and a bilingual programme followed in 1904.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.6] Success at university: the League won a campaign to make Irish compulsory for matriculation (the entrance qualification) to the National University of Ireland. The Senate decided this in 1910 and it applied from 1913.
[hist-ire2-s10.2.7] Language processions on St Patrick's Day began in 1903, the year it became a bank holiday in Ireland. People also took Irish names and used Irish-language signs.
[hist-ire2-s10.3.b] The League drew on a wide social range, but the same success that made it influential also pulled it into politics and exposed its limits.
[hist-ire2-s10.3.0] The League's membership included women, teachers and the urban middle class, and its leadership was based in Dublin. This differed from the GAA, which was rural and male.
[hist-ire2-s10.3.1] From about 1910 a growing number of IRB members (the Irish Republican Brotherhood, a secret society that wanted an independent republic) joined the League. They pushed it towards politics.
[hist-ire2-s10.3.2] Hyde resigned as president in 1915 after the League's Ard Fheis (annual convention) in July 1915 adopted a political aim. This is later context: it shows that the League's non-political stance did not survive.
[hist-ire2-s10.3.3] Limit: Irish did not revive as an everyday language, so the main aim was not reached, even though the League put it into schools and the university.
[hist-ire2-s10.3.4] Limit: the Church's support was limited. Fr Michael O'Hickey, professor of Irish at Maynooth, was dismissed in 1909 by the bishops (the college trustees) after campaigning for compulsory Irish in the National University.
[hist-ire2-s10.4.b] Hyde was the scholar who launched the language revival and gave it a non-sectarian, non-political character. He was both a founder of the movement and, in the end, a man overtaken by it.
[hist-ire2-s10.4.0] Douglas Hyde (1860–1949) was born in Castlerea, Co. Roscommon, the son of a Church of Ireland rector. He learned Irish from the people around him in Frenchpark and studied at Trinity College Dublin.
[hist-ire2-s10.4.1] He published collections of Gaelic songs and folktales, including Love Songs of Connacht (1893), and wrote under the pen name An Craoibhín Aoibhinn (the little branch of delight). His Irish-language play Casadh an tSúgáin was staged in 1901.
[hist-ire2-s10.4.2] He was a participant: he gave the 1892 lecture, co-founded the League in 1893 and led it as president for twenty-two years, until 1915.
[hist-ire2-s10.4.3] He was shaped by events: the League's politicisation after about 1910 overtook his non-political aim, and he resigned as president in 1915 when it adopted a political aim.
[hist-ire2-s10.4.4] Contemporaries admired his scholarship and his appeal across religious lines. Irish Irelanders disliked the League's non-political, non-sectarian stance, and republicans criticised him later as the League turned political.
[hist-ire2-s10.4.5] He later became a senator (1925) and the first President of Ireland (1938–45), and died in 1949.
[hist-ire2-s10.4.6] Historians credit him with launching the revival and a mass interest in the language, while recognising that its later political use went beyond his intentions.
[hist-ire2-s10.5.b] Moran gave the revival a sharper edge by defining the Irish nation as Gaelic and Catholic. His view challenged Hyde's inclusive approach.
[hist-ire2-s10.5.0] David Patrick Moran founded the weekly newspaper The Leader in 1900 and used it to argue for an Irish Ireland.
[hist-ire2-s10.5.1] He urged support for Irish language, industry and games, and attacked the Anglo-Irish literary movement and the Irish Party as West Britons, his term for Irish people who imitated English ways.
[hist-ire2-s10.5.2] In his view the nation was Gaelic and Catholic, so Protestants could be accepted only if they adopted this identity.
[hist-ire2-s10.5.3] His book The Philosophy of Irish Ireland (1905) collected his articles and set out his creed.
[hist-ire2-s10.5.4] The Leader promoted boycotts of English goods and entertainment, to support Irish industry, and attacked music-hall and English newspapers.
[hist-ire2-s10.5.5] Moran's influence divided the revival between Hyde's inclusive approach and a narrower Catholic and Gaelic nationalism.
[hist-ire2-s10.6.b] A group of mainly Protestant writers in English drew on Irish myth, folklore and peasant speech to create a national literature and theatre. Unlike the League, they wrote in English, which is why Irish Irelanders distrusted them.
[hist-ire2-s10.6.0] The National Literary Society was founded in Dublin in 1892, with Hyde and Yeats among its leaders.
[hist-ire2-s10.6.1] The Irish Literary Theatre was founded in 1899 by W.B. Yeats, Lady Gregory and Edward Martyn. Its first production, in May 1899, was Yeats's The Countess Cathleen. In 1901 it staged Hyde's Irish-language play Casadh an tSúgáin.
[hist-ire2-s10.6.2] Cathleen ni Houlihan, credited to Yeats with Lady Gregory's help, was first staged on 2 April 1902, with Maud Gonne (a nationalist activist) in the title role. It was a nationalist play.
[hist-ire2-s10.6.3] The Abbey Theatre opened on 27 December 1904, funded by the English patron Annie Horniman, with Yeats, Lady Gregory and J.M. Synge as directors.
[hist-ire2-s10.6.4] Lady Gregory (1852–1932), for example, was an Anglo-Irish landowner from Coole Park, Co. Galway. She wrote plays, collected folklore, and acted as patron and organiser of the Abbey as a director.
[hist-ire2-s10.6.5] John Millington Synge (1871–1909) drew on the speech of the Aran Islands and the west of Ireland. His The Playboy of the Western World was first performed at the Abbey in January 1907.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.b] Yeats, a Protestant poet and dramatist, tried to give Ireland a literature that would shape a national identity. He found that the nationalists he wrote for did not always accept him.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.0] William Butler Yeats (1865–1939) was born in Sandymount, Dublin, on 13 June 1865, the son of the painter John Butler Yeats. He spent much of his childhood in Sligo and London, and was a Protestant.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.1] His early poetry, such as The Wanderings of Oisin (1889), drew on Irish mythology. His friendship with the nationalist activist Maud Gonne and with John O'Leary, the old Fenian who influenced him, linked him to political circles.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.2] He was a participant who co-founded the National Literary Society, the Irish Literary Theatre and the Abbey (as a director), and was linked to the play Cathleen ni Houlihan.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.3] He was shaped by events, so his views changed: the Countess Cathleen and Playboy controversies and the Lane dispute pushed him towards a critique of nationalist Ireland.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.4] September 1913, published in the Irish Times on 8 September 1913, followed the Hugh Lane dispute. It condemned the narrow Catholic middle class for lacking the generosity of earlier Irish rebels, and its refrain is: Romantic Ireland's dead and gone.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.5] Contemporary nationalists admired his work but distrusted his Anglo-Irish background, because he was a Protestant writing in English. Moran mocked him, and Catholic critics attacked The Countess Cathleen.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.6] He later served as a senator (1922–28), won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1923, and died in 1939.
[hist-ire2-s10.7.7] Historians such as R.F. Foster (W.B. Yeats: A Life, volume 1 in 1997 and volume 2 in 2003) see him at the centre of Irish cultural life, while critics find an elitist streak in his politics.
[hist-ire2-s10.8.b] The revival was marked by public disputes about who defined Irish identity. Each controversy shows the clash between the Anglo-Irish and Irish Ireland visions.
[hist-ire2-s10.8.0] The Countess Cathleen (1899) was attacked as heretical by Catholic critics. Frank Hugh O'Donnell's pamphlet Souls for Gold (1899) was a leading attack.
[hist-ire2-s10.8.1] The Playboy riots of January 1907 followed because nationalist audiences objected to the play's picture of rural Irish people. They showed the clash between the Abbey's artistic freedom and the nationalist demand for flattering images of Irish life.
[hist-ire2-s10.8.2] The Hugh Lane dispute began when Sir Hugh Lane offered his collection of French paintings to Dublin and Dublin Corporation hesitated over funding and a site for a gallery (1912–13). Lane withdrew the offer and lent the pictures to the National Gallery in London in 1913.
[hist-ire2-s10.8.3] Lane died on the Lusitania on 7 May 1915. An unwitnessed codicil (an addition to his will) left the pictures to Dublin, leading to a long dispute that was only settled by a shared arrangement in 1959.
[hist-ire2-s10.8.4] Nationalists argued that Irish culture should be Gaelic and Catholic, while the Anglo-Irish writers claimed a place for English-language literature and for Protestant traditions.
[hist-ire2-s10.8.5] Tension between the two visions, Anglo-Irish and Irish Ireland, ran through the revival and remained after independence.
[hist-ire2-s10.9.b] After the Famine the Catholic Church tightened its hold on Irish life. Its influence grew through worship, buildings, schools and the clergy.
[hist-ire2-s10.9.0] Historians speak of a devotional revolution, a term coined by the historian Emmet Larkin (1972) for the post-Famine rise in Mass attendance and devotional practice under Cullen. From the 1850s there were parish missions, retreats, confraternities and devotions such as the Rosary and the Sacred Heart.
[hist-ire2-s10.9.1] New churches and cathedrals were built across the country, which made the Church's presence visible, funded by a more prosperous farming and trading middle class.
[hist-ire2-s10.9.2] The Church controlled most national schools through clerical managers (priests who ran the schools). It ran secondary schools through religious orders such as the Christian Brothers, Jesuits and convent teaching orders, so it shaped what young Catholics learned.
[hist-ire2-s10.9.3] St Patrick's College, Maynooth, trained a large clergy, which held a high social position in rural communities. Priests were often involved in politics, and individual priests were active in local GAA clubs.
[hist-ire2-s10.9.4] Sodalities and confraternities (lay associations for prayer and devotion) organised lay devotion, so ordinary parishioners, not only priests, took part in religious life.
[hist-ire2-s10.9.5] The Pioneer Total Abstinence Association was founded in 1898 by Fr James Cullen, a Jesuit, to fight drunkenness.
[hist-ire2-s10.10.b] From the 1880s Catholic identity and nationalist identity increasingly overlapped, and unionists reacted with fear. This was one of the main results of the Church's growing role.
[hist-ire2-s10.10.0] The Ne Temere decree, in force from Easter 1908, declared that marriages not before a Catholic priest were invalid for Catholics and required the children of mixed marriages to be raised as Catholics. This angered Protestants.
[hist-ire2-s10.10.1] The land reforms and expanding education produced a Catholic middle class, which provided the personnel of the nationalist movement.
[hist-ire2-s10.10.2] The Catholic bishops generally supported Home Rule from 1885, which tied Catholic identity to nationalism. The Irish Party was largely Catholic.
[hist-ire2-s10.10.3] Unionists reacted by presenting Home Rule as Rome Rule, their slogan. The Church's role in the Parnell split (the hierarchy condemned Parnell in December 1890 after the divorce scandal) and Ne Temere reinforced the fear.
[hist-ire2-s10.11.b] The GAA recovered in the 1890s and the two bodies became partners and rivals. Comparing them is a favourite exam task.
[hist-ire2-s10.11.0] The GAA was weakened by the Parnell split and the crisis of 1891 and recovered slowly through the 1890s, when clubs were reorganised and competitions resumed.
[hist-ire2-s10.11.1] The IRB remained influential in its leadership. The GAA also banned its members from playing foreign games, a rule formalised in the early 1900s.
[hist-ire2-s10.11.2] It bought grounds at Jones's Road in Dublin in 1908; the ground was later called Croke Park.
[hist-ire2-s10.11.3] The GAA was mass, rural, male and sport-based. The League drew women, teachers and the urban middle class, and its work was language and education.
[hist-ire2-s10.11.4] The GAA was politically committed from the start, whereas the League was non-political until 1915.
[hist-ire2-s10.11.5] Both were created by cultural nationalists and shared the aim of de-anglicisation and many members. Key names differ: Michael Cusack and Maurice Davin for the GAA, Hyde and MacNeill for the League.
[hist-ire2-s10.12.b] The revival gave Ireland a cultural identity but left argument about who belonged to it.

[hist-ire2-s10.12.1] Limit: the language did not revive as an everyday tongue, and the Catholic and Gaelic emphasis excluded many Protestants and unionists.
[hist-ire2-s10.12.2] Contemporary unionists saw the revival as a disguise for separatism, whereas Irish Irelanders such as Moran saw the Literary Revival as too Anglo-Irish.
[hist-ire2-s10.12.3] Historians differ on whether the revival caused the revolution of 1916, as Pearse and the IRB claimed, or merely provided a language for politics that had other causes.

CANDIDATES hist-ire2-s9: 9. Case study: the GAA to 1891
[hist-ire2-s9.0.b] By the early 1880s many Irish people felt that their own language, games and customs were being pushed aside by English ones, just as politics was stirring strong national feeling. The GAA grew out of both worries.
[hist-ire2-s9.0.0] The Famine and the heavy emigration that followed weakened rural Irish society and the Irish language, so English language and customs spread, especially in towns and schools.
[hist-ire2-s9.0.1] Anglicisation means the replacement of Irish language, dress, games and customs by English ones. De-anglicisation is the effort to reverse this by reviving Irish language, games and customs, and the GAA was an early example.
[hist-ire2-s9.0.2] The Gaelic Union (1880), successor to the Society for the Preservation of the Irish Language (1876), was formed to preserve Irish, so the language already had a society while games had none.
[hist-ire2-s9.0.3] The Land War began in 1879, when Michael Davitt founded the Land League with Parnell as president to defend tenant farmers against landlords. From 1880 the Irish Party (Home Rule Party), led by Parnell, sought self-government for Ireland.
[hist-ire2-s9.0.4] A Fenian revival was also under way. The IRB (Irish Republican Brotherhood, the Fenians) was a secret, oath-bound society that wanted an Irish republic won by force, so it looked for new organisations to join.
[hist-ire2-s9.0.5] The Catholic Church was a powerful influence on nationalist Ireland and was generally constitutional, preferring peaceful politics to secret societies. This is why the Church and the IRB later came into conflict inside the GAA.
[hist-ire2-s9.0.6] Irish Ireland was the later cultural-nationalist idea of building the nation on native language, games and traditions. It was developed after 1891, but the GAA is one of its roots.
[hist-ire2-s9.1.b] Before the GAA, organised sport in Ireland followed English rules and belonged mainly to the gentry and middle class, while the native game of hurling had declined. That gap is what the GAA set out to fill.
[hist-ire2-s9.1.0] Organised athletics followed English-style amateur rules that excluded “mechanics, artisans and labourers” (people who earned a living by manual work), so most working people and most farmers could not compete as amateurs.
[hist-ire2-s9.1.1] Rugby, cricket and later soccer were played in schools, in garrison towns (towns with an army barracks) and among the gentry. The Irish Rugby Football Union was formed in 1879 and the Irish Football Association (soccer, Belfast-based) in 1880.
[hist-ire2-s9.1.2] Hurling had declined since the Famine, as landlord patronage ended and rural communities shrank. It survived in parts of Galway and Munster, including Tipperary, Limerick, Cork and Clare.
[hist-ire2-s9.1.3] Hurling was played under varying local rules, and football had no national code, so teams from different districts could not easily play one another on agreed terms.
[hist-ire2-s9.1.4] Michael Cusack founded the Metropolitan Hurling Club in Dublin in 1882 and pushed for standard rules, which shows that the idea of a national code existed before the GAA.
[hist-ire2-s9.2.b] Two very different men started the GAA. Cusack supplied the drive and the ideas, and Davin supplied the respect that made others join.
[hist-ire2-s9.2.0] Michael Cusack was born at Carron, Co. Clare, in 1847 and died in 1906. He was a teacher who ran a Dublin academy preparing pupils for exams.
[hist-ire2-s9.2.1] Cusack was forceful and quarrelsome, which made him a tireless promoter but a difficult colleague, a point that matters when he falls out with fellow officers in 1886.
[hist-ire2-s9.2.2] Cusack’s fame outlived him in literature: he was the model for “the Citizen”, a character in James Joyce’s novel Ulysses.
[hist-ire2-s9.2.3] Maurice Davin was born in 1842 near Carrick-on-Suir, Co. Tipperary, and died in 1927. He was a champion athlete in weight-throwing and a champion rower.
[hist-ire2-s9.2.4] Davin was respected for his fairness, so he was the natural first president of the GAA (1884–1889). His standing gave the new body credibility with athletes and clubs.
[hist-ire2-s9.2.5] Do not mix the two up: Cusack was the Clare-born founder, agitator and secretary, removed in 1886, while Davin was the Tipperary sportsman and president, who resigned in 1889.
[hist-ire2-s9.3.b] In the autumn of 1884 Cusack went from writing about Irish athletics to calling a meeting, and the GAA was founded at Thurles within weeks.
[hist-ire2-s9.3.0] In October 1884 (on 11 October) Cusack’s article “A Word About Irish Athletics” appeared in United Ireland, calling for a national body to revive native athletics.
[hist-ire2-s9.3.1] United Ireland was Parnell’s nationalist weekly, edited by William O’Brien, so the call reached exactly the nationalist readers Cusack wanted.
[hist-ire2-s9.3.2] Cusack and Davin then sent out invitations to a meeting at Hayes’ Hotel, Thurles, Co. Tipperary.
[hist-ire2-s9.3.3] The meeting took place on Saturday 1 November 1884. Seven men attended, among them Cusack, Davin and John Wyse Power, a journalist.
[hist-ire2-s9.3.4] Davin was elected president and Cusack was elected one of the secretaries.
[hist-ire2-s9.3.5] The aims were to revive Irish games, to open athletics to all classes, and to promote physical fitness and national pride.
[hist-ire2-s9.3.6] The full title was the Gaelic Athletic Association for the Preservation and Cultivation of National Pastimes, where “pastimes” means games and sports.
[hist-ire2-s9.4.b] Cusack won the backing of the most powerful figures in nationalist Ireland. Their names gave the GAA respectability and publicity it could not have won alone.
[hist-ire2-s9.4.0] Thomas Croke, Archbishop of Cashel from 1875, had supported the tenants in the Land War and was a known nationalist, so his name carried weight with farmers.
[hist-ire2-s9.4.1] In a letter of December 1884 Croke agreed to become patron. He condemned the imitation of English games and manners and urged the revival of Irish pastimes as an act of national self-respect.
[hist-ire2-s9.4.2] Parnell and Davitt both agreed to be patrons (1884–85), which linked the GAA to the Home Rule movement and to the land movement.
[hist-ire2-s9.4.3] Davitt saw the GAA as a way of drawing working people and farmers into the national movement, and as a way of reviving Irish games to break from English influence. He wrote and spoke in support.
[hist-ire2-s9.4.4] William O’Brien, editor of United Ireland, promised the GAA regular space in the paper, which gave it free publicity.
[hist-ire2-s9.4.5] Croke’s letter was widely printed and made the GAA respectable, and many priests became club presidents and organisers.
[hist-ire2-s9.5.b] The GAA set rules that separated Irish sport from English sport and built a structure on the parish. These choices explain both its quick growth and some of its later quarrels.
[hist-ire2-s9.5.0] The first rules for hurling and Gaelic football were drawn up chiefly by Davin in 1885, replacing the old local variations with national codes.
[hist-ire2-s9.5.1] Competition was open to all classes, so the English amateur definition that excluded manual workers was rejected.
[hist-ire2-s9.5.2] The GAA was itself amateur (players were not paid), and it set out to keep money and professionalism out of sport.
[hist-ire2-s9.5.3] The foreign games ban stopped members from playing foreign games such as rugby, soccer, cricket and hockey. Its purpose was de-anglicisation, and the early ban concerned playing, not attending.
[hist-ire2-s9.5.4] Members of the Royal Irish Constabulary (the police force) and the British armed forces were also barred, because they represented British rule. The first ban covers games and the second covers people.
[hist-ire2-s9.5.5] Each club was based on a parish, with county committees and a Central Council above them, and an annual convention (the yearly delegate meeting) that was usually held in Thurles.
[hist-ire2-s9.5.6] The parish structure matched the Catholic Church’s own organisation, which made it easy for priests to take part.
[hist-ire2-s9.6.b] The parish club system let the GAA spread very fast, and in 1887 it launched its first All-Ireland championships.
[hist-ire2-s9.6.0] The GAA spread first in the south and west and then to the midlands and beyond. Within a few years hundreds of clubs had affiliated (joined the Association).
[hist-ire2-s9.6.1] Matches became local occasions, with large crowds, bands and parades of club men. The crowds also brought problems, such as drinking.
[hist-ire2-s9.6.2] The first All-Ireland championship was held in 1887 with an open draw and no provincial stage. It was decided in 1888, so its finals were played in 1888, not 1887.
[hist-ire2-s9.6.3] The first hurling final was played at Birr (then King’s County, now Co. Offaly) on 1 April 1888. Thurles, representing Tipperary, beat Meelick, representing Galway.
[hist-ire2-s9.6.4] The first football final was played in April 1888. Commercials, representing Limerick, beat Young Irelands (Dundalk), representing Louth.
[hist-ire2-s9.6.5] Cusack founded a weekly paper, The Celtic Times, in 1887 to promote Gaelic games.
[hist-ire2-s9.7.b] The GAA’s success made it a target for the Fenians and a worry for the Church, and its own leaders fell out. These are the first of the difficulties examiners ask about.
[hist-ire2-s9.7.0] Members of the IRB joined in large numbers, using clubs and county boards (the committees of each county) to recruit and to gain influence. Widespread local clubs gave them a ready network.
[hist-ire2-s9.7.1] Dublin Castle (the seat of British government in Ireland) watched the GAA closely, and RIC reports described it as a front for Fenianism.
[hist-ire2-s9.7.2] Cusack was removed as secretary in 1886 after quarrelling with fellow officers. The causes are disputed, so the safe statement is the date and the quarrels.
[hist-ire2-s9.7.3] At the Thurles convention of 1887, IRB-linked and moderate delegates contested control of the Central Council.
[hist-ire2-s9.7.4] Archbishop Croke and other bishops grew uneasy, and the Church was concerned about drink and discipline. Croke wrote to a GAA convention complaining of drinking at games, and in some places priests withdrew from clubs.
[hist-ire2-s9.7.5] Davin resigned as president in 1889 amid the disputes and divisions of those years.
[hist-ire2-s9.7.6] The IRB’s hold on the Central Council was strong by the later 1880s, and the stronger it grew, the more the Church held back.
[hist-ire2-s9.8.b] Two blows in quick succession, an expensive overseas tour and the split in Irish nationalism, brought the association close to collapse.
[hist-ire2-s9.8.0] In autumn 1888 about fifty hurlers and athletes, known as the American Invasion, toured the United States, with Irish-American support in cities such as New York and Boston.
[hist-ire2-s9.8.1] The tour lost money and left debts. It also disrupted the 1888 championship, which was left unfinished and played off in 1889, and some of the athletes stayed in America.
[hist-ire2-s9.8.2] The O’Shea divorce case (November 1890) damaged Parnell, and the Irish Party split in December 1890 (in Committee Room 15) into pro- and anti-Parnell groups.
[hist-ire2-s9.8.3] The split divided GAA members, with the IRB generally favouring Parnell and Church-minded members opposing him. This is the same man who had been a GAA patron in 1884 but now caused division.
[hist-ire2-s9.8.4] Parnell died on 6 October 1891, which left the association demoralised.
[hist-ire2-s9.8.5] By 1891 membership and the number of clubs had collapsed, and a few county boards were all that remained in many areas.
[hist-ire2-s9.8.6] The GAA recovered slowly in the 1890s, with a new generation of leaders and the support of the Gaelic revival (the renewed interest in Irish language and culture).
[hist-ire2-s9.9.b] The main exam question on this case study asks how the GAA dealt with its difficulties to 1891. The answers are its organisation, its patrons, the appeal of the games and a willingness to change leaders.
[hist-ire2-s9.9.0] The parish-based club system meant that local clubs could carry on even when the Central Council quarrelled.
[hist-ire2-s9.9.1] The patronage of Croke and the clergy gave the association moral authority, and priests helped to restore order in clubs.
[hist-ire2-s9.9.2] The appeal of hurling and football, and of local rivalry, was strong in itself and did not depend on politics.
[hist-ire2-s9.9.3] The removal of Cusack and the departure of Davin showed an attempt to settle disputes by changing leaders rather than breaking up the body.
[hist-ire2-s9.9.4] Links with the nationalist movement, through Parnell, Davitt and the IRB, gave the GAA energy but also exposed it to the Parnell split.
[hist-ire2-s9.9.5] Its identity as an Irish-only, amateur body made it a symbol of de-anglicisation, which the later Irish Ireland ideas built on.
[hist-ire2-s9.10.b] Documents on the early GAA are letters, speeches and reports. Each was written by someone with a purpose, so judging a source means asking who wrote it, when, why and for whom.
[hist-ire2-s9.10.0] Cusack’s article of October 1884 and his invitations are founding documents, expressing the aim of opening athletics to all classes. He was the founder, so he is not neutral.
[hist-ire2-s9.10.1] Croke’s letter of December 1884 is the best-known statement of the GAA’s national purpose, and it should be read as a clerical and nationalist appeal against English fashions.
[hist-ire2-s9.10.2] Croke’s later complaint to a convention about drinking at games shows a churchman’s concern about discipline, and a favourable picture of crowds and bands can be set against it.
[hist-ire2-s9.10.3] Davitt’s letters and speeches show the GAA’s links with the land movement, and Parnell’s patronage brought political prestige.
[hist-ire2-s9.10.4] Unionist newspapers and Dublin Castle reports describe the GAA as seditious or as a cover for the IRB. They were hostile, so they may exaggerate.
[hist-ire2-s9.10.5] Reports of conventions and county board meetings are evidence of the dispute between moderates and the IRB, though they are written by partisans (people taking a side).
[hist-ire2-s9.10.6] Marcus de Búrca, The GAA: A History (1980), is a full institutional history. W.F. Mandle, The Gaelic Athletic Association and Irish Nationalist Politics, 1884–1924 (1987), stresses the political and IRB links, and Paul Rouse, Sport and Ireland: A History (2015), is a later study.
[hist-ire2-s9.11.b] The GAA’s early years show both a cultural achievement and the cost of political entanglement. A good answer gives both sides and a judgement.
[hist-ire2-s9.11.0] Achievement: it created a national mass organisation open to all classes, standardised the rules of hurling and football and built a parish network.
[hist-ire2-s9.11.1] Limit: IRB entanglement, the quarrel with the Church, debts from the American tour and the Parnell split brought it close to collapse by 1891.
[hist-ire2-s9.11.2] Contemporary nationalists saw it as a school for national feeling, while unionists and the authorities saw it as Fenian, and bishops saw it as useful but dangerous.
[hist-ire2-s9.11.3] Later historians see the GAA as a central part of the cultural nationalism of the 1880s, which anticipated the Gaelic League (1893, founded by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill) and Irish Ireland.

CANDIDATES hist-ire2-s4: 4. Parnell’s fall, Redmond’s leadership and Home Rule’s rivals, 1886–1914
[hist-ire2-s4.0.b] By 1886 Parnell had built a disciplined Irish Party and an alliance with Gladstone's Liberals, and the First Home Rule Bill had just been defeated in the Commons in June 1886. Over the next four years he kept his distance from agrarian violence, and his enemies tried to destroy him with forged evidence and failed.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.0] Background: Parnell had been chairman of the Irish Parliamentary Party since 1880, and in the 1885 election the party won 85 of the 103 Irish seats. This strength is why Liberals and Conservatives alike had to take Home Rule seriously.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.1] The Plan of Campaign (October 1886) was launched by William O’Brien, John Dillon and Timothy Harrington. Tenants offered what they thought a fair rent and, if the landlord refused it, paid it into a fund instead.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.2] Parnell did not endorse the Plan, because he feared that evictions and violence would alarm British Liberals. His main concern was the Liberal alliance, so he spent much of 1887–89 away from Ireland and the daily running of the party.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.3] In March 1887 The Times began a series called Parnellism and Crime. On 18 April 1887 it printed a letter, apparently signed by Parnell, that excused the Phoenix Park murders (the killing of Lord Frederick Cavendish and T.H. Burke in May 1882), so it aimed to link him to assassination.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.4] The letter was one of several forged by Richard Pigott, a discredited Dublin journalist, who sold them to The Times.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.5] The government set up a Special Commission of three judges in 1888, by Act of Parliament, to examine the charges against Parnell and about 65 other Irish MPs. It sat from 1888 into 1889.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.6] Under cross-examination by Sir Charles Russell, Parnell’s counsel (lawyer), in February 1889 Pigott collapsed. He had misspelled the word hesitancy in the same way as in the forged letter, which exposed the forgery, and he fled to Madrid, where he shot himself on 1 March 1889.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.7] Parnell was cheered by MPs in the Commons and visited Gladstone at Hawarden in December 1889. In 1890 The Times paid him £5,000 in an out-of-court settlement.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.8] The Commission’s report (February 1890) rejected the forged letters, though it criticised the methods of the Land League (the tenant organisation of the Land War). Parnell now stood at the height of his reputation.
[hist-ire2-s4.1.b] Parnell’s private life, hidden for years, now decided his career. The divorce case of November 1890 turned the Liberal alliance and the Catholic hierarchy (the bishops) against him and split the party.
[hist-ire2-s4.1.0] Parnell had lived with Katharine O’Shea for years. Her husband Captain William O’Shea, a former Irish Party MP, filed for divorce in December 1889 and named Parnell as co-respondent (the man accused of adultery with the wife).
[hist-ire2-s4.1.1] The case was heard on 15–17 November 1890. Parnell did not contest it, so a decree nisi (the first order ending the marriage) was granted.
[hist-ire2-s4.1.2] On 25 November the Irish Party re-elected Parnell as chairman, before Gladstone’s letter to John Morley was published. The letter made Gladstone’s position plain: his leadership of the Liberals would be made almost useless if Parnell stayed.
[hist-ire2-s4.1.3] Non-conformist Liberals threatened to desert Gladstone, and the pressure on him to break with Parnell grew.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.b] Parnell refused to step aside, so the pressure from the Liberals turned into an open fight inside his own party and in the country.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.0] On 29 November Parnell issued a manifesto, To the People of Ireland. It attacked the Liberals and disclosed details of private talks with Gladstone, and it deepened the bitterness.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.1] On 3 December 1890 the Catholic bishops, with Archbishop Walsh prominent, condemned Parnell as unfit to lead. The manifesto came first, on 29 November; the bishops’ condemnation followed on 3 December.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.2] The debate in Committee Room 15 at Westminster lasted from 1 to 6 December 1890. On 6 December about 45 MPs led by Justin McCarthy walked out, leaving a minority of about 26–28 with Parnell.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.3] The anti-Parnellites (the majority) included Dillon, William O’Brien, McCarthy and the fierce critic Tim Healy, and were later organised as the Irish National Federation (1891). The Parnellites kept control of the Irish National League.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.4] Parnell’s candidates lost by-elections in North Kilkenny (December 1890), North Sligo (April 1891) and Carlow (July 1891), in vicious campaigns, which showed that the country as well as the party had turned against him.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.5] He married Katharine on 25 June 1891 and died at Brighton on 6 October 1891, aged 45. He was buried at Glasnevin, and a very large crowd attended.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.b] This part covers the years of Parnell’s peak influence, his fall and the overall judgement. The strengths and weaknesses below are the usual basis for essays on him.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.0] What he did: between 1886 and 1890 he combined an alliance with Gladstone with firm control over a disciplined party of about 85 MPs. He was born in 1846 and was 45 when he died, so his career ended while he was still in his prime.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.1] He was a participant in the Pigott and O’Shea affairs. He lost control of events after the divorce, when his refusal to step aside split the movement.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.2] Contemporary nationalists were divided: the Parnellites saw him as a leader betrayed by Liberals and bishops, while the anti-Parnellites saw a leader who put himself above the cause.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.3] Contemporary unionists (opponents of Home Rule) saw him as a dangerous agitator, until the Pigott collapse made The Times and his accusers look foolish.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.4] Strengths: party discipline, tactical skill, ability to unite Fenians (physical-force nationalists), land reformers and constitutionalists, and a clear command of Commons procedure.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.5] Weaknesses: aloofness and an autocratic style, dependence on the Liberals, ambiguity about violence in the Land War, and a private life that he did not manage, which handed his enemies the means to destroy him.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.6] Historians differ: F.S.L. Lyons in Charles Stewart Parnell (1977) stresses the tragic collision of ambition and scandal. Paul Bew in C.S. Parnell (1980) presents a conservative landlord politician whose strategy had limits.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.7] His legacy was the idea of an independent Irish party. He also left the Parnell legend of a betrayed leader, a martyr, which later nationalists and writers, including Yeats and Joyce, drew on.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.b] Gladstone’s last attempt at Home Rule was killed by the House of Lords, which could reject a bill passed by the Commons. The Irish Party then spent the 1890s in feud.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.0] The Liberals returned to office after the 1892 election with a small majority that depended on the Irish MPs. Gladstone, aged 83, introduced the Second Home Rule Bill on 13 February 1893.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.1] The Bill proposed an Irish legislature of two houses, and it kept a reduced number of Irish MPs at Westminster, to meet the objection that Ireland would otherwise be excluded from the parliament that still ruled the empire.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.2] The Commons passed the Bill in September 1893 by 301 votes to 267, after eighty-two sittings, so the elected house backed it and only the Lords stood in the way.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.3] The House of Lords threw it out on 8 September 1893 by 419 votes to 41, so a huge Conservative and Unionist majority in the Lords outweighed the Commons. Gladstone retired in March 1894.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.4] Unionists had mobilised against the Bill, so Ulster resistance was already organised, notably at the Ulster Convention in Belfast in June 1892.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.5] The Conservatives under Salisbury won the 1895 election, and Unionist governments held office for most of the next ten years. They tried to kill Home Rule with kindness, through land purchase, local government and the Congested Districts Board.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.6] In the 1890s the Irish National Federation (Dillon and McCarthy), Healy’s followers and Redmond’s small Parnellite group fought each other, and voters became disillusioned.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.7] The United Irish League was founded by William O’Brien at Westport, Co. Mayo, in January 1898 to revive the land agitation in the west. Do not confuse it with the Irish National League or the Irish National Federation.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.8] The parties reunited in January 1900, and Redmond was elected chairman in February 1900.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.b] Redmond rebuilt the party, won land reform and then used the Liberals’ dependence on his votes to put Home Rule on the statute book. The last step came at a price in Ulster.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.0] Redmond and William O’Brien took part in the Land Conference of December 1902–January 1903, with landlord representatives, which led to the Wyndham Act (1903).
[hist-ire2-s4.5.1] In May 1907 a national convention of the party rejected the Irish Council Bill, a limited devolution scheme introduced by Chief Secretary Augustine Birrell. It offered less than Home Rule, so the party refused it.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.2] Two general elections in 1910 (January and December) left the Liberals short of a majority without Irish nationalist votes. The nationalist MPs numbered in the low 80s and held the balance.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.3] The Lords rejected the People’s Budget (1909), so the Liberals, backed by Redmond’s party, passed the Parliament Act (1911). It removed the Lords’ veto, leaving only a delay of two years; it did not itself grant Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.4] Asquith introduced the Third Home Rule Bill in April 1912. It became law on 18 September 1914, and a Suspensory Act held it back until the end of the war, which Redmond accepted.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.5] Under pressure from the unionist threat, in March 1914 Asquith proposed that Ulster counties could opt out for six years. Redmond, who had resisted, accepted it, a concession he had previously refused.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.6] In June 1914 Redmond insisted that 25 of his nominees join the Irish Volunteers’ provisional committee, and the committee accepted, so he gained control of the new force.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.b] Redmond led the reunited party for eighteen years and believed that Home Rule could be won by constitutional methods and kept by loyalty to the empire. The First World War wrecked that belief.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.0] John Redmond (1856–1918) was born at Ballytrent, Co. Wexford, into a Catholic landed family. He was educated at Clongowes, became a barrister and was an MP from 1881.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.1] He stayed loyal to Parnell in 1890, which is why he led the small Parnellite group after Parnell died in 1891. He sat for Waterford City from 1891 until his death in March 1918.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.2] As a participant he reunited the party in 1900 and negotiated the Land Conference, the 1911 Parliament Act and the 1912 Bill, relying on his control of the party’s votes.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.3] Contemporary nationalists generally admired him as the leader who delivered Home Rule. Critics such as Griffith and the IRB attacked his reliance on Liberal goodwill.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.4] In August 1914 he offered the Volunteers for the defence of Ireland. His Woodenbridge speech, in Co. Wicklow on 20 September 1914, urged them to fight in the war, and this split the Irish Volunteers.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.5] The great majority followed him as the National Volunteers, and about 10,000–12,000 stayed with Eoin MacNeill’s Irish Volunteers (estimates vary).
[hist-ire2-s4.6.6] Strengths: party management, which kept the reunited party together, parliamentary skill, a genuine achievement in land and the Home Rule Act, and personal integrity.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.7] Weaknesses: dependence on the Liberals, failure to resolve the Ulster problem, acceptance of partition in principle in 1914 and poor judgement of the new separatism.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.8] Historians differ: Denis Gwynn’s The Life of John Redmond (1932) is sympathetic, and Dermot Meleady’s John Redmond: The National Leader (2013) presents a statesman wrecked by war. Others see a leader out of touch with the new generation.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.b] Separatism means full independence from Britain, whereas Home Rule meant a parliament in Dublin within the United Kingdom. Arthur Griffith proposed a separatist policy of abstention and economic self-reliance as an alternative to Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.0] Arthur Griffith, a Dublin printer and journalist, founded the newspaper The United Irishman in 1899 with William Rooney.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.1] Cumann na nGaedheal was founded in 1900 to unite groups promoting Irish language, industry and the national cause, so it gathered scattered separatist and cultural groups in one body.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.2] In The Resurrection of Hungary (1904) Griffith argued that Ireland should follow Hungary, which had won its own parliament within a dual monarchy (Austria-Hungary, in 1867) after refusing to sit in the Vienna parliament. This is abstentionism: refusing to attend the imperial parliament.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.3] At a convention in November 1905 the National Council adopted what became the Sinn Féin policy: abstain from Westminster, set up a national assembly in Dublin and protect Irish industry with tariffs (taxes on imports).
[hist-ire2-s4.7.4] The weekly Sinn Féin followed in 1906. The Dungannon Clubs of Bulmer Hobson merged with Cumann na nGaedheal in 1907 as the Sinn Féin League, and the party was fully constituted as Sinn Féin in 1908.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.5] In the North Leitrim by-election of February 1908 the Sinn Féin candidate C.J. Dolan, who had left the Irish Party, was beaten by roughly three to one.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.6] Sinn Féin lost support after 1908 and the party remained small, but its ideas about abstention and self-reliance were revived in 1917.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.b] The IRB (Irish Republican Brotherhood) was the secret Fenian organisation committed to physical force. A younger generation revived it, and the Ulster Volunteers (the unionist armed force formed in 1913) became the model for militarism, meaning armed volunteer forces used as a political instrument.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.0] Tom Clarke, a Fenian who had served about fifteen years in prison, returned from the USA in December 1907 and opened a tobacconist’s shop in Dublin.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.1] With Bulmer Hobson and Seán Mac Diarmada, Clarke revived the IRB and founded the paper Irish Freedom (1910).
[hist-ire2-s4.8.2] Eoin MacNeill’s article The North Began, published in An Claidheamh Soluis on 1 November 1913, argued that the Ulster Volunteers had shown that Irishmen could arm and organise to defend their rights.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.3] The Irish Volunteers were founded on 25 November 1913 at the Rotunda Rink in Dublin. MacNeill became chairman, and IRB men worked behind the scenes, so the separatists had a hidden influence.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.4] Cumann na mBan, a women’s organisation to support the Volunteers, was founded in April 1914.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.5] The Howth gun-running on 26 July 1914 landed about 900 rifles from Erskine Childers’s yacht Asgard. British soldiers then fired on a crowd at Bachelor’s Walk in Dublin, killing four.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.6] After Redmond’s Woodenbridge speech the Volunteers split in September 1914: the majority became the National Volunteers and a minority kept the name Irish Volunteers.
[hist-ire2-s4.9.b] The period shows constitutional nationalism at its strongest and the first serious challenge to it. Examiners reward a clear judgement backed by paired points.
[hist-ire2-s4.9.0] Achievements of the Home Rule movement: a united parliamentary party, land reform and, by 1914, a Home Rule Act on the statute book.
[hist-ire2-s4.9.1] Limits: Parnell’s fall wasted a decade, the Lords killed the 1893 Bill, and the Ulster question remained unsolved in 1914, so Home Rule was law but had not yet taken effect.
[hist-ire2-s4.9.2] Sinn Féin and the IRB offered a separatist alternative, but before 1914 they were small and divided. Their growth after 1914 owed more to war and the 1916 Rising than to their own efforts.
[hist-ire2-s4.9.3] Contemporary unionists judged Redmond a threat to the Union because Home Rule would weaken it; separatists judged him too moderate because he sought less than independence; most nationalists in 1914 supported him.
[hist-ire2-s4.9.4] Historians now often argue that Home Rule was not doomed before 1914 and that its collapse came from the war, the Rising and the conscription crisis.
