TARGET hist-eur3-s4: 4. Communism in Russia: Lenin, Stalin and the Soviet alternative
[hist-eur3-s4.0.b] Everything Lenin and Stalin did rests on a few starting ideas and facts: the Marxist theory the Bolsheviks believed, the poor country they ruled, and the party machine they used to rule it. Exam questions take these basics for granted.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.0] The Bolsheviks seized power in Petrograd on 7-8 November 1917 (25-26 October by the old Russian calendar) and set up the Council of People's Commissars (Sovnarkom, the new government) with Lenin as chairman.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.1] Marxism says history is a struggle between classes. The working class (the proletariat) would overthrow capitalism, and a 'dictatorship of the proletariat' (rule in the workers' name) would come before a classless communist society. Lenin added that a disciplined party of professional revolutionaries must lead the workers.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.2] Russia was backward: about four-fifths of the people were peasants living on the land, and it had little modern industry compared with Britain, Germany or the USA. This is why Stalin later pushed so hard for industrialisation.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.3] The party ruled through a ladder of bodies. The Party Congress and the Central Committee sat above the Politburo, the small group that made the real decisions, while the General Secretary ran the party machine and its appointments.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.4] The party controlled the soviets (the elected councils that were supposed to hold power) and the government, so whoever controlled the party controlled the state. This is why the post of General Secretary mattered so much in the succession struggle.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.5] The Western (Gregorian) calendar was adopted in February 1918, so every date here is a Western date unless stated, which avoids confusion with the old Russian calendar.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.6] Communism, as used here, is the Marxist ideology and system in which the state owns land, industry and banks and one party claims to rule for the working class.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.b] Lenin held on to power by force. In these years the Bolsheviks created a secret police, banned their rivals and won a civil war, and these methods became the foundation of the Soviet state.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.0] The Cheka, the Bolshevik secret police, was set up in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky. It used arrest and execution against opponents, including the Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs) and Mensheviks, rival socialist parties.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.1] Lenin was shot and wounded on 30 August 1918. The Red Terror, a campaign of mass arrests and executions, was proclaimed in September 1918.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.2] The Constituent Assembly (the elected parliament) met once in January 1918 and was dispersed by force. This is background, but it shows the Bolsheviks would not share power.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.3] Kadets (a liberal party), Mensheviks and SRs were suppressed and rival parties banned during the civil war. By its end the Communist Party, as the Bolsheviks were renamed in 1918, was the only legal party, so one-party rule was set in place.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.4] The Russian Civil War (1918-1921, with fighting continuing in the Far East until 1922) pitted the Red Army, led by Leon Trotsky as Commissar for War from 1918, against the White armies (anti-Bolshevik forces) and foreign intervention by Britain, France, Japan and the USA.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.5] The White leaders were Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich and Wrangel. The Reds won because they held the central position, were united and disciplined, and used terror, while the Whites were divided.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.b] To win the civil war Lenin ran the economy like a army supply system. The result was a crisis in 1920-1921 that threatened the Bolsheviks' own supporters.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.0] War Communism (1918-1921) nationalised industry and banks (took them into state ownership), forced peasants to hand over grain at fixed low prices (requisitioning) and rationed food to towns. Private trade was banned, which cut the incentive to grow food.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.1] Peasants hated requisitioning because the state took their grain for little in return. For example, the Tambov peasant rising of 1920-1921, led by Alexander Antonov, was the best known of many peasant revolts, and strikes and economic collapse followed, because the towns were starved of food and goods.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.2] The famine of 1921-1922 on the Volga and elsewhere killed about 5 million people. The American Relief Administration fed millions, which showed how far the policy had failed.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.3] In March 1921 sailors and workers at the Kronstadt naval base near Petrograd rose against Bolshevik rule. They had backed the Bolsheviks in 1917, and now demanded free elections to the soviets, freedom of speech and an end to the monopoly of power.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.4] The Red Army under Tukhachevsky crushed the rising by crossing the frozen Gulf of Finland. The rebels were sailors and workers who had backed the Bolsheviks in 1917.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.b] Faced with revolt, Lenin retreated. The New Economic Policy (NEP) loosened the economy to save the regime, while the party tightened its own discipline, and the state took the form it kept until 1991.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.0] At the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921 the Bolsheviks adopted the NEP. A tax in kind (a fixed share of the crop) replaced grain requisitioning, peasants could sell their surplus, and small private trade and small-scale industry were allowed.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.1] The state kept the 'commanding heights' of the economy, meaning large industry, banks and foreign trade. This is why the NEP was a retreat to save the regime, not a new socialist policy.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.2] The same congress banned factions, so no organised groups could exist inside the party. This was meant to protect unity at a dangerous time, and so Stalin later used the rule against his rivals.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.3] The NEP restored food supplies and output, and the economy recovered by the mid-1920s. It also produced Nepmen (private traders) and better-off peasants called kulaks, so many Communists saw it as a retreat from socialism.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.4] Lenin's slogan of December 1920 was 'Communism is Soviet power plus the electrification of the whole country'. GOELRO, the state electrification plan of 1920, aimed so that electric power could drive industry and modernise a backward country.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.5] The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was formally created on 30 December 1922, bringing together the Russian, Ukrainian, Belorussian and Transcaucasian republics.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.6] Lenin suffered strokes in May 1922, December 1922 and March 1923, and died on 21 January 1924. His embalmed body was displayed in a mausoleum in Red Square, Moscow, and Petrograd was renamed Leningrad that January. He left no clear successor.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.7] Cheka, GPU/OGPU and NKVD are different names for the police at different dates: the Cheka (1917), the GPU/OGPU (1922-34) and the NKVD from 1934. Do not call the 1920s police the NKVD.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.b] Lenin's illness opened a contest to rule the USSR. Stalin won not because he was the best known, but because he controlled the party machine and played his rivals against each other.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.0] Stalin was Commissar for Nationalities from 1917 to 1923 and General Secretary from April 1922. The post gave him control of party appointments, and the 'Lenin enrolment' of 1924 brought in large numbers of new members who owed their places to the party leadership.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.1] Lenin's Testament, dictated from December 1922 to January 1923, criticised all the leading candidates (Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin and Stalin). Its postscript of January 1923 called Stalin 'too rude' and proposed his removal as General Secretary, but it was read to delegates in 1924 and not published.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.2] Trotsky was brilliant and had created the Red Army, but he had joined the Bolsheviks only in 1917, seemed arrogant and was feared as a possible 'Bonaparte' (a general who might seize power).
[hist-eur3-s4.4.3] For example, Zinoviev led the Leningrad party and the Comintern, Kamenev the Moscow party, and Bukharin edited Pravda and was the party's leading theorist.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.4] The policy debate: Trotsky's 'permanent revolution' (spreading revolution abroad) and rapid industrialisation, against Stalin's 'socialism in one country' of 1924. Stalin's idea appealed to party members tired of waiting for world revolution.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.b] With the contest set, Stalin used the party machine to remove his rivals in turn. He first allied with some against others, then adopted the policy of those he had beaten.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.0] Stalin, Zinoviev and Kamenev formed a triumvirate (a ruling trio) against Trotsky in 1923-25. When Zinoviev and Kamenev joined Trotsky in the United Opposition of 1926, it was defeated, Trotsky and Zinoviev were expelled in November 1927, and the Fifteenth Congress expelled the opposition in December 1927.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.1] Trotsky was exiled to Alma-Ata, Kazakhstan, in January 1928 and deported from the USSR in February 1929. Ramon Mercader, an NKVD agent, killed him in Mexico in August 1940 on Stalin's orders.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.2] The Right Opposition (Bukharin, Alexei Rykov, head of the government, and Mikhail Tomsky, trade unions) backed the NEP and gradual growth.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.3] After the grain procurement crisis of 1927-28 Stalin adopted forced industrialisation and grain collection, so the Right was defeated in 1928-29, with Bukharin removed from the Politburo in November 1929.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.4] Stalin had beaten the Left with the Right's help, then took up the Left's policy. He won because of his control of the party machine, his rivals' mistakes and underestimation of him, the ban on factions and his skill in using one faction against another.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.b] Stalin made industrialisation the centre of Soviet policy. The Five-Year Plans were central targets set by the state, and they changed the landscape and the lives of workers.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.0] Stalin wanted to end backwardness, build socialism, strengthen the USSR against attack and secure his power.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.1] In February 1931 he warned managers: 'We are fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries. We must make good this distance in ten years. Either we do it, or they will crush us.'
[hist-eur3-s4.6.2] Gosplan, the state planning agency, drew up the First Five-Year Plan, which ran from October 1928 and was declared fulfilled in four years and three months, at the end of 1932. It aimed to turn the USSR into an industrial power.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.3] Targets were set for coal, steel, oil, electricity and machinery, with heavy industry given priority over consumer goods, because heavy industry built the economy and the army.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.4] The showpiece projects were Magnitogorsk, a steel city in the southern Urals built from 1929, the Dnieper dam in Ukraine (completed 1932) and the Moscow Metro (opened 1935).
[hist-eur3-s4.6.5] Magnitogorsk became a symbol of the drive for industrialisation and of the labour that went into it, because it was built quickly by workers drawn from the villages.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.6] Labour came from the villages. Wage differences and bonuses rewarded effort, discipline for lateness and absence was harsh, internal passports controlled movement, and propaganda praised 'shock workers' (workers who exceeded targets).
[hist-eur3-s4.6.7] Alexei Stakhanov, a Donbas coal miner, was reported on 31 August 1935 to have cut 102 tonnes of coal in one shift, about fourteen times the norm. The shift was specially prepared with helpers, and the movement was resented because it raised the norms (the output required) for ordinary workers.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.8] Engineers and managers were blamed for failures as 'wreckers' (people accused of sabotage), beginning with the Shakhty trial of 1928, when engineers in the Donbas mining town of Shakhty were accused of sabotage. This gave Stalin scapegoats for the plan's difficulties.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.b] The plans brought real growth but at enormous human cost, so examiners expect every achievement to be paired with its price.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.0] The Second Five-Year Plan (1933-1937) was more realistic and gave some attention to consumer goods and transport. The Third (1938-1941) shifted to armaments as war approached and was cut short by the German invasion in 1941. The economy was militarised by the late 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.1] Steel output rose from about 4 million tonnes in 1928 to about 18 million in 1940, and coal from about 35 million tonnes to about 165 million. These are official figures and some were inflated.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.2] There were large rises in oil and electricity too, and the USSR was one of the world's leading industrial powers by 1940. New industrial regions in the Urals and Siberia proved vital in the war.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.3] The costs were high: real wages and living standards fell in the early 1930s, housing was overcrowded, goods were of poor quality, and there was waste and inflated statistics.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.4] Forced labour by Gulag prisoners was used on projects such as the White Sea-Baltic Canal, built in 1931-33, and many of the prisoners died.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.b] Stalin saw the peasants as the obstacle to industrialisation. Collectivisation was meant to give the state control of grain, but it caused one of the worst disasters of the period.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.0] The reasons were the grain procurement crisis of 1927-28, the need to feed the growing towns and to export grain to pay for machinery, a wish to bring the peasants under state and party control, and ideological hostility to private farming and the kulaks.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.1] Collectivisation was launched at the end of 1929 when Stalin called for the 'liquidation of the kulaks as a class' (December 1929). Peasants were forced into kolkhozy (collective farms where land and livestock were pooled and members shared income) that delivered quotas to the state.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.2] Sovkhozy were state farms with paid workers. Machine tractor stations (MTS) supplied machinery to collective farms and also gave the party political control over them.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.3] Dekulakisation was the campaign against kulaks, the better-off peasants. In practice the label was applied to anyone who resisted, and families were shot, sent to camps or deported to Siberia, the Urals and Kazakhstan.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.4] Peasants resisted by slaughtering livestock, burning crops and revolting and there were also women's protests.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.5] In March 1930 Stalin's article 'Dizzy with Success' in Pravda blamed local officials for excesses and allowed peasants to leave the collectives. Many left, but pressure resumed later in 1930.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.6] The famine of 1932-1933 came when high requisitions were taken despite poor harvests. It killed several million people, mainly in Ukraine (where it is called the Holodomor), the North Caucasus, the Volga and Kazakhstan. Estimates differ widely, and whether it was a genocide is debated.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.7] The law of 7 August 1932 punished theft of collective farm property, movement was restricted, the famine was officially denied and grain was still exported.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.b] Collectivisation succeeded in giving the state control of grain, but it did not improve farming.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.0] Over 90 per cent of peasant households were collectivised by the late 1930s, so the state controlled the grain supply.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.1] Livestock numbers and farm output fell and took years to recover. Output was below 1928 levels for years, so the main gain was control, not abundance.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.2] Peasants' small private plots produced a large share of the country's vegetables, milk and meat, which shows how weak the collective farms were.
[hist-eur3-s4.10.b] A personality cult presents a leader as infallible and heroic through propaganda, ritual and images. Stalin's cult built on the Lenin cult that followed Lenin's death in 1924, which gave it a precedent.
[hist-eur3-s4.10.0] The cult began with the celebrations of Stalin's fiftieth birthday in December 1929, when he was hailed as Lenin's heir, a claim that gave him legitimacy against rivals.
[hist-eur3-s4.10.1] Official history was rewritten. The History of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (Bolsheviks): Short Course of 1938 put Stalin at the centre of the revolution, while Trotsky and other fallen leaders were written out and removed from photographs.
[hist-eur3-s4.10.2] Portraits, statues, poems, songs and films presented Stalin as the wise 'father of the peoples' and Lenin's true heir. Towns and places were named after him, and Tsaritsyn was renamed Stalingrad in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s4.10.3] The cult gave Stalin legitimacy as Lenin's successor, produced unity behind one leader during upheaval, and was a substitute for religious and tsarist loyalties.
[hist-eur3-s4.10.4] The Stalin Constitution of December 1936 promised universal suffrage, a secret ballot and civil rights on paper, while one-party rule and the NKVD were unchanged.
[hist-eur3-s4.11.b] Behind the cult stood a machine of fear. Control of everyday life by the secret police is why historians call the USSR a leading example of totalitarianism, a state that tries to control every part of public and private life through one party, an ideology, secret police, terror and propaganda.
[hist-eur3-s4.11.0] The OGPU was absorbed into the NKVD in 1934, and the NKVD ran the secret police and the Gulag camp system, whose prisoners built canals, railways and mines.
[hist-eur3-s4.11.1] The Gulag held more than a million prisoners at a time by the late 1930s, and millions passed through it. Many were peasants and political prisoners.
[hist-eur3-s4.11.2] Internal passports, introduced from December 1932, and registration of residence controlled movement, so peasants were tied to the countryside and 'undesirables' were kept out of the cities.
[hist-eur3-s4.11.3] The Kirov murder, the Moscow trials, the army purge and the Great Terror of 1936-1938 belong to a separate topic. There is more on this in Stalin's show trials.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.b] The regime tried to remake society as well as the economy, aiming at a new Soviet man and woman who valued collective achievement. Some changes widened opportunity while others closed freedom.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.0] Mass literacy campaigns and the expansion of schools and technical colleges trained workers and engineers. After 1931 schools returned to discipline, exams and uniforms, and education also served as indoctrination.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.1] The Young Pioneers and the Komsomol, the Communist youth league, brought children and young people into party organisations, so the party could shape them from an early age.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.2] Women gained legal equality, easy divorce and legal abortion (1920). The Zhenotdel, the party's women's department, ran from 1919 to 1930, and Alexandra Kollontai was a leading Bolshevik campaigner for women's rights.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.3] Women were drawn into industry and the professions, but carried a 'double burden' of work and housework. A 1936 law banned abortion and made divorce harder, to raise the birth rate.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.4] Church property was seized, clergy arrested and churches closed or destroyed. Moscow's Cathedral of Christ the Saviour was blown up in December 1931, the League of the Godless was founded in 1925 and became the League of the Militant Godless in 1929, and atheism was taught in schools.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.5] The 1920s were a time of experiment, for example Sergei Eisenstein's film Battleship Potemkin (1925). In April 1932 a Central Committee decree dissolved independent literary groups and set up a single Union of Soviet Writers.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.6] The First Congress of Soviet Writers in 1934 adopted socialist realism (art showing the heroic building of socialism) as the official method, with Maxim Gorky prominent. Cultural life was controlled by the party.
[hist-eur3-s4.12.7] Pravda attacked Shostakovich's opera Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk in January 1936, and the poet Osip Mandelstam was arrested in 1934 for a poem mocking Stalin and died in a transit camp in 1938.
[hist-eur3-s4.13.b] The regime sought collective values, and its claims of progress during the Depression made it attractive to some foreign observers, while others were horrified. Opinion at the time was divided.
[hist-eur3-s4.13.0] The 'new Soviet man' was a citizen with collective values. Heroes of labour, parades and posters, and public monuments expressed a culture of collective achievement.
[hist-eur3-s4.13.1] The Soviet alternative appealed in the Depression because the West had mass unemployment and falling output, while the USSR claimed full employment, rapid growth and planning. The planned economy looked attractive to some socialists, intellectuals and trade unionists in Britain, France and the USA.
[hist-eur3-s4.13.2] Lincoln Steffens, an American journalist, said after visiting in 1919: 'I have seen the future, and it works.' George Bernard Shaw visited and met Stalin in 1931, and some Western visitors toured factories and collective farms and returned with favourable reports.
[hist-eur3-s4.13.3] Sidney and Beatrice Webb, British socialists, published Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation? in 1935, presenting the system as a model for social planning.
[hist-eur3-s4.13.4] Walter Duranty of the New York Times denied there was a famine. Gareth Jones and Malcolm Muggeridge reported it in 1933 and were attacked for doing so.
[hist-eur3-s4.13.5] Liberals, conservatives, the churches and many socialists pointed to the famine, the lack of freedom and the show trials. Fear of communism also helped fascism in Europe.
[hist-eur3-s4.13.6] Recognition by the United States in November 1933 and membership of the League of Nations in September 1934 gave the regime growing respectability and international standing.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.b] Soviet foreign policy swung between isolation, co-operation with Germany, collective security and finally a pact with Hitler, shaped by fear of attack.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.0] The Treaty of Rapallo with Germany (April 1922) restored relations between two outcast states, and secret military co-operation, including German rearmament on Soviet soil, followed.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.1] The Comintern (Communist International), founded in March 1919, controlled foreign communist parties. Its 'social fascist' line against Social Democrats split the German left before 1933.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.2] After Hitler's rise the USSR turned to collective security (joint resistance to aggression). Maxim Litvinov was Foreign Commissar from 1930 to 1939, the USSR joined the League in 1934, signed pacts with France and Czechoslovakia in May 1935 and backed Popular Fronts of left-wing co-operation.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.3] Soviet aid went to the Spanish Republic from October 1936, but the USSR was excluded from the Munich Conference in September 1938, and Molotov replaced Litvinov in May 1939.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.4] The Nazi-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact of 23 August 1939, signed by Molotov and Ribbentrop, had a secret protocol dividing eastern Europe. It was a non-aggression pact, not a military alliance, and it gave Stalin time and a buffer.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.5] The Red Army entered eastern Poland on 17 September 1939. In spring 1940 the NKVD shot about 22,000 Polish officers, police and others in the Katyn massacre, and the USSR blamed Germany until 1990.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.6] The Winter War with Finland (November 1939 - March 1940) exposed Red Army weakness, and Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania were annexed in 1940.
[hist-eur3-s4.14.7] The USSR was unready in June 1941 because Stalin ignored repeated warnings of a German attack, and the army was weakened by the purges.
[hist-eur3-s4.15.b] The German invasion tested the Soviet system. After disastrous early errors, Stalin took charge and the state was reorganised for total war.
[hist-eur3-s4.15.0] Germany invaded on 22 June 1941 in Operation Barbarossa. The Red Army suffered huge early losses of men, territory and aircraft, and Leningrad was besieged from September 1941 to January 1944.
[hist-eur3-s4.15.1] Stalin withdrew briefly in late June 1941 before taking charge. He chaired the State Defence Committee (GKO), set up on 30 June 1941 to direct the war economy, and in a radio broadcast on 3 July 1941 addressed listeners as 'brothers and sisters'.
[hist-eur3-s4.15.2] Stalin became Supreme Commander in August 1941 and stayed in Moscow during the German advance of autumn 1941. The German advance was stopped before Moscow, and a Soviet counter-offensive under Georgy Zhukov followed in December 1941.
[hist-eur3-s4.15.3] About 1,500 industrial enterprises were moved east to the Urals, Siberia and Central Asia, mostly in the second half of 1941. There was total mobilisation of labour, including women.
[hist-eur3-s4.15.4] Output of tanks and aircraft rose sharply by 1942 and soon exceeded Germany's, including the T-34 tank.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.b] After the crisis, the Soviet Union turned the tide, but the regime kept terror alongside appeals to patriotism, and the war left it a superpower.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.0] The Battle of Stalingrad, from August 1942 to 2 February 1943, was the turning point in the east. A Soviet counter-encirclement in November 1942 trapped the German Sixth Army under Paulus, which surrendered.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.1] Kursk in July 1943 followed, so the Soviets kept the initiative, and the Red Army advanced west to Berlin in April-May 1945, with German surrender in May 1945.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.2] Order 227 of 28 July 1942, 'Not one step back', led to blocking detachments behind the front line and penal battalions for those who retreated. The NKVD enforced discipline behind the lines, including executions of deserters and punishment of soldiers who had been captured.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.3] Lend-Lease supplies from Britain and the USA, such as trucks, food and equipment, were a significant support to the Red Army, because they eased shortages of transport and food.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.4] Stalin appealed to patriotism over ideology. The conflict was called the 'Great Patriotic War' from 1941, appeals were made to Russian history and heroes, the Orthodox Church was allowed a patriarch again in September 1943 and the Comintern was dissolved in May 1943.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.5] Whole peoples accused of collaboration were deported: Volga Germans in 1941, Chechens and Ingush in February 1944 and Crimean Tatars in May 1944. Returning prisoners of war were treated as suspects and many were sent to camps.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.6] Soviet deaths in the Second World War were about 27 million, military and civilian combined, far more than any other combatant, which shows the scale of the sacrifice behind the victory.
[hist-eur3-s4.16.7] Stalin made disastrous early errors by ignoring warnings and forbidding retreat in 1941, then relied more on able generals such as Zhukov. He was given the rank of Generalissimo in June 1945, and most German army losses were on the Eastern Front.
[hist-eur3-s4.17.b] By 1945 the USSR had survived, and victory was turned into political capital for Stalin.
[hist-eur3-s4.17.0] The USSR emerged as a superpower because the Red Army was in control of eastern Europe.
[hist-eur3-s4.17.1] Victory was used to strengthen Stalin's authority and to claim that the system had been proved right.
[hist-eur3-s4.17.2] Stalin attended wartime conferences at Tehran (1943), Yalta (February 1945, with Churchill and Roosevelt) and Potsdam (July-August 1945, with Truman, and Churchill then Attlee).
[hist-eur3-s4.18.b] Lenin led the Bolsheviks from the 1903 split until 1924, and he created the institutions that Stalin inherited.
[hist-eur3-s4.18.0] Born Vladimir Ulyanov in 1870 at Simbirsk, he had an elder brother, Alexander, who was executed in 1887 for plotting against Tsar Alexander III.
[hist-eur3-s4.18.1] He returned to Russia in April 1917 and issued the April Theses, calling for power to pass to the soviets. He led the Bolsheviks in seizing power in October 1917 and headed Sovnarkom, the first Soviet government.
[hist-eur3-s4.18.2] He then set up the Cheka and terror, introduced War Communism, and made the retreat to the NEP in 1921 and created the USSR in 1922.
[hist-eur3-s4.18.3] He shaped events as architect of the one-party state, but was flexible enough to retreat to the NEP to save the regime. He also banned factions and wrote his Testament.
[hist-eur3-s4.18.4] He was a participant, not a witness: he led and decided at every stage until his strokes.
[hist-eur3-s4.18.5] To the party he was the unquestioned authority on Marxism, while to opponents (Mensheviks, SRs, Kronstadt sailors) he was a dictator. After his death in 1924 the Lenin cult grew.
[hist-eur3-s4.18.6] Historians divide: the continuity view (Richard Pipes is a leading example) holds that Lenin's terror, one-party rule and ban on factions made Stalinism possible, while the discontinuity view holds that the NEP and the Testament show a different path that Stalin abandoned.
[hist-eur3-s4.19.b] Stalin ruled from the late 1920s until his death in 1953, and he transformed the Soviet economy and society and built a cult around himself.
[hist-eur3-s4.19.0] Born Ioseb Dzhugashvili in 1878 at Gori, Georgia (Soviet sources later gave 1879), he trained for the priesthood at the Tiflis seminary. He became a Bolshevik revolutionary, was several times exiled to Siberia, and took the name Stalin, from the Russian for steel.
[hist-eur3-s4.19.1] He became General Secretary in 1922 and defeated his rivals by 1929. He directed the Five-Year Plans from 1928 and collectivisation from 1929, carried out the Terror of 1936-38, signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact in 1939 and was war leader from 1941 to 1945. He died on 5 March 1953.
[hist-eur3-s4.19.2] He drove a 'revolution from above', but was also shaped by the grain crisis, fear of foreign attack and the Bolshevik tradition of terror.
[hist-eur3-s4.19.3] Contemporaries differed. Lenin warned against him in the Testament, the cult called him 'father of the peoples', Western admirers such as Shaw and the Webbs praised him, and critics included Trotsky in exile.
[hist-eur3-s4.19.4] Nikita Khrushchev denounced the cult in his secret speech to the 20th Party Congress in February 1956, which is why the cult is seen as ending officially only after his death.
[hist-eur3-s4.19.5] Robert Conquest (The Great Terror, 1968, and The Harvest of Sorrow, 1986, on collectivisation and the famine) took the totalitarian view. Isaac Deutscher (Stalin: A Political Biography, 1949) saw a brutal moderniser, and social historians such as Sheila Fitzpatrick (Everyday Stalinism, 1999) studied everyday life and pressures from below.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.b] Judgements of the Soviet system balance achievements against the human costs of its methods. Examiners reward a stated verdict in the introduction and conclusion that pairs each achievement with its limit.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.0] Lenin, achievement: he won the civil war, held the state together and the NEP produced recovery.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.1] Lenin, limit: terror, one-party rule, the famine of 1921-1922 and Kronstadt.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.2] Industry, achievement: an industrial and military power by 1941, so it could out-produce Germany in the war.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.3] Industry, limit: low living standards, waste, forced labour and inflated statistics.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.4] Agriculture, achievement: state control of grain, so towns and exports could be supplied, and mechanisation through the MTS.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.5] Agriculture, failure: the famine, the destruction of the kulaks and output below 1928 levels for years.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.6] Society, achievement: literacy, education, and women's work and professional opportunities. Limit: no political freedom, persecution of religion and the arts, and the 1936 reversal on family policy.
[hist-eur3-s4.20.7] Foreign policy and war: survival, victory and superpower status, against the Nazi-Soviet Pact leaving the USSR unready in 1941 and about 27 million dead. Views differ: the totalitarian school against social historians, and continuity against a break between Lenin and Stalin.
