TARGET hist-eur2-s3: 3. Wilhelm II and Weltpolitik; case study: the naval policy of Wilhelm II, 1890–1914
[hist-eur2-s3.0.b] To judge Wilhelm II's policies you need to know what he inherited: a constitution that gave the Kaiser great power, a cautious foreign policy that he rejected, and a world in which Britain ruled the seas.
[hist-eur2-s3.0.0] Under the 1871 constitution the Kaiser appointed and dismissed the Chancellor, was supreme commander of the armed forces and directed foreign policy, so a strong-willed Kaiser could in theory rule personally.
[hist-eur2-s3.0.1] The Reichstag (the elected parliament, chosen by universal male suffrage) voted laws and the budget, so every Navy Law and every naval tax needed a Reichstag majority, however much the Kaiser wanted a fleet.
[hist-eur2-s3.0.2] Bismarck called Germany a satiated power after 1871, meaning it wanted no more territory in Europe. He kept France isolated, and Germany's first colonies date only from 1884–85, so his policy was cautious and centred on Europe.
[hist-eur2-s3.0.3] Wilhelm II forced Bismarck to resign in March 1890 (the resignation letter was dated 18 March and accepted on 20 March). This gives the contrast with Weltpolitik, the later policy of world ambitions.
[hist-eur2-s3.0.4] Britain was an island empire that depended on sea trade and imported food, so it treated naval supremacy as a matter of survival. The two-power standard, set by the Naval Defence Act of 1889, required the Royal Navy to be at least equal to the next two largest navies combined.
[hist-eur2-s3.0.5] Britain stayed out of alliances in a policy called splendid isolation, and its first peacetime alliance of this era was with Japan in January 1902.
[hist-eur2-s3.0.6] Europe was already divided into two blocs: the Triple Alliance of 1882 (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the Franco-Russian alliance (a military convention in 1892, an alliance in 1894).
[hist-eur2-s3.0.7] Naval terms: a battleship (or capital ship) is the heaviest warship and a cruiser is lighter and faster. A battle fleet is built to fight a decisive battle in home waters, whereas a cruiser fleet protects trade and colonies overseas.
[hist-eur2-s3.1.b] Wilhelm II was Kaiser from 1888 to 1918. His family ties, his disability and his restless temperament shaped how he ruled and why he loved the navy.
[hist-eur2-s3.1.0] He was born in Berlin on 27 January 1859, the eldest son of Crown Prince Frederick (later Frederick III) and Victoria, the eldest daughter of Queen Victoria, which made him her grandson. A breech birth damaged his left arm, which stayed withered and shorter.
[hist-eur2-s3.1.1] 1888 was the year of the three emperors. Wilhelm I died on 9 March, Frederick III reigned 99 days and died on 15 June, and Wilhelm II became Kaiser that day, aged 29.
[hist-eur2-s3.1.2] Do not confuse the three rulers: Wilhelm I was Kaiser from 1871 to 1888, Frederick III only in 1888, and Wilhelm II from 1888 to 1918.
[hist-eur2-s3.1.3] He was energetic, intelligent, impulsive, theatrical, militaristic and easily influenced, so his enthusiasms could swing quickly and his public words often embarrassed his ministers.
[hist-eur2-s3.1.4] His feelings about Britain mixed love and hate. George V and Tsarina Alexandra (the wife of Nicholas II) were his first cousins, and Nicholas II was a more distant relative, while he admired the Royal Navy but resented British power.
[hist-eur2-s3.1.5] His passion for the navy was long-standing: Queen Victoria made him an honorary Admiral of the Fleet of the Royal Navy in 1889, and he attended the Spithead naval review that year.
[hist-eur2-s3.1.6] Contemporaries were divided. Nationalists and navalists admired him as the symbol of Germany's rise, abroad he was regarded as erratic and a threat (foreign cartoonists drew him as a menace), and after 1908 he was criticised at home too.
[hist-eur2-s3.2.b] Wilhelm wanted to rule himself, which made a clash with the old Chancellor almost certain. After Bismarck went, a new course under Chancellor Caprivi changed German policy at home and abroad.
[hist-eur2-s3.2.0] Wilhelm's aim was personal rule (Persönliches Regiment): to govern himself rather than through a strong Chancellor. He forced Bismarck to resign in March 1890, a moment Punch marked with John Tenniel's cartoon "Dropping the Pilot".
[hist-eur2-s3.2.1] How far he really achieved personal rule is debated, because the Reichstag held the budget and his own changeable moods undermined his control.
[hist-eur2-s3.2.2] His Chancellors were Caprivi (1890–94), Prince Hohenlohe-Schillingsfürst (1894–1900), Bernhard von Bülow (1900–09) and Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg (1909–17).
[hist-eur2-s3.2.3] The new course (Neuer Kurs) was Caprivi's policy of 1890–94, not Weltpolitik. Abroad, the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia was not renewed in 1890, and the Heligoland-Zanzibar Treaty with Britain (1 July 1890) gave Germany Heligoland and Britain Zanzibar.
[hist-eur2-s3.2.4] At home Wilhelm began by easing the Anti-Socialist Law, which lapsed on 30 September 1890, and sponsored the workers' protection law of 1891 (Sunday rest, limits on child and women's labour).
[hist-eur2-s3.2.5] Caprivi's trade treaties of 1891–94 lowered tariffs on grain, which angered the agrarians (the landowners who wanted grain protected from foreign competition).
[hist-eur2-s3.2.6] The SPD kept growing, so Wilhelm then turned against it, and Caprivi fell in 1894.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.b] Wilhelm liked to act as his own diplomat, but his impulsive words and private deals repeatedly damaged Germany's image. Three separate indiscretions, and one failed treaty, show the pattern.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.0] First came the Kruger telegram of 3 January 1896, which angered British opinion because it congratulated the Boer president on defeating the Jameson Raid into the Transvaal.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.1] Second was the Hun speech, given on 27 July 1900 to troops leaving Bremerhaven for China. He told them to give no quarter and to make the name German feared as the Huns had made theirs, which damaged Germany's image abroad.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.2] Third was the Daily Telegraph interview, published on 28 October 1908. He said most Germans were hostile to Britain but that he was its friend, and claimed he had sent Queen Victoria a plan for the Boer War.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.3] He also said the fleet was aimed at the Far East, which offended Japan as well as Britain.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.4] There was uproar in the Reichstag and press across all parties. Bülow did not defend him, Wilhelm promised in November 1908 to respect his constitutional responsibilities, and he suffered a nervous collapse. He then withdrew from many public roles.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.5] He never fully trusted Bülow again, and Bülow resigned in July 1909. The direct cause was the defeat of his finance reform, but the loss of the Kaiser's trust meant he had no support to carry on.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.6] Wilhelm also practised personal diplomacy through the Willy-Nicky letters and telegrams, written in English, to Nicholas II.
[hist-eur2-s3.3.7] In July 1905 the two men signed the Treaty of Björkö, a defensive alliance, on Nicholas's yacht. It was never put into effect because the Russian government and Bülow objected, which shows how limited personal diplomacy was.
[hist-eur2-s3.4.b] Wilhelm's last years of real influence run from the war council of 1912 to defeat in 1918. Historians still argue about how far he controlled events and how much blame he carries.
[hist-eur2-s3.4.0] On 8 December 1912 Wilhelm met his army and navy chiefs, Moltke and Tirpitz, without the Chancellor, after a British warning that Britain would not stand aside if France were attacked.
[hist-eur2-s3.4.1] Moltke said war the sooner the better, Tirpitz asked for more time for the navy, and no decision for war was recorded. Röhl and Fischer's followers treat the meeting as evidence of planning, while others, such as Clark, see bellicose talk without consequence.
[hist-eur2-s3.4.2] In July 1914 he gave Austria-Hungary the blank cheque (5–6 July).
[hist-eur2-s3.4.3] He lost the army's confidence in autumn 1918. Chancellor Prince Max of Baden announced his abdication in Berlin on 9 November 1918, he crossed into the Netherlands on 10 November, and he abdicated formally on 28 November.
[hist-eur2-s3.4.4] He lived at Doorn in the Netherlands from 1920 and died there on 4 June 1941.
[hist-eur2-s3.4.5] He was a participant, not a bystander: he chose Tirpitz and Bülow and backed the fleet throughout. Yet he worked within pressures from nationalist groups, industry and the military, so he both shaped events and was shaped by them.
[hist-eur2-s3.4.6] John Röhl (a three-volume biography, 1993–2008) argues that Wilhelm exercised genuine personal rule and bears heavy responsibility for 1914.
[hist-eur2-s3.4.7] Christopher Clark (Kaiser Wilhelm II, 2000, and The Sleepwalkers, 2012) portrays him as erratic and often sidelined, with responsibility shared among all the Great Powers, while Fritz Fischer's school places German leadership at the centre of 1914.
[hist-eur2-s3.5.b] Weltpolitik, meaning world policy, was the attempt to turn Germany from a European power into a world power. It grew from economic need, domestic politics and national pride.
[hist-eur2-s3.5.0] Weltpolitik was the attempt from about 1897 to make Germany a world power through colonies, trade, influence and a battle fleet, as opposed to Bismarck's Europe-centred (continental) policy. It is usually dated from 1897, when Bülow and Tirpitz were appointed.
[hist-eur2-s3.5.1] In January 1896, at the 25th anniversary of the Reich, Wilhelm said the German Empire had become a world empire. In 1898 at Stettin he declared "Our future lies on the water."
[hist-eur2-s3.5.2] Economic causes: by 1900 Germany had overtaken Britain in steel production, and by 1914 its merchant fleet was second only to Britain's. A growing industry needed markets, raw materials and secure sea routes, and feared being shut out as Britain, France and Russia expanded.
[hist-eur2-s3.5.3] Sammlungspolitik (the politics of rallying together), associated with Johannes von Miquel, the Prussian finance minister, from 1897, aimed to unite industrialists, landowners and the middle classes behind the monarchy and against the SPD.
[hist-eur2-s3.5.4] Social imperialism is a historians' term, used by Wehler and others, for using overseas expansion to divert domestic discontent. Conservatives and many liberals hoped imperial success would unite the classes behind the monarchy and distract from the SPD.
[hist-eur2-s3.5.5] Pressure groups pushed the policy: the Colonial Society (founded 1887), the Pan-German League (founded 1891, named the Pan-German League from 1894) and the German Navy League (founded April 1898).
[hist-eur2-s3.5.6] Nationalism in Germany meant pride in the new Reich and a belief that a great nation needed colonies, world influence and a fleet. Germany felt late to empire and resented the empires of Britain and France.
[hist-eur2-s3.5.7] Social Darwinist ideas (that nations compete like species and only the strong survive) made rivalry between nations seem natural.
[hist-eur2-s3.6.b] Weltpolitik was carried out through a series of moves between 1896 and 1903. Each gave Germany little, but each alarmed another power.
[hist-eur2-s3.6.0] Bernhard von Bülow was appointed Foreign Secretary in June 1897 (formally in October) and was Chancellor from 17 October 1900 to July 1909. In his Reichstag speech of 6 December 1897 he said Germany wanted to put no one in the shade but demanded "a place in the sun".
[hist-eur2-s3.6.1] On 3 January 1896 Wilhelm sent the Kruger telegram, congratulating President Paul Kruger of the Transvaal on defeating the Jameson Raid (29 December 1895 to 2 January 1896) without appealing to the help of friendly powers, as it was reported. British press and public were outraged.
[hist-eur2-s3.6.2] Two German Catholic missionaries were murdered in Shandong province, China, on 1 November 1897. German ships seized Kiaochow Bay, with the port of Tsingtao, that month, and a 99-year lease was signed in March 1898.
[hist-eur2-s3.6.3] In the Boxer rising of 1900 the German minister in Peking, Baron von Ketteler, was killed in June. Field Marshal von Waldersee commanded the international relief force, which arrived after Peking had already been relieved.
[hist-eur2-s3.6.4] In 1899 Germany bought the Caroline, Mariana (except Guam) and Palau islands from Spain. Samoa was partitioned that year between Germany and the USA, with Britain withdrawing in return for compensation elsewhere.
[hist-eur2-s3.6.5] In October and November 1898 Wilhelm toured the Ottoman Empire (Constantinople, Jerusalem and Damascus), and at Damascus he declared himself the friend of the world's Muslims.
[hist-eur2-s3.6.6] The Baghdad Railway had a preliminary concession in 1899 and a full convention in 1903. It was meant to tie the Ottoman Empire to Germany and rival British and Russian influence in the Middle East, so both powers were suspicious; it was still unfinished in 1914.
[hist-eur2-s3.7.b] The balance of power means that no single state or bloc is strong enough to dominate. Weltpolitik brought Germany small gains but alarmed every other power, so it weakened Germany's own position.
[hist-eur2-s3.7.0] Britain had traditionally opposed any power dominating the Continent and treated naval supremacy as its own security. Germany's growing power, and later its fleet, threatened both, which pushed Britain towards France and Russia.
[hist-eur2-s3.7.1] Talks between Joseph Chamberlain, the British Colonial Secretary, and Germany in 1898–1901 for an Anglo-German alliance came to nothing. Bülow and Holstein thought Britain would have to pay a higher price later, so they did not press for agreement.
[hist-eur2-s3.7.2] Britain then turned to Japan (the alliance of January 1902) and France (the Entente Cordiale of April 1904) instead.
[hist-eur2-s3.7.3] Wilhelm's visit to Tangier on 31 March 1905 began the first Moroccan crisis. At the Algeciras Conference of 1906 Germany was isolated, backed only by Austria-Hungary, so the result was humiliation and a stronger Entente.
[hist-eur2-s3.7.4] In July 1911 Germany sent the gunboat Panther to Agadir, Morocco. After Lloyd George's Mansion House speech (July 1911) warned Germany, it accepted part of the French Congo in November 1911 in return for recognising French control of Morocco.
[hist-eur2-s3.7.5] Germans came to believe that Britain, France and Russia were hemming Germany in, a belief called Einkreisung (encirclement). Critics say German policy itself caused it.
[hist-eur2-s3.7.6] In short, Germany's gains were small, but it alarmed every other power, pushed Britain into the Entente and left Germany relying on Austria-Hungary.
[hist-eur2-s3.8.b] Before 1897 Germany's strength lay in its army and its navy was small. The decision to build a great battle fleet, taken in 1897-98, came from the Kaiser's enthusiasm, a book on sea power and a determined admiral.
[hist-eur2-s3.8.0] Before 1897 the German navy was small and largely a coastal defence force, and its earlier leaders favoured cruisers. Germany's strength was its army.
[hist-eur2-s3.8.1] Wilhelm was personally enthusiastic about a navy to rival Britain's. He admired the Royal Navy (he was an honorary British admiral from 1889 and attended the Spithead review that year) and wanted a fleet that matched Germany's new status.
[hist-eur2-s3.8.2] Alfred Thayer Mahan, a US naval officer, argued in The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783 (1890) that national greatness depended on a strong battle fleet and command of the sea. Wilhelm said he was devouring it and had copies placed on his warships.
[hist-eur2-s3.8.3] Alfred von Tirpitz was appointed State Secretary of the Imperial Navy Office (Reichsmarineamt) in June 1897 and held the post until March 1916. His memorandum of June 1897 named Britain as the most dangerous naval enemy.
[hist-eur2-s3.8.4] He called for a battle fleet concentrated in the North Sea, aimed in practice at Britain, rather than a cruiser fleet for overseas, because only a fleet in home waters could threaten the Royal Navy where it was most vulnerable.
[hist-eur2-s3.8.5] Wilhelm chose Tirpitz and backed the fleet throughout, so the Kaiser's enthusiasm and the admiral's plan reinforced each other.
[hist-eur2-s3.9.b] Tirpitz needed a reason why a smaller fleet could still work, and reasons at home to win Reichstag money. His risk theory and his domestic aims explain how the fleet was designed and sold.
[hist-eur2-s3.9.0] The risk theory (Risikogedanke) held that the fleet need not beat the Royal Navy, only be strong enough that a battle would cost Britain so much that it would be left exposed to other powers, France and Russia. Britain would therefore avoid war and treat Germany as an equal.
[hist-eur2-s3.9.1] Tirpitz's target is often given as a ratio of about 2:3 against Britain, which the Kaiser and Tirpitz judged enough to make the theory work. He also hoped the fleet would win an alliance or colonial concessions from Britain.
[hist-eur2-s3.9.2] The danger zone was the building period, when Britain might strike first before the fleet was strong enough. Germans feared a Copenhagen, after Britain's seizure of the Danish fleet in 1807.
[hist-eur2-s3.9.3] The fleet was also a national project to rally the middle classes behind the Reich and isolate the SPD, an example of Sammlungspolitik and social imperialism.
[hist-eur2-s3.9.4] The Navy Laws fixed the size of the fleet and the replacement of ships in law, so later Reichstags had to fund them and could not easily cut the programme.
[hist-eur2-s3.9.5] The Navy Office's News Bureau organised press and lecture campaigns, and academic supporters who lectured and wrote for the fleet were called Flottenprofessoren (fleet professors).
[hist-eur2-s3.9.6] The German Navy League (Deutscher Flottenverein), founded in April 1898 and funded in part by industrialists including Krupp, was a mass organisation promoting the fleet.
[hist-eur2-s3.9.7] Industry gained from the programme: Krupp of Essen supplied armour plate and guns, and Blohm & Voss of Hamburg built warships, so heavy industry gained orders.
[hist-eur2-s3.10.b] Germany turned the plan into law in 1898 and 1900. Britain at first reacted by reorganising its fleet rather than racing.
[hist-eur2-s3.10.0] The First Navy Law, passed by the Reichstag on 26 March 1898, provided for 19 battleships, with cruisers and coastal armoured ships, to be built by 1904. Spending was fixed in law up to 1904.
[hist-eur2-s3.10.1] The Second Navy Law (June 1900) doubled the battleship target to 38, to be reached over about twenty years, plus more cruisers.
[hist-eur2-s3.10.2] German anger helped pass it. From late December 1899 to January 1900 the Royal Navy stopped and searched German mail steamers (Bundesrath, Herzog and General) off south-east Africa, suspecting contraband for the Boers during the Boer War.
[hist-eur2-s3.10.3] The Bundesrath was stopped off Delagoa Bay on 29 December 1899, so the outrage that helped the Second Navy Law was German anger at Britain, not British anger at Germany.
[hist-eur2-s3.10.4] The Admiralty's attention turned increasingly to Germany. Sir John Fisher, First Sea Lord from October 1904 to 1910, scrapped many obsolete ships, brought warships home from distant stations and concentrated them to face the North Sea, and British naval estimates grew.
[hist-eur2-s3.10.5] Fisher's redeployment in 1904–05 was possible because the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902 let Britain reduce its Far East fleet and the Entente Cordiale of 1904 settled disputes with France.
[hist-eur2-s3.10.6] Invasion-scare literature spread British alarm: Erskine Childers's novel The Riddle of the Sands (1903) about a German invasion plan, and William Le Queux's The Invasion of 1910 (1906), serialised in the Daily Mail. A British Navy League had existed since 1894.
[hist-eur2-s3.11.b] In 1906 a new British ship changed the race. Germany answered with more building, and by 1909 the race had become a public scare in Britain.
[hist-eur2-s3.11.0] HMS Dreadnought was launched at Portsmouth on 10 February 1906 by Edward VII and completed in December 1906. She carried ten 12-inch guns, had steam turbines and reached a speed of about 21 knots.
[hist-eur2-s3.11.1] Dreadnought became the name for all battleships of her type, and pre-dreadnought for older ones, which she made obsolete. This cut Britain's existing lead but also gave Germany a chance to compete on equal terms.
[hist-eur2-s3.11.2] However, Britain still had a head start in building dreadnoughts and a far larger fleet overall, so the Dreadnought did not put Germany level with Britain.
[hist-eur2-s3.11.3] Germany answered with its own dreadnoughts. SMS Nassau, its first, was launched in March 1908. The supplementary law (Novelle) of 1906 added large cruisers and provided for widening the Kiel Canal so that dreadnoughts could use it.
[hist-eur2-s3.11.4] The 1908 law cut the life of a battleship from 25 to 20 years, so Germany would build four capital ships a year for 1908–11. The widened Kiel Canal reopened in June 1914.
[hist-eur2-s3.11.5] In 1908–09 a naval scare broke out in Britain over fears that Germany was building faster than announced. The public demanded eight new dreadnoughts with the slogan "We want eight and we won't wait."
[hist-eur2-s3.11.6] Asquith's Liberal government, with Reginald McKenna as First Lord of the Admiralty, agreed to eight dreadnoughts in the 1909–10 programme (four at once, four more later). Britain had decided to out-build Germany at any cost.
[hist-eur2-s3.11.7] Lloyd George's 1909 People's Budget had to pay for both the navy and social reform, so the race strained Britain's finances too.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.b] Both governments tried to talk their way out of the race, but each wanted the other to move first. After 1912 Germany gave up the race in practice.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.0] Britain proposed arms limitation at the Second Hague Conference (1907), but Germany refused to discuss it. In the talks of 1906-08 and up to 1911, Germany wanted a British neutrality promise before limiting building, while Britain wanted limits first.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.1] Bethmann Hollweg, Chancellor from July 1909, sought a naval and political agreement in 1909–11 but none was reached.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.2] Richard Haldane, British Secretary of State for War, visited Berlin on 8–11 February 1912, arranged through the shipping magnate Albert Ballin and the banker Sir Ernest Cassel. Germany asked for a pledge of British neutrality if Germany became involved in a war.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.3] Britain would promise only not to join an unprovoked attack on Germany, and wanted building slowed first. Neutrality would have left France and Russia to face Germany alone. Wilhelm also announced a new Navy Law in his Reichstag speech of 7 February 1912, just as the mission began.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.4] The 1912 supplementary law added three more battleships and a third active battle squadron. Winston Churchill, First Lord of the Admiralty from October 1911 to May 1915, told the Commons in March 1912 that Britain would keep a 60 per cent superiority in dreadnoughts over Germany.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.5] Churchill proposed a naval holiday (a pause in building) in 1912 and again in 1913, but Germany did not accept. In 1912 British battleships moved from the Mediterranean to home waters while France concentrated its fleet in the Mediterranean; the Grey-Cambon letters (November 1912) promised consultation, not alliance.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.6] From 1912–13 Germany shifted money to the army, which received large increases in 1912 and 1913. In 1913 Tirpitz told the Reichstag budget committee that Germany accepted a 16:10 ratio in battleships in Britain's favour, and in August 1914 Britain had a clear lead in dreadnoughts.
[hist-eur2-s3.12.7] A limited detente followed in 1913–14: an agreement on the future of the Portuguese colonies was initialled in 1913 and a Baghdad Railway agreement in June 1914. Mistrust remained, and the naval and military preparations of each side continued.
[hist-eur2-s3.13.b] The fleet failed to achieve its aims abroad and it also cost Germany heavily at home. The money problem fed straight into party politics.
[hist-eur2-s3.13.0] The fleet cost vast sums and absorbed resources that could have gone to the army, at a time when Germany's land frontiers were threatened by France and Russia. The navy took a growing share of the defence budget until 1912.
[hist-eur2-s3.13.1] Rising naval spending raised the national debt and taxation and led to the Reich finance crisis of 1908–09.
[hist-eur2-s3.13.2] In June 1909 Bülow's finance reform, which included an inheritance tax, was defeated in the Reichstag by the Conservatives and the Centre Party. Bülow resigned in July 1909, having lost the Kaiser's trust.
[hist-eur2-s3.13.3] The dispute sharpened the quarrel between the Conservatives, who opposed the inheritance tax, and the left. The SPD and left-liberals also opposed the cost of the fleet.
[hist-eur2-s3.13.4] The fleet rallied the right for a time, but it did not halt the SPD's growth: in the January 1912 Reichstag election the SPD won 110 seats and became the largest party.
[hist-eur2-s3.13.5] After 1912 Germany could no longer afford to compete in both navy and army, so the army received large increases in 1912–13 and the naval race slackened.
[hist-eur2-s3.14.b] The fleet failed in its main aim. It did not deter Britain; it turned a possible partner into an opponent, and in the war itself it achieved little.
[hist-eur2-s3.14.0] The fleet turned a possible partner into an opponent. Britain's agreements with Japan (1902, which let it reduce its Far East fleet), France (1904, settling Egypt and Morocco) and Russia (1907, covering Persia, Afghanistan and Tibet) formed, with the Franco-Russian alliance, the Triple Entente.
[hist-eur2-s3.14.1] Historians debate how far the navy, rather than colonial issues, caused these agreements. The 1904 Entente was mainly a colonial settlement, so the navy's role is disputed.
[hist-eur2-s3.14.2] The fleet did not deter Britain. It helped make Britain regard Germany as the main threat and bound Britain more closely to France, so Britain declared war on 4 August 1914, after Germany invaded Belgium.
[hist-eur2-s3.14.3] In the war the navy stayed mostly in harbour. At Jutland (31 May-1 June 1916) Britain lost more ships and men, about 6,000 British and about 2,500 German dead, but the German High Seas Fleet returned to port and rarely came out again, and the British blockade was never broken.
[hist-eur2-s3.14.4] Many sailors resented the fleet's inactivity and harsh discipline. The mutinies at Wilhelmshaven (late October 1918) and Kiel (from 3 November 1918) helped start the revolution that overthrew the Kaiser.
[hist-eur2-s3.14.5] The interned German fleet was scuttled at Scapa Flow on 21 June 1919 on the orders of Admiral Ludwig von Reuter.
[hist-eur2-s3.15.b] People at the time judged the fleet very differently depending on where they stood. A document question will often turn on spotting the writer's side.
[hist-eur2-s3.15.0] British contemporaries regarded the German fleet as a challenge to the Empire. The Admiralty, the public and the press spread alarm, including invasion scares and the Daily Mail, and the British Navy League added to it.
[hist-eur2-s3.15.1] German nationalists were proud of the fleet and presented it as essential for the defence of overseas trade, and they explained Britain's hostility through the encirclement view.
[hist-eur2-s3.15.2] The SPD and left-liberals objected to the cost of the fleet.
[hist-eur2-s3.15.3] The two nations' views were mirror images: Germany saw a peaceful trading nation needing protection, Britain saw a fleet in the North Sea aimed at itself.
[hist-eur2-s3.16.b] Historians disagree about why Germany built the fleet and what it caused. Match each historian to a clear view.
[hist-eur2-s3.16.0] Eckart Kehr (Battleship Building and Party Politics, German 1930) argued that the fleet served the domestic interests of industry and the middle classes, a view known as the primacy of domestic politics (Primat der Innenpolitik).
[hist-eur2-s3.16.1] Volker Berghahn (Der Tirpitz-Plan, 1971) argued that Tirpitz aimed to strengthen the monarchy against the Reichstag and to deter or blackmail Britain. Social imperialism is a term associated mainly with Wehler, not with Berghahn.
[hist-eur2-s3.16.2] Fritz Fischer (Griff nach der Weltmacht, 1961; in English Germany's Aims in the First World War, 1967) and War of Illusions (German 1969) argued that Germany's leaders deliberately sought world power, first by the fleet and then by war. Critics say he exaggerated the coherence of German aims.
[hist-eur2-s3.16.3] Paul Kennedy (The Rise of the Anglo-German Antagonism, 1860–1914, 1980) argues that the naval race was central to the breakdown of Anglo-German relations, though rooted in wider economic and political rivalry.
[hist-eur2-s3.16.4] Niall Ferguson (The Pity of War, 1998) argues that Britain had clearly won the naval race by about 1912, so it was not a direct cause of war in 1914.
[hist-eur2-s3.16.5] Other historians doubt that the race caused the war, because Anglo-German relations were improving in 1913–14 and the Entente was formed first because of colonial concerns.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.b] A judgement answer needs achievements set against limits. These paired points give the balance examiners reward.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.0] Achievement: Germany built the world's second-largest navy in under two decades, winning national pride and support for the Kaiser.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.1] Limit: the fleet never came near matching Britain, and the risk theory failed because Britain out-built Germany and made friends with its rivals.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.2] Achievement: Weltpolitik won Kiaochow, the Pacific islands, Samoa and the Baghdad Railway concession, and gave Germany a voice in world affairs.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.3] Limit: the gains were small and unprofitable, the Morocco crises ended in humiliation, and Germany became more isolated, relying on Austria-Hungary.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.4] Domestic achievement: the fleet and the colonial movement helped rally the right for a time, bringing Conservatives and National Liberals together behind the government.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.5] Domestic limit: its cost caused the 1908–09 finance crisis and did not halt the SPD's growth, since the SPD became the largest party in 1912.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.6] Contemporary view: German contemporaries regarded Weltpolitik and the fleet as a necessary national project, while British contemporaries regarded them as a direct threat.
[hist-eur2-s3.17.7] Later view: most historians judge Weltpolitik and the fleet a strategic mistake that helped make the Triple Entente and brought Britain into the war, though some stress that Anglo-German relations were improving by 1914.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s2: 2. The structures of European diplomacy, the New Imperialism and colonial rivalries, 1890–1914
[hist-eur2-s2.0.b] To follow the alliances after 1890 you need to know what they replaced. Bismarck had built a web of agreements to keep France isolated, and his successors let part of it drop, which opened the way for new groupings.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.0] French hostility to Germany after Germany annexed Alsace-Lorraine in 1871 (the wish for revenge, called revanche) was the fixed point of European diplomacy, so Bismarck's aim was to keep France without allies.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.1] Bismarck's alliances were the Dual Alliance of 1879 (Germany and Austria-Hungary), the Triple Alliance of 1882 (with Italy added) and the secret Reinsurance Treaty of 1887 (Germany and Russia, for three years). Together they kept France isolated and avoided a war on two fronts.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.2] Wilhelm II dismissed Bismarck in March 1890 and began a 'new course' in foreign policy. Leo von Caprivi became Chancellor (1890-94).
[hist-eur2-s2.0.3] In 1890 Caprivi and Friedrich von Holstein (a senior Foreign Office official) let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse. They thought it inconsistent with the Dual Alliance and harmful to relations with Britain.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.4] Letting the treaty go left Russia with no German link and no friends, which is why it looked elsewhere. This was the first step in the break-up of Bismarck's balance of power.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.b] Russia, left without a German partner, and France, still isolated since 1871, had a shared interest in finding a friend. Their agreement ended France's isolation and was a real military alliance, unlike the later ententes.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.0] French banks began making large loans to Russia from 1888, which gave the two countries a financial link before any political one.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.1] A French naval squadron visited Kronstadt (Russia's naval base) in July 1891, and in August 1891 the two powers agreed a political understanding to consult each other if war threatened.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.2] A military convention was signed in August 1892 and ratified by an exchange of letters completed in January 1894. It was secret and defensive, aimed at the Triple Alliance powers, so each ally was protected without attacking anyone.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.3] Its terms: if France was attacked by Germany, or by Italy supported by Germany, Russia would attack Germany; if Russia was attacked by Germany, or by Austria-Hungary supported by Germany, France would attack Germany.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.4] Both would also mobilise (call up their armies) if any Triple Alliance power mobilised, which meant a crisis involving one Triple Alliance member could draw in both allies.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.5] Because of this, Germany now faced the danger of a war on two fronts, the very risk Bismarck had worked to avoid, and this later shaped the Schlieffen Plan (Germany's war plan against France and Russia).
[hist-eur2-s2.1.6] French investment in Russian railways and industry cemented the alliance. It lasted until the Russian revolution and Russia's exit from the war in 1917-18.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.b] Britain had long avoided permanent peacetime alliances, a policy called 'splendid isolation'. By 1900 that policy was costing it more than it gave, so Britain began to settle quarrels with its imperial rivals and to find partners.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.0] Isolation lost its value because European opinion was hostile to Britain during the Boer War, and because Germany was starting to build a large navy that threatened British sea power. Joseph Chamberlain, the Colonial Secretary, sought an alliance with Germany in 1898-1901, but the talks failed.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.1] The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902) gave Britain support in the Far East against Russia, so it could keep more of its navy nearer home. It was renewed in 1905 and 1911.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.2] Théophile Delcassé, French foreign minister 1898-1905, was the French architect of an agreement with Britain, and Edward VII's visit to Paris in May 1903 helped warm opinion.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.3] The Entente Cordiale was signed on 8 April 1904 by Lord Lansdowne (British Foreign Secretary) and Paul Cambon (French ambassador in London). France accepted Britain's position in Egypt and Britain accepted France's in Morocco.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.4] It also settled disputes over the Newfoundland fisheries, West Africa, Siam, Madagascar and the New Hebrides, so it removed the colonial quarrels that had made the two powers rivals.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.5] The Entente was not an alliance, because neither promised to fight for the other. It tightened over time: secret Anglo-French military staff talks began in 1906, and in 1912 a naval arrangement concentrated the French fleet in the Mediterranean and the British fleet in the North Sea and Channel.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.b] Britain's quarrels with Russia in Asia were the last big obstacle to a grouping against Germany. Settling them completed the Triple Entente, which stood opposite the older Triple Alliance.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.0] Russia had been weakened by defeat by Japan in 1904-05 and both Russia and Britain were worried about Germany, which is why they were ready to compromise.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.1] The Anglo-Russian Convention (31 August 1907) was agreed by Sir Edward Grey (Foreign Secretary) and Alexander Izvolsky (Russian foreign minister).
[hist-eur2-s2.3.2] Its terms: Persia was divided into a Russian zone in the north, a British zone in the south-east and a neutral zone between. Afghanistan was a British sphere, and both promised to keep out of Tibet.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.3] The Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia was therefore a loose understanding with no common military commitment. It faced the formal Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, which had been renewed in 1887, 1891, 1902 and, for the last time, 1912.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.4] The Triple Alliance was weaker than it looked because Italy was unreliable. In 1902 the Prinetti-Barrere agreement secretly promised France that Italy would stay neutral if France was attacked, and in 1911-12 Italy fought the Ottoman Empire for Libya.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.5] From about 1905 the blocs hardened. Germany spoke of 'encirclement' (being surrounded by hostile powers), while Britain and France feared German ambitions, so each crisis looked like a test of strength between the blocs.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.6] In short, the balance of power had changed from Bismarck's flexible system, built around Germany, to two armed camps.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.b] From about 1880 to 1914 European states rushed to take colonies and spheres of influence (areas where one power claimed special rights). Historians call this the New Imperialism and ask first what economic motives drove it.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.0] It differed from earlier empire-building in its speed, its scale, the number of powers taking part, and its targets, mainly Africa and China. It also spread beyond Europe, to the USA and Japan.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.1] The second industrial revolution (the spread of industry using steel, electricity and chemicals) created demand for raw materials and markets, which is why industrial states looked overseas.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.2] Industrial states needed raw materials such as rubber, cotton, palm oil, copper, gold and diamonds, and later oil.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.3] They also wanted protected markets, because tariffs rose in Europe from the late 1870s, and outlets for investment overseas.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.4] The British economist J.A. Hobson, who reported on the Boer War for the Manchester Guardian, argued in Imperialism: A Study (1902) that surplus capital and underconsumption (too little buying power) at home drove capitalists to invest overseas and so drove empire.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.5] Lenin, in Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (written 1916, published 1917), called imperialism the last stage of capitalism, leading to war between capitalist states.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.6] Historians dispute the theory. Germany's colonies took a tiny share of its trade and investment, and much British investment went to the USA and Latin America, outside the empire.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.b] Profit was only one reason, and for many states not the main one. Strategy, prestige, religion and ideas of racial superiority also pushed governments and public opinion towards empire, and new technology made it practical.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.0] Strategic: states sought naval bases, coaling stations and secure routes. Britain occupied Egypt in 1882 to protect the Suez Canal (opened 1869), the route to India, and then moved to control the Nile valley to keep that route safe.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.1] Nationalist: colonies were seen as a mark of Great Power status, and the popular press, colonial societies and public opinion cheered each acquisition. After defeat in 1871 France sought compensation overseas under Jules Ferry (Prime Minister 1880-81 and 1883-85), who promoted expansion in Tunisia (1881) and Indochina (Tonkin).
[hist-eur2-s2.5.2] Missionary and humanitarian: churches wanted to spread Christianity and end the slave trade. The Scottish missionary David Livingstone (died 1873) and the explorer H.M. Stanley, who found him in 1871, made Africa known to the public.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.3] Ideological: Social Darwinism applied the phrase 'survival of the fittest', coined by Herbert Spencer (1864), to nations and races, so rule over others was justified as natural. Related was a belief in a duty to 'civilise' other peoples, France's mission civilisatrice.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.4] Kipling's poem 'The White Man's Burden' (1899), written about the USA in the Philippines, expressed this duty to civilise.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.5] Technology made conquest possible: quinine against malaria, steamships, railways, the telegraph and breech-loading rifles, and the Maxim gun (the first fully automatic machine gun, invented by Hiram Maxim in 1884) gave Europeans an overwhelming military advantage.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.6] Competition fed on itself, which is why even poor land was claimed. Once one power claimed territory, others annexed land before they could be shut out, even where the land was of little value.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.b] Africa was the main prize of the New Imperialism. In about thirty years almost the whole continent was divided, and a conference in Berlin gave the process its rules.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.0] In 1880 only about a tenth of Africa was under European control, mostly coastal areas such as Algeria, the Cape and Senegal; by 1914 about nine tenths of the continent had been taken (textbook estimates).
[hist-eur2-s2.6.1] The scramble was triggered by Leopold II's claims on the Congo, France's advance in West Africa and its protectorate over Tunisia (1881), and Britain's occupation of Egypt in 1882.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.2] The Berlin Conference (15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885) was called by Bismarck with French agreement. Fourteen states attended, including the USA and the Ottoman Empire. Do not confuse it with the Congress of Berlin of 1878, which dealt with the Balkans.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.3] Its General Act required powers to notify each other of claims and to show effective occupation (real control, at first of coasts), and declared free trade in the Congo basin and free navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.4] During the conference the powers recognised the claim of Leopold's association to the Congo; the Congo Free State was proclaimed afterwards, in 1885.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.5] No African ruler took part. The Conference did not itself divide Africa, but it made the partition faster and gave it a legal shape, because anyone could now claim land by occupying it and giving notice.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.b] Once the rules were set, the powers had competing plans. Where plans crossed they risked war, and where they could, they traded claims by agreement.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.0] Britain's aim was a belt of territory from the Cape to Cairo under British rule. Its leading backer was Cecil Rhodes, a mining magnate (diamonds and gold) who founded the British South Africa Company in 1889 and was Prime Minister of the Cape Colony 1890-96; Rhodesia was named after him.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.1] France sought an east-west belt linking its West African lands with Djibouti on the Red Sea. The two plans crossed at Fashoda in 1898.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.2] Germany, a latecomer, took South West Africa, Togo, Cameroon and German East Africa (1884-85). Italy took Eritrea and Italian Somaliland and, in 1911-12, Libya. Portugal held Angola and Mozambique.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.3] Disputes were often settled by compromise. In the Heligoland-Zanzibar Treaty (1 July 1890) Britain gave Germany the North Sea island of Heligoland and Germany recognised British control of Zanzibar.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.4] Africans resisted, for example Ethiopia, under Emperor Menelik II, defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa (1 March 1896), which kept it independent.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.5] By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent African states.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.b] The Congo shows the New Imperialism at its worst. Its ruler was a king acting as a private owner, and the system he set up brought a public scandal that forced change.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.0] Leopold II, King of the Belgians 1865-1909, founded the International African Association in 1876, and Stanley worked for him in the Congo from 1879.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.1] The Congo Free State was proclaimed in 1885 as Leopold's personal state, not a Belgian colony, on promises to end slavery and keep free trade.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.2] From the 1890s Africans were forced to collect wild rubber under quotas, because demand for bicycle and later motor-car tyres made it very profitable. This is known as the 'red rubber' system.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.3] Failure to meet quotas was punished by taking women as hostages, flogging with the chicotte (a whip), cutting off hands, and burning villages.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.4] The Force Publique, the colonial army of African soldiers under European officers, enforced the system. The population fell by millions from violence, disease, flight and famine, because the system destroyed villages and food supplies, but estimates vary widely.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.5] E.D. Morel, a shipping-company clerk in Liverpool, noticed that ships brought rubber and ivory from the Congo but carried back mostly guns and ammunition. This suggested forced labour, and he campaigned on it.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.6] Roger Casement, an Irish-born British consul, wrote a report on the Congo published in 1904. Morel, with Casement, founded the Congo Reform Association in 1904.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.7] Writers helped the campaign: Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness (1899) drew on his own Congo journey of 1890, and Mark Twain wrote King Leopold's Soliloquy (1905). Under international pressure Belgium annexed the colony in 1908 as the Belgian Congo, and Leopold died in 1909.
[hist-eur2-s2.9.b] In Asia the powers competed less for conquest than for ports, trade and influence, and China was the main target because it was too weak to resist. Two newer powers, the USA and Japan, joined in.
[hist-eur2-s2.9.0] In South-East Asia France formed French Indochina in 1887 (Vietnam and Cambodia, with Laos added in 1893). Britain annexed Upper Burma in 1886 and extended control over Malaya; the Dutch held the East Indies.
[hist-eur2-s2.9.1] Japan defeated China in the Sino-Japanese War (1894-95) and won Taiwan (Formosa). In the Triple Intervention of 1895 Russia, France and Germany forced Japan to give back the Liaodong peninsula.
[hist-eur2-s2.9.2] The powers then scrambled for concessions (1897-98). Germany seized Kiaochow (Jiaozhou) in 1897 after two German missionaries were killed, and leased it in 1898; Russia leased Port Arthur, Britain Weihaiwei and France Guangzhouwan (1898-99).
[hist-eur2-s2.9.3] Each claimed a sphere of influence: Russia in Manchuria, Germany in Shandong, Britain in the Yangtze valley and France in the south.
[hist-eur2-s2.9.4] The USA defeated Spain in the Spanish-American War (1898) and took the Philippines, Guam and Puerto Rico. In 1899 its Secretary of State, John Hay, sent the Open Door notes asking for equal trading rights for all in China.
[hist-eur2-s2.9.5] The Boxer Rising (1899-1901) was a Chinese uprising against foreigners and Christians. The foreign legations (embassies) in Beijing were besieged from June to August 1900 and relieved by an eight-nation force of Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, the USA, Italy and Austria-Hungary.
[hist-eur2-s2.9.6] Japan began to modernise after the Meiji Restoration of 1868, building a strong army and navy. It defeated Russia in the Russo-Japanese War (1904-05, ended by the Treaty of Portsmouth), the first time an Asian power beat a European Great Power in modern times.
[hist-eur2-s2.9.7] The defeat turned Russia's attention back to the Balkans and Europe, contributed to the revolution of 1905, and opened the way to the 1907 Entente with Britain.
[hist-eur2-s2.10.b] Fashoda was the sharpest colonial clash between two European powers in this period. It nearly caused war between Britain and France, but it ended in a settlement that led towards friendship.
[hist-eur2-s2.10.0] Britain had occupied Egypt in 1882 and treated the whole Nile valley as its sphere, while France wanted an east-west belt across Africa, so the two plans collided on the Upper Nile.
[hist-eur2-s2.10.1] In July 1898 Captain Jean-Baptiste Marchand's small French expedition reached Fashoda on the Upper Nile in Sudan.
[hist-eur2-s2.10.2] Kitchener defeated the Mahdist army at Omdurman on 2 September 1898, then reached Fashoda in September with a much larger force. Both sides refused to withdraw and war was feared.
[hist-eur2-s2.10.3] France agreed to withdraw in November 1898. Delcassé judged that France could not risk war with Britain's navy; France was isolated and facing the Dreyfus Affair at home.
[hist-eur2-s2.10.4] An Anglo-French agreement of March 1899 set the boundary between the two spheres: the Nile valley British and the area west of it French.
[hist-eur2-s2.10.5] Fashoda was the worst Anglo-French crisis of the period, but it showed the danger of colonial clashes and convinced both sides to settle disputes, which led to the 1904 Entente Cordiale.
[hist-eur2-s2.11.b] Britain's war against the Boer republics was a colonial war that damaged Britain's reputation in Europe. It exposed how few friends Britain had, which helped push it out of isolation.
[hist-eur2-s2.11.0] The Boer republics were the Transvaal (South African Republic, under President Paul Kruger) and the Orange Free State. Gold found on the Witwatersrand in 1886 drew in Uitlanders (foreign, mostly British, settlers in the Transvaal, who were denied the vote), and this led to disputes with Britain.
[hist-eur2-s2.11.1] The failed Jameson Raid (December 1895 to January 1896), an armed incursion into the Transvaal led by Rhodes's associate Leander Starr Jameson, soured relations, and Rhodes resigned as Cape Prime Minister. Wilhelm II's Kruger telegram of January 1896, congratulating Kruger, caused outrage in Britain.
[hist-eur2-s2.11.2] The war began on 11 October 1899. The Boers won early successes, including Black Week in December 1899 (three British defeats) and sieges of Ladysmith, Kimberley and Mafeking.
[hist-eur2-s2.11.3] Britain sent large reinforcements under Lord Roberts, and from late 1900 under Kitchener. Mafeking was relieved in May 1900, and the Boers turned to guerrilla war (small raids and ambushes).
[hist-eur2-s2.11.4] From 1900, and especially under Kitchener, the British burned Boer farms and moved Boer civilians into concentration camps. Over 20,000 Boer civilians died in them, most of them children. Black Africans were held in separate camps where many thousands died (figures uncertain).
[hist-eur2-s2.11.5] Emily Hobhouse exposed the camps. The Treaty of Vereeniging (31 May 1902) made the Transvaal and Orange Free State British colonies.
[hist-eur2-s2.11.6] The war showed Britain's isolation and aroused hostility in Europe, damaged British prestige and military reputation, and pushed Britain to end splendid isolation. Hobson's critique of imperialism arose from it.
[hist-eur2-s2.11.7] At home it fed popular excitement: when Mafeking was relieved in May 1900 there were wild celebrations in London (Mafeking night).
[hist-eur2-s2.12.b] Morocco was one of the last independent states in Africa. When France moved to control it, Germany tried to test the new Entente, and the result was the opposite of what Germany wanted.
[hist-eur2-s2.12.0] The 1904 Entente gave France a free hand in Morocco. Germany aimed to test or break the Entente, to win compensation, and to assert its say in colonial questions.
[hist-eur2-s2.12.1] Wilhelm II landed at Tangier on 31 March 1905 and declared support for the Sultan's independence. This was a challenge to France.
[hist-eur2-s2.12.2] German pressure forced Delcassé to resign in June 1905.
[hist-eur2-s2.12.3] The crisis ended at the Algeciras Conference (January to April 1906). France and Spain were given the policing of Morocco's ports, and France's influence over Morocco's finances was recognised.
[hist-eur2-s2.12.4] It was a diplomatic defeat for Germany, because Germany was supported only by Austria-Hungary, while Britain and Russia backed France.
[hist-eur2-s2.12.5] The crisis strengthened the Entente: British and French generals began secret staff talks in 1906, so a colonial settlement became closer to a quasi-alliance.
[hist-eur2-s2.13.b] Five years later France tightened its hold on Morocco and Germany challenged it again. This time Britain's warning showed how closely it now stood by France.
[hist-eur2-s2.13.0] In May 1911 French troops entered Fez. Alfred von Kiderlen-Wachter, the German foreign secretary, directed German policy.
[hist-eur2-s2.13.1] The German gunboat Panther arrived at Agadir on 1 July 1911. Germany demanded compensation.
[hist-eur2-s2.13.2] David Lloyd George, Chancellor of the Exchequer, gave the Mansion House speech on 21 July 1911, warning Germany that Britain would not be pushed aside.
[hist-eur2-s2.13.3] The crisis was settled by a Franco-German agreement on 4 November 1911: Germany accepted French control of Morocco in return for a strip of the French Congo added to Cameroon. The Treaty of Fez (30 March 1912) made Morocco a French protectorate.
[hist-eur2-s2.13.4] Many Germans saw the settlement as a humiliation because Germany had gained only a strip of the Congo. It drew Britain and France closer, and the Anglo-French naval arrangement followed in 1912.
[hist-eur2-s2.13.5] Italy seized the moment, for example, to attack the Ottoman Empire in September 1911.
[hist-eur2-s2.14.b] Colonial rivalry did not stay overseas. It shaped relations between the Great Powers, the mood of public opinion and even the way governments counted the cost of empire.
[hist-eur2-s2.14.0] Imperial rivalry produced crises that raised tension and caused war scares between Great Powers: Fashoda, the Boer War and Morocco.
[hist-eur2-s2.14.1] It was also settled by compromise: Heligoland-Zanzibar (1890), Fashoda (1899), and the agreements of 1904 and 1907. These colonial settlements became the basis of the ententes.
[hist-eur2-s2.14.2] It changed the balance of power, so alignments shifted. Britain moved from isolation into the Entente, and Germany's clumsy colonial diplomacy, such as its challenges in Morocco, left it more isolated.
[hist-eur2-s2.14.3] It fed nationalism, jingoism (aggressive patriotism) and militarism at home, through the popular press, celebrations such as Mafeking night (1900), and demands for bigger navies and armies.
[hist-eur2-s2.14.4] Economic effects were mixed. Colonies supplied raw materials and returns on investment, but they often cost more than they earned: Germany's colonies took a tiny share of its trade.
[hist-eur2-s2.14.5] Humanitarian opinion reacted. The Congo and Boer-camp scandals showed that public opinion could expose and restrain abuses.
[hist-eur2-s2.15.b] Examiners want a judgement with both sides. Colonial quarrels created friction, but they were mostly resolved, so the question is how much they mattered beside Europe's own rivalries.
[hist-eur2-s2.15.0] For tension: imperial rivalry created crises (Fashoda, the Boer War and Morocco) that raised tension and made Britain and Germany suspicious of each other.
[hist-eur2-s2.15.1] Against: imperial agreements also reduced tension, as the Entente Cordiale and the Anglo-Russian Entente show; the powers were often able to settle colonial quarrels by compromise.
[hist-eur2-s2.15.2] For: the Moroccan crises hardened the blocs, turning the Entente Cordiale from a colonial settlement into a quasi-alliance.
[hist-eur2-s2.15.3] Against: colonial disputes were mostly settled peacefully, so war in 1914 came from the Balkans and Europe's own alliance system rather than from colonial clashes.
[hist-eur2-s2.15.4] Contemporaries differed: Germans blamed British and French encirclement for their insecurity, while the British and French blamed German pressure for the Moroccan crises.
[hist-eur2-s2.15.5] Hobson and Lenin, writing from different positions, saw capitalism and imperialism as an underlying cause of war. Later historians see imperialism as one factor alongside alliances, militarism and nationalism.
[hist-eur2-s2.15.6] On the alliance system, it helped keep the balance of power and deter war by making each side cautious, but it also tied powers to each other, so that a local crisis could become a general war.
