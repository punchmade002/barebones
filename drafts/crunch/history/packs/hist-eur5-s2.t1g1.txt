TARGET hist-eur5-s2: 2. Independence struggles in Indochina and the East Indies
[hist-eur5-s2.0.b] Before 1941 France ruled Indochina and the Netherlands ruled the East Indies as colonies. To understand why that rule collapsed after 1945, you need to know what colonial rule was and what Japan did to its prestige.
[hist-eur5-s2.0.0] Colonial rule means that a foreign state controls a territory and runs it for its own benefit. France ruled Indochina and the Netherlands ruled the East Indies, and the region mattered to both for products and markets such as rubber, tin and trade.
[hist-eur5-s2.0.1] De-colonisation is the process by which colonies win independence from the imperial power. Here it was driven by nationalist movements, which had to fight or negotiate against European powers that wanted to return after 1945.
[hist-eur5-s2.0.2] Japan's conquests of 1941–42 and its occupation shattered the image of European invincibility, because Asian peoples watched European armies and officials driven out. This is why nationalists could now believe that European rule could be ended.
[hist-eur5-s2.0.3] The Cold War, the rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union, shaped the later struggle. After the communist victory in China (1949) and during the Korean War, the United States viewed Vietnam in Cold War terms, so it backed France against Ho.
[hist-eur5-s2.1.b] France did not rule its Indochinese possessions as a single colony. It built a federation in which different areas had different legal status, which is why the status of Cochinchina became a problem in 1946.
[hist-eur5-s2.1.0] French Indochina was formed as a federation in 1887 from Tonkin, Annam, Cochinchina and Cambodia (Tonkin, Annam and Cochinchina being the three regions of Vietnam), and Laos was added in 1893.
[hist-eur5-s2.1.1] The French ruled through a colonial administration headed by a governor-general, with the capital of the federation at Hanoi, so real power lay with French officials rather than local rulers.
[hist-eur5-s2.1.2] Cochinchina, in the south, was a direct colony run by French officials. Tonkin, Annam and Cambodia were protectorates, meaning territories that kept a local ruler but were controlled by France.
[hist-eur5-s2.1.3] The Vietnamese emperor was kept in place as a figurehead (a ruler with a title but no real power) at Hue.
[hist-eur5-s2.1.4] Do not confuse the Indochinese Federation of 1887, which was Indochina's own unit, with the French Union of 1946, which covered France and its overseas lands.
[hist-eur5-s2.2.b] Japan's arrival in 1940 began to loosen French control, and its coup in 1945 removed it. Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh used the gap to take power in August 1945.
[hist-eur5-s2.2.0] Japanese troops entered northern Indochina in September 1940 with the agreement of Vichy France (the wartime French government that cooperated with Germany), so the French administration was allowed to carry on for a time under Japanese pressure.
[hist-eur5-s2.2.1] Japan overthrew the French administration in a coup on 9 March 1945 and took full control, which ended French rule and left a power vacuum.
[hist-eur5-s2.2.2] The Viet Minh (League for the Independence of Vietnam) was founded in May 1941 under Ho Chi Minh. It was a front, an alliance of groups, combining communists and non-communist nationalists, so it could appeal to Vietnamese of many views. Vo Nguyen Giap led it in the field.
[hist-eur5-s2.2.3] Its wartime fighting against the Japanese was small-scale guerrilla activity (hit-and-run raids), not a large campaign. In 1945 the OSS, the American wartime intelligence service, gave it some supplies and training.
[hist-eur5-s2.2.4] A famine in northern Vietnam in 1944–45 killed many people. Estimates of deaths range from about 400,000 to about 2 million, so give the range rather than a single figure.
[hist-eur5-s2.2.5] The Viet Minh seized power in Hanoi and other towns in August 1945, an event called the August Revolution. Emperor Bảo Đại abdicated on 25 August 1945, which removed the figurehead emperor the French had kept at Hue.
[hist-eur5-s2.3.b] With the Japanese gone, Ho Chi Minh declared independence at once. But the Allies divided the country between their own forces, and France used the opening to return.
[hist-eur5-s2.3.0] On 2 September 1945 in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. He opened with words from the American Declaration of Independence (and also cited the French Declaration of the Rights of Man), turning the ideals of the Western powers against colonial rule.
[hist-eur5-s2.3.1] After Japan's surrender, Chinese Nationalist troops occupied the north (to the 16th parallel) and British forces the south. British troops helped the French regain control in Saigon in September 1945, so France had a foothold in the south.
[hist-eur5-s2.3.2] In the Franco-Vietnamese agreement of 6 March 1946, France recognised the Democratic Republic as a free state within the Indochinese Federation and the French Union. The status of Cochinchina was left to a promised referendum, which never took place.
[hist-eur5-s2.3.3] The French Union was created by the constitution of the Fourth Republic in 1946. It was a federation of France, its overseas departments and territories, and associated states.
[hist-eur5-s2.3.4] The French Union fell short of independence because France kept control of defence and foreign policy. A ‘free state’ inside it could not run its own army or its relations with other countries.
[hist-eur5-s2.4.b] Neither side trusted the March agreement. During 1946 talks in France failed, a bombardment hardened feeling, and fighting in Hanoi began a long war.
[hist-eur5-s2.4.0] Ho Chi Minh went to France for the Fontainebleau conference in the summer of 1946 and signed a temporary agreement on 14 September 1946, but the talks failed to settle the main disagreements.
[hist-eur5-s2.4.1] A French naval bombardment of Haiphong in November 1946 killed many civilians (no figure is agreed), which made war very likely.
[hist-eur5-s2.4.2] Fighting broke out in Hanoi on 19 December 1946, which started the First Indochina War (1946–54) between France and the Viet Minh.
[hist-eur5-s2.4.3] Ho Chi Minh wrote several letters and telegrams to President Truman in 1945–46 asking for American support. None was answered, so he approached the war without American help at this stage.
[hist-eur5-s2.5.b] France could not win by force alone, so it set up a Vietnamese alternative to Ho. At the same time the Cold War drew China and the United States into the conflict.
[hist-eur5-s2.5.0] France set up the State of Vietnam under Bảo Đại in 1949 as an alternative to the Democratic Republic, and Western states recognised it.
[hist-eur5-s2.5.1] Keep the names apart: the Democratic Republic of Vietnam was Ho's state in the North, while the State of Vietnam of 1949 was the French-backed one. The Republic of Vietnam of the South dates only from 1955.
[hist-eur5-s2.5.2] China's communist government, in power from October 1949, supplied the Viet Minh with weapons and military advisers from 1950, which strengthened Ho's army.
[hist-eur5-s2.5.3] By the early 1950s the United States paid for a large share of the French war effort, because it viewed the war as part of the struggle against communism.
[hist-eur5-s2.5.4] Ho's government in the north was recognised by China and the Soviet Union, so the two sides in Vietnam now had powerful outside backers.
[hist-eur5-s2.6.b] In 1953 the French tried to end the war with one decisive battle. At Dien Bien Phu the plan backfired and French hopes of victory ended.
[hist-eur5-s2.6.0] General Henri Navarre, French commander from 1953, wanted a decisive battle, so he fortified the valley at Dien Bien Phu in north-west Vietnam, close to Laos, to draw the Viet Minh into a set-piece fight.
[hist-eur5-s2.6.1] The siege ran from 13 March to 7 May 1954.
[hist-eur5-s2.6.2] Vo Nguyen Giap hauled artillery up the hills surrounding the valley and supplied his army over long routes by porters and bicycles, so the French garrison below was under heavy fire and could not be relieved.
[hist-eur5-s2.6.3] The French garrison surrendered, and about 10,000 French Union troops (soldiers from France and its overseas lands) were captured.
[hist-eur5-s2.6.4] The Indochina talks at Geneva opened on 8 May 1954, the day after the surrender.
[hist-eur5-s2.7.b] The French defeat made a settlement urgent. Geneva ended the French war but divided Vietnam, which set the stage for later conflict.
[hist-eur5-s2.7.0] The Geneva Conference, which opened on 26 April 1954, covered both Korea and Indochina. Pierre Mendès France became French prime minister in June 1954 promising a settlement within a month, so France was now ready to negotiate.
[hist-eur5-s2.7.1] The ceasefire agreements of July 1954, together known as the Geneva Accords, divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel and planned elections to unite the country in 1956.
[hist-eur5-s2.7.2] The Accords also made Laos and Cambodia independent.
[hist-eur5-s2.7.3] The United States did not sign the accords, which left it free to support a separate government in the South.
[hist-eur5-s2.8.b] The Accords did not bring unity. The division hardened, France left, and the United States took its place in the South.
[hist-eur5-s2.8.0] The elections planned for 1956 were never held, because the South's government, backed by the United States, refused to hold them.
[hist-eur5-s2.8.1] Ngô Đình Diem became premier of the South in 1954 and president in 1955 with United States backing.
[hist-eur5-s2.8.2] French forces withdrew from the South by 1956, and the United States replaced France as the main outside power in South Vietnam.
[hist-eur5-s2.8.3] In the North the Lao Dong (Workers') Party ran the state under Ho Chi Minh, with Soviet and Chinese aid.
[hist-eur5-s2.8.4] Ho's land reform of 1953–56, which redistributed land from landlords to peasants, was violent, and the government publicly admitted errors in 1956.
[hist-eur5-s2.9.b] The other two parts of French Indochina took a different path from Vietnam. Both gained independence from France in 1953.
[hist-eur5-s2.9.0] In Cambodia King Norodom Sihanouk's ‘royal crusade’, a campaign he led to press France for independence, helped win independence in 1953.
[hist-eur5-s2.9.1] Laos also became independent in 1953, though within the French Union at first. Geneva in 1954 confirmed the independence of both Laos and Cambodia.
[hist-eur5-s2.9.2] In Laos the communist Pathet Lao, allied to the Viet Minh, held areas in the north-east and fought the royal government after 1954.
[hist-eur5-s2.10.b] The Japanese occupation ended Dutch control and allowed Indonesian nationalists to build organisations and armed forces. When Japan surrendered, they declared independence before the Dutch could return.
[hist-eur5-s2.10.0] The Japanese occupation of 1942–45 ended Dutch rule. It drew heavily on Indonesian forced labourers, known as romusha, but it also put Indonesians in senior positions that they had never held under the Dutch.
[hist-eur5-s2.10.1] The Japanese trained Indonesian forces, notably the PETA volunteer army set up in 1943, so Indonesians gained weapons and military skills that could later be used against the Dutch.
[hist-eur5-s2.10.2] The Japanese allowed nationalists such as Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta to speak publicly, which gave them a platform to reach ordinary Indonesians.
[hist-eur5-s2.10.3] On 1 June 1945 Sukarno gave a speech setting out the Pancasila, five principles on which an independent Indonesia would be based.
[hist-eur5-s2.10.4] Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed Indonesian independence in Jakarta on 17 August 1945, two days after Japan announced its surrender. Sukarno became president and Hatta vice-president.
[hist-eur5-s2.10.5] The Dutch refused to recognise the proclamation until the transfer of sovereignty in 1949, so independence had to be won against them.
[hist-eur5-s2.10.6] Dutch forces returned with Allied support in late 1945. British and Indonesian forces fought at Surabaya in November 1945, and British forces withdrew in 1946, leaving the Dutch troops to face the Republic.
[hist-eur5-s2.11.b] The Dutch were willing to talk but not to give up control. The agreements they signed were vague, and each led to new fighting.
[hist-eur5-s2.11.0] The Linggadjati Agreement was initialled on 15 November 1946, with formal signing following in 1947. The Netherlands recognised the Republic's de facto authority (its authority in practice) over Java, Madura and Sumatra, but Dutch sovereignty was retained in the interim.
[hist-eur5-s2.11.1] It also provided for a federal United States of Indonesia linked to the Netherlands in a union. The Dutch and the Republic then disagreed over what it meant, and the Dutch reinterpreted it in their own favour.
[hist-eur5-s2.11.2] The first Dutch ‘police action’ (the Dutch name for a military offensive) began in July 1947 and occupied much of the Indonesian-held territory in Java and Sumatra.
[hist-eur5-s2.11.3] The UN Security Council called for a ceasefire in August 1947 and set up a Good Offices Committee, so the dispute now became an international matter.
[hist-eur5-s2.11.4] The Renville Agreement of January 1948 was signed aboard the US ship Renville and left Dutch forces holding large parts of Java and Sumatra.
[hist-eur5-s2.12.b] The Republic survived a crisis at home and a second Dutch attack. International reaction then turned against the Dutch.
[hist-eur5-s2.12.0] In September 1948 the Republic's forces put down a communist rising at Madiun, which helped to win American sympathy for the Republic because it showed the Republic would fight communists.
[hist-eur5-s2.12.1] The second police action of 19 December 1948 took Yogyakarta, the Republic's capital at the time, and imprisoned Sukarno and Hatta. A government in exile on Sumatra continued the resistance, so the capture did not end the war.
[hist-eur5-s2.12.2] The UN Security Council demanded a ceasefire and the release of the leaders. Its resolutions of December 1948 and January 1949 also called for restoration of the Republic's government, but did not formally condemn the Dutch.
[hist-eur5-s2.12.3] United States pressure on the Netherlands, which included the threat that Marshall Plan aid (the American programme of aid for rebuilding postwar Europe) could be affected, helped bring the Dutch to the negotiating table.
[hist-eur5-s2.13.b] Under pressure, the Dutch finally negotiated. The settlement gave Indonesia independence but left some disputes open.
[hist-eur5-s2.13.0] The Round Table Conference at The Hague (August–November 1949) settled the terms of independence.
[hist-eur5-s2.13.1] Sovereignty was transferred on 27 December 1949 to the Republic of the United States of Indonesia.
[hist-eur5-s2.13.2] Indonesia agreed to take over about 4.3 billion guilders of Dutch East Indies debt, a heavy burden for a new state.
[hist-eur5-s2.13.3] West New Guinea was left to later negotiation, so the Dutch kept it and the question became a dispute with Indonesia.
[hist-eur5-s2.13.4] A Netherlands-Indonesian Union was set up, but it was a weak link that did not last.
[hist-eur5-s2.14.b] Even while Indonesia struggled at home, Sukarno gave it a voice abroad by hosting a conference of Asian and African states.
[hist-eur5-s2.14.0] The Asian–African Conference at Bandung in April 1955 brought together 29 countries.
[hist-eur5-s2.14.1] It promoted non-alignment (staying out of both Cold War blocs) and anti-colonialism (opposition to colonial rule).
[hist-eur5-s2.14.2] Sukarno was its most prominent host, which raised his standing and Indonesia's among newly independent states.
[hist-eur5-s2.15.b] Independence did not bring stable government. Indonesia's parliamentary system produced weak governments, and the regions turned against Java.
[hist-eur5-s2.15.0] The first free national election, in September 1955, left four parties of similar size (PNI, Masjumi, Nahdlatul Ulama and the PKI), so no party could govern alone and unstable coalition cabinets followed.
[hist-eur5-s2.15.1] The regional rebellions of 1957–58, the PRRI in Sumatra and Permesta in Sulawesi, protested against Java's domination.
[hist-eur5-s2.15.2] The army put the rebellions down, which strengthened the army's political role.
[hist-eur5-s2.16.b] Faced with instability, Sukarno ended parliamentary government and ruled through the army and a balance of political forces.
[hist-eur5-s2.16.0] Sukarno introduced ‘Guided Democracy’ by decree on 5 July 1959, returning to the 1945 constitution and ruling with a National Front and the army. This ended parliamentary government.
[hist-eur5-s2.16.1] He promoted ‘Nasakom’, from nasionalisme, agama (religion) and komunisme, the union of nationalism, religion and communism, so that all three groups would back his rule.
[hist-eur5-s2.16.2] Sukarno had himself named president for life in 1963.
[hist-eur5-s2.16.3] The PKI, with about three million members by the mid-1960s, was the largest communist party outside the communist states, and the army distrusted it.
[hist-eur5-s2.17.b] Independence did not end the dispute with the Dutch. Sukarno turned on Dutch property and pressed Indonesia's claim to West New Guinea.
[hist-eur5-s2.17.0] From 1957 Sukarno took over Dutch businesses in Indonesia, and the Dutch-owned enterprises were placed under Indonesian control.
[hist-eur5-s2.17.1] West New Guinea stayed under Dutch rule until 1962, when it passed to a UN administration, and it was transferred to Indonesia on 1 May 1963.
[hist-eur5-s2.17.2] A UN-supervised 1969 vote, the Act of Free Choice, confirmed Indonesian rule in West New Guinea. It was held among about 1,000 chosen representatives and has been widely criticised.
[hist-eur5-s2.18.b] In the early 1960s Sukarno turned Indonesia's energies outward, which isolated it abroad and drained its economy at home.
[hist-eur5-s2.18.0] The Federation of Malaysia was formed in September 1963. Konfrontasi (confrontation), 1963–66, was Indonesia's opposition to it, including armed raids into Malaysian Borneo.
[hist-eur5-s2.18.1] Sukarno withdrew Indonesia from the United Nations in January 1965 and moved towards closer relations with China.
[hist-eur5-s2.19.b] Economic decline and the rivalry between the army and the PKI came to a head in 1965 and ended Sukarno's rule.
[hist-eur5-s2.19.0] Economic decline in the early 1960s, with high inflation and heavy spending on Konfrontasi, weakened Sukarno's position, and the army grew more hostile to the PKI.
[hist-eur5-s2.19.1] After an attempted coup on 30 September 1965 in which six generals were killed, General Suharto took control of the army.
[hist-eur5-s2.19.2] Anti-communist killings followed, with estimates ranging from about 500,000 to 1 million or more.
[hist-eur5-s2.19.3] The Supersemar order of 11 March 1966 stripped Sukarno of real power, and he was placed under house arrest.
[hist-eur5-s2.19.4] Suharto became acting president in 1967 and president in 1968. Sukarno died in June 1970.
[hist-eur5-s2.20.b] Examiners often ask for the problems of Sukarno's Indonesia in two groups, internal and external. Each problem needs a named event with a date.
[hist-eur5-s2.20.0] Internal problems: instability after the 1955 election, regional rebellion (PRRI and Permesta, 1957–58), the rivalry between the army and the PKI that led to the coup attempt of 30 September 1965, and economic decline in the early 1960s.
[hist-eur5-s2.20.1] External problems: the Dutch and West New Guinea (Dutch rule to 1962, transfer in 1963), Konfrontasi against Malaysia (1963–66), and relations with the UN and China (Indonesia left the UN in January 1965).
[hist-eur5-s2.21.b] Ho Chi Minh led the Vietnamese independence movement from the 1920s and was the head of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. His career shows how a communist and nationalist leader used war and diplomacy to win independence.
[hist-eur5-s2.21.0] Born Nguyen Sinh Cung in 1890 in central Vietnam, he left Vietnam in 1911 and spent about thirty years abroad, including in France, Britain, the Soviet Union and China. He used the name Nguyen Ai Quoc before adopting Ho Chi Minh in 1942.
[hist-eur5-s2.21.1] Abroad, in 1919 he presented a petition for Vietnamese rights to the Versailles peace conference, which was ignored. He joined the French Communist Party in 1920, worked with the Communist International, and in 1930 helped found the Indochinese Communist Party.
[hist-eur5-s2.21.2] He returned to Vietnam in 1941 and founded the Viet Minh, though the Chinese Nationalists held him in 1942–43. He was a participant in the August Revolution of 1945 and proclaimed the Democratic Republic on 2 September 1945.
[hist-eur5-s2.21.3] As president of the Democratic Republic he led the war against France, with Vo Nguyen Giap commanding the army, so Ho provided political leadership while Giap led the fighting.
[hist-eur5-s2.21.4] He was shaped by events as well as shaping them. The Japanese occupation broke French rule and the French attempt to return gave him the chance to lead a national movement, which he built on communist organisation.
[hist-eur5-s2.21.5] Contemporaries in France and the United States regarded him as a communist agitator. Many Vietnamese nationalists regarded him as the leader who had made independence possible.
[hist-eur5-s2.21.6] Historians debate whether his nationalism or his communism was the stronger force in his politics. They also debate the role of the Soviet Union and China in supporting him.
[hist-eur5-s2.21.7] Known as ‘Uncle Ho’, he led North Vietnam after the 1954 division, where the Lao Dong Party ran the state with Soviet and Chinese aid. He died in September 1969, before the end of the Vietnam War, and Vietnam was unified under communist rule in 1975.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.b] Sukarno was the first president of Indonesia and the central figure in the independence struggle against the Dutch. His career shows both what a gifted nationalist leader could achieve and where his methods failed.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.0] Born on 6 June 1901 in East Java, he studied engineering at Bandung (graduating in 1926) and founded the Indonesian Nationalist Party (PNI) in 1927. The Dutch imprisoned him in 1929 and released him in 1931, then exiled him to remote islands until 1942.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.1] Under the Japanese occupation he worked with the administration and encouraged mass mobilisation. Some Dutch and later Indonesian critics regarded this as collaboration, though it also gave him a mass audience.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.2] On 1 June 1945 his Pancasila speech showed his aim of balancing nationalists, Muslims and communists in one state. He was a participant in the proclamation of 17 August 1945, with Hatta as vice-president, and became the first president.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.3] He was a gifted orator who tried to hold together nationalists, religious groups, the army and the communists. He was never a leader of the PKI but worked closely with it in the early 1960s, which caused suspicion in the army.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.4] His balance collapsed in 1965–66. After the coup attempt, Suharto took control of the army, the Supersemar order of 11 March 1966 stripped Sukarno of real power, and he was placed under house arrest until his death in June 1970.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.5] Contemporaries were divided. Indonesians called him Bung Karno (brother Karno) and the father of the nation, while Dutch officials regarded him as a collaborator with Japan, and the army and the Islamic parties came to think him too close to the communists.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.6] His later rule is judged harshly by many historians: Guided Democracy ended parliamentary government, and the economy and a foreign policy of confrontation left Indonesia isolated by 1965. Others credit him with giving a diverse archipelago a national language, Indonesian, and a unifying ideology in the Pancasila.
[hist-eur5-s2.22.7] The historian Benedict Anderson, in Java in a Time of Revolution (1972), argued that the revolution was driven by young pemuda activists, trained under the Japanese, as much as by Sukarno and Hatta.
[hist-eur5-s2.23.b] Ho Chi Minh and Sukarno both won independence, but they won it differently, and the European powers responded in different ways.
[hist-eur5-s2.23.0] In Vietnam France fought a long war and was beaten at Dien Bien Phu, while in Indonesia the Dutch fought two police actions and then yielded to UN and US pressure.
[hist-eur5-s2.23.1] Ho's method was armed struggle led by a communist party, and it produced a party-state in the North run by the Lao Dong Party.
[hist-eur5-s2.23.2] Sukarno's method was negotiation, diplomacy and balancing, and in power it became a balancing act between nationalists, religious groups, the army and the communists, which collapsed in 1965.
[hist-eur5-s2.23.3] Contemporary and later views differ for both men: Ho was a communist agitator to some and the leader of independence to others, while Sukarno was father of the nation to some and a collaborator or a failed ruler to others.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.b] Both movements won independence, but at a cost, and neither produced stable government. Weigh achievements against limits to reach a judgement.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.0] Achievement: Vietnam and Indonesia both defeated attempts by returning colonial powers to restore their old authority, Indonesia in 1949 and the Viet Minh in 1954.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.1] Achievement: Indonesia gained sovereignty in 1949 after a diplomatic campaign that involved the UN and the United States, not only military action.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.2] Achievement: Sukarno's diplomacy at Bandung gave newly independent states a voice in world politics.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.3] Limit: the Dutch police actions, the French war and the Geneva settlement all showed that European powers could resist independence for years.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.4] Limit: Vietnam was divided in 1954, and the division became the basis of a larger war between the two Vietnamese states from the late 1950s.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.5] Limit: Guided Democracy and the economic decline of the 1960s ended in the 1965 coup, the killings that followed, and Suharto's rise to power.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.6] Limit: independence did not bring stable government, as Vietnam divided in 1954 and Indonesia moved from parliamentary democracy to authoritarian rule within a decade.
[hist-eur5-s2.24.7] Later view: Benedict Anderson argued that the Indonesian revolution of 1945 was driven by young pemuda activists, trained under the Japanese, as much as by leaders such as Sukarno and Hatta.

CANDIDATES hist-eur5-s1: 1. World War II and Asia; case study: British withdrawal from India, 1945–1947
[hist-eur5-s1.0.b] To follow the withdrawal of 1945–47 you need to know what Britain was leaving. India was Britain's largest colony, ruled by an outside power, and by the 1930s its nationalists were demanding self-rule.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.0] Britain ruled India directly through the Crown from 1858, after the rising of 1857; this system was called the Raj. The Viceroy (the Crown's representative in India) headed the government.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.1] Over 500 princely states (Indian-ruled states under British paramountcy, meaning Britain controlled their foreign affairs and defence) remained outside direct British rule, so any transfer of power also had to settle their future.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.2] The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, began by petitioning the British for a say in government, but by the 1930s it was demanding self-rule, which is why it became the main opponent of the Raj.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.3] Britain granted reforms in stages, in 1919 and 1935, giving Indians a growing share in provincial government without handing over control of India as a whole.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.4] Hindus formed the majority, and Muslims were about a quarter of the population of British India, concentrated in the north-west and the east. Many Muslims feared their rights would be weak under majority rule, and this fear underlies the later demand for Pakistan.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.b] Three ideas run through this whole topic. Learn them as they are used here, because they are used throughout.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.0] Colonial rule is control of a territory by an outside power. It is governed for the benefit of the imperial state, usually without the consent of the people ruled, which is why nationalists called it illegitimate.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.1] De-colonisation is the ending of European rule and the creation of independent states, mainly from 1945 to the 1960s. India's independence in 1947 was one of its first and largest cases.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.2] The (British) Commonwealth is an association of Britain and its former dominions and colonies. A dominion is a self-governing state that keeps the Crown as head of state; dominion status was what Britain offered India in 1942 and what India and Pakistan first received in 1947.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.3] In 1949 the Commonwealth changed so that republics could stay in; the London Declaration of April 1949 let India remain a member as a republic.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.4] Partition means dividing British India into two states, India and Pakistan, along religious lines. Other terms used below are satyagraha (non-violent resistance), Quit India, the Cabinet Mission, the Muslim League and the Indian National Congress.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.b] In the war against Japan, European armies lost one colony after another within months. This weakened the claim that Europeans were strong enough to rule Asians, and it gave nationalists new arguments.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.0] Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 (8 December in local time in Asia) and landed in British Malaya the same day. Hong Kong surrendered on 25 December 1941.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.1] Singapore, Britain's main base in the East, surrendered on 15 February 1942. About 80,000 British, Indian and Australian troops became prisoners of war, so the defeat was not only large but humiliating.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.2] Japan overran Burma in 1942, closing the Burma Road that supplied China and threatening India's eastern frontier, so the war now reached the edge of India itself.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.3] Japan also overran the Dutch East Indies in early 1942.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.4] Japanese troops entered northern Indochina in 1940, and Japan removed the French administration on 9 March 1945.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.5] Japanese propaganda promoted a ‘Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere’ and the slogan ‘Asia for the Asians’. This encouraged anti-colonial nationalists across the region.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.6] In short, European rule in Asia had been shown to be breakable, and Britain emerged exhausted. This is the main implication of the war for Britain's place in Asia.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.b] India was not only a battlefield but a source of soldiers and supplies. The war changed India's army, its politics and its attitude to British rule.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.0] About 2.5 million men served in the British Indian Army during the war, all of them volunteers, and the army expanded greatly as a result.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.1] The Indian National Army (INA) was first formed in 1942 under Mohan Singh from Indian prisoners taken at Singapore. Subhas Chandra Bose, a former Congress president, took it over in 1943, and it fought alongside Japan.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.2] Bose died in August 1945. After the war the British put INA officers on trial at the Red Fort in Delhi, beginning in November 1945, because they had fought against the Crown.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.3] The Red Fort trials caused a public outcry that united Congress and League opinion, so punishing the officers was politically risky. The sentences were not carried out.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.4] The Bengal famine of 1943 killed an estimated two to three million people and damaged British prestige in India. Wartime requisitioning, the loss of Burmese rice imports, inflation and a poor government response all contributed, and historians debate the causes.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.b] Mohandas Gandhi turned Congress from a body that petitioned into a mass movement. His method was non-violent civil disobedience.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.0] Gandhi returned from South Africa in January 1915 and became the dominant Congress leader after 1920.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.1] Satyagraha, meaning ‘truth-force’, was non-violent civil disobedience. Gandhi first used it in India in peasant campaigns at Champaran in Bihar (1917, indigo growers) and Kheda in Gujarat (1918, over land revenue).
[hist-eur5-s1.4.2] At Amritsar on 13 April 1919, General Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed crowd at Jallianwala Bagh. The British counted 379 dead and Indian estimates exceed 1,000, so say hundreds or give both; it turned many Indians against British rule.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.3] The Non-Cooperation movement of 1920–22 urged boycotts of British goods, courts, schools and councils. After the Chauri Chaura incident of February 1922, when a mob killed policemen, Gandhi called it off and was jailed in 1922.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.4] The Salt March ran from 12 March to 6 April 1930, about 240 miles (about 385 km) from the Sabarmati ashram near Ahmedabad to Dandi on the coast, where Gandhi made salt in defiance of the British salt monopoly.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.5] Tens of thousands were arrested in the civil disobedience that followed the Salt March, which shows how far the movement had spread.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.b] Constitutional reform brought Congress into government for the first time. The outbreak of war then tested the relationship again, and each wartime offer or demand ended in confrontation.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.0] The Government of India Act 1935 gave Indians self-government in the provinces. In the elections of early 1937, in eleven provinces, Congress won outright majorities in five and went on to form ministries (provincial governments) in seven.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.1] On 3 September 1939 the Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, declared war on India's behalf without consulting Indian leaders. Congress ministers resigned in October–November 1939 in protest.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.2] The Cripps Mission of March 1942 offered dominion status after the war, with the right to secede and the right of provinces to opt out. Congress rejected it because it allowed provinces to opt out and gave no immediate control of defence, so the offer was withdrawn.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.3] On 8 August 1942 at Bombay Congress passed the Quit India resolution, demanding British withdrawal. Gandhi's slogan was ‘Do or die’.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.4] Gandhi and the Congress leaders were arrested on 9 August. Widespread unrest and sabotage followed and were crushed within weeks; Gandhi was released in May 1944 because of ill health.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.b] Partition grew out of the Muslim League's campaign for separate Muslim states. The Congress leadership would not accept this for the whole of India, so each side's claim to speak for India's Muslims became the central dispute.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.0] The All-India Muslim League was founded at Dhaka in December 1906 to protect Muslim political interests, twenty-one years after Congress.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.1] Muhammad Ali Jinnah, a barrister and one-time Congress member, took the League's leadership in 1934 and was known as Quaid-i-Azam (great leader).
[hist-eur5-s1.6.2] The name Pakistan was coined in 1933 by Choudhry Rahmat Ali, a student at Cambridge. The League did poorly among Muslim voters in the 1937 elections.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.3] The League used the years of Congress rule to claim that Muslims could not be safe in a Hindu-majority state. Jinnah called a Day of Deliverance on 22 December 1939, when the Congress ministries resigned.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.4] The Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940 called for independent states in the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and east of India. It never used the word Pakistan.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.5] Jinnah argued that Hindus and Muslims were two nations, and that Congress claimed to speak for all Indians while representing mainly Hindus. This is the two-nation argument.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.b] By 1945 the League could claim real support. This made it impossible for Congress or Britain to bypass it, but it also exposed the hardest problem, the provinces where no clean division was possible.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.0] The Simla Conference, held from 25 June to 14 July 1945 and called by Viceroy Wavell, broke down because Jinnah insisted that only the League could name the Muslim members of the Viceroy's council.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.1] In the 1945–46 elections the League won all 30 Muslim seats in the central assembly and the great majority of Muslim provincial seats, which gave it a claim to speak for Muslim India. Congress won most general seats.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.2] Punjab (Muslim majority, with large Hindu and Sikh minorities) and Bengal (Muslim majority, with a large Hindu minority) had mixed populations, so any border would leave large minorities on the wrong side.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.3] For this reason Punjab and Bengal became the central problem in the partition settlement, and they were the places where the worst violence later occurred.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.b] The Labour government that took office in 1945 was committed to Indian self-rule, and Britain's post-war weakness made it hard to resist. Within a year events in India confirmed that the old system could not continue.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.0] Clement Attlee's Labour Party won a landslide in the general election of July 1945, and Attlee became prime minister on 26 July 1945, committed to self-rule for India.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.1] Britain had spent heavily on the war. It borrowed about 3.75 billion dollars from the United States in 1945–46, and its debts and the cost of occupation left it unable to afford a large army in India.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.2] By 1945 Britain also owed India over one billion pounds in sterling balances (credits for wartime supplies), which reversed the old financial relationship and reduced the economic value of the Raj.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.3] In February 1946 (18–23 February) ratings of the Royal Indian Navy mutinied at Bombay over pay, conditions and racial discrimination. The mutiny spread to other ports and was suppressed, but it showed that the armed forces could no longer be relied on to support British rule.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.b] In 1946 Britain tried to agree a plan for a united India with both parties. The attempt failed, and the failure turned into communal violence.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.0] The Cabinet Mission (Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps and A. V. Alexander) was in India from March to June 1946. Its plan of 16 May 1946 proposed a loose federation with three groups of provinces and a weak centre, so it rejected the full Pakistan demand.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.1] Both sides accepted the plan at first, then disagreed over the grouping of provinces, so the League withdrew its acceptance in July 1946 and the mission failed to win agreement.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.2] The League called Direct Action Day for 16 August 1946. Communal killings in Calcutta left thousands dead (estimates range from about 4,000 to 10,000) and many more injured.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.3] Violence spread to Noakhali in East Bengal and Bihar in October–November 1946, and to the Punjab in spring 1947, so fear of civil war grew.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.4] An interim government headed by Jawaharlal Nehru took office on 2 September 1946, and the League joined it on 26 October 1946. The two parties still could not work together.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.b] Early in 1947 Britain set a deadline and sent a new Viceroy to meet it. Once Mountbatten judged that unity was impossible, the timetable was shortened sharply.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.0] On 20 February 1947 Attlee announced that British rule would end by June 1948 at the latest, and Lord Wavell was replaced as Viceroy by Lord Mountbatten.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.1] Mountbatten, Attlee's choice, was a royal cousin and wartime Supreme Allied Commander in South-East Asia. He arrived in Delhi on 22 March 1947 and was sworn in as Viceroy on 24 March.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.2] He judged that a united India was no longer possible and pressed for a quick transfer of power, partly because he feared civil war if he delayed.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.3] Under the 3 June 1947 plan the assemblies of Punjab and Bengal voted on dividing their provinces, and a referendum in the North-West Frontier Province and Sylhet decided their future.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.4] The plan accepted partition. The date of 15 August 1947 was announced on 4 June, ten months earlier than the June 1948 deadline, once the principal Indian leaders agreed.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.b] Independence on 15 August 1947 created two new states and divided the Punjab and Bengal. Because the borders had to be drawn so quickly, the settlement was rushed.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.0] The Indian Independence Act received royal assent on 18 July 1947 and ended British rule, creating two independent dominions, India and Pakistan.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.1] Power was transferred at midnight on 14–15 August 1947 (Pakistan's day was 14 August, India's 15 August). Jinnah became Governor-General of Pakistan, Mountbatten stayed as India's first Governor-General and Nehru became prime minister.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.2] Pakistan was formed in two parts, West Pakistan and East Bengal (renamed East Pakistan in 1955), separated by about 1,600 km of Indian territory.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.3] Cyril Radcliffe, a London barrister who had never been to India, chaired the commissions that drew the Punjab and Bengal boundaries. He had about five weeks, and the award was completed about 12 August.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.4] The Radcliffe Line was published on 17 August 1947, two days after independence.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.5] The Indian Army, the civil service, the railways and other assets had to be divided between the two dominions in a few weeks, which put the administration under heavy strain.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.b] Partition caused mass forced migration, with killing on a huge scale. Alongside it came a dispute over the princely states, which has continued to the present.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.0] About 10 to 15 million people were displaced as Hindus and Sikhs moved from West Pakistan into India and Muslims moved into Pakistan. Give the range, because nobody knows the exact number.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.1] Communal violence in the Punjab and Bengal killed many people. Estimates of the death toll range from about 200,000 to about 2 million, so do not pick a single figure.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.2] The Punjab was the worst affected area, and trains carrying refugees were attacked, which is why the journeys themselves became a source of mass death.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.3] The princely states, over 500 in number, had to join India or Pakistan. The Nizam of Hyderabad and the Maharaja of Kashmir were the most contested cases.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.4] Hyderabad was a Hindu-majority state ruled by a Muslim Nizam, so its choice was disputed, and India annexed it by military action in September 1948.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.5] Kashmir had a Muslim-majority population under a Hindu Maharaja, Hari Singh. After a tribal invasion from Pakistan he signed the Instrument of Accession to India on 26 October 1947, and Indian troops were flown in, so fighting over Kashmir between India and Pakistan began that year.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.b] Independence did not end the conflicts that partition created, but India and Britain found a lasting arrangement. India stayed in the Commonwealth as a republic.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.0] Gandhi fasted in Calcutta (September 1947) and Delhi (January 1948) to stop communal violence. He was shot dead in Delhi on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.1] Jinnah, Governor-General of Pakistan, died in September 1948, only a year after the state he had campaigned for was created.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.2] The first Kashmir war of 1947–49 ended with a UN-brokered ceasefire from 1 January 1949, leaving a line that divided the state and is still disputed.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.3] The London Declaration of April 1949 allowed India to become a republic while remaining in the Commonwealth, accepting the King as symbolic head of the association.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.4] India became a republic on 26 January 1950, with Jawaharlal Nehru as prime minister, and under Nehru followed non-alignment (staying out of the rival blocs of the Cold War).
[hist-eur5-s1.13.5] India remained a major trading partner of Britain, with the sterling area (a group of countries whose currencies were tied to the pound) linking the two economies.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.b] The end of the Raj mattered differently to each side. For Britain it was a loss of power and a change in the Commonwealth, while for India and Pakistan it brought both freedom and a long legacy of conflict.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.0] For Britain, India was the largest and most valuable of its possessions, so its loss ended the Raj, which had ruled India directly since 1858.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.1] The loss reduced Britain's role in Asia and made the Commonwealth its main continuing link with former colonies.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.2] Burma (January 1948) and Ceylon (February 1948) became independent soon afterwards, so India's independence was an example for other colonies.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.3] India's decision to remain in the Commonwealth as a republic established that membership did not depend on accepting the monarch as head of state.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.4] For India and Pakistan the withdrawal brought independence and, in India, democracy, but it also brought partition, mass migration, killing and the Kashmir dispute.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.b] Historians offer several causes, and the best answers weigh them rather than listing them. The main explanations can be set out as paired causes.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.0] Nationalist explanation: decades of Congress agitation, from non-cooperation to Quit India, made British rule impossible to maintain without a degree of repression the British public would not accept.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.1] Economic explanation: Britain was exhausted by war and owed India over one billion pounds in sterling balances, which reversed the old financial relationship and reduced the value of holding the Raj.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.2] Political explanation: Labour's 1945 government was committed to Indian self-rule, and Attlee argued that Britain could not hold India against the will of its people.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.3] Security explanation: the INA trials and the 1946 naval mutiny led some British officials, including Viceroy Wavell, to doubt that the Indian Army and police could be relied on to suppress a mass rising.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.4] Communal explanation: the failure of Congress and the League to agree, and the violence in Calcutta, Bihar and the Punjab, led Mountbatten to fear civil war if he delayed.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.b] Leaving India proved far harder than deciding to leave. Each difficulty can be tied to a date and a person.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.0] Congress and the League could not agree: the Cabinet Mission failed in 1946 and the interim government could not work as a unit.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.1] Communal violence, beginning with Direct Action Day in Calcutta in August 1946 and spreading to Noakhali, Bihar and the Punjab, made the transfer of power more dangerous.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.2] The princely states, over 500 in number, had to be brought into India or Pakistan, and Hyderabad and Kashmir were disputed.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.3] Speed and the boundary: Mountbatten's critics argue that bringing the date forward by ten months left too little time to plan the boundary and policing, while his defenders argue that delay would have meant more violence.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.4] Radcliffe had about five weeks to draw the Punjab and Bengal boundaries, and the army, civil service and railways were divided in a few weeks, so the administration was under severe strain.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.b] Gandhi was the leading figure of the Indian independence movement and the most influential Indian voice in the British withdrawal. His life and methods show what non-violent mass politics could and could not achieve.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.0] Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869 at Porbandar in Gujarat. He trained as a barrister at the Inner Temple in London (called to the bar in 1891) and practised law in South Africa from 1893 to 1914.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.1] In South Africa he led campaigns against discriminatory laws affecting Indians, and developed the method of non-violent resistance that he later called satyagraha.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.2] After 1915 he built a mass movement among peasants and urban workers, wore simple dress and promoted khadi (hand-spun cloth) as a symbol of self-reliance.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.3] His stated aims were swaraj (self-rule), Hindu–Muslim unity, ending untouchability and non-violence. In Hind Swaraj (1909) he rejected industrial civilisation, and he called the untouchables Harijans, a term now regarded as patronising.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.4] At the London Round Table Conference of autumn 1931 he attended as Congress's sole representative; the talks failed over separate electorates and minorities.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.5] After the 1932 Communal Award gave Dalits separate electorates, Gandhi fasted in Yerwada jail, and the Poona Pact (September 1932) gave Dalits reserved seats in general electorates instead. B. R. Ambedkar led the Dalit side and remained a critic.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.6] He took part in every major Congress campaign and was jailed several times, including from August 1942 to May 1944.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.7] In late 1946 he walked through villages in Noakhali to calm communal violence. On 15 August 1947 he was in Calcutta fasting and praying rather than celebrating independence, and he was shot dead on 30 January 1948.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.b] Gandhi was seen very differently by those who lived through the period, and historians still disagree about how much he achieved. This is the material for any judgement of his importance.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.0] Many Indians revered him as the Mahatma (great soul).
[hist-eur5-s1.18.1] British officials often treated him as an obstacle, and Jinnah saw him as a Hindu leader who spoke for Hindus, not all Indians.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.2] Ambedkar was one of his sharpest critics, because he led the Dalit side in the clash over separate electorates.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.3] Judith Brown, in Gandhi: Prisoner of Hope (1989), presents him as a skilled leader who built a mass movement but whose achievement was limited.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.4] Other historians stress war, the INA, naval unrest, economics and Labour's policy over Gandhi's role, and Yasmin Khan, in The Great Partition (2007), stresses the haste and the part played by the state in the violence.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.5] Critics also point to his limited success on caste and his failure to prevent partition. In 1947 he was a participant, not the decision-maker, so he could not stop it.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.b] A good judgement sets what the independence movement achieved against what it could not do. The points below pair each achievement with a limit, and end with the views of the time and of later historians.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.0] Achievement: non-violent mass protest became the central method of Indian politics, and campaigns such as the Salt March drew in very large numbers of people.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.1] Limit: the Gandhian campaigns did not secure a united India, and could not prevent partition or the violence that followed.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.2] Achievement: the Quit India movement and the 1942 unrest showed the British that their authority could not rest on force alone.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.3] Limit: partition was agreed by the Congress and League leaders in June 1947, not by Gandhi, who opposed it, so the movement did not control the outcome.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.4] Achievement: the transfer of power ended British rule without a colonial war of the kind France fought in Indochina and Algeria.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.5] Limit: withdrawal was rushed in 1947, and the boundary settlement was made within weeks, which left the population to bear the cost.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.6] Limit: the Gandhian method relied on the moral restraint of the British government and on disciplined followers, and it did not stop the communal killings that followed Direct Action Day in 1946.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.7] Contemporary view: Gandhi was revered as the Mahatma by many Indians but criticised by Jinnah and Ambedkar. Later view: many historians judge Mountbatten's haste and the rushed Radcliffe work as major contributors to the scale of the violence.
