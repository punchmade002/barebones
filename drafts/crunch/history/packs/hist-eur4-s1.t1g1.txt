[hist-eur4-s1.0.b] The wartime alliance of the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union held while Germany was defeated, but the aims of its members diverged once the war ended.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.0] Yalta Conference (4–11 February 1945): Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin agreed that Germany would be divided into occupation zones and that Poland would have a government broadened to include non-communists, with free elections promised.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.1] The occupation zones had been agreed by the Allies in 1944. The Soviet Union took the east, the USA the south and centre-west, Britain the north-west and France the south-west.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.2] Berlin lay deep inside the Soviet zone and was itself divided into four sectors, one for each occupying power.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.3] Potsdam Conference (17 July–2 August 1945): Truman, Stalin and, after the British election, Attlee, disputed reparations and the German–Polish border.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.4] Stalin's security aim was a buffer of friendly governments along the Soviet western frontier, after invasions from the west in 1914 and 1941.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.5] The USA tested its first atomic bomb on 16 July 1945 and used atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August; the Soviet Union tested its own bomb in August 1949, ending the American monopoly.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.6] At Yalta the Allies also agreed that the Soviet Union would join the war against Japan, and the Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections in liberated states; Stalin's later disregard of it fed Western distrust.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.7] In October 1944 Churchill and Stalin had informally split influence in the Balkans (the percentages agreement), giving the Soviet Union the lead in Romania and Bulgaria and Britain in Greece.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.8] President Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945, and Harry Truman, who took a harder line towards Moscow, became president.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.9] The Red Army occupied Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, eastern Germany and much of Czechoslovakia by May 1945, so Soviet power on the ground shaped what the West could demand.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.10] Poland was the first flashpoint: the Soviet Union backed the communist-dominated Lublin government, while Britain and the USA backed the London-based government-in-exile.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.b] The Allies agreed broad aims for a defeated Germany, but each occupying power ran its own zone, so the aims were never fully put into practice.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.0] Potsdam set four aims for Germany: demilitarisation, denazification, democratisation and decentralisation, with the economy to be run as a single unit.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.1] Each power took reparations from its own zone, and the Soviet Union also dismantled factories and shipped machinery east from its zone.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.2] The Soviets wanted reparations from the industrial Ruhr in the west; the Western powers refused, because they wanted the western zones to recover.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.3] The territory east of the Oder and Neisse rivers was placed under Polish and Soviet administration until a peace treaty, and German inhabitants were expelled from it.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.4] Without a peace treaty, Germany remained under the control of the four occupying powers, which met as the Allied Control Council in Berlin.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.5] By 1947 the zones were drifting apart economically, and the Western powers concluded that the single economy the Allies had agreed on could not be restored.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.b] From 1946 the Western powers publicly named the Soviet Union as the threat and began to offer aid and military guarantees to Europe.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.0] George Kennan, a US diplomat in Moscow, sent his 'Long Telegram' (22 February 1946) arguing that Soviet expansion would have to be contained by firm resistance at points of pressure.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.1] Churchill's speech at Fulton, Missouri (5 March 1946) said that from Stettin on the Baltic to Trieste on the Adriatic an iron curtain had descended across the Continent.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.2] Stalin answered in an interview in Pravda, presenting Churchill's speech as a call for war against the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.3] In February 1947 Britain told Washington that it could no longer afford to support Greece and Turkey, which created the opening for the USA to take on that role.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.4] Truman Doctrine: on 12 March 1947 President Truman asked Congress for about 400 million dollars of aid to Greece and Turkey, which faced communist insurgency (Greece) and Soviet pressure over the Turkish straits; Congress approved it in May 1947.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.5] Marshall Plan: announced by Secretary of State George Marshall in a speech at Harvard on 5 June 1947, it provided about 13 billion dollars of US aid through the European Recovery Programme (1948–52).
[hist-eur4-s1.2.6] The Soviet Union and its eastern allies were pressed to refuse Marshall aid; Czechoslovakia and Poland had shown interest before withdrawing in July 1947.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.7] The Cominform, founded in September 1947, linked the communist parties of Europe under Soviet direction.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.8] The Greek Civil War (1946–49) between the royal government and communist guerrillas was the first test of the Truman Doctrine; the guerrillas were defeated in 1949 and Greece stayed in the Western camp.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.9] Marshall's offer was open to all of Europe, but Stalin forbade eastern states to take part, and the Soviet delegation walked out of the Paris conference in July 1947.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.10] Western Europe received Marshall aid as grants and loans, with 16 countries sharing it through the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation, set up in 1948.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.b] Sovietisation was the process by which communist parties, backed by the Red Army and Soviet security police, took full control of states that had previously had mixed or non-communist governments.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.0] In 1945–47 communist parties usually governed through coalitions with peasant, socialist and liberal parties, while controlling key posts such as the interior ministry and the police.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.1] The Hungarian communist leader Mátyás Rákosi later boasted of slicing away his opponents one thin piece at a time; the phrase salami tactics is usually attributed to him and describes the method used across the region.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.2] Poland: sixteen leaders of the Polish underground were arrested and tried in Moscow in 1945, and the peasant leader Stanisław Mikołajczyk left Poland in October 1947 after rigged elections.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.3] Romania: King Michael was forced to abdicate on 30 December 1947 and a people's republic was proclaimed.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.4] Czechoslovakia: in February 1948 non-communist ministers resigned over police control, and Klement Gottwald's communists took power without a general election.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.5] Show trials and purges eliminated rivals, including the Hungarian foreign minister László Rajk, executed in 1949 after a trial in Budapest.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.6] Land reform in 1945–47 broke up large estates in Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and the Soviet zone of Germany, and gave land to small farmers, which helped the communists win support.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.7] Soviet-style institutions followed: single-party rule, secret police, central planning, and collectivisation of farms in most states.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.8] Yugoslavia under Tito was expelled from the Cominform in June 1948, showing that communist states could break from Moscow without ceasing to be communist.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.9] Hungary: in the free election of November 1945 the communists won only about 17 percent against about 57 percent for the Smallholders; Rákosi then used the police and ministry of the interior to break the Smallholders, and fraud in the 1947 election gave the communists the largest share.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.10] Bulgaria: the Fatherland Front government, dominated by communists, abolished the monarchy after a referendum in 1946, and the opposition leader Nikola Petkov was executed in 1947.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.11] Czechoslovakia was the only eastern state to remain democratic until 1948; the death of the foreign minister Jan Masaryk on 10 March 1948, in a fall from a window, shocked Western opinion.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.12] Poland: the Polish Workers' Party and Polish Socialist Party were merged into the Polish United Workers' Party in December 1948, ending independent socialist opposition.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.13] Albania under Enver Hoxha and Yugoslavia under Tito had communist governments that came to power largely through their own wartime resistance rather than the Red Army.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.b] The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and built its own trade system, which later became the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.0] Eastern European states were bound to the Soviet Union by bilateral trade agreements in which their trade was directed towards Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.1] A Soviet-led aid and trade offer to eastern Europe in 1947, often called the Molotov Plan, was the alternative to Marshall aid.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.2] Comecon, the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, was set up in January 1949 to coordinate trade and planning among the Soviet bloc states.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.3] Eastern bloc states were not allowed to take part in the Marshall Plan, which divided Europe into two economic systems.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.4] Soviet reparations and dismantling in eastern Germany slowed recovery there and made the Soviet zone economically dependent on Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.b] The Western zones were merged and rebuilt, which the Soviet Union opposed, and the split between the two halves of Germany became permanent by 1949.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.0] The American and British zones were merged into Bizonia on 1 January 1947, and the French zone was added in 1948–49 after the London talks, creating a single western area.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.1] A currency reform in the Western zones on 20 June 1948 introduced the Deutsche Mark, which the Soviet authorities saw as a threat to their zone.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.2] The Soviet Union blockaded road, rail and canal access to West Berlin from June 1948, cutting the Western sectors off from supplies.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.3] The Western powers organised an airlift that flew in food, fuel and other supplies; it delivered over two million tons before the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.4] The Federal Republic of Germany was founded on 23 May 1949 with a Basic Law drafted by a parliamentary council; Konrad Adenauer became its first chancellor in September 1949.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.5] The German Democratic Republic was proclaimed on 7 October 1949 in the Soviet zone, where the Socialist Unity Party had been formed in April 1946 by merging the communist and social democratic parties.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.6] Berlin stayed divided: West Berlin was run by the Western powers and closely tied to the Federal Republic, while East Berlin was declared the capital of the German Democratic Republic.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.7] Stalin's aim in the blockade was to force the Western powers to abandon the new currency and their plans for a West German state, or to drive them out of Berlin.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.8] General Lucius Clay, the American military governor, and the Berlin mayor Ernst Reuter led the Western and Berlin response; Truman refused to withdraw.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.9] The airlift (Operation Vittles) flew into the Tempelhof, Gatow and Tegel airfields, with planes landing at times about every minute.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.b] Each bloc formed an alliance for collective defence, and the Warsaw Pact was created largely in response to West German membership of NATO.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.0] The Brussels Treaty of March 1948 linked Britain, France and the Benelux countries in a defensive alliance that became the basis for NATO.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.1] The North Atlantic Treaty was signed in Washington on 4 April 1949 by twelve states, including the USA, Canada and most West European countries; its Article 5 treats an attack on one member as an attack on all.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.2] West Germany joined NATO in May 1955, after its rearmament had been agreed.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.3] The Warsaw Pact was signed in Warsaw on 14 May 1955 by the Soviet Union and seven eastern European states, and Soviet forces were stationed in member states.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.4] The Warsaw Pact's founding members were the Soviet Union, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, East Germany and Albania; Albania stopped taking part in 1961 and formally withdrew in 1968.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.5] NATO's first Secretary General was Lord Ismay, and the alliance's purpose was commonly summed up as keeping the Americans in, the Russians out and the Germans down.
