[hist-ire3-s6.5.3] Neville Chamberlain's government agreed partly to improve relations as war approached, and Churchill opposed the surrender of the ports.
[hist-ire3-s6.5.4] De Valera had linked the ports to partition in the talks, but Britain would not move on the border.
[hist-ire3-s6.5.5] The ports allowed Ireland to remain neutral in 1939, a policy examined in the notes on the Second World War.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.b] The inter-party government formally ended the External Relations Act and left the Commonwealth, while the British guaranteed Northern Ireland's position.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.0] Fianna Fáil lost its majority in the election of February 1948, and John A. Costello of Fine Gael became Taoiseach on 18 February 1948 in a coalition including Labour, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan and others.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.1] Seán MacBride, the Minister for External Affairs, wanted to repeal the External Relations Act, which he regarded as an anomaly.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.2] Costello announced the decision while on a visit to Canada in September 1948.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.3] The Republic of Ireland Act was passed in December 1948 and came into force on Easter Monday, 18 April 1949, repealing the External Relations Act and ending the state's link with the Commonwealth.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.4] The British Ireland Act 1949 recognised that Ireland was no longer a member of the Commonwealth, but said Irish citizens would not be treated as foreigners.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.5] It also declared that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of the Parliament of Northern Ireland, which strengthened the unionist position.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.6] The southern parties united in an anti-partition campaign in 1949, but it achieved little.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.b] De Valera dominated the 1930s and 1940s and shaped the state's constitution, relations with Britain and neutrality. Judgements about him are divided.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.0] He led Fianna Fáil in government from 1932 to 1948 and again in 1951–54 and 1957–59, and was President of Ireland from 1959 to 1973; he died in 1975.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.1] He removed the oath, the Governor-General and the Privy Council appeal, drafted the 1937 constitution and secured the ports in 1938.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.2] He kept Ireland neutral in the Second World War and banned the IRA in 1936.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.3] He did not end partition, and the Economic War cost the farming economy dearly.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.4] The social and economic record was weak: high emigration, unemployment, a conservative Catholic constitution and wartime censorship.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.5] Contemporaries: supporters, especially small farmers and workers, saw him as the man who finished the work of independence, while opponents in Cumann na nGaedheal and the Blueshirts called him a dictator.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.6] Historians: Joe Lee (Ireland 1912–1985) credits him with consolidating democracy and with strong political skills, while criticising the economy; Deirdre McMahon (Republicans and Imperialists, 1984) examined his diplomacy with Britain.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.7] Biographers such as Tim Pat Coogan (1993) and Diarmaid Ferriter (Judging Dev, 2007) stress his stubbornness and also his pragmatism.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.b] Both leaders increased Irish independence, but by different methods and at different costs.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.0] Cosgrave's strengths: he secured the Free State's international recognition, used the Commonwealth to widen independence and avoided economic conflict with Britain.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.1] Cosgrave's limits: he left the oath, the Governor-General and the ports in place, and the Boundary Commission failed.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.2] De Valera's strengths: he removed the symbols of the Treaty, secured the ports and the annuities settlement and created a constitution that Britain accepted in practice.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.3] De Valera's limits: the Economic War, the failure to end partition and the fact that most of his changes relied on the groundwork laid by Cosgrave at the Imperial Conferences.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.4] Contemporary view: Fianna Fáil supporters said de Valera achieved real sovereignty; Fine Gael said he damaged the economy for the sake of symbols.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.5] Later view: the Republic of 1949 is often judged mainly a formal act, as de Valera's earlier changes had already made the state independent in practice.

CANDIDATES hist-ire3-s3: 3. Partition and Northern Ireland under the Unionist Party, 1912–1949
[hist-ire3-s3.0.b] Partition was the result of unionist resistance, British failure to find an all-Ireland settlement and the pattern of the 1918 vote.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.0] Unionist resistance: the Ulster Covenant of 1912 and the UVF made clear that Home Rule for all Ireland could be enforced only by force.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.1] Exclusion debates: county option for six years (March 1914), the failed Buckingham Palace Conference (July 1914) and the failed Lloyd George proposals of 1916 all assumed the exclusion of some Ulster counties.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.2] The 1916 plan collapsed because unionist ministers in the cabinet insisted that exclusion be permanent, while nationalists were told it would be temporary.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.3] The war and the Rising changed nationalist politics, and the Irish Convention of 1917–18 failed to produce agreement.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.4] The 1918 election confirmed the divide: Sinn Féin won most of the island, while unionists won 26 seats, mostly in the north-east.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.5] Only four of the nine Ulster counties had unionist majorities (Antrim, Down, Armagh and Londonderry), and Tyrone and Fermanagh had narrow nationalist majorities; the chosen six counties gave a safer unionist majority than nine.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.6] Northern Ireland had about 1.25 million people, of whom about two-thirds were Protestant and about one-third Catholic.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.7] British policy: Lloyd George's coalition, with a large Conservative element, was unwilling to coerce Ulster, and the Government of Ireland Act of 1920 gave the unionists the offer of their own parliament.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.b] The Act of 1920 created two Home Rule parliaments. Only the northern one worked, and the Treaty then allowed it to stay out of the Free State.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.0] The Government of Ireland Act (December 1920) set up a parliament for the six counties of Northern Ireland and one for the other 26 counties of Southern Ireland, with a Council of Ireland to link them.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.1] In the Northern election of 24 May 1921 the Unionists won 40 of 52 seats, and Sinn Féin and the Nationalists won six each but would not recognise the parliament.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.2] Craig became Prime Minister in June 1921, and George V opened the Northern parliament in Belfast on 22 June 1921.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.3] Southern Ireland's parliament never worked, since Sinn Féin won 124 of 128 seats and treated the election as one for the Dáil.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.4] Under the Treaty Northern Ireland could opt out of the Free State, and it did so on 7 December 1922, the day after the Free State came into being.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.5] The Treaty provided for a Boundary Commission, chaired by Richard Feetham, with Eoin MacNeill for the Free State and J.R. Fisher for Northern Ireland, since Craig refused to nominate.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.6] The commission's expected report was leaked to the Morning Post in November 1925; MacNeill resigned, and on 3 December 1925 the three governments agreed that the border would stay as it was.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.7] The Tripartite Agreement also released the Free State from its share of the imperial debt and ended the Council of Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.b] The founding of the state was accompanied by sectarian rioting and an IRA campaign, to which the government responded with exceptional powers.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.0] After the killing of an RIC officer in Cork in July 1920, thousands of Catholic and some Protestant labour supporters were expelled from the Belfast shipyards and engineering works.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.1] Riots in Derry in June 1920 and in Belfast through 1920–22 killed about 500 people, most of them Catholics, and drove many families from their homes.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.2] The Dáil organised a boycott of Belfast goods in 1920, which hurt Catholic workers and shopkeepers more than it hurt the unionist economy.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.3] The Ulster Special Constabulary, formed in late 1920, had full-time A Specials, part-time B Specials and reserve C Specials; it was recruited almost entirely from Protestants.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.4] The Royal Ulster Constabulary replaced the RIC in June 1922; on paper a third of its places were reserved for Catholics, but they never filled them.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.5] The Craig–Collins pacts of January and March 1922 failed, and the IRA offensive on the border in May 1922 led the government to intern republicans.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.6] The Civil Authorities (Special Powers) Act of April 1922 allowed internment, curfews, the banning of meetings and the arrest of suspects without warrant; it was renewed annually and made permanent in 1933.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.7] Several hundred men were interned in 1922, including some held on the prison ship Argenta.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.b] The Ulster Unionist Party governed without interruption and changed the electoral rules to keep it in power. Nationalists took little part in the system.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.0] Nationalists abstained from Stormont at first; the Nationalist Party under Joe Devlin took its seats in 1925, but Sinn Féin remained abstentionist.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.1] Tyrone and Fermanagh county councils and many other nationalist-controlled councils, which refused to recognise the Belfast government in 1920–22, were dissolved and replaced by commissioners.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.2] The Local Government Act of 1922 abolished proportional representation in local elections and redrew electoral areas.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.3] Proportional representation for Stormont elections was abolished in 1929 and single-member constituencies introduced, which helped the Unionists and weakened Labour and independents.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.4] Derry Corporation stayed under unionist control from 1923 until 1969 even though Catholics were a majority of the city's population; this is the best-known case of gerrymandering.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.5] The Unionist Party held every Stormont election from 1921 to 1969 by large margins, and a bloc vote on border issues kept Protestant workers within it.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.6] Stormont Parliament Buildings, on the outskirts of Belfast, were opened by the Prince of Wales in 1932, a symbol of the permanence of the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.b] Nationalists said that the state discriminated against Catholics. Unionists denied it, arguing that Catholics refused to take part in the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.0] Catholics were about a third of the population but held a small share of senior posts in the civil service, the judiciary, and local government.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.1] The shipyards and heavy industry employed few Catholics, and Protestant workers' networks and the Orange Order helped to secure employment.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.2] Local councils controlled by unionists allocated public housing, and nationalists argued that houses were allocated to Protestant families first.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.3] In the mid-1930s Basil Brooke urged unionists not to employ Catholics, a statement widely quoted as evidence of the attitudes of leading unionists.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.4] On 24 April 1934 Craig told the Stormont parliament that he boasted of a Protestant parliament and a Protestant state; he said this in reply to southern Catholic claims, and it is the best-known statement of the attitudes of the unionist government.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.5] Critics of the system, such as Michael Farrell in The Orange State (1976), see systematic discrimination, while others accept discrimination in some fields but stress that it was not centrally planned.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.6] Nationalist abstention in the early years and the Catholic Church's separate schools left the minority with a limited voice in the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.b] Northern Ireland entered the 1920s with high-value industries that depended on export markets, and the post-war slump and the Depression hit them hard.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.0] Shipbuilding at Harland and Wolff and linen were the two big employers, and both fell back after the post-war boom ended in 1920–21.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.1] Unemployment was around 20 per cent for much of the 1920s and reached around 25 per cent in the early 1930s, among the highest in the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.2] Agriculture remained important, with small farms exporting cattle and eggs to Britain.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.3] The government followed a policy of ‘step by step’ with Britain on welfare benefits, which kept pensions and unemployment pay in line with British levels, financed with an imperial contribution.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.4] Housing in the slums of Belfast was poor and overcrowded, and the government built little until the late 1930s.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.5] In October 1932 unemployed Catholic and Protestant workers joined in the Outdoor Relief strike and riots in Belfast against the harsh relief rules; police shot dead two people, and the Board of Guardians increased the relief.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.b] Partition broke up an economy that had been built on all-Ireland trade and weakened both parts.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.0] Customs controls on the border from 1923 interrupted the flow of goods and cattle, and the Free State's tariffs and Economic War of the 1930s made smuggling common.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.1] Belfast's linen, shipbuilding and engineering, which had sold to a UK and world market, lost the southern market they had counted on.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.2] The Free State lost the industrial north-east, which had provided the bulk of Irish manufactured exports, and was left with an economy dependent on agriculture.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.3] Dublin and Belfast, once part of a single banking and railway system, were run from different centres, and railways such as the Great Northern crossed the border.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.4] Society divided as well: Catholics in the North were cut off from the southern state's institutions, while Protestants in the South felt the pressure of the Catholic ethos.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.5] Politically the border became the main theme of Northern nationalism and southern foreign policy, as seen in Articles 2 and 3 of the 1937 constitution.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.b] Craig was a leader of Ulster resistance to Home Rule and the first Prime Minister of Northern Ireland. He is judged as the builder of a stable state and also as a leader of a sectarian one.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.0] Craig (1871–1940) was born in Belfast, the son of a wealthy whiskey distiller, and worked as a stockbroker; he served in the Boer War and became MP for East Down in 1906.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.1] He organised the UVF and the Larne gun-running of April 1914 alongside Carson.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.2] He was a junior minister in the British government from 1916 to 1921, ending as Parliamentary and Financial Secretary to the Admiralty.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.3] He succeeded Carson as Unionist leader in February 1921 and became Prime Minister of Northern Ireland in June 1921, serving until his death on 24 November 1940.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.4] He was made Viscount Craigavon in 1927.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.5] He negotiated with Collins in 1922 and with Lloyd George, but insisted that the border was settled; he refused to nominate to the Boundary Commission.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.6] His government created the RUC and the Special Powers Act, and abolished proportional representation, so critics see him as a sectarian figure.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.7] His defenders note that he held the state together against IRA violence and economic crisis and that his government copied British social policy.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.8] Contemporaries: unionists revered him as their founder, while nationalists considered him responsible for their exclusion.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.9] Historians: Patrick Buckland's biography (1980) stressed his cautious, conservative leadership and his failure to reach out to Catholics, while others criticise the lack of any attempt to win minority consent.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.b] Bates was the minister who ran the machinery of control for more than 20 years, and his name is associated with the security system and electoral changes.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.0] Richard Dawson Bates (1876–1949) was a Belfast solicitor who was secretary of the Ulster Unionist Council from 1906 to 1921 and helped to organise the UVF.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.1] He was Minister of Home Affairs from 1921 to 1943, responsible for the RUC, the Special Constabulary, the Special Powers Act and local government and elections.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.2] He introduced the Local Government Act of 1922 that abolished PR for local elections and the 1929 change for Stormont elections.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.3] He used his powers to ban republican parades and organisations, to arrest and intern suspects and to censor some publications.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.4] Contemporaries: nationalists described him as the architect of the system of control, and many unionists praised his efficiency.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.5] Historians: Buckland, in The Factory of Grievances (1979), describes how his department's decisions, even if cautious, left a legacy of grievances; Bates left office in 1943 in ill health.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.b] Judgements depend on whether the state is measured by survival and stability or by its treatment of the minority.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.0] Achievement: it survived the violence of 1920–22 and the Depression and secured the position of Northern Ireland within the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.1] Achievement: it maintained British standards in education, health and welfare through its step-by-step policy.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.2] Limit: it relied on emergency powers, a largely Protestant police and manipulated electoral rules, so the minority never accepted it.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.3] Limit: a one-party government with a permanent majority did not develop real opposition, and unemployment policy was timid.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.4] Contemporary view: unionists regarded it as a bulwark against a Catholic all-Ireland state, while nationalists saw a Protestant state.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.5] Later view: historians disagree on how far the system was planned discrimination, but accept that its legacy shaped the Troubles.

CANDIDATES hist-ire3-s10: 10. State and culture, North and South: language, religion, education and cultural identity, 1920–1949
[hist-ire3-s10.0.b] The Free State made the revival of Irish a central aim of cultural policy and used schools and the civil service to achieve it.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.0] Irish was made a compulsory subject in national schools in 1922, and many infant classes were taught wholly through Irish.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.1] A pass in Irish became necessary for State examinations at second level and for entry to the civil service and the Garda, so that language knowledge was tied to jobs.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.2] Ernest Blythe, Minister for Finance, was a strong advocate, and the civil service was required to learn Irish.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.3] The Gaeltacht Commission was set up in 1925 and reported in 1926, defining the Irish-speaking districts and recommending economic and educational support.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.4] An Gúm was founded in 1926 to publish books in Irish, including translations of foreign works, to supply school texts and readers.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.5] Taibhdhearc na Gaillimhe, an Irish-language theatre, opened in Galway in 1928.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.6] Preparatory colleges were set up to train teachers who could teach through Irish.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.7] Irish was recognised as the first official language by the 1937 constitution.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.b] The policy raised the number of people who could say they knew Irish, but few used it in daily life.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.0] Census returns showed a rise in the share of the population able to speak Irish, from about 18 per cent in 1926 to about a quarter in 1936, mostly as a result of schooling.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.1] The Gaeltacht, the real Irish-speaking districts, continued to shrink because of emigration and poverty.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.2] Many parents and teachers resented the compulsion, and some pupils learned little.
[hist-ire3-s10.1.3] Irish remained a symbol of national identity, and the state kept it as a school subject even where it failed as a spoken language.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.b] The Church ran most schools, and the state accepted its role.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.0] National schools were managed by clergy of the local denomination, and the state paid salaries and set the curriculum.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.1] The School Attendance Act of 1926 made school attendance compulsory for children from 6 to 14.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.2] Secondary schools were mostly fee-paying and run by religious orders, and few working-class children reached them.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.3] The Vocational Education Act of 1930 set up vocational committees to provide technical education.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.4] The Church influenced the content of teaching, and the 1937 constitution recognised the family and the Church's role.
[hist-ire3-s10.2.5] The Catholic Archbishop of Dublin, John Charles McQuaid, from 1940 was an influential voice on social and educational issues.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.b] Legislation reflected Catholic teaching on morality, and critics saw the result as an enforced conformity.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.0] The Censorship of Films Act of 1923 set up a film censor who could cut or ban films.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.1] In 1925 the Dáil changed its standing orders so that private bills for divorce could no longer be introduced; W.B. Yeats made a famous speech in the Seanad in defence of the Protestant minority.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.2] The Censorship of Publications Act of 1929 set up a board with the power to ban books and periodicals considered indecent or immoral.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.3] Among the writers banned were Samuel Beckett (More Pricks Than Kicks, 1934) and, later, Kate O'Brien and Seán Ó Faoláin.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.4] The Public Dance Halls Act of 1935 required licences, in response to clerical concerns about dances.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.5] The Criminal Law Amendment Act of 1935 banned the sale and import of contraceptives.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.6] Article 41 of the 1937 constitution banned divorce, and Article 44 recognised the special position of the Catholic Church.
[hist-ire3-s10.3.7] The Protestant population of the Free State fell in the 1920s and 1930s, partly through emigration, the Ne Temere rule on mixed marriages and Civil War violence.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.b] The state promoted a national culture through games, theatre and broadcasting, and the GAA enforced a cultural boundary.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.0] The Tailteann Games were held in Dublin in 1924, 1928 and 1932, a festival of sport and the arts modelled on the ancient games, and open to people of Irish descent abroad.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.1] The Abbey Theatre received a state subsidy from 1925, the first for a national theatre in the English-speaking world.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.2] The Gate Theatre was founded in 1928 by Hilton Edwards and Micheál Mac Liammóir to stage international plays.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.3] The state radio station 2RN began broadcasting from Dublin on 1 January 1926, and a high-power transmitter at Athlone from 1932 was followed by the name Radio Éireann in 1938.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.4] The Irish Folklore Commission was established in 1935 under Séamus Ó Duilearga, and the Schools' Collection of 1937–38 had children collect local folklore.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.5] The GAA's Rule 27, in the rulebook from 1905, banned members from playing foreign games such as soccer and rugby, and was later extended to attending them; it was repealed in 1971.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.6] In 1938 the GAA removed President Douglas Hyde as patron after he attended an international soccer match in Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s10.4.7] Culture was linked to nationalism by the idea of a Gaelic and Catholic Ireland, which excluded many Protestants and urban people.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.b] Education was contested between the Unionist government, which wanted a non-denominational state system, and the churches.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.0] The Lynn Committee was appointed in 1921 to review education under the first Minister of Education, Lord Londonderry, and reported in 1923.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.1] The Education Act (Northern Ireland) 1923, known as the Londonderry Act, created county education committees and placed schools under local education authorities, with public elementary schools to be free of church control.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.2] Protestant churches objected to the exclusion of religious instruction, and the Act was amended in 1925 to allow Bible instruction, and the Orange Order and the Protestant clergy gained a voice in management.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.3] The Catholic Church refused to transfer its schools to state control, and Catholic schools stayed outside the system with only partial public funding.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.4] The 1930 Amendment Act allowed transferred Protestant schools to have four representatives of the transferors and two of the local authority on their committees, which Catholics saw as favouring Protestant interests.
[hist-ire3-s10.5.5] The result was a divided school system, which remained a source of segregation and a grievance for Catholics.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.b] Unionist and nationalist communities expressed separate identities in a state that favoured the first.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.0] Orange Order parades on 12 July and loyalist bands were central to unionist culture and enjoyed official protection.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.1] The Protestant ethos of the state was symbolised by the flag, the Union Jack, and by the royal visits and ceremonies at Stormont.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.2] Nationalists supported the GAA, Irish-language classes and Catholic schools, but the Irish language received little state support.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.3] Under the Special Powers Act the authorities could ban processions and republican emblems, and nationalist parades were more likely to be banned.
[hist-ire3-s10.6.4] Craig's statement of 1934 that Northern Ireland was a Protestant state was matched in the South by the Catholic emphasis of the 1937 constitution.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.b] Hone was a leading modernist painter who turned to stained glass and became the best-known Irish stained-glass artist of her time.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.0] Evie Hone (1894–1955) was born in Dublin, and studied art in London and later in Paris under André Lhote and Albert Gleizes.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.1] With Mainie Jellett she was one of the first Irish painters to work in a cubist style.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.2] In the 1930s she turned to stained glass, learned from Wilhelmina Geddes, and worked with An Túr Gloine, the studio founded by Sarah Purser in 1903 to revive Irish stained glass.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.3] She converted to Catholicism in 1937, and much of her work was for churches.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.4] My Four Green Fields, a window symbolising the four provinces, was made for the Irish pavilion at the 1939 New York World's Fair and won first prize for stained glass.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.5] The Crucifixion east window of Eton College Chapel was commissioned in 1949 to replace glass destroyed by a bomb in 1940, and was installed in 1952.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.6] Contemporaries praised her work as a modern Irish art with international standing.
[hist-ire3-s10.7.7] Historians such as Nicola Gordon Bowe place her in the movement to give Ireland a modern cultural identity after independence.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.b] Cultural policy gave the new state a distinct identity, but it also narrowed the range of what was acceptable.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.0] Achievement: institutions such as An Gúm, the Folklore Commission, the Abbey and Radio Éireann preserved and promoted Irish culture.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.1] Limit: compulsory Irish did not make it the daily language of most people, and the Gaeltacht continued to decline.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.2] Achievement: arts and crafts, including the work of Hone, flourished with state and church patronage.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.3] Limit: censorship and moral legislation restricted writers and individual choice, and minorities felt excluded.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.4] In the North the divided education system and separate cultural traditions deepened the communal division.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.5] Contemporary view: supporters saw cultural policy as defending Irish identity; critics, including writers such as Ó Faoláin, saw a narrow and conformist vision.
[hist-ire3-s10.8.6] Later view: historians stress the tension between revival and restriction, and the influence of the Church.
