TARGET hist-ire2-s7: 7. Unionisation of the working classes and the Dublin strike and lockout, 1913
[hist-ire2-s7.0.b] Ireland's Industrial Revolution was confined mainly to Belfast and the north-east. Dublin, by contrast, was a port, administrative and commercial city with little large-scale industry, so its working class had little steady work and lived in some of the worst housing in the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.0] Ireland was part of the United Kingdom, and Dublin was run by Dublin Castle (the British administration), Dublin Corporation (the city council) and the Dublin Metropolitan Police (DMP). Workers had few ways to press for change except through their own organisations.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.1] Dublin's tenements were old Georgian town houses on the north side and in the Liberties, divided into single rooms. The 1911 census showed more than 20,000 families living one family to a room, which meant severe overcrowding.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.2] Rooms had no running water and the whole house shared a yard toilet. Landlords collected high rents for these rooms, and some landlords sat on Dublin Corporation, so the people who could improve conditions profited from them.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.3] Dublin had a very high death rate, among the highest of any city in the United Kingdom, with tuberculosis and infant mortality especially severe. Overcrowded and unsanitary housing helped spread disease.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.4] Most unskilled men, such as dockers and carters, were casual labourers at the docks, in carting and in building. They were hired by the day at the stand with no security and very low pay.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.5] Women worked in laundries, textile mills and biscuit factories and as domestic servants, again for low wages.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.6] On 2 September 1913 two tenement houses in Church Street collapsed, killing seven people. It happened in the middle of the lockout and exposed the state of Dublin housing to the public.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.7] A Local Government Board housing inquiry sat in 1913-14 to report on the conditions of tenement life.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.b] A trade union is a body of workers that bargains collectively with employers over pay and conditions; its weapon is the strike (workers withdrawing their labour). Irish unions began as craft bodies for skilled workers, and from the 1880s and 1890s a new kind of union reached the unskilled.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.0] Craft unions organised skilled trades such as printers, carpenters and engineers. Many were branches of British unions, and they had little interest in the unskilled.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.1] The Irish Trades Union Congress was founded in 1894 so that Irish unions would have a common voice, though most unions remained British-based.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.2] New unionism began in Britain in the 1880s; its best-known success was the London dockers' strike of 1889. It organised unskilled workers into general unions (open to any worker, not one trade) that were willing to strike.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.3] James Connolly founded the Irish Socialist Republican Party in Dublin in 1896, because he argued that socialism and national independence were linked.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.4] In 1907 James Larkin, then an organiser for the National Union of Dock Labourers, led a strike of Belfast dockers and carters. Catholic and Protestant workers united for a time, but riots in August 1907 left two men dead.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.5] Larkin broke with the British union, and so founded the Irish Transport and General Workers' Union (ITGWU) in Dublin in 1909, with its headquarters at Liberty Hall.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.6] The ITGWU won disputes in Dublin and Cork and fought a lockout (employers shutting workers out) in Wexford in 1911. Larkin also began the weekly Irish Worker in 1911 to speak for the union.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.7] The Irish Labour Party was founded in 1912, at the Irish TUC meeting in Clonmel, so that workers would have their own voice in parliament.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.b] Larkin was the charismatic organiser who built the ITGWU and led the Dublin workers in 1913. His career shows both the strength and the weakness of a leader who relied on inspiration and confrontation.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.0] James Larkin was born in Liverpool to Irish parents in the 1870s (he gave 1876; other sources say 1874) and worked as a docker before becoming a union organiser. He died in 1947.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.1] He was a participant in the events he shaped. He led the Belfast strike of 1907 as a National Union of Dock Labourers organiser, founded the ITGWU in 1909, began the Irish Worker in 1911 and was the main figure in Dublin in 1913.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.2] His tactics, known as Larkinism, included the sympathetic strike (workers striking in support of others) and the refusal of union members to handle tainted goods, meaning goods from a firm in dispute. This spread disputes widely and gave small groups great power.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.3] He was a powerful speaker. On 31 August 1913 he appeared in disguise on the balcony of the Imperial Hotel, Sackville Street, which Murphy owned, to address the banned meeting, and was then arrested.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.4] He was sentenced to seven months for sedition in October 1913 but was released on 13 November 1913 after a public campaign.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.5] He left Ireland for the United States in October 1914, which meant Connolly took over in Dublin. He was imprisoned there in 1920 and deported in 1923, and later returned to Irish politics.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.6] Contemporaries divided. Dublin workers revered him as a champion, while employers, Murphy's Irish Independent, much of the press and the Church condemned him as a dangerous agitator.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.7] Historians credit him with the first mass organisation of unskilled Irish workers. Some argue that his temper and intransigence (refusal to compromise) hardened the dispute in 1913 and made a settlement harder.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.b] Connolly was the thinker of the Irish labour movement, who argued that socialism and national freedom were inseparable. Where Larkin organised the masses, Connolly supplied the ideas and, in 1914, a workers' army.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.0] James Connolly was born in Edinburgh in 1868 to Irish parents, served in the British army and became a socialist organiser before moving to Dublin in 1896. He was executed on 12 May 1916 after the Easter Rising.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.1] Socialism means common ownership of the means of production and an end to the class system. Connolly's version tied it to Irish national independence.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.2] He founded the Irish Socialist Republican Party in 1896 and edited its paper, the Workers' Republic, to spread his ideas among workers.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.3] He lived in the USA from 1903 to 1910, working with the Industrial Workers of the World, and wrote Labour in Irish History (1910). His other writings include Labour, Nationality and Religion (1910) and The Re-Conquest of Ireland (1915).
[hist-ire2-s7.3.4] He returned in 1910, became Ulster organiser of the ITGWU and helped found the Labour Party in 1912.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.5] During the lockout he was Larkin's lieutenant. When Larkin was jailed and then left for the USA, Connolly led the union and the Irish Citizen Army, becoming its commander in 1914.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.6] For example, contemporaries on the left admired him, while the Church and employers distrusted his socialism. Many nationalists saw him as a distraction from the national cause until 1916.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.7] Historians judge him the leading Irish socialist thinker. They debate whether his turn to the Rising in 1916 showed that he put nation before class.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.b] William Martin Murphy led the employers' attempt to destroy the ITGWU before it could win recognition. His power and his principles explain why a local dispute became a city-wide lockout.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.0] William Martin Murphy (1844-1919) was Dublin's leading capitalist. He was chairman of the Dublin United Tramways Company (DUTC), owner of the Irish Independent and leader of the Dublin Employers' Federation.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.1] He argued that the ITGWU's sympathetic strikes and boycotts were tyranny over business and over other workers, and that employers had the right to manage without interference.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.2] In July and August 1913 Larkin's union won concessions from several Dublin firms, so Murphy decided to challenge it before it grew stronger, on the trams and in the Independent's despatch department.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.3] In August 1913 Murphy dismissed ITGWU members at the Independent and the DUTC; the tram strike began on 26 August.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.b] A tram strike on the first day of Horse Show week grew into the lockout of thousands of workers. Keep the dates apart: 26 August was the strike, 31 August the baton charge, 3 September the Document.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.0] On Tuesday 26 August 1913, the first day of Horse Show week, tram drivers and conductors left their cars, causing chaos in the city.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.1] Murphy replaced strikers with other workers and the police protected the trams. The ITGWU responded by calling out other workers who handled tram-related goods.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.2] On 29 August Larkin burned the police proclamation banning a meeting, and promised to speak in Sackville (now O'Connell) Street on Sunday 31 August 1913.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.3] On 31 August the DMP baton-charged the crowd in Sackville Street, injuring hundreds, in what became known as Bloody Sunday. Two men died of baton wounds in the disturbances of that weekend.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.4] On 3 September 1913 about 400 employers agreed to require every worker to sign the Document, a pledge to give up the ITGWU and not support it. Many locked out those who refused, so the strike became a lockout.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.5] About 20,000 workers were out of work by autumn (estimates range up to about 25,000), with many thousands more dependants affected.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.6] The Board of Trade's Askwith inquiry (September-October 1913), held under Sir George Askwith, condemned the Document, but the employers refused its proposals.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.7] George Russell (AE) published an open letter to Dublin's employers in the Irish Times on 7 October 1913, attacking them for starving workers into submission.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.b] Help from British unions kept the workers going, but the British TUC refused the sympathetic action Larkin wanted. Without it, hunger slowly decided the dispute.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.0] British trade unions sent food. The first food ship, the SS Hare, reached Dublin on 27 September 1913, and money and supplies kept coming from British unions.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.1] Food and relief were distributed from Liberty Hall, with soup kitchens and food parcels, so that strikers' families could survive without wages. Constance Markievicz worked in the soup kitchen.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.2] Larkin was sentenced to seven months for sedition in October 1913 and released on 13 November after a public campaign. Connolly took over the running of the union.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.3] The Save the Kiddies scheme of October 1913 was Dora Montefiore's plan to send strikers' children to homes in Britain. Archbishop William Walsh condemned it as a threat to the children's Catholic faith, and clergy and others stopped children leaving.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.4] On 23 November 1913 the Irish Citizen Army was founded at Liberty Hall to protect workers from the police, and was drilled by Captain Jack White.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.5] A special British TUC congress in December 1913 rejected sympathetic action and a boycott of Dublin goods, a heavy blow to the workers.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.6] Hunger drove men back, so the ITGWU advised a return in January 1914. The dispute ended in January-February 1914, gradually rather than on one day.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.7] Many men returned without signing the Document, but employers kept the right to bar ITGWU members in some firms and some workers were blacklisted or not re-employed, so the outcome favoured the employers.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.b] The union lost the lockout but survived, and the dispute left a lasting mark on Irish labour and politics.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.0] Many strikers returned without recognition of the union and the ITGWU lost members, but it survived and grew during the First World War, which shows that the defeat was not final.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.1] The Irish Citizen Army, born of the lockout, took part in the 1916 Rising under Connolly.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.2] The dispute made labour a political force in Dublin and began the alliance of labour and nationalism that Connolly favoured.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.3] It split nationalist opinion, for example over whom to support. Griffith's Sinn Fein and the Irish Party were largely unsympathetic to the strikers, while Connolly, Yeats, AE and Constance Markievicz supported them.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.4] The Church sympathised with the poor but condemned the tactics and the Save the Kiddies scheme, and warned against socialism.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.5] The housing inquiry and public sympathy increased pressure for slum clearance in Dublin, so poverty, the Church Street collapse and the lockout fed into one another.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.b] The 1913 case study is documents-based, so you must be able to place any source. Each side wrote to serve its own interest, and the sources include newspaper reports, a poem, an open letter and cartoons.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.0] Murphy's Irish Independent, a paper owned by the chief employer, attacked Larkin as a dangerous agitator and presented the strike as an attack on trade and liberty.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.1] The Irish Worker, Larkin's weekly, such as its attacks on Murphy, and Connolly's writings portrayed the employers as aggressors starving workers into submission.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.2] Archbishop Walsh criticised the housing conditions but, as a churchman, warned against socialist ideas and condemned the removal of children.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.3] Yeats's poem September 1913, published in the Irish Times on 8 September 1913, was written in the dispute over Hugh Lane's gallery, which Murphy's Independent opposed. It attacks the narrow, commercial Dublin middle class, with the line: "Romantic Ireland's dead and gone".
[hist-ire2-s7.8.4] AE's open letter to the employers (Irish Times, 7 October 1913) is a strong middle-class voice for the workers, written by the poet George Russell, not by a trade unionist.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.5] Cartoons in the Irish Worker and in Dublin papers show Murphy and the police as oppressors, or Larkin as a demagogue (a leader who stirs up the crowd), depending on the paper.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.6] To place a document, identify the stage (tram strike, Bloody Sunday, the Document, the food ships, the end), the author's interest and the audience.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.b] Judgements on 1913 compare the strength of organised labour with the cost of defeat. Use paired points so that each achievement is set against a limit.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.0] Achievement: the ITGWU organised thousands of unskilled workers, which showed that they could be organised, kept a mass movement alive for about five months and won widespread sympathy.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.1] Limit: it failed to defeat the employers, because it lacked British union support for sympathetic action and ended with hunger forcing a return.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.2] Larkin's strengths were inspiration and organising ability. His weaknesses were intransigence and the extension of the dispute into a general conflict.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.3] Murphy's strengths were unity and resources. His weaknesses were a hardness that cost him public sympathy and drew the attention of the British government.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.4] Contemporaries disagreed because their interests differed: employers called it a defence of liberty, workers a defence of the right to organise.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.5] Historians see 1913 as a defeat that strengthened the labour movement, and as a source of the Citizen Army and the radicalisation that fed 1916.
