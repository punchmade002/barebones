TARGET hist-ire2-s7: 7. Unionisation of the working classes and the Dublin strike and lockout, 1913
[hist-ire2-s7.0.b] Dublin had few factories and a large pool of unskilled labour, and its working class lived in some of the worst housing in the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.0] Dublin’s tenements were old Georgian townhouses on the north side and in the Liberties, divided into single rooms; more than 20,000 families lived in one room each (1911 census).
[hist-ire2-s7.0.1] Many houses were owned by landlords who collected high rents for rooms with no running water and a shared yard toilet, and some landlords sat on Dublin Corporation.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.2] Dublin had a very high death rate, among the highest of any city in the United Kingdom, with tuberculosis and infant mortality especially severe.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.3] Most unskilled men were casual labourers at the docks, in carting and building, hired by the day at the stand with no security and very low pay.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.4] Women worked in laundries, textile mills, biscuit factories and as domestic servants for low wages.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.5] On 2 September 1913 two tenement houses in Church Street collapsed, killing seven people and exposing the state of Dublin housing in the middle of the lockout.
[hist-ire2-s7.0.6] A Local Government Board housing inquiry in 1913–14 reported on the conditions of tenement life.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.b] Irish unions began as craft bodies for skilled workers; from the 1890s a new unionism reached unskilled workers.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.0] Craft unions, mainly branches of British unions, organised skilled tradesmen such as printers, carpenters and engineers, and had little interest in the unskilled.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.1] The Irish Trades Union Congress was founded in 1894 to give Irish unions a common voice, though most unions remained British-based.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.2] New unionism, which spread from Britain in the 1880s, organised unskilled workers into general unions, and used the strike, including the sympathetic strike.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.3] James Connolly founded the Irish Socialist Republican Party in Dublin in 1896, arguing that socialism and national independence were linked.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.4] In 1907 James Larkin, then an organiser for the National Union of Dock Labourers, led a strike of Belfast dockers and carters that united Catholic and Protestant workers for a time; riots in August left two dead.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.5] Larkin broke with the British union and founded the Irish Transport and General Workers’ Union in Dublin in 1909, with Liberty Hall as its headquarters.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.6] The ITGWU won disputes in Dublin and Cork and fought a lockout in Wexford in 1911; Larkin founded the weekly Irish Worker in 1911.
[hist-ire2-s7.1.7] The Irish Labour Party was founded in 1912, at the Irish TUC meeting in Clonmel, to represent workers in parliament.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.b] Larkin was the charismatic organiser who built the ITGWU and led the Dublin workers in 1913.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.0] James Larkin was born in Liverpool in the 1870s (he gave 1876; some sources say 1874) to Irish parents, and worked as a docker before becoming a union organiser; he died in 1947.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.1] He was a participant: he led the Belfast strike of 1907, founded the ITGWU in 1909 and was the main figure in the Dublin events of 1913.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.2] His tactics, known as Larkinism, included the sympathetic strike and the refusal of union members to handle goods from firms in dispute (tainted goods).
[hist-ire2-s7.2.3] He was a powerful speaker; he appeared in disguise on the balcony of the Imperial Hotel, Murphy’s property, on 31 August 1913, to address the banned meeting.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.4] He was sentenced to seven months for sedition in October 1913 but released on 13 November after a public campaign.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.5] He left Ireland for the United States in October 1914 and was imprisoned there in 1920 and deported in 1923; he later returned to Irish politics and died in 1947.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.6] Contemporary employers, the conservative press and Archbishop Walsh’s supporters condemned him as an agitator, while Dublin workers revered him as a champion.
[hist-ire2-s7.2.7] Historians credit him with the first mass organisation of unskilled Irish workers, though some argue that his temper and tactics made compromise harder in 1913.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.b] Connolly was the thinker of the Irish labour movement, who argued that socialism and national freedom were inseparable.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.0] James Connolly (1868–1916) was born in Edinburgh to Irish parents, served in the British army, and became a socialist organiser in Edinburgh before moving to Dublin in 1896.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.1] He founded the Irish Socialist Republican Party in 1896 and edited its paper, the Workers’ Republic.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.2] He lived in the USA from 1903 to 1910, organising for socialist bodies and the Industrial Workers of the World, and wrote Labour in Irish History (1910).
[hist-ire2-s7.3.3] He returned in 1910, became Ulster organiser of the ITGWU and helped found the Labour Party in 1912.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.4] During the lockout he was Larkin’s lieutenant; when Larkin was jailed and then left for the USA, Connolly led the union and the Irish Citizen Army, becoming its commander in 1914.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.5] Contemporaries on the left admired him, while the Church and employers distrusted his socialism; many nationalists saw him as a distraction from the national cause until 1916.
[hist-ire2-s7.3.6] Historians judge him the leading Irish socialist thinker, with debate over whether his turn to the Rising in 1916 showed that he put nation before class.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.b] William Martin Murphy led the employers’ attempt to destroy the ITGWU before it could win recognition.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.0] William Martin Murphy (1844–1919) was Dublin’s leading capitalist: chairman of the Dublin United Tramways Company, owner of the Irish Independent, and an Irish Party supporter turned critic.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.1] He was president of the Dublin Chamber of Commerce and the leading figure of the Dublin Employers’ Federation, which had hundreds of members.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.2] Murphy argued that the ITGWU’s sympathetic strikes and boycotts were tyranny over business and workers, and that employers had the right to manage without interference.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.3] In July and August 1913 Larkin’s union had won gains from several Dublin firms, and Murphy decided to challenge it on the trams and in the Independent’s despatch department.
[hist-ire2-s7.4.4] In mid-August 1913 Murphy dismissed workers at the Independent and DUTC who were members of the ITGWU.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.b] A tram strike on the first day of Horse Show week grew into the lockout of thousands of workers.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.0] On Tuesday 26 August 1913, the first day of Horse Show week, tram drivers and conductors walked off their cars, causing chaos in the city.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.1] Murphy replaced strikers with other workers and the police protected them; the ITGWU responded by calling out other workers who handled tram-related goods.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.2] Larkin burned a police proclamation banning a meeting and promised to speak in Sackville Street (now O’Connell Street) on Sunday 31 August 1913.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.3] On 30 August baton charges in Ringsend and the city killed two men; on 31 August police baton-charged the crowd in O’Connell Street, injuring hundreds in what was called Bloody Sunday.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.4] On 3 September about 400 employers agreed that all workers must sign the Document, a pledge to leave the ITGWU and not support it, and many locked out those who refused.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.5] About 20,000 workers were out of work by autumn (estimates range up to 25,000), which meant many thousands more dependants were affected.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.6] The Board of Trade’s Askwith inquiry (late September–October 1913) condemned the Document, but the employers refused its proposals.
[hist-ire2-s7.5.7] George Russell (AE) published an Open Letter to the Masters of Dublin in the Irish Times on 7 October 1913, attacking the employers for starving workers into submission.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.b] Help from British unions kept the workers going, but the British TUC refused to take the sympathetic action Larkin wanted.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.0] British trade unions sent food: the first food ship, the Hare, arrived in Dublin on 27 September 1913, and money and supplies continued to arrive from British unions.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.1] Strike pay and relief were distributed from Liberty Hall, with soup kitchens and food parcels.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.2] The Save the Kiddies scheme, promoted by Dora Montefiore and others in October 1913, planned to send strikers’ children to homes in Britain; Archbishop Walsh condemned it as a threat to the children’s faith, and priests and the Ancient Order of Hibernians prevented children leaving.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.3] On 23 November 1913 the Irish Citizen Army was founded at Liberty Hall to protect pickets from police, drilled by Captain Jack White.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.4] A special British TUC congress in December 1913 rejected calls for a sympathetic strike or a boycott of Dublin goods, a heavy blow to the ITGWU.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.5] By January 1914 hunger was driving workers back, and the ITGWU advised them to return; the dispute formally ended around 18 January 1914, many men returning without signing the Document.
[hist-ire2-s7.6.6] Employers kept the right to bar ITGWU members in some firms and some workers were blacklisted, so the outcome favoured the employers.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.b] The union lost the lockout but survived, and the dispute left a lasting mark on Irish labour and politics.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.0] Many strikers returned without recognition of the union, and the ITGWU lost members, but it survived and grew during the First World War.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.1] The Irish Citizen Army, born of the lockout, took part in the Easter Rising of 1916 under Connolly.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.2] The dispute made labour a political force in Dublin and began the alliance of labour and nationalism that Connolly favoured.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.3] It split nationalist opinion: Griffith’s Sinn Féin and the Irish Party were largely unsympathetic to the strikers, while Connolly, Pearse’s circle, Yeats, AE and Constance Markievicz expressed support.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.4] The Church sympathised with the poor but condemned the tactics and the Save the Kiddies scheme, and opposed socialism.
[hist-ire2-s7.7.5] The housing inquiry and public sympathy increased pressure for slum clearance in Dublin.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.b] Documents on 1913 include employer statements, newspaper reports, Church statements, poems and cartoons.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.0] Murphy’s Irish Independent portrayed Larkin as a dangerous agitator and the strike as an attack on trade and liberty.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.1] The Irish Worker and Connolly’s writings portrayed the employers as aggressors starving workers into submission.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.2] Archbishop Walsh criticised the housing conditions but warned against socialist ideas and condemned the removal of children.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.3] Yeats’s poem September 1913 (published 8 September) was written in the dispute over Hugh Lane’s gallery, in which Murphy’s Independent opposed funding, and it attacks the narrow, commercial Dublin middle class.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.4] AE’s Open Letter to the employers is a strong middle-class voice for the workers, written by a poet, not a trade unionist.
[hist-ire2-s7.8.5] Cartoons in the Irish Worker and in Dublin papers show Murphy and the police as oppressors, or Larkin as a demagogue, depending on the paper.

[hist-ire2-s7.9.b] Judgements on 1913 compare the strength of organised labour with the cost of defeat.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.0] Achievement: the ITGWU organised thousands of unskilled workers, kept a mass movement alive for five months and won widespread sympathy.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.1] Limit: it failed to defeat the employers, lacked British union support for sympathetic action and ended with hunger forcing a return.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.2] Larkin’s strengths were inspiration and organising ability; his weaknesses were intransigence and the extension of the dispute into a general conflict.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.3] Murphy’s strengths were unity and resources; his weaknesses were hardness that cost him public sympathy and drew the attention of the British government.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.4] Contemporaries disagreed: employers called it a defence of liberty, workers a defence of the right to organise.
[hist-ire2-s7.9.5] Historians see 1913 as a defeat that strengthened the labour movement, and as a source of the Citizen Army and the radicalisation that fed 1916.
