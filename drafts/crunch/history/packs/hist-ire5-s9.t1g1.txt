[hist-ire5-s4.1.1] He presided over the Battle of the Bogside and the disbanding of the B Specials, and was pressed by London to carry out the reforms of the Hunt Report.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.2] His government announced a points system for housing allocation, the creation of a Housing Executive (established in 1971) and reform of local government.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.3] Unionist hardliners saw these steps as surrender, while nationalists said they came too late.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.4] He resigned on 20 March 1971 after the British government refused to send more troops and reinforce the security policy he wanted.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.5] Brian Faulkner succeeded him as Prime Minister on 23 March 1971.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.b] The split in the IRA at the end of 1969 created the Provisional IRA, which argued that the Catholic community needed armed defence.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.0] The IRA split in December 1969 into the Provisional IRA, led by figures such as Seán Mac Stíofáin, and the Official IRA, led by Cathal Goulding.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.1] The Provisionals argued that the IRA must defend Catholic areas and that British forces should be driven out, a position that gained support after August 1969.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.2] Provisional Sinn Féin was set up in January 1970 to match the new military wing.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.3] The Falls Road curfew of 3 to 5 July 1970 saw troops search houses for weapons, and four civilians were killed during the operation.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.4] The Social Democratic and Labour Party was founded on 21 August 1970 by nationalist politicians including Gerry Fitt, John Hume, Austin Currie and Paddy Devlin, with Fitt as leader.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.5] The Alliance Party was founded in April 1970 as a cross-community party, and it later chose Oliver Napier as leader.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.6] The Official IRA, which stressed a left-wing political strategy, declared a ceasefire in May 1972, and the Provisionals became the main republican force.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.7] The SDLP withdrew from Stormont in July 1971 after the army shot dead two men in Derry, Séamus Cusack and Desmond Beattie.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.b] Loyalist paramilitary groups grew in response to the republican campaign, and they attacked Catholic civilians and republicans.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.0] The Ulster Volunteer Force was revived in 1966, and in the early years of the Troubles it carried out attacks on Catholic targets.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.1] The Ulster Volunteer Force was revived in 1966 and murdered three people that year, and in the early years of the Troubles it carried out attacks on Catholic targets.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.2] The Ulster Defence Association, a loose umbrella of loyalist groups, was formed in 1971 and grew rapidly in working-class Protestant areas; it was a legal organisation until 1992.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.3] The bombing of McGurk's Bar in Belfast on 4 December 1971 killed 15 Catholic civilians; the police first blamed the IRA, but the Police Ombudsman concluded in 2011 that the Ulster Volunteer Force was responsible.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.4] Loyalist groups claimed that their violence defended the Union and Protestant areas, but most of their victims were Catholic civilians.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.5] William Craig founded the Ulster Vanguard movement in 1972, which held large rallies against the loss of Stormont's powers.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.6] Loyalist paramilitaries killed hundreds of people between 1969 and 1993, the great majority of them civilians, mostly Catholics.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.b] Faulkner was the Prime Minister who introduced internment and then saw the government collapse under the pressure of violence and British policy.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.0] Brian Faulkner, born in 1921, had been Minister of Home Affairs in 1959–63 and Minister of Commerce under O'Neill until he resigned in 1969; he became Prime Minister in March 1971.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.1] He succeeded James Chichester-Clark on 23 March 1971, winning a party vote against William Craig.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.2] He asked London to authorise internment, which began on 9 August 1971, and he defended it as necessary to stop the IRA.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.3] He participated in the talks with Westminster on the control of security, and he insisted that Stormont should keep its powers over policing and order.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.4] Contemporary unionists praised his firmness on security, while nationalists saw internment as a deliberate attack on the Catholic community.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.5] He resigned on 24 March 1972 when Westminster took over security, and historians generally see him as a pragmatic, able politician whose attempts at reform were swamped by violence he could not contain.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.6] He offered the opposition committee chairmanships at Stormont in 1971, and the SDLP rejected the offer and left Stormont after the July 1971 shootings in Derry.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.b] Internment was introduced on 9 August 1971 and was applied mainly to republicans, and the violence that followed killed civilians.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.0] Internment, the detention of suspects without trial, began on 9 August 1971 in Operation Demetrius, which arrested about 340 people in its first sweep.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.1] Those arrested were almost entirely Catholic, and many had no paramilitary links; no loyalist was interned until 1973.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.2] Interrogation methods known as the five techniques, which included hooding and sleep deprivation, were used on fourteen internees, known as the Hooded Men.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.3] The Compton and Parker inquiries examined the methods, and in 1978 the European Court of Human Rights found that the techniques were inhuman and degrading, but not torture.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.4] At Ballymurphy in west Belfast, eleven civilians died during army operations of 9 to 11 August 1971, including a priest, Father Hugh Mullan; a coroner's inquest in 2021 found that those killed were innocent.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.5] Nationalists saw internment as proof of bias, and it increased recruitment to the Provisional IRA; 1971 was the first year in which Troubles deaths passed 100.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.b] A civil rights march in Derry against internment ended with British paratroopers killing thirteen civilians, an event that deepened the conflict.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.0] The NICRA march of 30 January 1972 in Derry protested against internment and was banned by the authorities.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.1] Soldiers of the 1st Battalion, Parachute Regiment, entered the Bogside, and they fired on marchers and bystanders, killing thirteen people on the day.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.2] A fourteenth man who was wounded died some months later, and most of the dead were shot while unarmed or while fleeing.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.3] The Widgery Report of 19 April 1972, by the Lord Chief Justice, largely accepted the army's account, and nationalists widely rejected it.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.4] The Saville Inquiry, reporting in June 2010, found that the soldiers' firing was unjustified and unjustifiable and that none of the casualties had posed a threat; the Prime Minister, David Cameron, apologised.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.5] The killings led to the burning of the British embassy in Dublin on 2 February 1972, and they increased recruitment to the Provisional IRA.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.b] Stormont was suspended and government was taken over by Westminster after the British government refused to hand security powers to Stormont.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.0] Faulkner demanded control of security from Westminster, but the British government under Edward Heath refused.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.1] Faulkner and his ministers resigned on 24 March 1972 in protest, and Westminster suspended the Stormont parliament from 30 March.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.2] The Northern Ireland (Temporary Provisions) Act 1972 introduced Direct Rule from 30 March 1972, placing the province under a Secretary of State.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.3] William Whitelaw was the first Secretary of State for Northern Ireland and governed with civil servants, two ministers of state and the army.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.4] Direct Rule was meant to be temporary, but apart from the brief power-sharing executive of 1974 it lasted throughout the period to 1993, because the parties could not agree a power-sharing settlement acceptable to both communities.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.5] The Stormont parliament was formally abolished in 1973 by the Northern Ireland Constitution Act.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.6] Whitelaw granted special category status to paramilitary prisoners in June 1972, and he met leaders of the Provisional IRA in London on 7 July 1972 during a short ceasefire, talks which failed.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.7] Direct Rule lasted because unionists would not accept an assembly with nationalist participation, nationalists would not accept a return to majority rule, and London wished to avoid a return to the old system.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.b] The IRA bombings of 21 July 1972 killed nine people in Belfast, and the army's Operation Motorman broke the IRA's no-go areas.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.0] On 21 July 1972, the Provisional IRA set off about twenty bombs in Belfast in the space of about an hour and a half, a day that became known as Bloody Friday.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.1] Nine people died in the bombings, and about 130 were injured, and the attacks shocked public opinion in Britain and Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.2] Operation Motorman, launched on 31 July 1972 with more than 12,000 soldiers, moved into the no-go areas in Belfast and Derry, including the Creggan and the Bogside.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.3] The operation dismantled the barricades and restored government control over areas that the IRA had controlled.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.4] The IRA's campaign continued after Motorman, and 1972 was the deadliest year of the Troubles, with nearly 500 deaths.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.5] By the end of 1972 about 700 people had died in the conflict since 1969.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.0] Discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting gave the civil rights movement a sustained base of grievance.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.1] The state's security forces, the B Specials and the RUC, were seen by Catholics as partisan, which turned protest into a security issue.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.2] The Provisional IRA gave the Catholic community a military organisation, and its campaign drew the British army into the conflict.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.3] Loyalist paramilitaries defended Protestant areas and attacked Catholic civilians, which increased the cycle of reprisal.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.4] Internment and Bloody Sunday hardened attitudes on both sides and made political compromise harder.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.5] Stormont's inability to share power with nationalists meant there was no political institution capable of containing the violence.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.6] The decline of Stormont had many causes: civil rights protest, the failure to reform fast enough, the IRA campaign, and the British government's decision to take over security.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.0] Achievement: the end of Stormont removed an institution that nationalists had rejected since 1921, and it opened the way for new political settlements.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.1] Failure: the Stormont government could not contain the violence, and its reliance on internment alienated the Catholic community.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.2] Contemporary view: unionists saw the suspension of Stormont as a betrayal by Westminster, while nationalists welcomed it as the end of an unfair system.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.3] Later view: historians argue that the 1972 settlement was forced by violence, and that the Unionist government's decline was a product of its own policies as well as republican action.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.4] Contemporary view: the British government saw Direct Rule as a temporary measure, but it proved to be a long-term arrangement.
