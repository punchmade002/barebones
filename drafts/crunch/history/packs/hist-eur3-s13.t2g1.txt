CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s13: 13. Church-state relations under Mussolini and Hitler; Anglo-American popular culture: radio and cinema
[hist-eur3-s13.0.b] Church-state relations in Italy began with a quarrel that was almost sixty years old. Mussolini inherited it and chose to settle it rather than fight the Church.
[hist-eur3-s13.0.0] In 1870 Italian troops took Rome and ended the Papal States (the lands the popes had ruled as kings). The popes refused to recognise the Kingdom of Italy and called themselves "prisoners in the Vatican". This dispute is known as the Roman Question.
[hist-eur3-s13.0.1] For decades Catholics were told not to take part in national politics, so the quarrel kept many devout Italians at a distance from the state that ruled them.
[hist-eur3-s13.0.2] Mussolini became Prime Minister in October 1922 and by 1926 ruled a one-party state, a dictatorship in which only the Fascist Party was allowed, so the Church had to deal with one ruler rather than many parties.
[hist-eur3-s13.0.3] Fascism began anticlerical (hostile to the Church), but Mussolini dropped this stance because he needed the support of Catholics and conservatives to hold power.
[hist-eur3-s13.0.4] The Vatican let the Catholic Popular Party (the Partito Popolare, or Popolari) go, so the Church no longer had a party of its own in politics. Its leader, the priest Luigi Sturzo, resigned in 1923 and went into exile in 1924.
[hist-eur3-s13.1.b] By the late 1920s each side had something the other could give. Neither was acting out of friendship: both were pursuing their own interests.
[hist-eur3-s13.1.0] Mussolini wanted the support of a Catholic nation, because the Church's approval would give his regime legitimacy (acceptance as the rightful government) at home and prestige abroad.
[hist-eur3-s13.1.1] Pope Pius XI (pope from February 1922 until his death in February 1939) wanted the Roman Question settled, so that the Church's position in Italy rested on a firm legal footing.
[hist-eur3-s13.1.2] He also wanted Catholic life protected, including Church schools, Catholic marriage and Catholic Action (the Church's organisation for lay people, meaning ordinary Catholics who are not clergy), and he saw Mussolini as an ally against communism.
[hist-eur3-s13.2.b] The Lateran Treaties were the great settlement between the Italian state and the papacy. They were three agreements in one, each dealing with a different part of the quarrel.
[hist-eur3-s13.2.0] They were signed at the Lateran Palace in Rome on 11 February 1929 by Mussolini and Cardinal Pietro Gasparri, the Vatican Secretary of State (the pope's chief minister), and were ratified (formally approved) in June 1929.
[hist-eur3-s13.2.1] The three documents were a treaty (creating Vatican City), a financial convention (dealing with compensation) and a concordat (an agreement between the pope and a state about the Church's place in that country).
[hist-eur3-s13.2.2] The treaty made Vatican City an independent sovereign state of about 44 hectares, so the pope ruled his own territory. In return the papacy recognised the Kingdom of Italy with Rome as its capital.
[hist-eur3-s13.2.3] The financial convention gave the Church compensation of 1.75 billion lire (750 million in cash and 1 billion in state bonds) for the Papal States lost in 1870.
[hist-eur3-s13.2.4] Under the concordat, Catholicism became the sole religion of the state, and religious teaching was provided in state primary and secondary schools, which gave the Church a say in how the young were taught.
[hist-eur3-s13.2.5] Church marriage became valid in civil law, so couples married in church were legally married. Bishops had to swear loyalty to the state.
[hist-eur3-s13.2.6] Catholic Action was allowed to continue, but only if it stayed out of politics. This condition shows that the Church's gains came with strings attached.
[hist-eur3-s13.3.b] The settlement paid off for Mussolini almost at once, and it proved more lasting than his regime.
[hist-eur3-s13.3.0] In the plebiscite election of March 1929 voters were offered a single list of Fascist candidates and approved it by an overwhelming majority, with Catholic organisations urging a yes vote. This showed what Church backing was worth to him.
[hist-eur3-s13.3.1] Mussolini gained the support of the Catholic majority, and the Church's endorsement helped legitimise the regime in the eyes of many Italians.
[hist-eur3-s13.3.2] He also won prestige abroad. Pius XI publicly praised him, describing him as a man sent by Providence (God's guiding care).
[hist-eur3-s13.3.3] The settlement is often called Mussolini's most lasting achievement. It outlived Fascism, and the concordat was revised only in 1984, when Catholicism ceased to be the state religion of Italy.
[hist-eur3-s13.4.b] The partnership soon met its first limit. A dictatorship wants to shape the young, and the Church had its own organisations for doing exactly that.
[hist-eur3-s13.4.0] Catholic Action's youth and student groups worried the regime, because the Fascists saw them as rivals to the Fascist youth movement.
[hist-eur3-s13.4.1] Fascist squads attacked Catholic centres, and the regime dissolved the Catholic youth organisations in May 1931, because it would not tolerate a rival for the loyalty of the young.
[hist-eur3-s13.4.2] Pius XI replied with the encyclical (a formal letter from the pope to the whole Church) Non abbiamo bisogno (We have no need), issued in Italian on 29 June 1931. It condemned the regime's claim to monopolise the young.
[hist-eur3-s13.4.3] The dispute ended in a compromise agreement in September 1931. Catholic Action survived under the bishops, but it was confined to religious, cultural and charitable activities, and its youth groups were kept out of politics and sport.
[hist-eur3-s13.4.4] Apart from this encyclical, the Church's objections were mostly made in private diplomacy and in the Vatican's press, and Pius XI rarely challenged Mussolini openly.
[hist-eur3-s13.5.b] Italy's alliance with Nazi Germany pulled the regime towards racial policy. This tested the Church's relationship with the regime a second time.
[hist-eur3-s13.5.0] Following the Axis (the alliance between Italy and Germany), Mussolini's regime published the Manifesto of the Race on 14 July 1938, which defined the racial basis of Italian identity.
[hist-eur3-s13.5.1] Racial laws followed between September and November 1938, in line with Germany's policy. They excluded Jews from state schools, the professions and the armed forces, and banned marriage between Italians and Jews.
[hist-eur3-s13.5.2] Pius XI objected above all to the marriage ban, which also covered Jews who had become Catholics, because he saw it as a breach of the marriage terms of the concordat.
[hist-eur3-s13.5.3] In September 1938 he told Belgian pilgrims, "Spiritually, we are Semites." The remark stressed the link between Christians and Jews, but it was a spoken comment, not a break with the regime.
[hist-eur3-s13.5.4] The Church protested but did not break with the regime.
[hist-eur3-s13.5.5] Pius XI died in February 1939. His successor, Pius XII (Eugenio Pacelli, elected in March 1939), was more cautious.
[hist-eur3-s13.6.b] Hitler faced a different religious map from Mussolini's. Germany was split between two large churches, and both had reasons to be wary of a Nazi government.
[hist-eur3-s13.6.0] Germany was about two-thirds Protestant and one-third Catholic. The Protestants were divided into 28 regional (Land) churches, so there was no single Protestant Church for Hitler to deal with.
[hist-eur3-s13.6.1] Catholics remembered the Kulturkampf, Bismarck's struggle with the Catholic Church in the 1870s, and so they knew what it was to have the state turn against them.
[hist-eur3-s13.6.2] Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933. The Enabling Act (23 March 1933, which let him make laws without parliament) passed with the votes of the Catholic Centre Party, and Gleichschaltung began, the bringing of every organisation into line with Nazi control.
[hist-eur3-s13.6.3] The Nazi Party programme of 1920 promised "positive Christianity". Hitler courted the churches in 1933 while planning to subordinate them (bring them under Nazi control).
[hist-eur3-s13.6.4] Leading Nazis such as Alfred Rosenberg, Martin Bormann and Himmler were hostile to Christianity. Rosenberg's The Myth of the Twentieth Century (1930) was placed on the Church's Index of forbidden books in 1934.
[hist-eur3-s13.6.5] The German bishops lifted their ban on Catholics joining the Nazi Party on 28 March 1933, which removed one barrier between Catholics and the new regime.
[hist-eur3-s13.7.b] Within months of taking power Hitler offered the Catholic Church the kind of agreement Mussolini had made. For him it was a way to win respectability while ending Catholic politics.
[hist-eur3-s13.7.0] The Reich Concordat was signed in Rome on 20 July 1933 by Cardinal Eugenio Pacelli (the Vatican Secretary of State, later Pius XII) and Vice-Chancellor Franz von Papen.
[hist-eur3-s13.7.1] The Church kept freedom of worship, its schools, religious teaching and its religious organisations. In return clergy were barred from party politics and bishops swore loyalty to the state.
[hist-eur3-s13.7.2] The Centre Party, the main Catholic party, dissolved itself on 5 July 1933, as the Concordat was being concluded, so Catholic organisations in Germany lost much of their political role.
[hist-eur3-s13.7.3] Hitler's gains were international respectability, because a treaty with the Vatican made his regime look acceptable abroad, and the end of political Catholicism.
[hist-eur3-s13.8.b] The Concordat did not protect the Church for long. The regime wanted total loyalty, so it squeezed Catholic organisations and then persecuted the clergy.
[hist-eur3-s13.8.0] On 30 June 1934, in the Night of the Long Knives, Erich Klausener, head of Catholic Action in Berlin, was murdered, a warning that Catholic leaders were not safe.
[hist-eur3-s13.8.1] Catholic youth groups were squeezed and then banned as the Hitler Youth became compulsory (the Hitler Youth law was passed in December 1936), because the regime wanted to claim the young for itself.
[hist-eur3-s13.8.2] By the end of the 1930s Catholic schools had been replaced by state community schools, and the Catholic press was curbed.
[hist-eur3-s13.8.3] In 1936-37 the regime staged morality trials of priests and members of religious orders, show trials meant to blacken the clergy's reputation.
[hist-eur3-s13.8.4] Clergy were sent to camps, such as Dachau, where there was a priests' block.
[hist-eur3-s13.8.5] Popular protest could win locally: the order removing crucifixes from schools in Oldenburg was withdrawn after protests in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s13.8.6] In short, the Nazi regime violated the Concordat within a few years, closing Catholic schools and youth organisations and arresting priests and members of religious orders.
[hist-eur3-s13.9.b] The Reichskirche (Reich Church) was Hitler's plan for the Protestants. With 28 regional churches, one Church under Nazi control looked like an easy way to bring all Protestants into line.
[hist-eur3-s13.9.0] The Reichskirche was to be a single, centralised Protestant Church loyal to the Nazi state. It would replace the 28 regional churches and end their independence.
[hist-eur3-s13.9.1] Its backers were the German Christians (Deutsche Christen), a pro-Nazi movement within Protestantism founded in 1932, who wanted a Christianity fitted to Nazi ideas.
[hist-eur3-s13.9.2] Two of their demands were the Aryan paragraph, which barred pastors of Jewish descent so that Jewish Christians were excluded from church office, and talk of dropping the Old Testament.
[hist-eur3-s13.9.3] The German Christians won about two-thirds of the votes in the Protestant church elections of July 1933, which gave them control of the church machinery.
[hist-eur3-s13.9.4] Ludwig Müller, Hitler's adviser on church affairs, became Reich Bishop in September 1933, but his leadership was challenged within the churches.
[hist-eur3-s13.10.b] The Reichskirche failed. Its own extremism drove moderate Protestants away, and the regime could not bring the churches to heel even when it tried direct control.
[hist-eur3-s13.10.0] A mass German Christian rally in Berlin's Sportpalast in November 1933, demanding that the Old Testament be dropped, drove moderates away.
[hist-eur3-s13.10.1] Müller never controlled the churches and was sidelined.
[hist-eur3-s13.10.2] In 1935 the regime set up a Ministry for Church Affairs under Hanns Kerrl to impose control directly, with little more success.
[hist-eur3-s13.10.3] The Protestant churches split three ways: the German Christians, the Confessing Church, and a cautious neutral majority.
[hist-eur3-s13.11.b] Protestants who refused the Reich Church formed their own opposition. It was about defending the Church's independence, not about overthrowing Hitler.
[hist-eur3-s13.11.0] The Confessing Church grew from Martin Niemöller's Pastors' Emergency League, formed in September 1933 with several thousand pastors.
[hist-eur3-s13.11.1] The Barmen Declaration of 31 May 1934, mainly drafted by the Swiss theologian Karl Barth, said that the Church answered to Christ, not to the state or a Führer (the Nazi leader).
[hist-eur3-s13.11.2] It defended the Church's independence and its teaching, and so rejected the state's claim to control what the Church taught.
[hist-eur3-s13.11.3] It did not set out to overthrow the regime, and few of its members spoke up for Jews who were not Christians.
[hist-eur3-s13.12.b] Two men came to stand for Protestant resistance. Their fates were very different, and examiners expect you to keep them apart.
[hist-eur3-s13.12.0] Martin Niemöller was a U-boat commander in the First World War and then a pastor at Dahlem in Berlin. He at first welcomed the Nazis, but he founded the Pastors' Emergency League and led the Confessing Church.
[hist-eur3-s13.12.1] He was arrested on 1 July 1937 and tried in 1938. He was held in Sachsenhausen and then Dachau concentration camps as Hitler's personal prisoner until 1945, so he survived the war.
[hist-eur3-s13.12.2] Dietrich Bonhoeffer, born in 1906, was a Lutheran theologian of the Confessing Church. From 1935 he ran its secret seminary at Finkenwalde, which the Gestapo (the Nazi secret state police) closed in 1937.
[hist-eur3-s13.12.3] He moved from church opposition to active resistance through contacts in military intelligence (the Abwehr) and the plotters against Hitler, and he was arrested in April 1943.
[hist-eur3-s13.12.4] Bonhoeffer was executed at Flossenbürg concentration camp on 9 April 1945.
[hist-eur3-s13.13.b] Catholic opposition came from the top of the Church, through the pope's encyclical and the sermons of one bishop. Both defended Catholic institutions and teaching against Nazi persecution.
[hist-eur3-s13.13.0] Mit brennender Sorge (With burning concern), dated 14 March 1937, was an encyclical by Pius XI written in German. Cardinal Michael von Faulhaber, Archbishop of Munich, and Pacelli helped draft it.
[hist-eur3-s13.13.1] It condemned breaches of the Concordat and the worship of race and state, so it attacked both the regime's broken promises and its ideology.
[hist-eur3-s13.13.2] It was smuggled into Germany and read from Catholic pulpits on Palm Sunday, 21 March 1937. The Gestapo seized copies and closed the printers, and the persecution of the clergy increased.
[hist-eur3-s13.13.3] Clemens August von Galen, Bishop of Münster from 1933 and nicknamed the Lion of Münster, attacked Gestapo seizures of church property in sermons in July and August 1941. He also attacked the killing of disabled people in the T4 euthanasia programme.
[hist-eur3-s13.13.4] The T4 programme was officially halted in August 1941, but the killings continued in secret.
[hist-eur3-s13.13.5] The regime did not arrest Galen, for fear of Catholic opinion in wartime.
[hist-eur3-s13.14.b] Church protest was real but narrow. The churches mostly defended themselves, and historians still argue about how far that was a moral failure.
[hist-eur3-s13.14.0] The churches defended their own institutions and teaching far more than the victims of the regime. Their protests were limited to defending themselves and to opposing the euthanasia programme.
[hist-eur3-s13.14.1] There was little public protest at the persecution of the Jews. Kristallnacht (9-10 November 1938) passed without an official protest from either church.
[hist-eur3-s13.14.2] Most clergy and churchgoers conformed, for example by staying silent, and many backed the war.
[hist-eur3-s13.14.3] Pius XII's Christmas broadcast of 1942 spoke of people killed because of their nationality or race, without naming the Jews or Germany. Critics such as John Cornwell (Hitler's Pope, 1999) call this silence complicity.
[hist-eur3-s13.14.4] Defenders of Pius XII say that public protest would have made things worse, and they point to Catholic help for Jews in hiding.
[hist-eur3-s13.14.5] Bormann, Himmler and Rosenberg intended to destroy the churches' influence after the war. The German Faith Movement and neo-pagan ideas (a revival of pre-Christian religion) had little popular following.
[hist-eur3-s13.15.b] Put side by side, the two stories follow one pattern. The difference lies in how much each dictator needed the Church.
[hist-eur3-s13.15.0] Both dictators began with a concordat, in 1929 and in 1933, and both later clashed with the Church over youth and race.
[hist-eur3-s13.15.1] Mussolini needed the Church and kept a working partnership, so his clashes ended in compromise in 1931 and in protest without a break in 1938.
[hist-eur3-s13.15.2] Hitler saw the churches as rivals for total loyalty, so he moved from courtship to persecution.
[hist-eur3-s13.15.3] The churches survived both regimes as the largest institutions outside party control.
[hist-eur3-s13.16.b] From the early 1920s radio brought news, music, sport, comedy and the voices of leaders into the home. Governments quickly saw that it could inform, entertain and persuade all at once.
[hist-eur3-s13.16.0] Cheap sets made radio affordable, so Britain had about 9 million radio licences by 1939.
[hist-eur3-s13.16.1] The British Broadcasting Company was founded in 1922, with John Reith as its general manager.
[hist-eur3-s13.16.2] On 1 January 1927 it became the British Broadcasting Corporation under a royal charter (a formal grant from the Crown), with Reith as its first director-general until 1938.
[hist-eur3-s13.16.3] The BBC was a monopoly funded by the licence fee that listeners paid, with no advertising, so it did not depend on advertisers.
[hist-eur3-s13.16.4] Reith's public-service ideal, broadcasting for the public good rather than profit, was to "inform, educate and entertain", and Sunday programming was strict.
[hist-eur3-s13.16.5] The Empire Service began in 1932, so that BBC programmes reached listeners across the British Empire.
[hist-eur3-s13.17.b] The American model was the opposite of the BBC's. Radio there was paid for by advertisers, and it challenged British ideas of what radio should be.
[hist-eur3-s13.17.0] NBC (founded 1926) and CBS (founded 1927) were American networks, meaning chains of stations broadcasting the same programmes. They were commercial, paid for by advertisers, who sponsored whole programmes.
[hist-eur3-s13.17.1] Programmes included comedy, variety, dance bands and serials, which were nicknamed soap operas after their soap-company sponsors.
[hist-eur3-s13.17.2] Radio Luxembourg began English-language programmes in 1933, beamed into Britain, especially on Sundays. It played the dance music and American-style shows that the BBC avoided.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s1: 1. Origins and growth of the fascist regimes: Mussolini's Italy
[hist-eur3-s1.0.b] To see why fascism took hold, start with the state it replaced and what the First World War did to it. Italy's war ended in debt, grief and disappointment.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.0] Before 1922 Italy was a constitutional monarchy under King Victor Emmanuel III, with an elected Chamber of Deputies. The King appointed the Prime Minister and commanded the army, which is why his decisions mattered so much in 1922 and 1943.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.1] Italy entered the First World War in 1915 on the Allied side under the secret Treaty of London, which promised it territory from Austria-Hungary. This promise is the yardstick Italians later used to judge the peace.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.2] The war cost heavy casualties and heavy debt, and the peace settlement gave Italy less than the Treaty of London had promised. This fed the sense of a 'mutilated victory' (vittoria mutilata, the poet D'Annunzio's phrase): Italians felt they had won the war but been cheated of the prize.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.3] The Russian Revolution of 1917 made socialists and communists across Europe look like a real threat to property, the Church and the monarchy. Italy's propertied classes therefore watched Italian socialism with fear.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.4] The League of Nations, set up by the peace settlement, was meant to settle disputes between states and could impose sanctions (economic penalties) on an aggressor. Its weakness in Mussolini's later foreign policy depends on this role.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.b] Fascism grew where parliamentary government seemed weak and where fear of revolution and economic hardship had left people frightened and angry.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.0] Italy had five prime ministers between 1919 and 1922 (Orlando, Nitti, Giolitti, Bonomi and Facta). Proportional representation, introduced in 1919, gave parties seats in line with their share of the vote and so produced weak coalitions dependent on the Socialists and the Catholic Popolari (the Catholic People's Party).
[hist-eur3-s1.1.1] The Biennio Rosso (1919-1920, the 'two red years') brought strikes, peasant land seizures and factory occupations in the north. Landowners, industrialists and the middle classes feared a socialist revolution, so they were ready to back anyone who promised order.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.2] Inflation, unemployment and food shortages added to the anger of ex-servicemen, who believed the state had failed to reward them for their sacrifice.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.3] Fascism drew support from ex-servicemen, the lower middle class, large landowners in the Po valley and industrialists. The last two wanted an end to strikes and socialist organisation.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.4] The poet Gabriele D'Annunzio seized Fiume with volunteers (September 1919 to December 1920). This showed the weakness of the state and gave Fascism its style: black shirts, balcony speeches, the Roman salute and mass ritual.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.b] Fascism was not a single fixed doctrine, but its movements shared a recognisable set of beliefs and methods. Each of these can be illustrated from Mussolini's Italy.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.0] Fascism combined nationalism, a single party, a leader, violence against opponents, and hostility to socialism and liberal democracy.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.1] Extreme nationalism: the nation is placed above the individual, so personal rights and interests count for less than the nation's greatness.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.2] The leader principle and personality cult: power is concentrated in one leader who is said to embody the nation, and propaganda presents him as infallible and heroic.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.3] One party and a totalitarian state: Mussolini's own formula, from a 1925 speech, was 'everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state'.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.4] Fascists were hostile to communism, socialism and liberal democracy, which they blamed for class conflict and national weakness.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.5] Fascism glorified violence, war and youth, through uniformed squads and mass rallies, because it saw struggle as the way to make a strong nation.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.6] Fascism promised corporatism, in which employers and workers would co-operate in state-controlled corporations instead of fighting a class conflict.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.7] Comparison only: other inter-war authoritarian regimes included Horthy's Hungary (1920), Primo de Rivera's Spain (1923-30), Pilsudski's Poland (1926), Salazar's Portugal (1932) and Metaxas's Greece (1936). Most were conservative military or clerical dictatorships rather than mass fascist movements.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.b] Three further terms recur in every answer on Mussolini, and examiners expect them to be used precisely.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.0] Dictatorship is rule by one leader or party without free elections, an independent parliament or a free press. Mussolini's rule fits this from 1925-26, once opposition parties and the free press had gone.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.1] A personality cult presents the leader as infallible and heroic through propaganda, ritual and his image. Mussolini was Il Duce (the leader).
[hist-eur3-s1.3.2] Totalitarianism means the state seeks to control all public and private life through one party, secret police and propaganda. The word was first used by an opponent, Giovanni Amendola, in 1923, and Mussolini then adopted it.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.3] Italy was not fully totalitarian, because the King, the Church, the army and big business kept independent power. This is why the King was able to dismiss Mussolini in 1943.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.b] The Fascist movement gained power through organised violence against its opponents, often tolerated by local police and army commanders.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.0] Benito Mussolini, a former editor of the socialist newspaper Avanti!, founded the Fasci di Combattimento in Milan in March 1919. Its programme mixed radical and nationalist ideas, and it was a movement, not yet a party.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.1] The squadristi were armed squads, often led by ex-servicemen, who attacked socialist offices, trade union halls and Popolari premises, especially in the Po valley and Tuscany. They were often tolerated by local police and army commanders.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.2] Their violence was aimed at the organisations that had led the 1919-1920 unrest, so Fascism presented itself as the defender of order against socialism. Frightened property owners therefore welcomed it.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.3] In the May 1921 election the Fascists, standing inside Liberal leader Giovanni Giolitti's electoral bloc, won 35 seats and Mussolini himself was elected. Giolitti underestimated them and believed he could control them.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.4] Mussolini signed a Pacification Pact with socialist leaders in August 1921 to end the violence, but local squads ignored it and the pact collapsed. The movement was becoming too big for one man to restrain.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.5] Mussolini founded the National Fascist Party (PNF) in November 1921, turning the Fasci movement into a party.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.6] By 1922 the Fascists had taken control of many local councils in northern and central Italy. This gave them local bases of power independent of Rome.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.b] The March on Rome was a show of force that persuaded the King to hand power to Mussolini rather than declare a state of siege (emergency military rule).
[hist-eur3-s1.5.0] Between 27 and 30 October 1922, about 30,000 Fascists converged on Rome under four leaders, the quadrumvirs: Italo Balbo, Emilio De Bono, Cesare Maria De Vecchi and Michele Bianchi. Estimates range from about 25,000 to 30,000.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.1] Mussolini himself waited in Milan. Prime Minister Luigi Facta asked the King for a state of siege so that the army could stop the march.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.2] King Victor Emmanuel III refused to sign the state of siege decree, fearing civil war and doubting the army's loyalty. He invited Mussolini to form a government on 29 October, and Mussolini took office on 30 October 1922.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.3] The March succeeded because it was a show of force and a bluff. The King handed power over legally, with the support or acceptance of conservatives, so in form it was an appointment rather than a coup.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.4] Mussolini's first government was a coalition in which Fascists held only a minority of posts alongside Liberals, Popolari and nationalists, so he needed parliament's support. Parliament granted it emergency powers for a year in November 1922.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.b] Once in office Mussolini set about turning a minority coalition into a Fascist-controlled state. He created party and armed bodies loyal to himself and changed the electoral rules.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.0] The Fascist Grand Council (December 1922) became the party's governing body, so that the party had a command centre of its own.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.1] The Fascist militia (MVSN, the Voluntary Militia for National Security, January 1923) turned the squads into a state-paid force loyal to Mussolini rather than the King.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.2] The Acerbo Law of 1923 gave two-thirds of the seats in the Chamber of Deputies to the list with the most votes, provided it had at least 25 per cent. Such a rule made a Fascist majority almost certain.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.3] In the April 1924 election the Fascist-led national list won about 65 per cent of the vote (about two-thirds), in a campaign marked by intimidation and violence against opponents.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.b] The murder of a socialist deputy in 1924 threatened Mussolini's government. He survived the crisis and used it to remove parliamentary and press freedom.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.0] On 30 May 1924 Giacomo Matteotti, a Socialist deputy, denounced electoral fraud and violence in the Chamber of Deputies.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.1] Matteotti was abducted in Rome on 10 June 1924 by men connected to the Fascist squads and was murdered, so Fascist violence now reached a parliamentarian. His body was found in August.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.2] Opposition deputies walked out of the Chamber in what became known as the Aventine Secession (1924), hoping the King would dismiss Mussolini. The King did not act, and the walkout left Mussolini free to dominate parliament.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.3] On 3 January 1925 Mussolini told the Chamber that he accepted political responsibility for the violence, which marked the start of his open dictatorship.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.4] The Leggi Fascistissime (1925-26) made Mussolini responsible only to the King and let him rule by decree. They banned opposition parties, censored the press, and replaced elected mayors with appointed podesta (1926).
[hist-eur3-s1.7.5] The same laws set up a Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State (1926). Political prisoners were sent to confino (internal exile) on islands such as Lipari, and the OVRA secret police hunted opponents.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.6] Violet Gibson, an Irishwoman and the daughter of a Lord Chancellor of Ireland, shot Mussolini in Rome on 7 April 1926 and wounded his nose. The attack was used to justify further repression.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.7] A 1928 electoral law let the Grand Council draw up a single list of 400 candidates for voters to accept or reject. The 1929 plebiscite (a yes or no vote) approved it with about 98 per cent voting yes.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.b] Mussolini aimed to bring industry and labour under state direction through fascist unions and corporations, so that class conflict would end. In practice the system remained more theory than reality.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.0] The Rocco Law of 1926 allowed only state-recognised syndicates (unions and employers' bodies) to represent workers and employers, and it banned strikes and lockouts. Workers thus lost their independent unions.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.1] The Charter of Labour of 1927 set out the principles of the corporate system, in which employers and workers would be organised together under the state, so that class conflict would end.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.2] Corporations covering the main economic sectors were set up in 1934, so that each sector had a state-controlled body. In 1939 the Chamber of Deputies was replaced by the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.3] The corporate state existed largely on paper, because employers kept control of production and the corporations had little real power.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.b] Mussolini fought a series of publicised 'battles' to show that Fascism could make Italy strong. Most gave prestige rather than real success.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.0] The Battle for Grain (from 1925) aimed at self-sufficiency in wheat by raising tariffs and expanding cereal production on poor land. It did not make Italy self-sufficient in food.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.1] The Battle for Births, promoted from about 1927, offered rewards for large families and taxed bachelors, aiming at a bigger population for a stronger nation. The population target was not met.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.2] Quota 90 (the Battle for the Lira) was announced in Mussolini's Pesaro speech of August 1926 and reached in 1927. It revalued the lira to 90 to the pound sterling for national prestige, but it made exports dearer, deflated the economy and cut wages.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.3] The Pontine Marshes south of Rome were drained and resettled in the 1930s, for example, a project presented as a triumph of the regime. Autostrade (motorways) and rail electrification were other visible successes.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.4] After the 1929 crash the state rescued banks and industry through the Institute for Industrial Reconstruction (IRI, 1933), which left the state owning a large share of banking, shipbuilding and steel.
[hist-eur3-s1.10.b] The regime used mass organisations, the press and film to win consent and to present Mussolini as the embodiment of the nation. Consent was backed by fear.
[hist-eur3-s1.10.0] The Opera Nazionale Balilla (1926) brought boys and girls into a state youth organisation, so that children were taught fascist values from childhood. It was reorganised as the Gioventu Italiana del Littorio in 1937.
[hist-eur3-s1.10.1] The Dopolavoro (after-work) organisation from 1925 provided, for example, sport, excursions and leisure for adult workers under fascist supervision, extending party influence into free time.
[hist-eur3-s1.10.2] Elementary schools used a single state textbook from 1929, and university professors had to swear an oath of loyalty to the regime in 1931. Only about a dozen of over 1,200 refused.
[hist-eur3-s1.10.3] The Istituto Luce, founded in 1924, produced newsreels and documentary films, which reached ordinary people and showed Mussolini at work, at rallies and at public works. The Ministry of Popular Culture (Minculpop, 1937) controlled the press, radio, cinema and theatre.
[hist-eur3-s1.10.4] The cult of Il Duce used posters, photographs of him harvesting grain or bare-chested, and balcony speeches from the Palazzo Venezia in Rome, his office. Wall slogans included 'Mussolini is always right' and 'Believe, obey, fight'.
[hist-eur3-s1.10.5] The regime used the Catholic Church and public ritual to build legitimacy, although the Church kept its own youth and press organisations. The Lateran Treaties of 11 February 1929 settled the Roman Question and created Vatican City, a success for the regime.
[hist-eur3-s1.10.6] Consent was won by propaganda and public works, while the militia, the Special Tribunal, the OVRA and confino deterred opposition. This is why propaganda and terror must be explained together.
[hist-eur3-s1.11.b] Mussolini used foreign adventures to build prestige. In these years he was also wary of Hitler, and he acted first against weak neighbours.
[hist-eur3-s1.11.0] The Corfu incident of August 1923 began when General Enrico Tellini of an Italian boundary commission was murdered on the Greek-Albanian border. Italy bombarded and occupied Corfu until Greece paid compensation.
[hist-eur3-s1.11.1] Italy annexed the port of Fiume in 1924 under a treaty with Yugoslavia, which ended a long dispute left over from the peace settlement.
[hist-eur3-s1.11.2] After Austrian Nazis murdered the Austrian Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss in July 1934, Mussolini moved troops to the Brenner Pass, showing he opposed German control of Austria at that time.
[hist-eur3-s1.11.3] In April 1935 Italy joined Britain and France in the Stresa Front, an agreement to resist German challenges to the peace settlement.
[hist-eur3-s1.12.b] The invasion of Abyssinia was Mussolini's biggest foreign gamble. It exposed the weakness of the League of Nations and pushed Italy towards Germany.
[hist-eur3-s1.12.0] After the Walwal incident (December 1934), a clash on the Abyssinian border, Italy invaded Abyssinia (Ethiopia) on 3 October 1935. The attack was also revenge for Italy's defeat by Ethiopia at Adowa in 1896.
[hist-eur3-s1.12.1] Italy used poison gas against the Abyssinians, a breach of the rules of war that shocked opinion abroad. Emperor Haile Selassie appealed to the League of Nations in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s1.12.2] The League imposed sanctions, but they excluded oil, so they did not stop the invasion.
[hist-eur3-s1.12.3] The Hoare-Laval Plan of December 1935, devised by the British Foreign Secretary Samuel Hoare and French Prime Minister Pierre Laval, would have given Italy much of Abyssinia. It was abandoned after public protest in Britain and France.
[hist-eur3-s1.12.4] Addis Ababa was captured on 5 May 1936, so Victor Emmanuel III was proclaimed Emperor of Ethiopia.
[hist-eur3-s1.13.b] Isolated by the sanctions, Mussolini drew closer to Hitler. Step by step this alliance committed Italy to a war it was unprepared to fight.
[hist-eur3-s1.13.0] Italy and Germany drew together after German support during the Abyssinian war. They also intervened jointly for Franco in the Spanish Civil War (1936-39).
[hist-eur3-s1.13.1] Mussolini announced the Rome-Berlin Axis in a Milan speech on 1 November 1936. It was only an understanding between the two states, not a formal alliance.
[hist-eur3-s1.13.2] Italy joined the Anti-Comintern Pact in 1937 and accepted the Anschluss (Germany's union with Austria) in 1938, although in 1934 it had opposed German control of Austria.
[hist-eur3-s1.13.3] Mussolini posed as a peacemaker at the Munich Conference (September 1938), which won him prestige, and invaded Albania in April 1939.
[hist-eur3-s1.13.4] On 22 May 1939 Italy signed the Pact of Steel, a military alliance with Germany. Yet when war began in September 1939 Italy declared itself non-belligerent.
[hist-eur3-s1.13.5] Italy declared war on Britain and France on 10 June 1940, when France was already beaten. Defeats in Greece (invaded from October 1940) and North Africa then made Italy dependent on German help.
[hist-eur3-s1.14.b] Military defeats and an Allied invasion of Sicily brought the regime down in 1943. Mussolini's rule in northern Italy continued under German control until 1945.
[hist-eur3-s1.14.0] Allied forces landed in Sicily on 10 July 1943, and the defeat made Fascist leaders doubt Mussolini. At a meeting on the night of 24-25 July the Fascist Grand Council passed Dino Grandi's motion to return command of the armed forces to the King.
[hist-eur3-s1.14.1] King Victor Emmanuel III dismissed Mussolini on 25 July 1943 and had him arrested. Marshal Pietro Badoglio formed a new government.
[hist-eur3-s1.14.2] Italy's armistice with the Allies was announced on 8 September 1943, so German forces, fearing Italy would change sides, took control of much of the north and centre.
[hist-eur3-s1.14.3] German paratroopers and SS commandos, among them Otto Skorzeny, rescued Mussolini from a mountain hotel at Gran Sasso on 12 September 1943.
[hist-eur3-s1.14.4] Mussolini headed the Italian Social Republic, known as the Salo Republic after its seat on Lake Garda. It was a German puppet state, and it executed his son-in-law Galeazzo Ciano and other Grand Council members at Verona in January 1944.
[hist-eur3-s1.14.5] Mussolini and his mistress Clara Petacci were shot by partisans near Lake Como on 28 April 1945, and their bodies were displayed in Piazzale Loreto in Milan.
[hist-eur3-s1.15.b] Mussolini led Italy from 1922 to 1943 and was the founder and leader of the Fascist movement.
[hist-eur3-s1.15.0] Born in 1883 at Predappio in the Romagna region, he trained as a teacher, became a socialist journalist, and edited Avanti! from 1912.
[hist-eur3-s1.15.1] He was expelled from the Socialist Party in 1914 for supporting Italian entry into the First World War. He served in the army and was wounded in 1917.
[hist-eur3-s1.15.2] As founder of the Fasci di Combattimento (1919) and the National Fascist Party (1921), he led the movement that took power in 1922. He was Prime Minister from 1922, ruled as dictator from 1925 and was deposed in 1943.
[hist-eur3-s1.15.3] He was a participant who shaped events: he organised the March on Rome from Milan, took personal responsibility for the violence in January 1925 and made the key decisions on Abyssinia, the Axis and entry into the war.
[hist-eur3-s1.15.4] Contemporaries were divided. Many conservatives, the Vatican and foreign statesmen admired him for defeating socialism (Churchill praised him on a visit to Rome in 1927). Socialists, Popolari and liberals such as Giovanni Amendola, who died in 1926 after a Fascist beating, denounced his violence.
[hist-eur3-s1.15.5] Historians differ: Renzo De Felice argued that the regime enjoyed real popular consent in 1929-1936, while Denis Mack Smith portrayed Mussolini as an opportunist and propagandist more concerned with image than with real achievement.
[hist-eur3-s1.15.6] Many historians now regard Italian Fascism as an incomplete totalitarianism, since the King, the Church, the army and big business kept independent power. This is why the King was able to dismiss him in 1943.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.b] Judgements of the regime depend on whether its stability and public works are weighed against its violence, economic failures and disastrous war.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.0] Achievement: Mussolini ended parliamentary rule and established a one-party state by 1926, with little open resistance for most of the 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.1] Limit: the corporate state existed largely on paper, as employers kept control of production and the corporations had little real power.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.2] Achievement: the Lateran Treaties of 1929 settled the Roman Question, and the Pontine Marshes scheme, the autostrade motorways and rail electrification gave the regime visible successes.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.3] Limit: the Battle for Grain did not make Italy self-sufficient in food, and the deflationary policy of Quota 90 damaged industry and jobs.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.4] Achievement: Italy gained territory in Abyssinia and Fiume, which the regime presented as the fulfilment of national destiny.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.5] Limit: the Abyssinian war was costly, the alliance with Germany drew Italy into a war it was ill-prepared to fight and left it dependent on Germany, and the regime collapsed in 1943 after defeats in North Africa and Sicily.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.6] Contemporary view: many Italians and foreign conservatives saw Mussolini as the saviour of order who had defeated Bolshevism. Later view: his regime is judged by its violence, its alliance with Hitler, the 1938 racial laws and the defeat it brought on Italy.
[hist-eur3-s1.16.7] Overall, Mussolini was very effective at winning and keeping power (1922-1926) but much less effective at transforming the economy, society or Italy's place in the world.
