TARGET hist-ire5-s6: 6. Direct Rule and the search for a settlement, 1974–1993
[hist-ire5-s6.0.b] After the power-sharing executive fell in 1974, Northern Ireland went back to being governed directly from London. This is the background to everything in this subsection, because no local government existed to solve the conflict.
[hist-ire5-s6.0.0] Direct Rule (government of Northern Ireland straight from Westminster) was first imposed in March 1972, when the Stormont parliament was suspended. The power-sharing executive took office on 1 January 1974 and fell on 28 May 1974, so Direct Rule returned.
[hist-ire5-s6.0.1] Under Direct Rule a Secretary of State and the Northern Ireland Office governed, which meant that decisions were made by British ministers rather than by elected local politicians, so local politicians had no real power.
[hist-ire5-s6.0.2] The Secretaries of State were Merlyn Rees (1974 to 1976), Roy Mason (1976 to 1979), Humphrey Atkins (1979 to 1981), Jim Prior (1981 to 1984), Douglas Hurd (1984 to 1985), Tom King (1985 to 1989), Peter Brooke (1989 to 1992) and Patrick Mayhew (1992 to 1997).
[hist-ire5-s6.0.3] Power sharing (unionists and nationalists sharing government) was the aim of most British and Irish policy after 1974, but it was not restored by 1993, so Direct Rule remained the basis of British policy throughout.
[hist-ire5-s6.0.4] The Troubles began in 1969, and the Provisional IRA, loyalist paramilitaries and the British army were already in conflict when Direct Rule returned in 1974.
[hist-ire5-s6.0.5] Terrorism, as used in this topic, means violence against civilians and the state for political ends. It is used here of the IRA and loyalist paramilitaries, and the British response was a mixture of security measures and politics.
[hist-ire5-s6.0.6] The consent principle, that Northern Ireland's status would not change without the consent of a majority of its people, was already stated in 1973. It is the thread that runs through every step to 1993.
[hist-ire5-s6.1.b] While London governed, it tried one more devolved solution and then changed its security policy. In 1976 these changes shifted who fought the conflict and how prisoners were treated.
[hist-ire5-s6.1.0] The Constitutional Convention was elected on 1 May 1975 to find a devolved settlement (one where Northern Ireland has its own local government). The unionist coalition (UUUC) held a majority in it.
[hist-ire5-s6.1.1] The unionist majority recommended a return to majority rule, which meant government by the largest community. Westminster rejected this because it lacked cross-community support, and the Convention was dissolved in March 1976.
[hist-ire5-s6.1.2] Ulsterisation was the policy, under Roy Mason from 1976, of giving the RUC (the police) and the Ulster Defence Regiment, or UDR (a locally recruited army regiment), the lead in security in place of the British army. The aim was to reduce the army's role.
[hist-ire5-s6.1.3] Criminalisation was the policy of treating paramilitary offenders as ordinary criminals, not as political prisoners, so that violence would lose any political status.
[hist-ire5-s6.1.4] Special category status, which had given paramilitary prisoners some of the privileges of political prisoners, was ended on 1 March 1976 for prisoners convicted of offences from that date.
[hist-ire5-s6.1.5] Diplock courts, in which a judge sits without a jury for scheduled (terrorist-related) offences, had been introduced in 1973. They were part of the same approach.
[hist-ire5-s6.2.b] In August 1976 a shocking accident turned ordinary people on to the streets in protest at the violence. The Peace People became the best-known peace movement of the Troubles, though it did not last.
[hist-ire5-s6.2.0] On 10 August 1976 three children were killed in Belfast when a car, driven by an IRA volunteer who had been shot dead by soldiers, crashed into them. This tragedy was the cause of the movement.
[hist-ire5-s6.2.1] Betty Williams, a witness to the crash, and Mairead Corrigan, the children's aunt, founded the Peace People in August 1976 and led the first peace march. The journalist Ciaran McKeown was a third founder.
[hist-ire5-s6.2.2] The movement was one of ordinary people who held large peace rallies in Belfast and across Northern Ireland. It appealed to both communities and called for an end to paramilitary violence on both sides.
[hist-ire5-s6.2.3] Williams and Corrigan were awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for 1976. The award was announced in 1977, so the prize should not be dated 1977.
[hist-ire5-s6.2.4] Contemporaries praised the movement, but some republicans and loyalists dismissed it as naive. Its support faded after 1977 as the violence continued.
[hist-ire5-s6.3.b] When special category status ended, republican prisoners fought back with protests inside the Maze prison. The protests escalated step by step to the hunger strikes of 1980 and 1981, in which the government and the prisoners each refused to give way.
[hist-ire5-s6.3.0] The H-Blocks were the H-shaped cell blocks of the Maze prison, also called Long Kesh. Republican prisoners there wanted political status, meaning treatment as prisoners of war or political prisoners rather than as ordinary criminals.
[hist-ire5-s6.3.1] The blanket protest began in 1976, when prisoners refused to wear prison uniform and wore only blankets. In 1978 it escalated into the dirty protest.
[hist-ire5-s6.3.2] The first hunger strike (a refusal to eat) began in October 1980 and ended on 18 December 1980.
[hist-ire5-s6.3.3] The second hunger strike began on 1 March 1981 under Bobby Sands, to demand the restoration of political status. He was elected MP for Fermanagh and South Tyrone in April 1981 while on hunger strike, which gave the protest wide publicity.
[hist-ire5-s6.3.4] Sands died on 5 May 1981 after 66 days. Nine further hunger strikers died between May and August 1981, including Francis Hughes, Patsy O'Hara and Kieran Doherty, making ten deaths in all.
[hist-ire5-s6.3.5] Margaret Thatcher declared that 'crime is crime is crime; it is not political', so her government refused to concede political status.
[hist-ire5-s6.3.6] The hunger strike ended on 3 October 1981 without formal political status. Concessions were made afterwards, including on clothing and remission (shortened sentences).
[hist-ire5-s6.4.b] The hunger strikes changed republican tactics. The publicity and sympathy they created showed that republicans could win votes, so Sinn Féin began to contest elections alongside the IRA campaign.
[hist-ire5-s6.4.0] The strikes brought wide sympathy and recruits to republicans, and they hardened attitudes on both sides. They were also a propaganda success (propaganda means messages used to shape opinion), because each death drew attention to the republican case.
[hist-ire5-s6.4.1] In June 1981 two prisoners, including Kieran Doherty, were elected to the Dáil (the Irish parliament), which showed that there was a republican vote to be won.
[hist-ire5-s6.4.2] Owen Carron, Sands's election agent, won the Fermanagh and South Tyrone by-election in August 1981 as an Anti H-Block candidate, so Sands's seat stayed in republican hands.
[hist-ire5-s6.4.3] Sinn Féin adopted an electoral strategy in 1981, summed up in the phrase 'the Armalite and the ballot box', used by Danny Morrison at the party's Ard Fheis (annual conference) in October 1981. It meant seeking votes while the armed campaign went on.
[hist-ire5-s6.4.4] In the October 1982 Northern Ireland Assembly election Sinn Féin won five seats. Gerry Adams was among those elected, but the members abstained, which means they refused to take part.
[hist-ire5-s6.4.5] In June 1983 Gerry Adams won the West Belfast seat at Westminster, which Gerry Fitt had held since 1966. Sinn Féin members refused to take their seats there, so they abstained from Westminster as well.
[hist-ire5-s6.4.6] The strategy was a deliberate move to build political support. Critics regarded it as a cover for continued IRA violence.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.b] Adams was the Sinn Féin leader who turned the party into an electoral force, and he became the main republican voice in dealings with the British and Irish governments.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.0] Gerry Adams was born in 1948 in Belfast and grew up in a republican family in West Belfast.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.1] He was interned (imprisoned without trial) in the early 1970s. In July 1972 he was released to take part in the secret talks between the Provisional IRA and William Whitelaw in London.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.2] He was elected president of Sinn Féin in November 1983, a post he held until 2018, and he led the party's move into electoral politics.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.3] He took part in talks with the SDLP's John Hume in 1988 and again from 1991 to 1993, so he helped to shape the search for a settlement.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.4] In October 1988 the Home Secretary, Douglas Hurd, banned the broadcast of the voices of Sinn Féin spokespeople, including Adams, as a propaganda move. Broadcasters used actors' voices, and the ban ended in 1994.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.5] He was shaped by events too. He was shot and wounded by loyalist gunmen in Belfast in March 1984, lost his Westminster seat to Joe Hendron of the SDLP in 1992, and regained it in 1997.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.6] Contemporaries disagreed about him. Supporters regarded him as the political face of the republican movement, while critics, including unionists and many nationalists, regarded him as the political wing of the IRA.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.7] Supporters credit him with leading republicans from armed struggle towards negotiation. Critics, including unionists, argued that he used Sinn Féin's electoral strategy to complement the IRA campaign, so that republicans kept both.
[hist-ire5-s6.5.8] Adams has always denied membership of the IRA, though others have alleged it.
[hist-ire5-s6.6.b] In 1983 the Dublin government tried to agree a common nationalist view of the future. Its report offered Britain three options, and Margaret Thatcher's blunt rejection of them in 1984 showed how far Britain was willing to go.
[hist-ire5-s6.6.0] The New Ireland Forum was set up in 1983 by the Dublin government, under the Taoiseach Garret FitzGerald. It included Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael, Labour and the SDLP, while Sinn Féin was excluded.
[hist-ire5-s6.6.1] The unionist parties declined to take part, which meant that its report reflected the views of nationalist parties and the Dublin government only, so unionists could reject it.
[hist-ire5-s6.6.2] The Forum's report, published on 2 May 1984, set out three options, such as a unitary Irish state, a federal or confederal state, and joint authority by Britain and Ireland. It said that the unitary state was the preferred option.
[hist-ire5-s6.6.3] At a press conference on 19 November 1984, Margaret Thatcher rejected all three options, saying 'Out, out, out'.
[hist-ire5-s6.6.4] Her rejection marked the limits of British willingness to change Northern Ireland's constitutional position in the mid-1980s, though within a year she signed an agreement giving Dublin a role.
[hist-ire5-s6.7.b] In October 1984 the IRA bombed the Conservative Party conference in Brighton. The attack showed the danger of the IRA campaign.
[hist-ire5-s6.7.0] On 12 October 1984 the IRA bombed the Grand Hotel in Brighton, where the Conservative Party conference was being held.
[hist-ire5-s6.7.1] Five people were killed in the bombing, and Margaret Thatcher escaped. The IRA said it would try again.
[hist-ire5-s6.7.2] Patrick Magee was later convicted of planting the bomb.
[hist-ire5-s6.8.b] Thatcher was the British Prime Minister who led the government through the hunger strikes and signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement, a shift in British policy.
[hist-ire5-s6.8.0] Margaret Thatcher was Prime Minister from May 1979 to November 1990, and she made security and the defeat of terrorism central to her policy in Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s6.8.1] Her years in office were shaped by violence. On 27 August 1979 the IRA killed Lord Mountbatten at Mullaghmore, Co. Sligo, and on the same day the Warrenpoint ambush killed eighteen soldiers. Later came the hunger strikes and the Brighton bombing.
[hist-ire5-s6.8.2] She refused to grant political status to the hunger strikers, saying 'crime is crime is crime', and her government held firm during the 1981 protest.
[hist-ire5-s6.8.3] She rejected the three options of the New Ireland Forum in November 1984, saying 'Out, out, out', yet in November 1985 she signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement with the Taoiseach Garret FitzGerald at Hillsborough Castle.
[hist-ire5-s6.8.4] She was personally involved in the negotiations with FitzGerald in 1984 and 1985, and she signed the Agreement despite strong opposition from unionists.
[hist-ire5-s6.8.5] Contemporary unionists regarded her as having betrayed the Union, while nationalists and Dublin welcomed the Agreement as a sign of British recognition of an Irish interest. Republicans regarded her as intransigent over the hunger strikes.
[hist-ire5-s6.8.6] Historians differ on her motives. Some stress the hope of improved security cooperation with Dublin, others the aim of undercutting Sinn Féin's growth after 1981, and others her relations with Dublin and Washington. The Agreement did not end the violence.
[hist-ire5-s6.9.b] The Agreement gave the Irish government a formal consultative role in Northern Ireland. It was the biggest British policy shift since Sunningdale (the 1973 power-sharing agreement), and it survived fierce opposition.
[hist-ire5-s6.9.0] It was signed on 15 November 1985 at Hillsborough Castle by Margaret Thatcher and Garret FitzGerald.
[hist-ire5-s6.9.1] Thatcher's motives included improving cross-border security and winning back nationalist support from Sinn Féin, which was growing after 1981. The Irish government wanted to protect the SDLP, and the Forum had also shown that Dublin wanted a role.
[hist-ire5-s6.9.2] It set up an Intergovernmental Conference of ministers, with a permanent joint secretariat at Maryfield near Belfast. Through it the Irish government could put forward views on Northern Ireland, though its role was consultative only.
[hist-ire5-s6.9.3] Article 1 affirmed that there would be no change in the status of Northern Ireland without the consent of a majority of its people. This was the consent principle, and it reassured unionists in theory.
[hist-ire5-s6.9.4] Unionists responded with the slogan 'Ulster says No', a large rally at Belfast City Hall on 23 November 1985 and a day of action in March 1986. Fifteen unionist MPs resigned their Westminster seats in January 1986, forcing by-elections, but the Agreement remained in force.
[hist-ire5-s6.9.5] Republicans rejected the Agreement because it left British jurisdiction in place, and Fianna Fáil under Charles Haughey opposed it in the Dáil. The SDLP welcomed it as a limited but real step towards Irish involvement.
[hist-ire5-s6.9.6] It was the biggest British policy shift since Sunningdale, and it survived despite the unionist campaign.
[hist-ire5-s6.10.b] Molyneaux led the Ulster Unionist Party through the period of the Anglo-Irish Agreement and was the main voice of mainstream unionist opposition.
[hist-ire5-s6.10.0] James Molyneaux was born in 1920 and was MP for South Antrim from 1970 and for Lagan Valley from 1983. He became leader of the Ulster Unionist Party in 1979 and led it until 1995.
[hist-ire5-s6.10.1] He led mainstream unionist opposition to the Anglo-Irish Agreement, and he worked with Ian Paisley, the confrontational leader of the DUP, on the 'Ulster says No' campaign.
[hist-ire5-s6.10.2] He took part in the unionist campaign of 1985 and 1986, which included the 'Ulster says No' rally at Belfast City Hall on 23 November 1985 and the resignation of fifteen unionist MPs from Westminster in January 1986.
[hist-ire5-s6.10.3] He favoured the integration of Northern Ireland into the United Kingdom's system of government rather than devolution (a separate local parliament).
[hist-ire5-s6.10.4] He cultivated good relations with Conservative ministers in the 1990s, and John Major's government at times relied on Ulster Unionist votes at Westminster, which gave him influence.
[hist-ire5-s6.10.5] Contemporaries valued his caution and his commitment to the Union, although some unionists felt he was too passive in opposing the Agreement.
[hist-ire5-s6.10.6] Historians regard him as a steady, quiet leader of mainstream unionism who opposed the Agreement without the confrontational style of Paisley. His strategy of opposition did not stop the Agreement.
[hist-ire5-s6.11.b] Hume led the SDLP from 1979 and became the leading constitutional nationalist voice. His dialogue with Sinn Féin reshaped the search for peace.
[hist-ire5-s6.11.0] John Hume became leader of the SDLP in 1979 and was elected to the European Parliament the same year.
[hist-ire5-s6.11.1] He supported the Anglo-Irish Agreement, arguing that it gave nationalists a role in Northern Ireland's government.
[hist-ire5-s6.11.2] He described the conflict as a problem of three relationships: within Northern Ireland, between North and South and between Ireland and Britain. This is why he believed that no solution could come from one relationship alone.
[hist-ire5-s6.11.3] He began the dialogue with Gerry Adams in 1988 and argued that the IRA campaign must end if nationalists were to achieve their goals by dialogue.
[hist-ire5-s6.11.4] He took part in the Brooke–Mayhew talks of 1991 and 1992, such as the attempt to set up a new devolved settlement.
[hist-ire5-s6.11.5] Contemporaries regarded him as a moderate and effective nationalist leader, while unionists criticised him for talking to Sinn Féin.
[hist-ire5-s6.11.6] Historians credit him with shaping the nationalist case for a peace process based on consent and dialogue, because his work with Adams and with the Irish government influenced the Downing Street Declaration.
[hist-ire5-s6.12.b] By 1988 the governments and republicans were fighting a battle over opinion as well as a military one. At the same time, the leaders of constitutional nationalism and republicanism began to talk.
[hist-ire5-s6.12.0] In October 1988 the Home Secretary, Douglas Hurd, banned the broadcast of the voices of Sinn Féin spokespeople and others. Broadcasters used actors' voices, and the ban ended in 1994.
[hist-ire5-s6.12.1] The ban was a propaganda measure by the government, intended to deny republicans a public platform, so it shaped opinion against them.
[hist-ire5-s6.12.2] The Hume–Adams dialogue began in 1988, when the SDLP leader and the Sinn Féin president held talks that were publicly acknowledged.
[hist-ire5-s6.12.3] The talks were controversial, with unionists and some nationalists objecting to contact between the SDLP and Sinn Féin while the IRA campaign continued.
[hist-ire5-s6.12.4] The dialogue resumed in 1991 and produced joint statements in 1993. Historians regard it as an important precursor to the peace process, though it did not end the IRA campaign in this period.
[hist-ire5-s6.13.b] The Enniskillen bombing of 1987 became one of the best-known atrocities of the Troubles. It increased pressure for peace and damaged the IRA's standing.
[hist-ire5-s6.13.0] On 8 November 1987, Remembrance Sunday, an IRA bomb exploded at the war memorial in Enniskillen, County Fermanagh, killing eleven people.
[hist-ire5-s6.13.1] The victims were mostly elderly civilians who had gathered for the ceremony. A twelfth person, Ronnie Hill, died in 2000 after a long coma.
[hist-ire5-s6.13.2] The IRA said it had intended to attack soldiers, but it admitted that the bombing was an error, and the attack was widely condemned.
[hist-ire5-s6.13.3] Gordon Wilson, whose daughter Marie was killed, said in a BBC interview 'I bear no ill will', and he called for an end to violence.
[hist-ire5-s6.13.4] The bombing was condemned in Britain, in Ireland and in the United States, and it damaged the IRA's standing.
[hist-ire5-s6.14.b] After the Agreement, the British government tried to bring the constitutional parties together again. Peter Brooke started the process, and Patrick Mayhew continued it, but no settlement followed.
[hist-ire5-s6.14.0] Peter Brooke, as Secretary of State, said in November 1990 that Britain had no selfish strategic or economic interest in Northern Ireland. This signalled that Britain would not stand in the way of an agreed outcome.
[hist-ire5-s6.14.1] The Brooke initiative of 1991 and the Mayhew talks of 1992 brought the parties together in multi-party talks, such as the British government and the constitutional parties, so they were a different format from Hume–Adams.
[hist-ire5-s6.14.2] The talks did not produce agreement, so no devolved government was restored.
[hist-ire5-s6.15.b] After the failed talks and continued violence, the British and Irish prime ministers issued a joint statement of principles. It set out the principles on which later peace efforts were built.
[hist-ire5-s6.15.0] The Downing Street Declaration was issued on 15 December 1993 by Prime Minister John Major and Taoiseach Albert Reynolds.
[hist-ire5-s6.15.1] It said that the Irish people as a whole had the right to self-determination, which could be exercised by agreement between North and South. It also said that a united Ireland would require the consent of a majority in Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s6.15.2] It said that the British government had no selfish strategic or economic interest in Northern Ireland and would legislate for a united Ireland if a majority wished it. It invited republicans to renounce violence and join talks.
[hist-ire5-s6.15.3] The Irish government committed itself to set up a Forum for Peace and Reconciliation, and to examine Articles 2 and 3 of its constitution (the articles that claimed the whole island).
[hist-ire5-s6.15.4] Nationalists and the Dublin government welcomed it, while unionists denounced it as a sign that Britain was moving towards a united Ireland. Sinn Féin and the IRA considered it, and the IRA ceasefire came in 1994, which falls outside this subsection.
[hist-ire5-s6.16.b] To judge these steps, weigh what each achieved against what it failed to do. The Agreement and the Declaration are the two main steps, and examiners reward answers that compare them.
[hist-ire5-s6.16.0] Achievement: the Anglo-Irish Agreement gave the Irish government a formal consultative role in Northern Ireland's affairs, a larger role than Sunningdale had given it.
[hist-ire5-s6.16.1] Achievement: the Downing Street Declaration set out principles of consent and self-determination that became the basis for later agreements.
[hist-ire5-s6.16.2] Limit: between 1974 and 1993 no devolved government was restored, and Direct Rule remained the basis of British policy.
[hist-ire5-s6.16.3] Limit: the Hume–Adams dialogue and the Brooke and Mayhew talks did not end the IRA campaign. Violence continued throughout the period, as Brighton and Enniskillen show.
[hist-ire5-s6.16.4] Contemporary view: nationalists and the Irish government regarded the Anglo-Irish Agreement and the Declaration as steps towards justice and peace.
[hist-ire5-s6.16.5] Contemporary view: unionists regarded the Agreement as an infringement of their constitutional rights and denounced the Declaration as a sign that Britain was moving towards a united Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s6.16.6] Later view: historians regard the period as one in which the framework for peace was built gradually, through dialogue, agreements and the acceptance of consent as a principle.

CANDIDATES hist-ire5-s5: 5. Case study: the Sunningdale Agreement and the power-sharing executive, 1973–1974
[hist-ire5-s5.0.b] Sunningdale only makes sense against the collapse of the old system of government in Northern Ireland. By 1973 Britain was looking for a new settlement that both communities could accept.
[hist-ire5-s5.0.0] Stormont was Northern Ireland's devolved parliament (it handled local affairs while Westminster kept overall control), and the Unionist Party had dominated it since 1921. Nationalists felt excluded because of discrimination and gerrymandering (drawing electoral boundaries to favour one side).
[hist-ire5-s5.0.1] Violence escalated from 1969. Internment (imprisonment without trial) began on 9 August 1971, and Bloody Sunday followed on 30 January 1972, so confidence in the old system collapsed.
[hist-ire5-s5.0.2] As a result Stormont was suspended and Direct Rule from London began in March 1972, with William Whitelaw as the first Secretary of State for Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s5.0.3] Direct Rule meant a Secretary of State answerable to Westminster ran Northern Ireland, so the British aim was to replace it with a devolved government acceptable to both communities.
[hist-ire5-s5.0.4] The SDLP (Social Democratic and Labour Party, the main nationalist party, founded in August 1970 by Gerry Fitt, John Hume and others) and the Alliance Party (a cross-community party, also founded in 1970) were the new parties of moderate opinion.
[hist-ire5-s5.0.5] Unionism was split between supporters of reform and power sharing and opponents of both, so any new settlement had to win over a divided majority community.
[hist-ire5-s5.1.b] Power sharing was the central idea of the 1973 settlement. It was an answer to the old Stormont system, in which one community held all the power.
[hist-ire5-s5.1.0] Power sharing means unionists and nationalists both holding executive office (seats in the government), so that neither community can rule alone.
[hist-ire5-s5.1.1] The executive was the government of Northern Ireland, the Assembly was its parliament, and the Council of Ireland was a separate all-Ireland body. Keep the three distinct, because answers often confuse them.
[hist-ire5-s5.1.2] Unionists were split between supporters and opponents of reform and power sharing.
[hist-ire5-s5.2.b] The first step in the 1973 plan was a vote on Northern Ireland's constitutional position. It was meant to reassure unionists, but it settled less than it appeared to.
[hist-ire5-s5.2.0] The border poll was held on 8 March 1973. It asked voters whether Northern Ireland should remain part of the United Kingdom or join a united Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s5.2.1] Nationalists boycotted it, so the result was never going to measure their view.
[hist-ire5-s5.2.2] About 98.9 per cent of the votes cast were to stay in the United Kingdom, but turnout was only about 59 per cent because of the boycott.
[hist-ire5-s5.2.3] The poll therefore confirmed that a majority of those who voted wanted the Union, but it did not settle the question for nationalists, since the boycott left their view unrecorded.
[hist-ire5-s5.3.b] With the poll held, Britain set out its plan for a new devolved government. It combined power sharing inside Northern Ireland with a link to the Republic.
[hist-ire5-s5.3.0] The White Paper (a government policy document) of March 1973, Northern Ireland Constitutional Proposals, proposed an Assembly elected by proportional representation, so that minority parties would win seats in line with their votes.
[hist-ire5-s5.3.1] It also proposed an executive in which unionists and nationalists shared power, meaning that power sharing would be built into the government itself.
[hist-ire5-s5.3.2] The White Paper proposed a Council of Ireland linking Belfast and Dublin as an Irish dimension (a recognised role for the Republic). Many unionists regarded this as a first step towards a united Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s5.3.3] The Northern Ireland Constitution Act of July 1973 abolished the old Stormont parliament, so that the new Assembly could replace it.
[hist-ire5-s5.3.4] Under the Act the Secretary of State kept responsibility for security, so the new executive would not control the police or army.
[hist-ire5-s5.4.b] The Assembly election tested whether power sharing had enough support. It showed how badly unionism had split.
[hist-ire5-s5.4.0] The Assembly election was held on 28 June 1973 and used the single transferable vote (a form of proportional representation in which voters rank candidates and surplus votes transfer), which gave small parties a fair chance of seats.
[hist-ire5-s5.4.1] The Assembly had 78 members. The SDLP won 19 seats and the Alliance Party won 8.
[hist-ire5-s5.4.2] Unionists were divided between Faulkner's supporters in the Ulster Unionist Party, who accepted power sharing, and unionists who opposed it.
[hist-ire5-s5.4.3] The opponents of power sharing included unionists inside the Ulster Unionist Party itself, Ian Paisley's Democratic Unionists and William Craig's Vanguard. Unionist seat totals are disputed, so none are given here.
[hist-ire5-s5.4.4] This split mattered because the new system depended on unionist consent, and the party that was meant to deliver it was fighting itself.
[hist-ire5-s5.5.b] After the election the parties that accepted power sharing negotiated an executive. Its make-up shows how power sharing worked in practice.
[hist-ire5-s5.5.0] Agreement to form an executive was reached in November 1973, so it could take office on 1 January 1974.
[hist-ire5-s5.5.1] Brian Faulkner (Unionist) was Chief Executive, and Gerry Fitt (SDLP) was Deputy Chief Executive.
[hist-ire5-s5.5.2] John Hume (SDLP) was Minister of Commerce, and Oliver Napier (Alliance) was in charge of legal affairs.
[hist-ire5-s5.5.3] Austin Currie (SDLP) held Housing, and Paddy Devlin (SDLP) held Health and Social Services.
[hist-ire5-s5.5.4] The voting membership was 11: six pro-agreement Unionists, four SDLP and one Alliance. This put unionists and nationalists in government together for the first time.
[hist-ire5-s5.5.5] Faulkner led this executive, but his own party was split, so the party's ruling body would soon turn against it.
[hist-ire5-s5.6.b] The executive was agreed inside Northern Ireland, but the Irish dimension needed the two governments. They met with the Northern Ireland parties at Sunningdale to settle it.
[hist-ire5-s5.6.0] The conference was held at Sunningdale Park in Berkshire, England, from 6 to 9 December 1973.
[hist-ire5-s5.6.1] The British Prime Minister Edward Heath and the Taoiseach (Irish prime minister) Liam Cosgrave attended, along with the three parties that backed power sharing.
[hist-ire5-s5.6.2] The Northern Ireland parties present were Faulkner's Unionists, the SDLP (Fitt, Hume and others) and the Alliance Party (Napier).
[hist-ire5-s5.6.3] The agreement was announced on 9 December 1973, and the British government said it was committed to implementing it.
[hist-ire5-s5.6.4] Heath was Prime Minister at Sunningdale, while Harold Wilson was Prime Minister during the strike. This is a common confusion in dated answers.
[hist-ire5-s5.7.b] The agreement dealt with three linked questions: how Dublin and Belfast would co-operate, what the status of Northern Ireland was, and how law and order would be policed.
[hist-ire5-s5.7.0] The agreement set up a Council of Ireland with a council of ministers, seven members from each side, to give the Irish dimension a formal body.

CANDIDATES hist-ire5-s4: 4. The outbreak of the Troubles: from 1969 to Direct Rule, 1972
[hist-ire5-s4.0.b] By 1969 Northern Ireland had been governed for almost fifty years by one party, and many Catholics felt shut out of the state. Knowing what had built up before August 1969 explains why the violence broke out so quickly once it started.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.0] The Government of Ireland Act 1920 set up the Northern Ireland parliament (Stormont), and the Unionist Party governed from 1921 to 1972. Westminster kept sovereignty but in practice rarely interfered in Northern Ireland's internal affairs.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.1] The Ireland Act 1949 guaranteed that Northern Ireland would stay in the United Kingdom unless its parliament (later its people) consented, which is why unionists treated any talk of change as a threat to the border.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.2] Catholics complained of discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting. The Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association (NICRA), founded in 1967, campaigned on these grievances through marches.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.3] A NICRA march in Derry on 5 October 1968 and the attack on a People's Democracy march at Burntollet in January 1969 deepened tension, and the Prime Minister, Terence O'Neill, resigned on 28 April 1969. Such marches mattered because they brought the grievances onto the streets.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.4] The RUC (Royal Ulster Constabulary) was the regular police, armed and mostly Protestant. The B Specials (the Ulster Special Constabulary) were a part-time reserve, almost entirely Protestant, and Catholics distrusted both.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.5] The IRA's border campaign of 1956-62 had failed, and Stormont had used internment (detention without trial) against it in the 1950s, so the IRA was weak and divided in 1969.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.b] Four terms run through this period, and each is used in a specific way in this topic. Defining them first makes the events easier to follow.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.0] Terrorism means violence against civilians and the state for political aims. In this topic it applies to both republican and loyalist paramilitaries, and examiners reward answers that name both sides.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.1] Sectarianism means hostility between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance, so an attack on a Catholic or Protestant as such, not as a soldier or politician, is sectarian.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.2] Internment means detaining suspects without trial, so people could be held on suspicion alone. It was introduced in August 1971 and applied almost entirely to Catholics.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.3] Direct Rule means that Northern Ireland was governed directly from Westminster, through a Secretary of State, instead of by the Stormont parliament. It began on 30 March 1972.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.b] The Apprentice Boys' parade in Derry in August 1969 led to street fighting, and British troops were sent in to restore order. The crisis then forced London to take a hand in policing and reform.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.0] On 12 August 1969 the parade of the Apprentice Boys of Derry passed close to the Bogside (a Catholic area of Derry), and stone-throwing and clashes with the RUC began.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.1] The Battle of the Bogside lasted from 12 to 14 August 1969. Residents put up barricades, which created the 'Free Derry' area.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.2] British troops were deployed in Derry on 14 August 1969 and in Belfast shortly afterwards, on 15 August. This was the start of the army's long involvement in internal security. The UK Prime Minister was Harold Wilson (Labour).
[hist-ire5-s4.2.3] Rioting in Belfast on 14 and 15 August 1969 saw Catholic streets such as Bombay Street in the Lower Falls burned and several people killed, most of them Catholics.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.4] Thousands of families, most of them Catholic, fled their homes in Belfast, and refugee centres were set up in the Republic and in Catholic parishes. August 1969 is also often treated as the start of the Troubles in their most violent form.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.5] The Downing Street Declaration of 19 August 1969, issued by Wilson and Chichester-Clark, restated that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of its people, and promised reforms.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.6] The Hunt Report, published on 10 October 1969 by a committee led by Lord Hunt, recommended a reformed, unarmed RUC under a police authority, so that the police would no longer look like a partisan armed force. It also said the B Specials should be replaced.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.7] The B Specials (Ulster Special Constabulary) were stood down in April 1970. The Ulster Defence Regiment, a part-time regiment of the British army formed in 1970, took over their local security duties.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.b] Chichester-Clark's two years as Prime Minister saw reform under pressure from London and a rising tide of violence. He could satisfy neither unionist hardliners nor nationalists.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.0] James Chichester-Clark became Prime Minister on 1 May 1969, defeating Brian Faulkner in the party vote by 17 votes to 16, a very narrow margin that left Faulkner as his rival.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.1] He presided over the Battle of the Bogside and the disbanding of the B Specials, and London pressed him to carry out the reforms of the Hunt Report.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.2] His government announced a points system for housing allocation, which made the awarding of council houses fairer, the creation of a Housing Executive (established in 1971) and reform of local government.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.3] Unionist hardliners saw these steps as surrender, while nationalists said they came too late, so the reforms won him little credit from either side.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.4] He resigned on 20 March 1971 after London would not give him the security measures he wanted.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.5] Brian Faulkner succeeded him as Prime Minister on 23 March 1971, so the man he had narrowly beaten in 1969 took over because security policy had failed.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.b] August 1969 convinced many Catholics that the IRA had failed to protect them, and the movement split. At the same time new political parties appeared, offering constitutional alternatives to violence.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.0] The IRA split in December 1969 into the Provisional IRA, whose chief of staff was Seán Mac Stíofáin, and the Official IRA, led by Cathal Goulding.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.1] The split followed the failure to defend Catholic areas in August 1969, which discredited the old leadership, and a dispute over abstentionism (refusing to take seats in parliaments) and the political, socialist direction.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.2] The Provisionals argued that the IRA must defend Catholic areas and that British forces should be driven out, in pursuit of a united Ireland. This position gained support after August 1969.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.3] Provisional Sinn Féin was set up in January 1970 to match the new military wing, so the Provisionals had a political party as well as an army.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.4] The Falls curfew of 3 to 5 July 1970 saw troops search houses for weapons in the Lower Falls, and four civilians were killed. The operation alienated Catholics from the army.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.5] The Alliance Party was founded in April 1970 as a cross-community party, and it later chose Oliver Napier as leader.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.6] The Social Democratic and Labour Party (SDLP) was founded on 21 August 1970 by nationalist politicians including Gerry Fitt (leader), John Hume, Austin Currie and Paddy Devlin. It stood for constitutional nationalism, seeking change by political means.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.7] The SDLP withdrew from Stormont in July 1971 after the army shot dead two men in Derry, Séamus Cusack and Desmond Beattie.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.8] The Official IRA, which stressed a left-wing political strategy, declared a ceasefire in May 1972, which left the Provisionals as the main republican force.

[hist-ire5-s4.5.0] The Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) was revived in 1966 and killed three people that year. In the early years of the Troubles it carried out attacks on Catholic targets. It was a small, secretive killer group.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.1] The Ulster Defence Association (UDA), a loose umbrella of loyalist vigilante groups, was formed in 1971 and grew rapidly in working-class Protestant areas. It was a legal organisation until 1992, which marks it out from the UVF.

[hist-ire5-s4.5.4] William Craig founded the Ulster Vanguard movement in 1972, which held large rallies against the loss of Stormont's powers.

[hist-ire5-s4.10.4] The IRA's campaign continued after Motorman, which is why 1972 was the deadliest year of the Troubles, with nearly 500 deaths.

[hist-ire5-s4.11.3] The Provisional IRA gave the Catholic community a military organisation, and its campaign drew the British army into the conflict.

[hist-ire5-s4.12.b] Direct Rule can be judged as an achievement or a failure, and contemporaries judged it differently from later historians. Examiners reward paired points on both sides.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.0] Achievement: the end of Stormont removed an institution that nationalists had rejected since 1921, and it opened the way for later settlements.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.1] Failure: the Stormont government could not contain the violence, and its reliance on internment alienated the Catholic community.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.2] Contemporary view: unionists saw the suspension of Stormont as a betrayal by Westminster, while nationalists welcomed it as the end of an unfair system, and were relieved.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.3] Contemporary view: the British government saw Direct Rule as a temporary arrangement, but it proved to be a long-term one.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.4] Later view: historians argue that the 1972 settlement was forced by violence, and that the Unionist government's decline was a product of its own policies as well as republican action.
