[hist-eur1-s8.5.3] He was a participant in the Chartist campaigns of 1839, 1842 and 1848, and he presented the 1848 petition to Parliament.
[hist-eur1-s8.5.4] He led the National Land Company, which bought land for members and collapsed after financial mismanagement.
[hist-eur1-s8.5.5] The Chartist demonstration on Kennington Common on 10 April 1848 was intended to present a petition, but it dispersed peacefully after the authorities banned a procession to Westminster.
[hist-eur1-s8.5.6] Contemporaries split over him: many working-class radicals admired him, while government officials and middle-class reformers regarded him as reckless.
[hist-eur1-s8.5.7] Older accounts blame his vanity and his quarrels with Lovett and the moral-force Chartists; Dorothy Thompson and other historians stress his gifts as an organiser and speaker for the rank and file; his mental breakdown in 1852 ended his influence and he died in 1855.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.0] The Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to male householders in the boroughs who paid rent of £10 a year and removed seats from rotten boroughs for the new industrial towns, but it still left only about one adult man in five with a vote.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.1] The Second Reform Act of 1867 extended the vote to many urban working men, the secret ballot came in 1872, and universal male suffrage followed only in 1918.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.2] The Second Republic in France gave universal male suffrage in 1848, and the North German Confederation adopted it for its Reichstag in 1867.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.0] Sir Robert Peel, Conservative leader and Prime Minister in 1834–35 and again from 1841 to 1846, was a central figure in the reform of British government.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.1] As Home Secretary he founded the Metropolitan Police in London in 1829, a professional force that became a model for others.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.2] He conceded Catholic emancipation in 1829, with the Duke of Wellington, to avoid civil conflict in Ireland, and it angered many Tories.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.3] The Tamworth Manifesto of December 1834 set out a moderate programme of reform within the Conservative tradition, which became the basis of the modern Conservative Party.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.4] He reintroduced income tax in 1842 to pay for tariff reductions, and his budgets of 1842 and 1845 cut tariffs on many imports.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.5] The Bank Charter Act of 1844 regulated the issue of banknotes by the Bank of England, and the Factory Act of 1844 limited hours for women and children.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.6] Peel repealed the Corn Laws in 1846, partly in response to the Irish potato famine, which split the Conservative Party; he resigned in June 1846 and died after a riding accident in 1850.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.7] The Corn Laws repeal was supported by free-trade reformers such as Richard Cobden and John Bright, who had campaigned through the Anti-Corn Law League.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.8] The Conservative Party's critics, notably Disraeli, attacked him as a traitor in 1846; historians such as Norman Gash judge him one of the greatest British statesmen of the century.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.0] Owen worked outside government to create model communities, while Peel reformed the state from within through legislation and institutions.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.1] Owen's aims were co-operation and communal ownership, whereas Peel wanted to preserve private property and the existing social order, while making it more efficient.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.2] Owen's reforms were confined to one community, while Peel's measures such as the Metropolitan Police and the Bank Charter Act affected the entire country.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.3] Owen's ideas were rejected by most of the political establishment, while Peel's policies were accepted as sound by much of the middle class.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.0] Achievement: trade unions lost their illegal status after 1824 and the New Model unions gave skilled workers lasting organisation.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.1] Limit: the Combination Acts, the Tolpuddle sentences and the failure of the GNCTU showed how hard it was to organise workers in the early period.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.2] Achievement: Chartism created a mass movement that kept parliamentary reform on the agenda, and several Chartist demands were later achieved.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.3] Limit: Chartism's petitions were rejected, its leaders were divided, and the movement lost momentum after 1848.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.4] Achievement: Peel's reforms modernised the police, the banking system and tariff policy, and they helped the British economy grow.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.5] Overall: the responses to industrialisation included unions, socialist theory and Chartist agitation, yet the main political gains came from state reform led by Peel rather than from working-class movements, which won their goals only much later.
