TARGET hist-ire3-s1: 1. The Home Rule Bill, World War I and the 1916 Rising, 1912–1916
[hist-ire3-s1.0.b] The events of 1912 to 1916 rest on a few older ideas that examiners expect you to know. Irish politics was split between those who wanted a devolved parliament, those who wanted to keep the Union, and a small group who believed only force would free Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.0] Home Rule meant a devolved parliament in Dublin (it would handle Irish affairs) while Ireland stayed in the United Kingdom, which had been created by the Union of 1801. The First Home Rule Bill (1886) and the Second (1893) both failed.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.1] The Irish Parliamentary Party (IPP), reunited in 1900 and led by John Redmond, was the main nationalist party. It used constitutional methods, meaning votes and debates at Westminster, to win Home Rule.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.2] Unionists, who wanted to keep the Union, were strongest in Ulster, so they opposed Home Rule fiercely. Edward Carson and James Craig led them. Their strength there is why Home Rule split the country.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.3] Cultural nationalism grew with the Gaelic League (founded 1893). Labour unrest followed in the 1913 Dublin Lockout, when employers locked out striking workers, and the Irish Citizen Army, a workers' defence force, was formed in 1913 (Connolly later became its leader).
[hist-ire3-s1.0.4] The Fenians believed Ireland could be freed only by armed rebellion. Their secret, oath-bound society, the Irish Republican Brotherhood (IRB), was founded in 1858 by James Stephens, and it planned the 1916 Rising.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.5] Physical force means armed violence used to win political aims. The Rising revived this tradition against the constitutional methods of the IPP. The Volunteers were later called the IRA, from about 1919.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.b] After 1910 the Liberal government needed Irish votes to survive, and a new law had weakened the Lords' power to block bills. Together these made Home Rule a real prospect.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.0] Two general elections were held in 1910, in January and December. Each left the Liberals short of a majority, so the IPP held the balance of power (its votes decided whether the government survived).
[hist-ire3-s1.1.1] Redmond's price for supporting the Liberals was Home Rule, because that was the purpose of his party. This is why the government of Asquith, the Liberal Prime Minister, took up a Home Rule Bill.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.2] The Parliament Act 1911 replaced the House of Lords' veto with a delay of two years. This removed the Lords' power to block a bill for good.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.3] Under the Act, a bill passed by the Commons in three successive sessions could become law without the Lords' consent. The Lords could therefore slow the Home Rule Bill but could no longer stop it.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.4] Asquith introduced the Third Home Rule Bill in the House of Commons on 11 April 1912.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.b] The Bill offered Ireland a limited parliament, not independence. It then passed through the three Commons votes that the Parliament Act required.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.0] The Bill set up an Irish parliament in Dublin made up of a Senate of 40 and a House of Commons of 164, with an executive (the ministers who run government) responsible to it.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.1] Westminster kept control of the Crown, war and peace, the army and navy, foreign affairs and customs and excise, so Ireland would have had only limited self-government. Most taxation also remained under Westminster.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.2] The Bill reserved the police to British control for six years. This is why Home Rule would not have given Dublin full control of law and order at the start, although the Bill never actually operated.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.3] Ireland would send 42 MPs to Westminster instead of the 103 Irish seats of the time, which shows that Irish representation there would shrink under Home Rule.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.4] The Commons passed the Bill in January 1913 and the Lords rejected it on 30 January. The Commons passed it again in July 1913 and the Lords rejected it on 15 July. The Lords' rejections mattered less than usual, because the Parliament Act only allowed them to delay.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.5] The Commons passed it a third time in May 1914. Under the Parliament Act this meant it could become law without the Lords, so Home Rule seemed certain.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.b] Unionists answered the Bill with mass protest and then armed organisation. Nationalists formed their own volunteers in reply, and by 1914 both sides had guns.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.0] Edward Carson, a Dublin-born barrister, led Irish unionists, and James Craig organised the campaign in Ulster. His Dublin birth is a reminder that unionism was not only an Ulster belief.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.1] On Ulster Day, 28 September 1912, about 237,000 men signed the Ulster Covenant, a pledge to resist Home Rule. About 234,000 women signed a parallel Declaration.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.2] The Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) was formed in January 1913 to resist Home Rule by force, which meant that the unionists were now organised as an army.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.3] In March 1914 the Curragh incident occurred, when British cavalry officers said they would rather be dismissed than act against Ulster. This showed that the army could not be relied on to coerce the unionists.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.4] The UVF landed about 25,000 rifles at Larne, Co. Antrim, on the night of 24–25 April 1914 (some sources say 20,000). The government did not stop it.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.5] The Irish Volunteers were founded at the Rotunda in Dublin on 25 November 1913. Eoin MacNeill was the public leader, while the IRB, notably Bulmer Hobson, helped organise them from behind the scenes.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.6] On 26 July 1914 the Volunteers landed about 900 rifles at Howth, Co. Dublin, from Erskine Childers's yacht Asgard.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.7] That afternoon soldiers of the King's Own Scottish Borderers fired on a crowd at Bachelor's Walk in Dublin and killed three civilians. Nationalists contrasted this with the lack of action at Larne.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.b] By mid-1914 Home Rule was about to become law, but exclusion of Ulster was unresolved. Then the outbreak of war removed the issue from the agenda.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.0] In March 1914 Asquith proposed county option, under which each Ulster county could vote to be excluded for six years. Carson rejected it, and Redmond reluctantly accepted it as a concession.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.1] In June 1914 Redmond made the Irish Volunteers' committee admit 25 of his own nominees.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.2] An Amending Bill to provide for Ulster was introduced in the House of Lords in June 1914. The Lords changed it to exclude all nine counties of Ulster, and it never became law.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.3] The Buckingham Palace Conference, held on 21–24 July 1914, failed to agree a border for the excluded counties. The sticking point was Tyrone and Fermanagh. This is why no agreed settlement was reached before the war began.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.4] Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914. On 18 September 1914 the Home Rule Act (formally the Government of Ireland Act 1914) received royal assent, which made it law.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.5] A Suspensory Act, passed at the same time, delayed the operation of Home Rule until the war ended, with an undertaking that Ulster would get special treatment by an amending Act. So Home Rule was law but could not take effect.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.b] Redmond tied the Home Rule cause to Britain's war effort. The result was a split in the Volunteers and, over time, a loss of confidence in his party.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.0] On 3 August 1914 Redmond offered the Irish Volunteers in the House of Commons, to defend Ireland alongside the Ulster Volunteers.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.1] At Woodenbridge, Co. Wicklow, on 20 September 1914, Redmond urged the Volunteers to join the British army and fight in the war.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.2] The Volunteers split. About 170,000 (some sources say about 150,000) followed Redmond as the National Volunteers, while about 11,000 (some say 9,000) stayed with MacNeill as the Irish Volunteers.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.3] About 200,000 Irishmen served in the British forces, and Irish deaths are estimated between about 30,000 and about 50,000. Conscription (compulsory service) did not apply to Ireland in 1916.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.4] The 10th (Irish) Division fought at Suvla Bay, Gallipoli, in August 1915. The 16th (Irish) Division fought at Guillemont and Ginchy on the Somme in September 1916, and the nationalist MP Tom Kettle was killed at Ginchy.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.5] The 36th (Ulster) Division, drawn largely from the UVF, attacked at Thiepval on 1 July 1916, the first day of the Somme, and suffered over 5,000 casualties in two days.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.6] Farmers gained from high food prices and Belfast shipbuilding and engineering boomed. Prices rose faster than wages in the towns, which meant that poor townspeople suffered, especially in Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.7] Redmond's party lost ground because it had delivered only a suspended Home Rule Act. Separatists, who wanted a republic, grew angrier as the war dragged on, and the IRB saw the war as its opportunity.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.b] The Rising was planned by a secret group inside the IRB, which treated Britain's war as the chance to strike. German help was promised but badly co-ordinated.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.0] The IRB set up a Military Council in 1915. It had seven members: Patrick Pearse, Joseph Plunkett, Éamonn Ceannt, Tom Clarke, Seán MacDiarmada, James Connolly and Thomas MacDonagh.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.1] Connolly, who led the Irish Citizen Army and had been planning a rising of his own, was co-opted (brought in) in January 1916, so the plans of the IRB and the Citizen Army were joined.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.2] The funeral of the Fenian Jeremiah O'Donovan Rossa on 1 August 1915 was organised as a show of strength. Pearse's graveside oration gave him a platform and ended with the line 'Ireland unfree shall never be at peace'.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.3] Roger Casement went to Germany in autumn 1914 to seek arms and an Irish Brigade made up of Irish prisoners of war. Very few prisoners joined, so the brigade was a failure.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.4] The Castle Document, a forgery circulated in Dublin on 19 April 1916, claimed that the British planned to arrest the Volunteer leaders. It persuaded MacNeill to accept the plan for a rising.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.5] A German ship, the Aud, disguised as a Norwegian ship, carried about 20,000 rifles for Tralee Bay, Co. Kerry. The Royal Navy intercepted it on Good Friday, 21 April 1916, and its captain scuttled it in Cork Harbour on 22 April, so the arms were lost.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.6] Casement came ashore from a German submarine at Banna Strand, Co. Kerry, on 21 April and was arrested. That night three Volunteers drowned at Ballykissane pier on their way to meet him.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.7] MacNeill reversed when he learned of the loss of the Aud and issued a countermand (an order cancelling the Rising), published in the Sunday Independent on Easter Sunday, 23 April.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.8] The Military Council put the Rising back to noon on Easter Monday, 24 April, so only part of the Volunteers turned out, because many had obeyed the countermand.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.b] About 1,200 Volunteers and Citizen Army members turned out on the first day, took over strong points in Dublin and proclaimed a republic. They were outgunned and outnumbered, and the fighting was mostly in Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.0] On Monday 24 April the rebels seized the General Post Office (GPO) in O'Connell Street as their headquarters. Pearse was President of the Provisional Government and Commandant-General, and Connolly commanded the Dublin forces.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.1] Pearse read the Proclamation of the Irish Republic outside the GPO. It was addressed to 'Irishmen and Irishwomen' and guaranteed religious and civil liberty and equal rights and opportunities. It was signed by the seven members of the Military Council.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.2] The main garrisons were the GPO (Pearse, Connolly), Boland's Mill (de Valera), the South Dublin Union (Ceannt, with W.T. Cosgrave serving there) and Jacob's biscuit factory (MacDonagh). Others were St Stephen's Green and the College of Surgeons (Michael Mallin and Countess Markievicz) and the Four Courts (Edward Daly).
[hist-ire3-s1.7.3] The rebels did not take Dublin Castle, Trinity College or the docks, which stayed in British hands, so the British kept their centre of government.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.4] Women took part too, including those of the Citizen Army and Cumann na mBan (the women's republican organisation), and Markievicz served as an officer. Elizabeth O'Farrell carried the surrender order.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.5] Martial law (rule by the army) was proclaimed on 25 April, and Sir John Maxwell took command on 28 April. At Mount Street Bridge on 26 April a few Volunteers inflicted heavy casualties on the Sherwood Foresters.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.6] The gunboat Helga shelled Liberty Hall, and artillery and fire wrecked much of O'Connell Street.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.7] Outside Dublin, Thomas Ashe's Volunteers fought at Ashbourne, Co. Meath, on 28 April, Enniscorthy was held for several days, and Liam Mellows led a rising in Galway.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.b] Fire and shelling forced the rebels out of their headquarters within a week. The surrender ended the fighting but left a heavy toll, especially among civilians.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.0] The GPO was evacuated on Friday 28 April after fire, and the garrison retreated to Moore Street, where The O'Rahilly was killed. The fire made the building impossible to hold.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.1] Pearse surrendered to Brigadier-General Lowe on Saturday 29 April. The other garrisons surrendered on 30 April.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.2] About 485 people were killed: about 260 civilians, about 140 British soldiers and police, and about 80 rebels. Thousands were wounded.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.3] These figures are research estimates, which is why they are given as 'about'. The large number of civilian dead is one reason later historians judged the Rising harshly.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.b] The Rising was at first unpopular in Dublin. The speed and manner of the executions, together with mass arrests, turned opinion towards the rebels.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.0] As rebel prisoners were marched away, some Dubliners jeered them, and the IPP and most newspapers condemned the Rising. This shows how little public support the rebels had at first.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.1] About 3,500 people were arrested and about 1,800 were interned (held without trial) in Britain, many at Frongoch camp in north Wales.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.2] Ninety men were sentenced to death by courts-martial (military trials), and 15 were shot between 3 and 12 May 1916: 14 at Kilmainham Gaol and Thomas Kent at Cork. The other 75 sentences were commuted (reduced), including those of de Valera and Markievicz.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.3] The dates were: 3 May Pearse, Clarke and MacDonagh; 4 May Plunkett, Daly, Michael O'Hanrahan and Willie Pearse; 5 May John MacBride; 8 May Ceannt, Mallin, Seán Heuston and Con Colbert; 9 May Kent; and 12 May Connolly and MacDiarmada.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.4] Joseph Plunkett married Grace Gifford the night before his execution. Connolly, wounded in the fighting, was shot seated in a chair. The slow drip of executions over nine days turned sympathy towards the dead men.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.5] Maxwell was the military commander. Bishop O'Dwyer of Limerick refused to discipline priests and criticised the executions. Augustine Birrell resigned as Chief Secretary on 3 May, and Asquith visited Dublin in May.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.6] Lloyd George's talks in June and July 1916 offered Home Rule at once with temporary exclusion of six counties. They failed when Unionist ministers demanded permanent exclusion.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.7] Casement was tried for treason and hanged at Pentonville Prison on 3 August 1916. The British circulated extracts from his diaries to damage his reputation. The Frongoch prisoners were released at Christmas 1916, and a general amnesty followed in June 1917.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.b] Pearse was the chief ideologue of the Rising (the man whose ideas drove it) and its leading public figure. He is judged both as an educator and cultural figure and as an advocate of armed sacrifice.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.0] Pearse was born in Dublin on 10 November 1879 to an English father and an Irish mother. He joined the Gaelic League in 1896 and qualified as a barrister, though he hardly practised.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.1] He edited the League's paper An Claidheamh Soluis from 1903 and founded St Enda's, a bilingual school, in 1908. His essay The Murder Machine (1912) attacked the state education system.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.2] He joined the IRB in 1913 and became Director of Military Organisation of the Irish Volunteers. In 1915 he joined the Military Council, so the educator became a revolutionary who saw the war as an opportunity.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.3] In his graveside oration for O'Donovan Rossa on 1 August 1915 he said 'Ireland unfree shall never be at peace'. This speech remains his best-known statement of separatist nationalism.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.4] His 1915 writings praised war as cleansing and sanctifying. Critics later called this 'blood sacrifice', the idea that a willing sacrifice of life would renew the nation, but Pearse did not use the phrase as a slogan himself.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.5] In 1916 he was President of the Provisional Government and Commandant-General at the GPO, read the Proclamation and ordered the surrender on 29 April. He was executed at Kilmainham Gaol on 3 May 1916, aged 36, and his brother Willie was shot on 4 May.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.6] Contemporaries were divided: Redmond's supporters and many Dubliners condemned him in April 1916, while by autumn he was honoured as a martyr.
[hist-ire3-s1.10.7] Historians differ. Ruth Dudley Edwards (Patrick Pearse: The Triumph of Failure, 1977) treated him as a gifted but troubled figure, Conor Cruise O'Brien criticised the cult of sacrifice, and Joost Augusteijn (2010) stressed his development as an educator and a revolutionary.
[hist-ire3-s1.11.b] Markievicz was a participant in the Rising and its most prominent female combatant.
[hist-ire3-s1.11.0] Constance Gore-Booth was born in London in 1868 and brought up at Lissadell, Co. Sligo. She studied art at the Slade School in London and in Paris, and in 1900 married the Polish count Casimir Markievicz.
[hist-ire3-s1.11.1] She joined Inghinidhe na hÉireann (a women's nationalist society) and Sinn Féin in 1908. In 1909 she helped Bulmer Hobson found Fianna Éireann, a republican youth organisation.
[hist-ire3-s1.11.2] She joined the Irish Citizen Army and in the 1913 Lockout ran the soup kitchen at Liberty Hall, which fed families of locked-out workers.
[hist-ire3-s1.11.3] In the Rising she was second-in-command to Michael Mallin at St Stephen's Green and the College of Surgeons, and she surrendered with the garrison.
[hist-ire3-s1.11.4] At her court-martial on 4 May 1916 she was sentenced to death. Maxwell commuted it to penal servitude for life, citing her sex, and she was held in Aylesbury Prison until the amnesty of June 1917.
[hist-ire3-s1.11.5] Contemporaries admired her courage but also mocked her aristocratic background.
[hist-ire3-s1.11.6] Later historians stress her place as an early woman in revolutionary politics and her commitment to labour and women's causes. She died in 1927.
[hist-ire3-s1.12.b] Judgements of the Rising depend on whether it is measured by its military result, by the aims of the Proclamation, or by its political effects.
[hist-ire3-s1.12.0] Military failure: the rebels held no major target outside their garrisons, were contained within a week, and had few arms. Political success: the executions, internment and the Proclamation's republican claim gave the survivors a moral authority the Parliamentary Party could not match.
[hist-ire3-s1.12.1] Contemporary view: unionists and Redmond's party called it a German-backed rebellion, and the British called it the 'Sinn Féin rebellion', a misnomer since Sinn Féin did not organise it. The Catholic press, which at first condemned it, shifted after the executions.
[hist-ire3-s1.12.2] Later view: revisionist historians stressed its small support, secret planning and the fact that civilians were the largest group of dead. Other historians stressed the effect of the British reaction in changing opinion.
[hist-ire3-s1.12.3] Yeats's poem Easter, 1916 has the line 'All changed, changed utterly: A terrible beauty is born'. This sums up the sense that Irish politics had changed, though historians still ask how far Ireland really was changed.
[hist-ire3-s1.12.4] Home Rule was overtaken by events: the Act was suspended and never came into force, and partition came instead through the Government of Ireland Act of 1920.
[hist-ire3-s1.12.5] The Rising set up a debate over legitimacy (whether the rebels had the right to speak for the nation) that shaped later republican claims. Its fiftieth anniversary in 1966 and centenary in 2016 became public occasions.
