TARGET hist-eur1-s8: 8. Responses to industrial society: trade unionism, socialism, Chartism, mass politics and Peel
[hist-eur1-s8.0.b] Industrialisation created a new class of factory workers who had little power over their lives. Every movement in this subsection was an attempt to answer that problem, in one of three ways.
[hist-eur1-s8.0.0] Industrialisation created a large factory working class, crowded into fast-growing towns, with long hours, low pay, child labour and slum housing, and with no vote. This is why unions, socialists and Chartists all appeared when they did.
[hist-eur1-s8.0.1] Britain before 1832 was ruled by a Parliament chosen by a small, mostly propertied electorate. "Rotten boroughs" (tiny electorates controlled by a patron, Old Sarum being the best-known example) had MPs, while Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds and Sheffield had none.
[hist-eur1-s8.0.2] The first kind of response was practical self-help, such as trade unions (associations of workers who join together to bargain with employers) and co-operatives (businesses owned by their members).
[hist-eur1-s8.0.3] The second kind was new theories about how society should be organised: socialism, communism and anarchism.
[hist-eur1-s8.0.4] The third kind was political campaigning, either for the vote from below (Chartism) or for reform from above, carried out by governments (Peel). Essays often compare these three kinds.
[hist-eur1-s8.1.b] Workers' first attempts to organise were met with fear and repression. A trade union is an association of workers who combine to bargain over wages and conditions, and in this period the law treated that as a danger to the state.
[hist-eur1-s8.1.0] The Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800 (Pitt's government) banned workers from combining to raise wages or change conditions, out of fear of revolution after 1789.
[hist-eur1-s8.1.1] The Acts were repealed in 1824 after a campaign by Francis Place, a radical tailor, and Joseph Hume MP. A wave of strikes followed repeal. This shows that repeal released pent-up grievances.
[hist-eur1-s8.1.2] Because of the strikes, the Act of 1825 let unions meet and bargain over wages and hours but made "molestation", obstruction and intimidation offences, which restricted strike action.
[hist-eur1-s8.1.3] In 1834 six Dorset farm labourers, the Tolpuddle Martyrs, were led by George Loveless, a Methodist preacher. They were sentenced to seven years' transportation (forced exile to Australia) for swearing a secret oath, which the law treated as unlawful.
[hist-eur1-s8.1.4] A large London protest march in April 1834 and a public campaign followed, and the six were pardoned in 1836. This shows that public opinion could force the government to back down.
[hist-eur1-s8.1.5] The Grand National Consolidated Trades Union (GNCTU), founded early in 1834, tried to unite all trades in one union. Robert Owen was its leading figure, though he did not found it.
[hist-eur1-s8.1.6] The GNCTU collapsed later in 1834, because of lockouts (employers shutting workers out), the Tolpuddle case, disputes and lack of funds.
[hist-eur1-s8.2.b] After the failure of one big union for everyone, skilled workers built smaller, more cautious unions that lasted. These New Model unions set the pattern for the rest of the century.
[hist-eur1-s8.2.0] The Amalgamated Society of Engineers (1851) took skilled men only, charged a high subscription of a shilling a week and employed full-time officials, which made it stable.
[hist-eur1-s8.2.1] Its central fund paid benefits for sickness, unemployment, accident and funerals, and it was cautious about strikes.
[hist-eur1-s8.2.2] The carpenters copied the model in the Amalgamated Society of Carpenters and Joiners (1860, Robert Applegarth). The London leaders of these new amalgamated unions in the 1860s were known as "the Junta". This shows the ASE model was thought a success, so others copied it.
[hist-eur1-s8.2.3] Unions were mainly for skilled craftsmen, who could afford the subscriptions. Unskilled workers stayed largely unorganised until later in the century.
[hist-eur1-s8.2.4] The Trades Union Congress first met in Manchester in 1868. This gave unions a national voice, so they could lobby Parliament together.
[hist-eur1-s8.2.5] The Trade Union Act of 1871 made unions legal and protected their funds, but the Criminal Law Amendment Act of the same year restricted picketing (standing outside a workplace to persuade others to join a strike).
[hist-eur1-s8.3.b] Examiners reward answers on Europe that go beyond Britain. On the continent the law was generally harsher towards unions, and workers' political parties appeared in the German lands.
[hist-eur1-s8.3.0] In France the Le Chapelier Law of 1791 banned combinations of workers, so French unions began under a legal ban lasting well into the nineteenth century.
[hist-eur1-s8.3.1] Napoleon III granted the right to strike in 1864 and tolerated unions from 1868. Full legalisation came only in 1884, after this period.
[hist-eur1-s8.3.2] In the German lands Ferdinand Lassalle founded the General German Workers' Association at Leipzig in 1863, an early political party for workers.
[hist-eur1-s8.3.3] August Bebel and Wilhelm Liebknecht founded the Social Democratic Workers' Party at Eisenach in 1869, a separate party from Lassalle's.
[hist-eur1-s8.4.b] Socialism was an idea before it was a mass movement. Early reformers wanted to replace competition with co-operation, and the word was in use well before Marx wrote.
[hist-eur1-s8.4.0] Socialism means common or public ownership of the means of production (land, factories, mines) so that wealth is shared. The word came into use in Britain and France in the late 1820s and 1830s, used by Owen's followers and by Saint-Simon's.
[hist-eur1-s8.4.1] Henri de Saint-Simon (1760 to 1825) wanted the whole of society organised by scientists, bankers and industrialists for the good of the poorest and most numerous class. His followers, the Saint-Simonians, were active in the 1830s.
[hist-eur1-s8.4.2] Charles Fourier (1772 to 1837) planned co-operative communities called phalanxes, of about 1,600 people living in a building called a phalanstère and sharing work they found attractive. This is why he, not Saint-Simon, is the model-community planner.
[hist-eur1-s8.4.3] Robert Owen, manager of New Lanark from 1800, set up New Harmony in Indiana in 1825, which failed within a few years. He is counted among the utopian socialists, those who relied on model communities.
[hist-eur1-s8.4.4] Louis Blanc's The Organisation of Labour (1839) urged state-funded "social workshops" run by workers, and "the right to work". He sat in the Provisional Government in February 1848. This is why his ideas lay behind the 1848 demand for the right to work.
[hist-eur1-s8.4.5] Pierre-Joseph Proudhon's What is Property? (1840) declared "Property is theft!" He was the first writer to call himself an anarchist, and he influenced Bakunin.
[hist-eur1-s8.5.b] By 1847 socialist ideas had reached German exile workers in London. Their League turned to Marx and Engels, who also gave the earlier socialists the label "utopian".
[hist-eur1-s8.5.0] The Communist League was formed in London in 1847, growing out of the League of the Just, a society of German exile workers.
[hist-eur1-s8.5.1] At its congress late in 1847 the League commissioned Marx and Engels to write its programme, the Communist Manifesto. This is why the Manifesto is tied to this League.
[hist-eur1-s8.5.2] "Utopian" and "scientific" socialism are terms Engels used in his pamphlet Socialism: Utopian and Scientific (1880). Marx and Engels called the early socialists utopian because they relied on model communities and appeals to the rich, and called their own theory scientific socialism.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.b] Karl Marx was the most influential theorist of socialism. He was an exile and a writer rather than a leader of crowds, and his followers became a mass movement only after his death.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.0] Marx was born on 5 May 1818 in Trier, in the Prussian Rhineland. His father, a lawyer, had converted from Judaism to Protestant Christianity.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.1] He studied law and then philosophy at Bonn and Berlin and took a doctorate at Jena in 1841. He was influenced by Hegel's dialectic (the idea that change comes through the conflict of opposites), which later shaped his view of history.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.2] He edited the Rheinische Zeitung in Cologne in 1842. The government suppressed it in 1843, so he moved to Paris (1843 to 1845), where he met Engels in 1844. He was expelled from Paris in 1845 and lived in Brussels from 1845 to 1848.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.3] Friedrich Engels (1820 to 1895) was the son of a textile manufacturer from Barmen and worked in the family firm in Manchester, where he wrote The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845). He co-wrote the Manifesto and supported Marx financially for decades.
[hist-eur1-s8.6.4] In 1848 and 1849 Marx was a participant in the revolutions. He returned to Germany and edited the Neue Rheinische Zeitung in Cologne from June 1848 to May 1849, and was expelled from Prussian territory in May 1849.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.b] Marx spent his last decades in London, little known at first and then notorious. Judgements of him have changed as his ideas spread and the predictions were tested.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.0] His other writings include The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852), on Louis-Napoleon's coup, and The Civil War in France (1871), defending the Paris Commune.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.1] He settled in London in August 1849 and lived there until his death on 14 March 1883, first in Soho and later in north London, often in poverty. Three of his children died young. He studied in the British Museum reading room and is buried in Highgate Cemetery.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.2] Contemporaries knew little of him in the 1850s and 1860s. Prussian police agents watched the London exiles, and the Cologne Communist Trial of 1852 convicted members of the Communist League. This is why the public did not fear him until later.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.3] After he defended the Paris Commune in 1871, the press called him a leader of the Commune's supporters, and he became famous and feared. He was also quarrelsome with rivals such as Bakunin and Lassalle.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.4] Historians judge him the most influential socialist thinker, because his ideas shaped later socialist parties and twentieth-century communist states. His analysis of class and economics also influenced history, sociology and economics.
[hist-eur1-s8.7.5] His prediction that revolution would come first in the most advanced industrial countries was not fulfilled, and workers' living standards rose.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.b] Marx's importance rests on a theory that explained history by class struggle and promised that capitalism would be overthrown. Each of his main works and organisations belongs to a different date.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.0] The Communist Manifesto (published in London in German in February 1848, written with Engels for the Communist League) opens "A spectre is haunting Europe – the spectre of communism" and declares "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."
[hist-eur1-s8.8.1] It divides society into the bourgeoisie (the owners of factories and capital) and the proletariat (wage workers), and argues that capitalism would produce its own gravediggers. It ends with the call "Working men of all countries, unite!"
[hist-eur1-s8.8.2] The Manifesto did not cause the 1848 revolutions. It appeared just before them and had little immediate influence on them. This is why historians do not treat it as a cause of 1848.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.3] Historical materialism holds that the economic base, meaning who owns the means of production, shapes the political and ideological superstructure of law, religion and ideas. Marx wrote that religion is "the opium of the people" (1844).
[hist-eur1-s8.8.4] Marx saw history in stages: ancient slavery, feudalism, capitalism, then socialism or communism. Each stage is ended by class struggle, so capitalism would fall to a proletarian revolution.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.5] After the revolution would come a workers' state, his "dictatorship of the proletariat" (a phrase from 1850), and then a classless communist society in which the state would wither away.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.6] Das Kapital, volume 1 (Hamburg, 1867), set out the theory of surplus value: workers produce more value than they are paid in wages, and the capitalist keeps the difference as profit, which Marx called the unpaid part of labour. Engels edited volumes 2 (1885) and 3 (1894) from Marx's manuscripts.
[hist-eur1-s8.8.7] The International Workingmen's Association, the First International, was founded at St Martin's Hall, London, on 28 September 1864. Marx wrote its Inaugural Address and its rules and led its General Council. It linked British trade unionists with French and German socialists. This is why it brought different national movements together.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.b] Higher essays set Marx beside other thinkers of the age. The comparison works best when you say what each thought drives change and how their ideas were received.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.0] Marx and Metternich held opposite views of society. Marx saw conflict between classes and believed change was inevitable, while Metternich wanted order and hierarchy and aimed to prevent change.
[hist-eur1-s8.9.1] Marx and Darwin both offered a theory of change through struggle over long periods of time. Marx's struggle was between classes, which he expected to end in revolution.
[hist-eur1-s8.10.b] Bakunin was a Russian revolutionary and the most famous anarchist of the period, a man of action who spent years in prison. His rivalry with Marx split the First International.
[hist-eur1-s8.10.0] Mikhail Bakunin was born on 30 May 1814 on the Premukhino estate, Tver province, into a Russian noble family. He served as an artillery officer but resigned his commission in 1835, studied philosophy in Moscow and Berlin (1840), and became a revolutionary only in the 1840s.
[hist-eur1-s8.10.1] In 1848 and 1849 he was a participant in the revolutions: he attended the Slav Congress in Prague in June 1848 and joined the Dresden rising of May 1849.
[hist-eur1-s8.10.2] He was sentenced to death in Saxony (1850) and again in Austria (1851), and each sentence was commuted. He was handed over to Russia in 1851. Each sentence was commuted, so he survived to continue his career.
[hist-eur1-s8.10.3] He was held in the Peter and Paul Fortress in St Petersburg and then in Schlüsselburg, and was exiled to Siberia in 1857. In 1861 he escaped by way of Japan and the United States, reaching London at the end of that year.
[hist-eur1-s8.10.4] In 1873 he wrote Statism and Anarchy. He died on 1 July 1876 in Bern, Switzerland.
[hist-eur1-s8.10.5] Contemporary governments saw him as a dangerous conspirator, while some radicals admired him as a heroic rebel and champion of the oppressed. This is why he was watched and imprisoned.
[hist-eur1-s8.10.6] Historians see him as the founder of the anarchist movement, though a poor organiser and less influential than Marx on mass labour parties. His anarchist influence was strongest in Spain, Italy and the Jura region of Switzerland, where the Jura Federation was based.
[hist-eur1-s8.11.b] Bakunin and Marx both wanted to end capitalism but disagreed about what should replace it. Their quarrel broke the International, the first organisation to bring socialists of different countries together.
[hist-eur1-s8.11.0] Bakunin's anarchism meant the abolition of the state, organised religion and all coercive authority, so that people would live without rulers. He wrote in 1842: "The passion for destruction is a creative passion."
[hist-eur1-s8.11.1] He expected revolution to come from spontaneous risings of peasants and the poor as well as workers, not only an industrial proletariat. He favoured secret revolutionary societies.
[hist-eur1-s8.11.2] Bakunin joined the First International in 1868. He argued that Marx's workers' state would become a new tyranny of a ruling minority, while Marx wanted workers' parties to win political power.
[hist-eur1-s8.11.3] At the Hague Congress in September 1872 Bakunin and his ally James Guillaume were expelled. The General Council moved to New York, and the International faded and was dissolved in 1876, at Philadelphia.
[hist-eur1-s8.12.b] The most common Higher pairing on this subsection is two or three of Marx, Bakunin and O'Connor. Each comparison below is a pair of contrasting points, which you can turn into a judgement.
[hist-eur1-s8.12.0] On the state, Marx wanted workers to win power and use a workers' state, while Bakunin wanted no state at all.
[hist-eur1-s8.12.1] On who would make revolution, Marx looked to organised industrial workers, while Bakunin looked to all the oppressed, including peasants.
[hist-eur1-s8.12.2] On method, Marx stressed theory and patient organisation, while Bakunin stressed spontaneous insurrection.
[hist-eur1-s8.12.3] In their lives, Marx was a London theorist, while Bakunin was a participant on the barricades.
[hist-eur1-s8.12.4] On aims, O'Connor sought the vote and land within the British state, while Marx sought revolution and the end of capitalism.
[hist-eur1-s8.12.5] On method, O'Connor used mass agitation, while Marx used theory and an international organisation.
[hist-eur1-s8.12.6] Both O'Connor and Marx failed to achieve their aims in 1848, but both had longer-term influence.
[hist-eur1-s8.13.b] Democracy means government by the people through elected representatives chosen by all, here all adult men. The franchise (the right to vote) widened slowly in Britain and faster in some continental states, usually granted from above.
[hist-eur1-s8.13.0] The Reform Act of 1832, passed by Earl Grey's Whig government after a long crisis, gave the borough vote to men who owned or occupied property worth £10 a year. In the counties the vote went to forty-shilling freeholders plus some leaseholders and tenants.
[hist-eur1-s8.13.1] It took both MPs from 56 boroughs and one from 30 others, and gave seats to industrial towns such as Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds and Sheffield.
[hist-eur1-s8.13.2] Even so, only about one adult man in five in England and Wales could vote, and almost no workers, so the Act disappointed the working class. Estimates vary, so give no exact figure. This is why working men turned to Chartism.
[hist-eur1-s8.13.3] The Second Reform Act of 1867 (Derby and Disraeli) gave the vote to many urban working men, and the Ballot Act of 1872 introduced the secret ballot. Almost all men over 21 (and some women over 30) got the vote in the Representation of the People Act of 1918.
[hist-eur1-s8.13.4] In France the Provisional Government decreed universal male suffrage in March 1848, raising the electorate from fewer than 250,000 to about nine million. Louis-Napoleon kept it and used plebiscites (direct votes on his proposals).
[hist-eur1-s8.13.5] The North German Confederation of 1867 elected its Reichstag by universal male suffrage, for men over 25 and by secret ballot. The German Empire kept this in 1871, and Bismarck hoped conservative rural voters would outvote the liberals.
[hist-eur1-s8.14.b] Not every campaign of the period was a working-class one. The Anti-Corn Law League was a middle-class pressure group that showed how organised public opinion could change policy, and it won where Chartism lost.
[hist-eur1-s8.14.0] The League was founded in Manchester in 1838 by Richard Cobden and John Bright, to end the Corn Laws (duties on imported grain that kept bread dear).
[hist-eur1-s8.14.1] It used lecturers, pamphlets, a national fund and mass meetings, a model of how a pressure group could organise opinion. This is why it could reach many voters.
[hist-eur1-s8.14.2] It succeeded in 1846 when the Corn Laws were repealed, whereas the working-class Chartists failed, partly because much middle-class support went to the League.
[hist-eur1-s8.15.b] Chartism was the first national working-class political movement in Britain. It grew from hardship and from the feeling that the political system had shut workers out.
[hist-eur1-s8.15.0] Chartism grew from disappointment with the 1832 Act, which gave workers no vote, and anger at the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 and its workhouses.
[hist-eur1-s8.15.1] Other causes were the trade depression from 1837 to the early 1840s, which brought unemployment (the 1840s are called the "Hungry Forties"), the decline of handloom weavers, and the failure of trade unionism after 1834.
[hist-eur1-s8.15.2] The London Working Men's Association was formed in 1836 by William Lovett, a cabinet-maker, with Henry Hetherington also a founder. The People's Charter, published in May 1838, was drafted by Lovett with help from Francis Place.
[hist-eur1-s8.15.3] The six points were universal male suffrage, the secret ballot, annual parliaments, equal electoral districts, abolition of the property qualification for MPs (so poor men could stand), and payment of MPs.
[hist-eur1-s8.15.4] The first National Convention met in London in February 1839 and later moved to Birmingham.
[hist-eur1-s8.15.5] The National Charter Association, founded in Manchester in July 1840, was in effect the first working-class political party, with local branches and paid-up members. This is why it counts as a party, not just a campaign.
[hist-eur1-s8.15.6] The Northern Star, O'Connor's newspaper, was the movement's voice and reached a wide readership.
[hist-eur1-s8.16.b] Chartists tried petitions first and then stronger action, and each campaign was beaten. The three petitions and the three famous events are easily confused, so learn them with their dates.
[hist-eur1-s8.16.0] The first petition (1839) had about 1.3 million signatures and was rejected in July 1839. The second (1842) had over three million, the largest genuine total; Thomas Duncombe MP presented it and it was rejected in May 1842.
[hist-eur1-s8.16.1] The 1848 petition claimed about 5.7 million signatures, but a Commons committee counted under two million, including false names such as "Victoria Rex" and "the Duke of Wellington". Each petition was rejected by the Commons.
[hist-eur1-s8.16.2] At Newport, Monmouthshire, on 4 November 1839, John Frost, a former mayor of Newport, led armed men into the town with Zephaniah Williams and William Jones. Soldiers fired from the Westgate Hotel and about twenty Chartists were killed.
[hist-eur1-s8.16.3] Frost and two others were sentenced to death, but the sentences were commuted to transportation for life to Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania). So the government spared them, but removed them from Britain.
[hist-eur1-s8.16.4] The Plug Plot strikes of August 1842 spread through Lancashire, Yorkshire, the Potteries and the Midlands. Strikers pulled the plugs from factory boilers to stop work, and mass arrests followed.
[hist-eur1-s8.16.5] On 10 April 1848 a mass meeting on Kennington Common, south London, was to march the petition to Parliament. The government banned the procession, enrolled tens of thousands of special constables, and the Duke of Wellington organised the military defence of London.
[hist-eur1-s8.16.6] O'Connor persuaded the crowd to disperse, and the petition went to Parliament in cabs. The forged signatures then brought ridicule on the movement.
[hist-eur1-s8.17.b] The Land Plan was a scheme to take workers out of the factories and back on the land. It was also a way to win the vote, because land ownership carried a vote in the counties.
[hist-eur1-s8.17.0] The Chartist Co-operative Land Company of 1845 (later the National Land Company) sold shares to members and bought estates. Members then drew lots for smallholdings. This meant workers could escape the factories and, because of the county vote, gain political rights.
[hist-eur1-s8.17.1] Estates included O'Connorville at Heronsgate, Hertfordshire, and Charterville at Minster Lovell, Oxfordshire. The plan also aimed to give smallholders the county vote.
[hist-eur1-s8.17.2] A select committee of 1848 found it legally and financially unsound, and an Act of Parliament wound it up in 1851.
[hist-eur1-s8.18.b] Chartism was divided about methods and faced a determined government. It rose in bad times and faded in good ones, which was fatal to a movement that depended on hardship.
[hist-eur1-s8.18.0] Moral force Chartists such as William Lovett relied on education and persuasion. Physical force Chartists such as George Julian Harney (and James Bronterre O'Brien at times) were ready to threaten or use force.
[hist-eur1-s8.18.1] O'Connor's speeches threatened force while he avoided armed risings. The division in methods weakened the movement.
[hist-eur1-s8.18.2] Government repression (arrests, troops, and the new railways and telegraph, which moved forces quickly) wore the movement down, and Parliament refused to listen. This is why physical force never had a chance of succeeding.
[hist-eur1-s8.18.3] Chartism had little middle-class support, which went to the Anti-Corn Law League. It rose in slumps and faded in good times, and the prosperity of the 1850s ended it.
[hist-eur1-s8.18.4] The 1848 fiasco at Kennington damaged it. Ernest Jones led the later Chartists in the 1850s, and the last Chartist convention met in 1858.
[hist-eur1-s8.18.5] Its achievement was to be the first national working-class political movement, which is why historians value it despite its failure.
[hist-eur1-s8.18.6] Five of the six points later became law: the property qualification was abolished in 1858, the secret ballot came in 1872, payment of MPs in 1911 and manhood suffrage in 1918, and districts became more equal. Only annual parliaments never came.
[hist-eur1-s8.19.b] O'Connor was the most prominent Chartist leader of the 1840s and the owner of the movement's newspaper. His energy gave Chartism a national voice but his quarrels divided it.
[hist-eur1-s8.19.0] Feargus O'Connor was born in Co. Cork in 1796 (some older sources give 1794). He came from a Protestant landed family with a radical tradition, being the nephew of the United Irishman Arthur O'Connor, and was called to the Irish bar.
[hist-eur1-s8.19.1] He was elected MP for Co. Cork in December 1832 as a Repealer allied with Daniel O'Connell, and was unseated on petition in 1835 because he lacked the property qualification. He quarrelled with O'Connell and moved into English radical politics.
[hist-eur1-s8.19.2] He founded the Northern Star in Leeds in November 1837. It was the most widely read radical newspaper in Britain, often read aloud in pubs and workplaces, and sold about 50,000 copies a week at its peak in 1839 (estimates vary). It moved to London in 1844.
[hist-eur1-s8.19.3] He was a participant in the Chartist campaigns of 1839, 1842 and 1848, toured the industrial North as a speaker and dominated the National Charter Association. He was sentenced to 18 months for seditious libel in 1840, held in York Castle and released in August 1841.
[hist-eur1-s8.19.4] The Land Plan was his scheme, reflecting his belief in returning workers to the land. In 1847 he was elected MP for Nottingham, the only Chartist elected to Parliament, and in 1848 he presented the petition to the Commons.
[hist-eur1-s8.19.5] After Kennington his authority waned. He had a breakdown in 1852, was confined in an asylum in Chiswick, and died on 30 August 1855. He was buried at Kensal Green, London, with a very large funeral procession.
[hist-eur1-s8.20.b] O'Connor divided opinion in his own day and divides historians still. The verdict depends on whether you blame him for Chartism's failure or credit him with holding it together.
[hist-eur1-s8.20.0] Contemporaries were split. To many workers he was a hero, "the Lion of Freedom", a Chartist song and popular name for him, but to Lovett, the moral-force men, middle-class reformers and the government he was a vain, reckless demagogue.
[hist-eur1-s8.20.1] Older accounts, such as Mark Hovell's The Chartist Movement (1918), drew on Lovett's account and blamed him for Chartism's failure.
[hist-eur1-s8.20.2] Dorothy Thompson (The Chartists, 1984) and James Epstein (The Lion of Freedom, 1982) see a gifted speaker and organiser who held a national movement together and spoke for the rank and file.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.b] Robert Peel was a reforming statesman who worked from inside government, and Norman Gash rates him one of the greatest statesmen of the century. He was a Conservative who believed in reforming real abuses so as to preserve the established order.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.0] Peel was born on 5 February 1788 near Bury, Lancashire, the son of Sir Robert Peel, 1st Baronet, a cotton manufacturer and MP. He went to Harrow and Christ Church, Oxford, and became an MP in 1809.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.1] He was Chief Secretary for Ireland from 1812 to 1818, setting up the Peace Preservation Force in 1814 and earning O'Connell's nickname "Orange Peel". He chaired the 1819 currency committee, after which Britain returned to gold.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.2] As Home Secretary (1822 to 1827 and 1828 to 1830) he reformed the criminal law, abolishing the death penalty for many offences, and the Gaols Act of 1823 reformed prisons. This shows he was a reformer in law before he became Prime Minister.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.3] The Metropolitan Police Act of 1829 created a professional, uniformed, unarmed force for London, with headquarters at Scotland Yard. Its first commissioners were Charles Rowan and Richard Mayne, and its first patrols began in September 1829.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.4] Its officers wore blue uniforms and top hats and carried truncheons, not firearms, and were nicknamed "Peelers" and "Bobbies". The City of London kept its own force. The Metropolitan Police became a model for forces across Britain, though Peel did not found a national police force.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.5] In 1829 Peel and the Duke of Wellington conceded Catholic emancipation after O'Connell won the Clare by-election in 1828, to avoid conflict in Ireland. It angered many Tories, and Peel resigned his Oxford University seat over it.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.6] The Tamworth Manifesto of December 1834, an address to his constituents at Tamworth, accepted the Reform Act as "a final and irrevocable settlement" and promised careful reform of real abuses while preserving established institutions. It is seen as the founding statement of the Conservative Party.
[hist-eur1-s8.21.7] He was Prime Minister from December 1834 to April 1835 and again from August 1841 to June 1846.
[hist-eur1-s8.22.b] As Prime Minister Peel tried to make the state efficient and trade free, while keeping private property and the existing social order. His budgets and Acts of 1842 to 1844 are the core of his record.
[hist-eur1-s8.22.0] Laissez-faire is the belief that the state should leave trade, prices, wages and hours to the free market. Peel's free trade applied it, while the Factory Acts showed its limits.
[hist-eur1-s8.22.1] He reintroduced income tax in 1842, at 7d in the pound on incomes over £150, for three years at first and then renewed. It let him cut or abolish duties on hundreds of imports in the budgets of 1842 and 1845, and trade and revenue grew.
[hist-eur1-s8.22.2] The Bank Charter Act of 1844 gradually concentrated the issue of banknotes in the Bank of England, so that note issue beyond £14 million had to be backed by gold.
[hist-eur1-s8.22.3] Other banks' note issues were frozen and phased out. This was to keep banknotes trustworthy, so confidence in the currency was protected.
[hist-eur1-s8.22.4] The Mines Act of 1842, Lord Ashley's measure, forbade women, girls and boys under ten to work underground.
[hist-eur1-s8.22.5] The Factory Act of 1844 set the minimum age at 8, limited children aged 8 to 13 to six and a half hours a day and women and young persons to twelve hours, and required machinery to be fenced.
[hist-eur1-s8.22.6] The Railway Regulation Act of 1844 (Gladstone, at the Board of Trade) required at least one train a day each way at a penny a mile, the cheap "parliamentary trains".
[hist-eur1-s8.22.7] Peel opposed the Ten Hours Bill, which passed in 1847 under Russell's government after his fall.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.b] Repealing the Corn Laws was Peel's greatest act and the end of his career as Conservative leader. It brought lower food prices but split his party.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.0] Peel was influenced by free-trade arguments and by the Anti-Corn Law League, whose leaders Cobden and Bright had campaigned for repeal. The urgent cause was the potato blight in Ireland from 1845, and Peel bought American maize for Ireland.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.1] The Corn Importation Act received royal assent in June 1846. It passed with Whig votes, while about two-thirds of Conservative MPs voted against, and duties were reduced to a nominal one shilling a quarter by 1849, not ended overnight.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.2] Peel was defeated on an Irish Coercion Bill on 25 June 1846 and resigned in June 1846, on the same night that the Corn Laws were repealed.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.3] The Conservatives split into protectionists, led by Lord Derby (then Lord Stanley) and Benjamin Disraeli, and Peelites, including William Gladstone, who joined the Liberals by 1859. The Conservatives were long out of majority power.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.4] Peel died after being thrown from his horse on Constitution Hill, London, on 29 June 1850, and died on 2 July.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.5] Contemporaries differed. Disraeli and the protectionists called him a traitor to his party, while the middle class and many workers praised cheap bread and sound finance.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.6] Historians such as Norman Gash (Mr Secretary Peel, 1961, and Sir Robert Peel, 1972) rate him one of the greatest statesmen of the century, an administrator who put the national interest above party.
[hist-eur1-s8.23.7] Critics note that he twice split his party (1829, 1846), so his career left divisions, and that he resisted a wider franchise and the Ten Hours Act.
[hist-eur1-s8.24.b] This is the most frequent Higher question on the subsection. Owen tried to change society from outside Parliament, Peel from inside government, and the best answers weigh evidence on both sides.
[hist-eur1-s8.24.0] Both were sons of the textile industry: Owen (born 1771 in Newtown, Wales, died 1858) a self-made Welsh mill manager, and Peel the son of a cotton millionaire. This shows that industrial wealth produced reformers of different kinds.
[hist-eur1-s8.24.1] In method, Owen worked by example, model communities, education and co-operation outside Parliament, while Peel reformed the state from within through legislation and administration.
[hist-eur1-s8.24.2] In aims, Owen wanted to replace competition with co-operation and common ownership. Peel wanted to preserve property, Church and Crown by making the state efficient and trade free.
[hist-eur1-s8.24.3] There was a link: Owen's campaign helped bring about the Cotton Mills and Factories Act of 1819, introduced by Sir Robert Peel the elder. It banned children under nine from cotton mills and limited hours for those under sixteen, but had no inspectors and was poorly enforced.
[hist-eur1-s8.24.4] Peel's changes (police, tax, free trade, the Bank Act, Factory and Mines Acts) affected the whole country and lasted, and much of the middle class accepted his policies as sound.
[hist-eur1-s8.24.5] Owen's New Lanark worked but was rarely copied, and most of the political establishment rejected his ideas. New Harmony and the GNCTU failed, but his ideas inspired the co-operative movement (the Rochdale Equitable Pioneers' Society, 1844) and factory reform.
[hist-eur1-s8.24.6] Most answers judge Peel the more effective in practice and Owen the more original. This is why the verdict usually favours Peel on effect and Owen on originality.
[hist-eur1-s8.25.b] Judge the responses by what they achieved at the time and what they achieved later. The paired points below give the evidence for an achievement-against-limits answer.
[hist-eur1-s8.25.0] Trade unions: they were legal after 1824, recognised in 1871, and the New Model unions after 1851 gave skilled workers stable organisation. But Tolpuddle showed repression, the GNCTU collapsed and the unskilled were excluded.
[hist-eur1-s8.25.1] Socialism: it produced a new and lasting body of ideas and an international organisation in 1864. But support was tiny before 1871, the movement was divided between Marx and Bakunin, and no revolution came.
[hist-eur1-s8.25.2] Chartism: it was a mass movement that kept democracy on the agenda, and most of its points were achieved later. But every petition failed, its leaders were divided, and it was gone by the late 1850s.
[hist-eur1-s8.25.3] Mass politics: manhood suffrage came in France in 1848 and in the North German Confederation in 1867, while Britain moved slowly in 1832 and 1867. The vote was widened more often from above than won from below.
[hist-eur1-s8.25.4] Peel: he made lasting reforms from above in the police, finance, free trade and factory law. But he did not extend the vote, and he left a divided party.
[hist-eur1-s8.25.5] Overall, the responses included unions, socialist theory and Chartist agitation, yet the main political gains came from state reform led by Peel, while working-class movements won their goals only much later.

CANDIDATES hist-eur1-s7: 7. The changing economy and society: agriculture, industrialisation, urban growth and education
[hist-eur1-s7.0.b] Between 1815 and 1871 Europe was changing from a world of farmers and craft workers to one of factories and towns. Britain led the way, so it helps to know what the change was and why it began there.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.0] Industrialisation, often called the 'Industrial Revolution', was the shift from hand production at home or in small workshops (the domestic system, where families worked for merchants in their own houses) to machine production in factories powered first by water and then by steam.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.1] The British industrial revolution began in the later eighteenth century in cotton textiles, coal and iron, which is why Britain is described as the first industrial nation.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.2] Britain industrialised first because it had coal and iron ore close together, capital (money to invest) from trade and farming, and overseas and colonial markets to sell to.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.3] It also had a large merchant fleet and navy, rivers, canals and ports for moving goods, and political stability, so investors could trust that their factories would not be seized or wrecked by war.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.4] Britain's population roughly doubled between 1801 and 1851 (the first census was in 1801). This gave industry both workers to hire and customers to buy its goods.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.5] A growing population was fed because farming had improved, which is why changes in agriculture and industry belong together in this topic.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.6] Before 1815 most Europeans lived on the land and worked in farming. Western Europe was ahead of central and eastern Europe in both farming and industry, so change was uneven across the continent.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.b] Britain's farming was transformed from the eighteenth century onwards. The first big change was in how land was held, because farmers could not improve land that was shared and scattered.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.0] Enclosure replaced open fields and common land with compact, hedged farms. In the open-field system villagers farmed scattered strips and shared common land, which made it hard for anyone to try new methods.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.1] Most enclosure was done by private Acts of Parliament. There were thousands of them, and most were passed between 1750 and 1850.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.2] The General Enclosure Acts of 1801 and 1845 simplified the procedure, so landowners no longer needed a separate Act for every village.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.3] The result was larger, more efficient farms and higher output, because owners could drain land, choose crops and breed animals without interference from neighbours.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.4] The cost fell on the poor. Cottagers and labourers (people with a cottage and a few rights on the common but little or no land) lost their common rights and their access to land.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.5] Without land of their own, many became wage labourers, moved to the towns or emigrated, which fed the growth of the industrial towns.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.b] Enclosed farms could be run more intelligently. New crop rotations and better breeding allowed farmers to feed more people and more animals from the same land.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.0] The Norfolk four-course rotation grew wheat, turnips, barley and clover in turn. It ended the fallow year (a year when land was left unplanted to recover), so every field produced something every year.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.1] Turnips and clover fed livestock through the winter, so farmers could keep more animals and did not have to slaughter many in autumn. More animals also meant more manure to fertilise the fields.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.2] The rotation was popularised in the eighteenth century, and Charles 'Turnip' Townshend is associated with it.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.3] As background from before 1815, Jethro Tull's seed drill (early 1700s) sowed seed in rows at a regular depth, reducing the waste of scattering seed by hand.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.4] Also as background, Robert Bakewell in Leicestershire used selective breeding (choosing the best animals as parents) to improve sheep and cattle, breeding them for meat.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.b] From the 1840s science and machines began to raise yields and cut the labour needed on farms. This helped output, but it also left farm workers more insecure.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.0] Guano (dried seabird droppings, rich in nutrients) was imported from Peru as fertiliser from the 1840s, so farmers could restore soil without leaving it fallow.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.1] John Bennet Lawes patented superphosphate fertiliser in 1842 and founded the Rothamsted experiments in Hertfordshire, which tested crops and fertilisers scientifically.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.2] Justus von Liebig's book on chemistry applied to agriculture (1840) showed that plants take nutrients from the soil, and field drainage of wet land also raised yields from the 1840s.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.3] The threshing machine, which separates grain from straw, reduced the need for hand labour at harvest. Reapers followed, such as the American McCormick reaper shown at the Great Exhibition in 1851.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.4] Steam power came onto farms later in the period, so machines could do work once done by hand.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.5] Anger over threshing machines, which took away winter work, fed the Swing Riots of 1830 in southern and eastern England, when farm labourers broke the machines.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.6] Life for agricultural workers was harsh: low wages, insecure seasonal work, the loss of common rights and poor cottages. Exams ask about both farm and factory workers.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.b] Grain was Britain's most important crop, and landowners wanted its price kept high. The Corn Laws were their protection.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.0] The Corn Law of 1815 banned imports of foreign wheat until the home price was high. This protected British grain growers by keeping out cheap foreign grain.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.1] The same law kept bread dear for townspeople and factory workers, which is why it became a target of free-trade critics.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.2] The Corn Laws were repealed in 1846.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.b] Farming on the continent changed more slowly than in Britain, and the pattern of landholding depended on what each state had done to the old system of lords and peasants.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.0] In France the Revolution of 1789 abolished feudal dues (payments peasants owed to their lords). By 1815 peasants owned much of the land in small farms.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.1] French farms stayed small and methods were slow to change. Many peasants stayed on the land, which slowed migration into industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.2] In Prussia the Stein–Hardenberg reforms began with the October Edict of 1807 (Stein), which abolished hereditary serfdom (peasants bound to the land and owing service to a lord), in effect from 1810.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.3] Hardenberg's Regulation Edict of 1811 let peasants become owners of their land, but only by surrendering part of it, often a third to a half, to the lord. This is why the nobility kept large estates.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.4] The Junker estates (the big estates of the Prussian landowning nobility) of the east grew large and were modernised. They produced grain for export and for the growing cities.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.5] New crops spread on the continent: the potato became the staple (main everyday food) of the poor, and sugar beet was grown in France and the German lands.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.b] The poor depended on a few crops, so when those failed the result was hunger across much of Europe. The crisis also pushed people to leave.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.0] The potato blight (a disease that rots the crop) struck from 1845, and grain harvests were poor in 1846 and 1847. Food prices rose and there was hunger and food riots across western and central Europe.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.1] In Britain the 1840s are often called the 'Hungry Forties'.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.2] The hunger came together with a commercial and industrial depression in 1847, which helped create the conditions for the revolutions of 1848.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.3] Emigration from the German lands to the United States grew strongly in the late 1840s and 1850s, as rural people and artisans (skilled craft workers) sought land and work abroad.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.4] Ireland also lost many people. In the Famine of 1845–49 about a million died and about a million emigrated.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.b] Three industries led the first industrial revolution: coal, iron and cotton textiles, with the factory and steam power at the centre. Steam and cotton show how invention changed where and how people worked.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.0] James Watt improved the steam engine, patenting a separate condenser in 1769 and adding rotary motion (turning a wheel) from the 1780s. His partner was Matthew Boulton of Birmingham.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.1] Steam was used to pump water out of coal mines and to drive machinery in factories. Because engines did not need a river, factories no longer had to be built beside fast water.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.2] Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769) and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779) mechanised spinning, the making of thread from fibre.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.3] Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785) spread weaving into factories as well. Hand-loom weavers, who worked at home, declined as a result.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.4] Raw cotton for the Lancashire mills was imported in large quantities, mostly from the southern United States.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.5] Britain became the leading exporter of cotton goods, which is why Lancashire was the centre of the industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.b] Machines, rails and bridges all needed iron, and later steel. Britain's lead in metals showed in 1851, when it put its industry on show.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.0] Abraham Darby's coke smelting at Coalbrookdale in Shropshire (1709) used coke (coal heated to remove impurities) to smelt iron. It was background to the growth of the iron industry, which produced iron on a larger scale.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.1] Iron was used for rails, bridges and machines, which is why iron and coal grew together with railways and factories.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.2] Henry Bessemer's converter (1856) made steel cheaply. Steel is stronger than iron, and cheap steel was the basis of later heavy industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.3] The Great Exhibition of 1851 was held in the Crystal Palace in Hyde Park, London. It showed Britain as the 'workshop of the world', a nickname for the leading industrial nation.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.b] Railways combined iron, coal and steam. They changed how goods and people moved and created demand for more industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.0] The Stockton and Darlington Railway opened in 1825, so steam locomotives were already in use before the Liverpool line.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.1] The Stephensons' Rocket (built by George and Robert Stephenson) won the Rainhill Trials in 1829, showing that steam locomotives could haul trains effectively.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.2] The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened on 15 September 1830. It was the first inter-city railway worked by steam locomotives, with timetabled passenger trains.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.3] A railway boom, known as 'railway mania', followed in Britain in the mid-1840s, and the rail network expanded rapidly.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.4] Railways made transport of goods and people cheaper and faster, and they created national markets instead of local ones.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.5] They created demand for coal, iron and engineering, and they gave work in construction, so railways pulled other industries along.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.b] Britain's lead did not mean the rest of Europe followed at once. Obstacles delayed continental industry, but Belgium overcame them first.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.0] The continent was slower because of the French wars, poorer transport, internal tariffs (taxes on goods crossing borders) and many separate states in the German lands, less accessible coal and smaller markets.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.1] A peasantry that stayed on the land also meant fewer workers moved into factories.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.2] Continental states copied British machines and hired British engineers to catch up.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.3] Belgium was the first country on the continent to industrialise, with coal in Hainaut and Liège. It had the advantage of coal, which powered engines and furnaces.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.4] John Cockerill, who was English-born, set up ironworks and engineering works at Seraing, near Liège, in 1817.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.5] The Brussels–Mechelen railway, opened on 5 May 1835, was Belgium's first railway, steam-worked and the first stage of a state-built network.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.6] Continental industry relied more on investment banks and on state help, through railways and tariffs, than British industry did. In Britain firms grew largely from their own profits and private capital.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.b] France industrialised more slowly than Britain or Belgium. Its growth was mixed, with some regions advanced and a large rural population behind.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.0] Textile industry developed in Alsace (Mulhouse) and Normandy (Rouen), such as cotton spinning and weaving.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.1] The Schneider brothers took over the ironworks at Le Creusot in 1836, for example, building up iron and engineering in central France.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.2] The Saint-Étienne–Andrézieux line of 1827 carried coal and was horse-drawn at first.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.3] The Railway Law of 11 June 1842 set out a national network in which the state provided the land and track bed while private companies laid the track and ran the lines.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.4] Growth was faster in the 1850s and 1860s under the Second Empire.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.b] The German lands industrialised late but fast. Removing barriers between states and building on rich coalfields let them catch up.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.0] The Zollverein (customs union) of 1834 removed internal tariffs between member states, so trade and investment grew across the German lands.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.1] The first German railway ran from Nuremberg to Fürth in Bavaria and opened on 7 December 1835.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.2] The Ruhr and Silesian coalfields grew rapidly, which gave cheap fuel for iron and steel.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.3] Alfred Krupp's works at Essen expanded from the 1850s, and the steel industry of the Ruhr was built on coal and iron from the region.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.4] Friedrich List argued in The National System of Political Economy (1841) for railways and protective tariffs for young German industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.5] By 1871 Germany was becoming the leading industrial power on the continent.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.b] The factory changed how people lived their working lives. Instead of working at home at their own pace, workers had to obey the machine and the clock.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.0] The factory system concentrated workers under supervision, working fixed hours to the pace of the machines for wages, with fines and discipline for lateness or mistakes.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.1] Hand workers such as handloom weavers declined because machines produced cloth more cheaply.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.2] Hours were long, often 12 to 14 a day in cotton mills, so workers had little time or energy outside work.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.3] Children and women made up a large share of the textile workforce, and children could start work at an early age because they were cheap to employ.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.4] Unfenced machinery caused accidents and injuries, and little or no compensation was paid to injured workers.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.5] Poor ventilation, dust and heat made mills unhealthy, so lung disease and exhaustion were common.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.6] In the mines, children and women worked underground in dangerous conditions, such as hauling coal in the dark.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.b] Workers resisted the machines, reformers gathered evidence, and Parliament passed laws. Each law covered a particular group and place, so you need to know which is which.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.0] The Luddites (1811–16, in the Midlands and the North of England) broke machines, which showed the hostility of hand workers who feared losing their living.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.1] The Sadler Committee of 1832 (led by Michael Sadler MP) gathered evidence of the abuse of child workers. Lord Ashley, later 7th Earl of Shaftesbury, campaigned for factory reform.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.2] The Factory Act of 1833 barred children under nine from textile mills (silk mills excepted), and limited children of 9–13 to nine hours and young persons of 13–18 to twelve hours a day.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.3] The 1833 Act also required two hours' schooling a day for child workers, and four inspectors were appointed to enforce it.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.4] The Mines Act of 1842 banned women and girls, and boys under ten, from working underground.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.5] The Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited women and young persons in textile mills to ten hours a day. A Factory Act of 1844 came in between.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.b] Many British thinkers and governments believed the state should leave the economy alone. This belief explains both why change was so fast and why the response to its harms was slow.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.0] Laissez-faire is the belief that the state should leave the economy, prices, wages and trade to the free market.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.1] Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) argued that free trade and competition produce prosperity. Thomas Malthus (Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798) and David Ricardo (Principles of Political Economy, 1817) were later economists in this tradition.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.2] In practice laissez-faire meant free trade, shown by the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.3] The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 gave relief to the able-bodied poor normally only inside a workhouse, where conditions were made worse than those of the poorest independent labourer. This was the principle of 'less eligibility'.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.4] The limits: the state did intervene in child and female labour (the Factory Acts and Mines Act) and in public health, but not in men's wages or hours.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.5] The continent was more interventionist: Prussia and France promoted railways, tariffs and banks. List argued that young German industry needed protection.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.b] Industry and farming change drew people into towns. Cities grew faster than anyone could house them, which is the root of the slum and disease problems of the industrial towns.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.0] Manchester, nicknamed 'Cottonopolis', grew from about 75,000 people in 1801 to about 300,000 in 1851.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.1] Liverpool, Leeds, Birmingham and Glasgow also grew quickly, because they were centres of trade and industry that offered work.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.2] Migration from the countryside had push causes (enclosure, mechanisation, rural poverty and the 1840s crisis) and pull causes (factory work and wages).
[hist-eur1-s7.16.3] Irish migrants came to British cities in large numbers, especially in the 1840s, because the Famine drove them from home.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.4] Paris grew mainly by migration from the provinces, from about 550,000 in 1801 to about a million by the mid-1840s, with overcrowding in the old centre.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.5] In the Ruhr, Essen, Bochum and Dortmund grew fast in the 1850s and 1860s, drawing workers from rural Prussia. Mass Polish-speaking migration came later, mainly after 1871, so it falls outside this period.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.6] The 1851 census showed that, for the first time, more than half the population of England and Wales lived in towns.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.b] Towns had grown far faster than their housing and water. The result was disease, as the poor lived in places with no drains.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.0] Slums had back-to-back houses (built in rows sharing walls, with no back door or through air), cellar dwellings, overcrowding, no drains, shared privies, polluted water and smoke. Death rates were high, especially among infants.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.1] Little Ireland in Manchester was an Irish slum by the River Medlock, described by Engels.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.2] Friedrich Engels, in The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845, published in German), described Manchester, a witness account that influenced the debate on reform.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.3] Cholera, a disease spread by dirty water, struck Britain in 1831–32, 1848–49 and 1853–54, and Paris in 1832 and 1849. Thousands died in each epidemic. Typhus and tuberculosis also killed.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.b] Inquiry came first and law followed, but reform was slow because many believed the state should not interfere.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.0] Edwin Chadwick's Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain (1842) linked dirt and poor drainage to disease and early death.

[hist-eur1-s7.18.2] It was largely permissive, meaning towns were not forced to act. Local boards were compulsory only where the death rate was very high.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.3] In 1854 the London physician John Snow traced the Broad Street (Soho) cholera outbreak to a contaminated pump, showing that cholera spread through water.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.4] After the 'Great Stink' of summer 1858, Joseph Bazalgette built London's main drainage system (sewers) from 1859.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.5] The Peabody Trust (1862), founded by the American banker George Peabody, built model dwellings for London's poor.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.6] The Public Health Act of 1875 made sanitary provision compulsory for local authorities, after the period.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.b] As economies and states changed, governments wanted schooled populations. Prussia led, and France followed with a law of its own.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.0] States expanded schooling to produce disciplined, literate workers and soldiers, and loyal citizens and subjects. Churches also had religious and moral aims.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.1] Fear of disorder pushed states too, because schools taught obedience, and later came the need for voters who could read.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.2] Prussia had compulsory schooling from the eighteenth century, confirmed by Frederick the Great's General School Regulations of 1763. The state supervised village schools and teachers, and teacher-training seminaries followed in the nineteenth century.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.3] Prussia was seen as Europe's model because it had among the highest literacy rates.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.4] The Guizot Law of 28 June 1833 (François Guizot, Minister of Public Instruction) required each commune (the smallest local government unit) to maintain a primary school, in practice for boys.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.5] It also required each department to have a teacher-training college (école normale). Schooling was not free or compulsory for all, with free places for the poor only.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.6] Victor Duruy's law of 1867 required communes of 500 or more inhabitants to provide a girls' school.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.b] Britain left schooling to churches for a long time, and the state slowly came in with money, inspection and, in 1870, its own schools.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.0] Schools were run by voluntary religious societies: the Anglican National Society (1811) and the Nonconformist British and Foreign School Society (1814).
[hist-eur1-s7.20.1] They used the monitorial system of Andrew Bell and Joseph Lancaster, in which older pupils (monitors) taught younger ones, so one teacher could manage a large class cheaply.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.2] The first government grant for education came in 1833: £20,000, given to the two societies.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.3] The Committee of the Privy Council on Education (1839) was set up to supervise a larger grant, raised to £30,000, with James Kay (later Kay-Shuttleworth) as secretary. School inspectors began in 1839.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.4] The Newcastle Commission, appointed in 1858 and reporting in 1861, found many children were poorly taught.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.5] Robert Lowe's Revised Code of 1862 introduced 'payment by results', so grants depended on pupils' results in reading, writing and arithmetic.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.6] The Elementary Education Act of 1870, introduced by W. E. Forster, set up elected school boards to build board schools where voluntary schools were insufficient. Fees were still charged and boards could make attendance compulsory locally; national compulsion came in 1880.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.b] Schooling raised literacy, the ability to read and write, and literacy changed politics.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.0] Historians measure literacy by the ability to sign the marriage register. It was highest in Prussia, Scandinavia and Scotland, lower in England and France, and much lower in southern and eastern Europe.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.1] Literacy was higher in Protestant northern Europe and lower in the south and east, so the gap was wide across the continent.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.2] More people could read newspapers, books and pamphlets, and a cheap popular press grew.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.3] Political ideas, nationalism and mass politics spread, because ideas could now reach the middle and working classes in print.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.b] Examiners reward judgement. Weigh what the period gained against what it cost, and note that change was uneven.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.0] Gains: rising output and national wealth, and more food from improved farming.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.1] Costs: loss of land and common rights, child labour and dangerous work, slums and epidemics, and the 1840s hunger.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.2] Gains: railways and cheaper goods, and by the end of the period reform of factories, public health and education.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.3] Costs: government response was slow and uneven because of laissez-faire, so harm continued even after reform began.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.4] Uneven change: Britain went first and furthest and Belgium early, France was slower with its peasant farms, and the German lands grew rapidly after 1850. Eastern and southern Europe were largely agricultural in 1871.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.5] Contemporary views: critics at the time such as Engels, Chadwick and the factory reformers saw misery. Supporters saw progress, as at the Great Exhibition.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.6] Later views: historians still debate whether workers' living standards rose or fell in the early decades (the 'standard of living debate'), with broad agreement that they rose after about 1850.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.7] Verdict: real gains in wealth and education came at a heavy social cost.
