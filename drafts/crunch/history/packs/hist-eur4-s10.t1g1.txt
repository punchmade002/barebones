[hist-eur4-s10.0.b] To understand why writers mattered so much in this period, you need to know what they were up against. In the communist states writing was a political act, and in the West the Cold War shaped what intellectuals argued about.
[hist-eur4-s10.0.0] Stalin's terror (the 1930s to his death in 1953) filled the Gulag, the Soviet system of labour camps, so the camps were a fact of life that the state did not want discussed.
[hist-eur4-s10.0.1] In 1956 Khrushchev, Stalin's successor, denounced Stalin's crimes in his secret speech. The loosening of controls that followed is called the thaw, and it made the 1962 publication of One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich possible.
[hist-eur4-s10.0.2] Communist states controlled publishing and the press through censorship and the official Writers' Unions, and the KGB (the Soviet secret police) policed dissent, so a writer who criticised the system risked losing the right to publish, prison or exile.
[hist-eur4-s10.0.3] A dissident writer is a writer who openly opposed a communist state's policy or ideology and faced censorship, prison or exile as a result.
[hist-eur4-s10.0.4] The Cold War divided Europe, and Western intellectuals argued fiercely over communism, the USA and colonialism, which is why many Western writers of this period took political sides.
[hist-eur4-s10.0.5] Before 1945 Western Europe was largely Christian in practice, whereas the communist parties in the East were officially atheist, so religion was a matter of habit in the West and of conflict with the state in the East.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.b] Solzhenitsyn was the Soviet novelist whose writing on the Gulag made the camp system known to the world and made him the most famous dissident in the Soviet Union. His own years as a prisoner gave him his subject.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.0] He was born in Kislovodsk on 11 December 1918 and served as an artillery officer (a captain) in the Red Army in the Second World War.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.1] In February 1945 he was arrested at the front for criticising Stalin in private letters to a friend, which shows how far the terror reached, since even private remarks were punished.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.2] He was sentenced to eight years in labour camps and then to internal exile (forced residence inside the USSR) in Kazakhstan from 1953. The exile ended in 1956, so he spent eight years in camps and about three in exile.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.3] In 1954 he was treated for cancer in a hospital in Tashkent and was cured, an episode that lies behind his novel Cancer Ward.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.4] One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich appeared in the Soviet journal Novy Mir in November 1962 with Khrushchev's personal approval, which was possible because of the thaw.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.5] The novel describes a single day of a camp prisoner, Ivan Denisovich Shukhov, in a Stalin-era camp. It brought the camps into Soviet public discussion because it was an officially approved publication.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.6] After Khrushchev fell in October 1964 censorship tightened, so The First Circle and Cancer Ward could be published only abroad, in 1968, and he was expelled from the Soviet Writers' Union in November 1969.
[hist-eur4-s10.1.7] He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1970, but he did not travel to receive it, fearing that he would not be allowed back into the USSR.
[hist-eur4-s10.2.b] Once his official publishing stopped, Solzhenitsyn wrote the book that made him an enemy of the state. Its publication abroad led straight to his expulsion, and he continued to write and speak from exile.
[hist-eur4-s10.2.0] The Gulag Archipelago is non-fiction: a work of testimony, history and reflection on the camp system, drawing on the accounts of many prisoners. Unlike Ivan Denisovich, it was banned in the USSR.
[hist-eur4-s10.2.1] The KGB seized a copy of the manuscript in 1973, and this led him to authorise publication. It appeared in Paris at the end of 1973 and had a major impact in the West.
[hist-eur4-s10.2.2] He was arrested on 12 February 1974 and flown the next day to West Germany, where he was stripped of his citizenship. The expulsion therefore followed the Archipelago, not Ivan Denisovich.
[hist-eur4-s10.2.3] In exile he lived first in West Germany, then in Switzerland, and from 1976 in Vermont in the USA.
[hist-eur4-s10.2.4] In his Harvard commencement address of June 1978 he criticised Western materialism and weakness as well as communism, which disappointed many of his Western admirers.
[hist-eur4-s10.2.5] From Vermont he continued to write The Red Wheel, his multi-volume historical cycle on the Russian Revolution.
[hist-eur4-s10.2.6] He returned to Russia in 1994 and died in Moscow in August 2008.
[hist-eur4-s10.3.b] Solzhenitsyn fits the definition of a dissident writer exactly, and both his contemporaries and later historians judged him in divided ways.
[hist-eur4-s10.3.0] He is a dissident writer because he opposed the Soviet system through his writing, exposing the camps that the state wanted hidden, and he was punished for it with censorship, expulsion from the Writers' Union and exile.
[hist-eur4-s10.3.1] The Soviet authorities called him a traitor and expelled him, while many Western intellectuals admired him, though some later disliked his nationalist and religious views.
[hist-eur4-s10.3.2] Historians regard his work as central to Western understanding of Soviet terror, because he gave the West evidence from the inside, although some question the reliability of his testimony and his later political stances.
[hist-eur4-s10.3.3] In Russia after 1994 his reputation was divided between admirers who saw him as the nation's conscience and critics who disliked his Russian nationalism and his religious conservatism.
[hist-eur4-s10.4.b] Beauvoir (1908-1986) is best known for The Second Sex, but she was also a novelist, memoirist and critic who questioned Western society and its values from the Paris intellectual left. Here she is the writer and critic.
[hist-eur4-s10.4.0] In 1945 she joined Jean-Paul Sartre in founding the review Les Temps modernes, which became the leading journal of left-wing intellectuals in France.
[hist-eur4-s10.4.1] Her novel The Mandarins portrayed the Parisian intellectual left after the Second World War and won the Prix Goncourt, a leading French literary prize, in 1954.
[hist-eur4-s10.4.2] Her memoirs, including Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter (1958), described her youth, and they also describe her relationship with Sartre.
[hist-eur4-s10.4.3] That relationship was a lifelong partnership by choice without marriage. Admirers presented it as a model of equality, while critics called it a privilege of the elite.
[hist-eur4-s10.4.4] When Sartre died in 1980 she wrote about his death in Adieux: A Farewell to Sartre (1981).
[hist-eur4-s10.4.5] Her essay La vieillesse (The Coming of Age, 1970) attacked the way society treated the elderly, so her criticism reached beyond politics to everyday attitudes.
[hist-eur4-s10.4.6] The Second Sex (1949), which described women as defined as the Other, made her a founder of modern feminism.
[hist-eur4-s10.5.b] Beauvoir used her reputation to take public stands, especially over Algeria, and her view of communism changed as she saw it at first hand.
[hist-eur4-s10.5.0] She took part in the political debates of the 1960s and 1970s, supporting the left and speaking out for the rights of women and of prisoners.
[hist-eur4-s10.5.1] In September 1960 she signed the Manifesto of the 121, which supported French conscripts who refused to fight in the Algerian war.
[hist-eur4-s10.5.2] She campaigned against torture by the French army and in 1962 championed the case of Djamila Boupacha, an Algerian woman who alleged torture by French forces.
[hist-eur4-s10.5.3] With Sartre she visited the Soviet Union, China (1955) and Cuba (1960), where she observed communism at first hand, and her views on it changed over time.
[hist-eur4-s10.5.4] Contemporaries praised her as a writer and public intellectual, while conservative critics attacked her for challenging traditional morality.
[hist-eur4-s10.5.5] Historians now judge her as one of the most influential critics of the twentieth century, though her later political views are contested.
[hist-eur4-s10.6.b] Writers in the communist states could not publish freely, so they found other ways to reach readers. They circulated unofficial copies, sent work abroad and relied on Western radio to carry banned works back.
[hist-eur4-s10.6.0] Samizdat (Russian for self-published) was the system by which typed and copied manuscripts were passed from hand to hand in the Soviet Union and Czechoslovakia. The authors' work could thus circulate without passing the censor.
[hist-eur4-s10.6.1] Some writers instead smuggled their work abroad to be published there. Western radio stations such as Radio Free Europe and the BBC then broadcast banned works to the East.
[hist-eur4-s10.6.2] Boris Pasternak's Doctor Zhivago was first published in Italy in 1957. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1958 and, under pressure from the Soviet authorities, declined it.
[hist-eur4-s10.6.3] In 1966 Andrei Sinyavsky and Yuli Daniel were sentenced to labour camps (seven and five years) for publishing satires abroad under pseudonyms, and in 1964 Joseph Brodsky was tried for social parasitism. Both cases drew international protest.
[hist-eur4-s10.6.4] Andrei Sakharov, the physicist central to the Soviet hydrogen bomb, became a human rights campaigner. He was exiled to Gorky in January 1980 and released in December 1986 under Gorbachev.
[hist-eur4-s10.6.5] Dissident writers faced expulsion, imprisonment or exile, and Solzhenitsyn's expulsion in 1974 was the most famous example.
[hist-eur4-s10.7.b] Outside the Soviet Union, writers in Czechoslovakia and Poland also challenged the state. In the 1970s international agreements gave dissidents a new standard to appeal to.
[hist-eur4-s10.7.0] Milan Kundera's novel The Joke (1967) and the Czechoslovak Writers' Union congress of June 1967, which criticised censorship, were part of the intellectual ferment that came before the Prague Spring of 1968.
[hist-eur4-s10.7.1] Václav Havel, a Czech playwright, wrote The Power of the Powerless in 1978. He argued that ordinary people could resist by "living within the truth", as it is reported.
[hist-eur4-s10.7.2] Havel helped to found Charter 77, a human rights manifesto of January 1977, and he was imprisoned from 1979 and released in early 1983, so most of 1979-83 was spent in jail. He became president of Czechoslovakia in December 1989.
[hist-eur4-s10.7.3] Polish writers also took part: Czesław Miłosz, who defected in 1951, won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1980, the same year as Solidarity's rise.
[hist-eur4-s10.7.4] The Helsinki Final Act of 1975, in which the signatories (the USSR included) accepted human rights commitments, gave dissidents a standard of rights to appeal to, since their government had signed it.
[hist-eur4-s10.8.b] Social criticism means writing that attacks the faults of a society. Western writers were free to publish, and they used that freedom to criticise totalitarianism (a state that controls every part of life) and the conformity of their own societies.
[hist-eur4-s10.8.0] George Orwell's Animal Farm (1945) and Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949) criticised Stalinism and the abuse of power, and both became standard texts in the Cold War.
[hist-eur4-s10.8.1] Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932) was read alongside Nineteen Eighty-Four as a warning against conformity and totalitarian control, whether communist or consumerist.
[hist-eur4-s10.8.2] Albert Camus's The Plague (1947) used an epidemic as an allegory (a story with a hidden political meaning) for resistance to tyranny, and he received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1957.
[hist-eur4-s10.8.3] Camus broke with Sartre in 1952, after his book The Rebel (1951), over the question of communism and violence. He was killed in a car crash in January 1960.
[hist-eur4-s10.9.b] In France, Germany and Britain, writers also turned their criticism on their own societies, on their national pasts and on class.
[hist-eur4-s10.9.0] Jean-Paul Sartre's existentialism, expressed in Being and Nothingness (1943) and in his plays, argued that people are free and responsible for their choices. It dominated French thought in the late 1940s and 1950s.
[hist-eur4-s10.9.1] Sartre's plays and essays also criticised bourgeois society (the comfortable middle class) and colonialism, and in 1964 he declined the Nobel Prize in Literature by his own choice.
[hist-eur4-s10.9.2] Günter Grass's The Tin Drum (1959) examined Germany's wartime past and the moral compromises of ordinary people, and he received the Nobel Prize in 1999.
[hist-eur4-s10.9.3] Heinrich Böll's novels criticised the restoration of West German society after 1945 and he received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1972.
[hist-eur4-s10.9.4] In Britain, John Osborne's play Look Back in Anger (1956) and the writers known as the Angry Young Men attacked class and complacency in post-war society.
[hist-eur4-s10.10.b] Secularisation means religion losing belief, practice and influence in public life. In much of Western Europe it advanced quickly from the 1960s, and the authority of the churches over everyday life declined.
[hist-eur4-s10.10.0] Attendance at Mass and other religious services fell in most Western European countries from the 1960s, and the fall was sharpest among young people.
[hist-eur4-s10.10.1] There were national differences: attendance fell sharply in Britain, the Netherlands, France and Scandinavia, while in Ireland and Poland religious practice remained high for longer.
[hist-eur4-s10.10.2] The Netherlands is a striking case, because Catholic Mass attendance fell dramatically between the 1960s and 1980s and many left the Church.
[hist-eur4-s10.10.3] Secularisation meant that the state, education and family life increasingly ran without reference to religious authority.
[hist-eur4-s10.10.4] The decline of clergy vocations (people choosing to become priests, monks or nuns) was visible by the 1970s, when seminaries and religious orders in many countries recruited far fewer men and women.
[hist-eur4-s10.11.b] No single event caused the decline of religion. Several social changes worked together, and the Catholic Church was also divided from inside.
[hist-eur4-s10.11.0] Rising affluence and growing education were causes of secularisation.
[hist-eur4-s10.11.1] Television brought new ideas and entertainment into the home, and urban life loosened the close communities in which church attendance had been a habit.
[hist-eur4-s10.11.2] The welfare state took over services once run by the churches, so people needed the church less.
[hist-eur4-s10.11.3] In the 1960s people questioned authority of all kinds, including the authority of the churches, and this was one cause of secularisation.
[hist-eur4-s10.11.4] The reforms of the Second Vatican Council (a Catholic Church council) and the controversy over contraception created divisions in the Church.
[hist-eur4-s10.11.5] In Humanae Vitae (July 1968) Pope Paul VI reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception, which many Catholics found hard to accept.
[hist-eur4-s10.12.b] While Christian practice declined, migration brought a new religion into Western Europe. It made religion visible in public life again in societies that were becoming more secular.
[hist-eur4-s10.12.0] Muslim communities grew in Western Europe through migration from North Africa (especially to France), Turkey (especially to West Germany) and South Asia (especially to Britain).
[hist-eur4-s10.12.1] These communities set up prayer rooms and mosques, so a new religion became a visible part of many European cities.
[hist-eur4-s10.13.b] In the East, churches faced persecution or state control rather than the gradual decline seen in the West. Some, however, kept real influence and became centres of resistance.
[hist-eur4-s10.13.0] The Catholic Church in Czechoslovakia was suppressed after the communist takeover of 1948, and bishops were imprisoned.
[hist-eur4-s10.13.1] In Poland the Church retained real independence and mass support. Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński was interned from 1953 to 1956, but its influence grew after the election of the Polish pope, Karol Wojtyła (John Paul II), in 1978.
[hist-eur4-s10.13.2] In Hungary Cardinal József Mindszenty was arrested in December 1948 and sentenced to life imprisonment in February 1949. He was freed in the uprising of October 1956 and took refuge in the US legation in Budapest from November 1956 until 1971, about 15 years.
[hist-eur4-s10.13.3] The Evangelical (Protestant) Church in East Germany adopted a policy of coexistence with the state while maintaining its own institutions and social work.
[hist-eur4-s10.13.4] In East Germany church-led peace prayers in Leipzig in autumn 1989 preceded the mass demonstrations, so the churches provided a protected space where protest could begin.
[hist-eur4-s10.13.5] The Russian Orthodox Church was permitted to operate under tight state control and KGB infiltration. Khrushchev's anti-religious campaign of 1959-64 closed thousands of churches.
[hist-eur4-s10.13.6] The contrast with the West is that religion in the East was persecuted or controlled by the state, whereas in the West it declined by voluntary choice.
[hist-eur4-s10.14.b] The ecumenical movement means cooperation and moves towards unity among Christian churches of different denominations. After 1945, as Christian practice fell in the West, the churches cooperated more closely than at any time since the Reformation.
[hist-eur4-s10.14.0] The World Council of Churches was founded at Amsterdam in August 1948 by Protestant, Anglican and Orthodox churches. The Roman Catholic Church did not join, and the Russian Orthodox Church joined in 1961.
[hist-eur4-s10.14.1] Taizé, an ecumenical community in Burgundy, France, founded in 1940 by Brother Roger (Roger Schutz), drew young people from many countries in the 1960s and 1970s.
[hist-eur4-s10.14.2] In January 1964 Pope Paul VI met Patriarch Athenagoras of Constantinople in Jerusalem, a step towards reconciling the Catholic and Orthodox churches.
[hist-eur4-s10.14.3] In December 1965 the mutual excommunications of 1054, by which the two churches had condemned each other, were lifted.
[hist-eur4-s10.15.b] Historians debate how far writers and religious movements shaped political change and how far they merely reflected it. The judgement is balanced on both sides.
[hist-eur4-s10.15.0] Achievement: dissident writing kept the truth about Stalin's crimes alive and gave the West evidence, which helped to create an intellectual opposition in the communist states.
[hist-eur4-s10.15.1] Limit: samizdat had a small readership, and most citizens were not directly reached by dissident writing, so the regimes survived until 1989-91 for other reasons.
[hist-eur4-s10.15.2] Contemporaries: Soviet authorities called Solzhenitsyn a traitor, while many in the West praised dissident writers as heroes of freedom.

[hist-eur4-s10.15.4] Religion: secularisation in the West was a broad social trend with several causes, not the result of a single event or movement.
[hist-eur4-s10.15.5] In the East, religion was a source of resistance in Poland and East Germany, which is the reverse of the Western trend.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s2: 2. The main crises of the Cold War, Khrushchev and détente
[hist-eur4-s2.0.b] The crises of 1958–62 and the détente that followed only make sense against a few basic facts: who the two sides were, how the Soviet state was run, and what nuclear weapons could do. The Cold War in Europe lasted from about 1945 to 1991.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.0] The Cold War was a rivalry short of direct war between the USA and its Western allies (capitalist democracies) and the Soviet Union and its Eastern European satellites (communist one-party states). The superpowers never fought each other directly.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.1] Instead the rivalry was fought through alliances, the arms race, propaganda, espionage, the space race and a series of crises, so each crisis was a test of nerve that stopped short of war.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.2] The two sides formed alliances: NATO in 1949 and the Warsaw Pact in 1955. Germany was divided in 1949 into the Federal Republic (West) and the German Democratic Republic (East).
[hist-eur4-s2.0.3] Berlin lay deep inside East Germany and was split into four sectors. The three Western sectors formed West Berlin, a Western island inside communist territory, which is why Berlin kept causing crises.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.4] In the Soviet Union the Communist Party ruled alone. Its leader was the First Secretary of the Central Committee, the Presidium (later called the Politburo) was the inner leadership, and the chairman of the Council of Ministers (the premier) headed the government.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.5] Stalin ruled by terror, purges (waves of arrests and executions), the Gulag labour camps and a cult of personality (public worship of the leader). His death on 5 March 1953 opened a struggle for the succession.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.6] The USA used atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 and the Soviet Union tested its first in 1949. Both later built hydrogen bombs. Medium-range missiles reach targets within a region, while intercontinental missiles (ICBMs) reach the other superpower's homeland.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.7] Fidel Castro took power in Cuba in January 1959 and Cuba then moved into the Soviet camp, which put a Soviet ally close to the USA.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.b] The crises before Khrushchev set the pattern of confrontation without direct war, and they explain why NATO was armed and why Germany stayed divided.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.0] The Berlin Blockade (June 1948 to May 1949) was the first major crisis. It was ended by the Western airlift, and it confirmed the division of Germany. NATO was founded in 1949.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.1] The Korean War began in June 1950 with the North Korean invasion of the South. It persuaded Western governments that communism was expansionist, so they rearmed.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.2] As a result NATO was rearmed and given a unified command under Eisenhower, appointed its first Supreme Allied Commander in Europe in December 1950, and the West pushed to rearm West Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.3] Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 opened a more flexible phase, and an armistice in Korea followed in July 1953.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.b] Khrushchev led the Soviet Union from Stalin's death in 1953 to 1964 and set the terms of the Cold War between de-Stalinisation and nuclear confrontation. He was shaped by Stalin's system, and then attacked it.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.0] He was born in 1894 in Kalinovka, a village in Kursk province, of peasant stock. He worked as a miner and metalworker before rising through the Communist Party.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.1] Under Stalin he became first secretary of the Moscow party organisation in 1935 and of the Ukrainian party in 1938, which shows he was a trusted servant of the Stalinist system.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.2] He took part in Stalin's purges in Moscow and Ukraine in the late 1930s. This later made his attacks on Stalin's terror open to challenge, because he had helped to carry it out.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.3] In the West contemporaries saw him as blustering, unpredictable and dangerous, but also as a man who could be dealt with, as shown by his 1959 visit to the USA and his 'kitchen debate' with Vice-President Nixon.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.4] Mao Zedong's China called him a 'revisionist' who had betrayed Marxism-Leninism. By 1964 his Soviet colleagues saw him as a reckless leader of 'hare-brained schemes'.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.5] Historians such as William Taubman, in Khrushchev: The Man and His Era (2003), describe a reformer who opened Soviet society after Stalin but whose policies were improvised and often failed, and who never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.b] After Stalin's death no one had a clear right to rule, so Khrushchev won power by defeating rivals one at a time. Note that he held the party post first and became head of government only later.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.0] He became First Secretary of the Central Committee in September 1953, the party post that was the real centre of power.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.1] Lavrentiy Beria, the former secret police chief, was arrested in June 1953 and executed in December 1953, which removed the most feared rival.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.2] Georgy Malenkov was forced to resign as premier in 1955, weakening another rival who had been the head of government since Stalin's death.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.3] In June 1957 Khrushchev defeated the 'anti-party group' of Molotov, Malenkov and Kaganovich, with the help of Marshal Zhukov and the Central Committee.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.4] In March 1958 he became chairman of the Council of Ministers (premier) as well as First Secretary, so from then on he held both the leading party post and the top government post.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.b] At the 20th Party Congress in 1956 Khrushchev did two things that changed Soviet politics: he attacked Stalin's record, and he redefined the Soviet struggle with the West as competition rather than war.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.0] On 25 February 1956, in a secret speech to a closed session of the 20th Party Congress, he denounced Stalin's cult of personality and his terror against party members.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.1] The attack was limited, because it condemned Stalin's crimes against party members and the cult of personality, not communism, collectivisation or the one-party state.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.2] The text of the speech was published in the West in 1956, within months, so it soon became known across the world and not just in the Soviet bloc.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.3] Its shock in Eastern Europe fed the Polish October of 1956, in which Władysław Gomułka came to power, and the Hungarian Uprising, because people hoped the bloc's rulers would loosen their control.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.4] At the same congress he argued that war with the capitalist states was not inevitable. This became known as peaceful coexistence: competition would be economic and ideological, not military.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.b] Khrushchev's denunciation of Stalin was followed by real changes, which made Soviet life less frightening, though they stopped well short of democracy.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.0] He released many political prisoners from the Gulag, the system of labour camps, so that people who had been held under Stalin returned to ordinary life.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.1] The secret police were curbed, their role reduced, and they were placed under party control, so they could no longer act as a power above the party.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.2] A cultural 'thaw' allowed more open discussion in literature, but it had limits, because the party still decided how far criticism could go.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.3] At the 22nd Party Congress in October 1961 Stalin's body was removed from the Lenin Mausoleum, a public sign that his reputation had been cast down.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.b] Khrushchev tried to raise food output and make the economy more responsive. His schemes brought early gains but then failures, and the failures fed the resentment that ended his rule.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.0] The Virgin Lands campaign from 1954 ploughed up grassland in Kazakhstan and western Siberia to grow grain, and it produced good harvests in its early years.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.1] Soil erosion, drought and poor management later brought failures, most seriously in 1963, when the harvest was bad and grain had to be imported from the West.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.2] After his 1959 visit to the USA he also launched a maize (corn) campaign, copying the American farms he had seen.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.3] In 1957 industry was decentralised into regional economic councils (sovnarkhozy), so that decisions moved from Moscow ministries to the regions.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.4] In 1962 the party was split into industrial and agricultural branches. Both reforms were resented by officials, because they threatened their jobs, and both were reversed by his successors.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.b] Khrushchev promised Soviet citizens a better life and a race to overtake the USA. Space successes gave him prestige abroad, but the economy did not deliver at home.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.0] He launched a large programme of new flats and promised more consumer goods, and he promised that the Soviet Union would overtake the USA.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.1] The 1961 party programme at the 22nd Congress promised that communism would be built 'in the main' by 1980, a target that raised hopes that could not be met.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.2] In June 1962 a strike at Novocherkassk over higher food prices and wage cuts was crushed when troops fired on the crowd and more than twenty people were killed, a sign of the limits of his reforms.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.3] Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, was launched on 4 October 1957, and Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on 12 April 1961.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.4] These successes alarmed the West, which feared a 'missile gap', meaning that the Soviet Union might be ahead in long-range missiles, and they boosted Soviet prestige.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.b] Peaceful coexistence showed in a series of gestures to the West and to Yugoslavia, but the limits of the policy showed when the Soviet bloc itself was challenged.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.0] In May 1955 the Austrian State Treaty was signed: Soviet and Western troops left Austria and it became neutral, which showed Moscow could agree to a withdrawal.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.1] In 1955 Khrushchev visited Josip Broz Tito in Belgrade to mend the split between the Soviet Union and Yugoslavia that had opened in 1948.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.2] The Geneva summit of July 1955 brought together the USA, the USSR, Britain and France, and it was the first post-war summit of the four powers, so it signalled that talking was possible.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.3] The Soviet crushing of the Hungarian Uprising in November 1956 showed that he would use force to keep the bloc together, whatever he said about coexistence.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.4] In July 1959, at the American National Exhibition in Moscow, he and Vice-President Nixon held the 'kitchen debate' on the merits of communism and capitalism.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.5] In September 1959 he visited the USA and held talks with President Eisenhower at Camp David, which produced the 'spirit of Camp David', a brief thaw that the U-2 incident later ended.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.b] Khrushchev's changes divided the communist world. Mao's China rejected them, and the quarrel meant that Moscow could no longer speak for all communists.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.0] Mao rejected de-Stalinisation and peaceful coexistence, regarding them as a betrayal of Marxism-Leninism. The ideological dispute grew from the late 1950s.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.1] The dispute became open in 1960, when Soviet advisers were withdrawn from China, and open polemics (public attacks in print) followed in the early 1960s.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.2] The split meant the Soviet Union faced two rival communist powers and could not count on Chinese support in Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.3] Albania sided with China, stopped taking part in the Warsaw Pact in the early 1960s and formally withdrew in 1968. The communist world was no longer united behind Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.b] Berlin was the most dangerous flashpoint in Europe, because West Berlin was an enclave inside East Germany and a window to the West. In 1958 Khrushchev tried to force the Western powers out.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.0] West Berlin was a showcase of Western prosperity inside communist East Germany, so every East German could compare the two systems.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.1] It was also the main escape route for East Germans. About 2.7 million people left East Germany for the West between 1949 and 1961 (sources vary slightly), mostly young and skilled.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.2] This drained the GDR's workforce and threatened its survival, which is why Moscow and East Berlin wanted the exit closed.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.3] On 27 November 1958 Khrushchev issued an ultimatum giving the Western powers six months to end their occupation of West Berlin and make it a demilitarised 'free city'. Otherwise he would hand control of access to East Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.4] The West refused and the deadline passed without Khrushchev carrying out his threat, so the ultimatum failed.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.b] After the thaw of 1959, two events in 1960 and 1961 ended hopes of a Berlin settlement and made a harder solution likely.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.0] On 1 May 1960 an American U-2 spy plane flown by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union near Sverdlovsk.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.1] Eisenhower would not apologise, so the Paris summit of May 1960 collapsed and the thaw of 1959 ended.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.2] Powers was exchanged for a Soviet spy in February 1962, so the affair ended with a swap rather than a trial verdict standing.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.3] At the Vienna summit in June 1961 Khrushchev pressed the new US president, John F. Kennedy (inaugurated January 1961), to accept a Berlin settlement.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.4] Kennedy refused and strengthened US forces, so the crisis deepened over the summer.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.b] Faced with the refugee flow and a failed ultimatum, East Germany sealed the border. The Wall solved the GDR's problem but became the great symbol of a divided Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.0] East German forces under Walter Ulbricht began building the Berlin Wall overnight on 12–13 August 1961, with Soviet approval. At first it was barbed wire, then concrete, and it closed the border between the two halves of the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.1] In October 1961 US and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, but both sides drew back.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.2] The Western powers protested but did not use force against the Wall. They accepted a divided city as better than war, which Khrushchev had judged correctly.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.3] Between 1961 and 1989 the Wall and the border fortifications were guarded, and people who tried to cross were shot or arrested.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.4] The refugee flow stopped and the GDR stabilised, and Soviet leaders regarded the Wall as a means of doing exactly that.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.5] On 26 June 1963 Kennedy spoke in West Berlin and used the words 'Ich bin ein Berliner' to show Western support.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.6] The Wall stood until 9 November 1989 and was the symbol of the divided Europe until then, because it showed that people had to be kept in by force.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.b] The Cuban Missile Crisis was the closest the Cold War came to nuclear war, and it was resolved by a compromise.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.0] Khrushchev wanted to protect Castro after the Bay of Pigs invasion of April 1961, a failed US-backed invasion by Cuban exiles, not an invasion by American troops.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.1] He also wanted to balance American Jupiter missiles in Turkey and Italy, and to make up for Soviet inferiority in long-range missiles, since medium-range missiles in Cuba could reach much of the USA. Possibly he also wanted leverage over Berlin.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.2] Photographs from a U-2 flight of 14 October were shown to Kennedy on 16 October 1962 and revealed Soviet medium-range missiles being installed in Cuba.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.3] Kennedy's advisers (ExComm, the Executive Committee of the National Security Council) weighed an air strike against a naval blockade and chose the blockade.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.4] Kennedy announced a naval 'quarantine' of Cuba on 22 October 1962 and demanded the removal of the missiles. He avoided the word blockade because a blockade is an act of war.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.5] For about a week the two superpowers were on the brink of war.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.6] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed to withdraw the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.7] The secret part of the deal was a US promise to remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey, which was done in 1963 and not made public at the time.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.b] For Europeans the crisis was a warning, because their fate seemed to be settled by the two superpowers while they looked on.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.0] The European allies were informed, not consulted. Kennedy sent former secretary of state Dean Acheson to brief President de Gaulle in Paris, and de Gaulle gave firm support.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.1] British Prime Minister Harold Macmillan was in regular contact with Kennedy during the crisis.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.2] Europeans feared that the Soviets would answer a move on Cuba with a move on Berlin, so the crisis showed that Berlin and Cuba were linked.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.3] The crisis made West European leaders more aware that decisions on nuclear war would be taken in Washington and Moscow, which strengthened de Gaulle's case for an independent French deterrent.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.4] Khrushchev's prestige was damaged at home and in China, because the retreat looked like a defeat.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.5] Both sides then sought safeguards: the Moscow–Washington hotline (1963) and the Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed on 5 August 1963.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.b] Each superpower relied on nuclear weapons to deter attack, and the arms race under Khrushchev made that deterrence ever more dangerous.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.0] Nuclear deterrence means preventing attack by holding enough nuclear weapons to destroy an aggressor even after its first strike, a condition later called mutually assured destruction (MAD), a 1960s term.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.1] In the mid-1950s the USA followed 'massive retaliation' (the threat of an all-out nuclear response, set out by John Foster Dulles in 1954). NATO shifted to 'flexible response', a range of possible responses, in 1967.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.2] The Soviet Union tested the Tsar Bomba, about 50 megatons, on 30 October 1961, the largest nuclear explosion ever set off.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.3] Western Europe depended on the US nuclear umbrella under NATO, yet there were doubts about whether the USA would risk its own cities to defend Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.4] Britain tested its first atomic bomb in 1952 and France in 1960, as part of an independent deterrent. France left NATO's integrated military command in 1966.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.b] The fear that deterrence could fail led to limited agreements. Each was a step, but none ended the arms race.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.0] The Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed in Moscow on 5 August 1963 by the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union, banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.1] It did not ban underground tests, and France and China did not sign it, so its effect was limited.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.2] The Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968 is a different treaty: non-nuclear states promised not to acquire nuclear weapons, while the nuclear powers agreed to pursue disarmament.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.3] SALT I was signed in Moscow on 26 May 1972 by Nixon and Brezhnev. It limited anti-ballistic missile systems (the ABM Treaty) and placed an interim limit on strategic missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.4] SALT II was signed in Vienna in June 1979 but was never ratified by the US Senate, and it was shelved after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.b] Détente means the easing of tension, from about 1969 to the late 1970s, through summits, arms-control treaties and treaties on European borders. It came about because both sides had reasons to want it.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.0] Détente is usually dated from about 1969, with Nixon, Brezhnev and Brandt, and not from 1963. The hotline and Test Ban Treaty were its forerunners.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.1] The shock of Cuba had shown both sides how close nuclear war could come, and the cost of the arms race was heavy.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.2] By about 1970 the two sides had rough nuclear parity, so neither could expect to win an arms race.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.3] The Soviet Union wanted Western trade and technology and recognition of the post-war borders.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.4] The USA was entangled in Vietnam, and the Sino-Soviet split gave Moscow another reason to ease tension in the West.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.5] West Germany wanted to ease the division of Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.b] West Germany led the way in détente with Ostpolitik, a policy of reaching agreements with the East. It was Brandt's policy, not Adenauer's, and it settled many of the questions left by the war.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.0] Willy Brandt, West German Chancellor from 1969 to 1974, pursued Ostpolitik to improve relations with Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.1] The Treaty of Moscow of 12 August 1970 renounced the use of force between the Federal Republic and the Soviet Union, so it removed a fear of war from the German question.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.2] The Treaty of Warsaw of December 1970 recognised the Oder–Neisse line as Poland's western border, and Brandt knelt at the Warsaw Ghetto memorial, a gesture of remorse that won wide attention. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1971.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.3] The Four Power Agreement on Berlin of 3 September 1971 guaranteed access between West Berlin and West Germany and confirmed the Western presence in the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.4] The Basic Treaty of December 1972 established relations between the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic, which led to both joining the United Nations in 1973.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.b] The Helsinki conference was the high point of détente. It gave each side something it wanted, and over time the Western gain proved the more important.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.0] The Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe produced the Helsinki Final Act on 1 August 1975, signed by 35 states including the USA, Canada and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.1] It treated the post-war borders in Europe as inviolable, a Soviet gain, because Moscow had long wanted its control of Eastern Europe accepted.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.2] It committed the signatories to respect human rights, a Western gain, and later Eastern dissidents used this commitment to press their governments.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.3] It was not a peace treaty and was not legally binding, so it relied on the signatories' political commitment.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.b] Détente broke down in the late 1970s. Its end opened the second Cold War, a phase of renewed confrontation.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.0] Soviet forces invaded Afghanistan in December 1979, which ended détente because it convinced the USA that Moscow was expansionist again.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.1] The USA responded with a grain embargo in January 1980 and, with some allies, a boycott of the Moscow Olympics in 1980.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.2] SALT II, signed in June 1979, was shelved and never ratified by the US Senate, so the arms limits it would have set never took effect.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.3] Détente had collapsed because each side read it differently and the arms building had continued.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.b] A new arms race in Europe led to the deployment of intermediate-range missiles in the early 1980s, and it provoked mass protest.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.0] The Soviet Union deployed SS-20 intermediate-range missiles aimed at Western Europe from the late 1970s, which threatened Western European cities.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.1] NATO's double-track decision of 12 December 1979 agreed to deploy Pershing II and ground-launched cruise missiles unless talks with the Soviet Union succeeded.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.2] The talks failed. Deployment began in November 1983 and the Soviet delegation walked out of the Geneva arms talks that month.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.3] Large peace demonstrations took place in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands in 1981–83 against the new missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.4] One example was the Greenham Common women's peace camp in Berkshire, England, which began in 1981.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.b] Ronald Reagan's election brought a harder line towards Moscow. This raised tension to its highest point since Cuba before talks reversed it.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.0] Reagan was elected in November 1980. In March 1983 he called the Soviet Union an 'evil empire', and he announced the Strategic Defense Initiative ('Star Wars') in the same month.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.1] In November 1983 NATO held the exercise Able Archer 83. Soviet fears of a surprise attack were heightened, though historians still debate how alarmed Soviet leaders really were.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.2] Mikhail Gorbachev became Soviet leader in March 1985, which opened a new phase because he was willing to negotiate real cuts.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.3] At the Reykjavik summit in October 1986 the two leaders discussed deep cuts in weapons.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.4] The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was signed in Washington in December 1987 and removed the Euromissiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.b] Examiners ask which events changed the course of the Cold War, so know the order and what each one changed.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.0] Berlin Blockade, 1948–49: confirmed the division of Germany and led to NATO.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.1] Stalin's death in 1953 began a more flexible phase, and the Korean armistice followed in July 1953.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.2] Hungary, November 1956: the crushing of the uprising showed that the Soviet Union would use force to keep its bloc together.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.3] Berlin Wall (1961) and Cuba (1962) were the high points of confrontation, after which both sides sought safeguards such as the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.4] Prague Spring: Warsaw Pact forces invaded Czechoslovakia in August 1968, confirming the Brezhnev Doctrine, the Soviet claim that it could intervene to keep communism in power in the bloc, but it did not end détente.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.5] Helsinki, 1975: the high point of détente, which gave dissidents in the East a human rights standard.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.6] Afghanistan and the double-track decision, 1979, ended détente and began the second Cold War, and Reagan's election in 1980 hardened it.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.7] Gorbachev from 1985, the Reykjavik summit of October 1986 and the INF Treaty of 1987 reversed the arms race, and 1989 ended Soviet control of eastern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.b] Khrushchev's removal showed that the party, not the leader, held the final power. It was also unusual, because he was not killed or imprisoned.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.0] On 14 October 1964 the Central Committee removed him from the posts of First Secretary and chairman of the Council of Ministers. He had been on holiday on the Black Sea coast and was recalled.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.1] Leonid Brezhnev became First Secretary and Alexei Kosygin became chairman of the Council of Ministers, so the two posts were again held by different men.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.2] His critics cited the Cuban retreat, the failing agriculture, the repeated reorganisations that angered party officials, and his erratic personal style.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.3] He was pensioned off and lived in retirement, largely at a dacha (country house) outside Moscow, until his death in 1971. He was the first Soviet leader removed without being killed or imprisoned.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.4] His fall confirmed that the Soviet leadership would not tolerate a single figure dominating policy, which shaped the more collective style of the Brezhnev years.
[hist-eur4-s2.25.b] Détente is judged by what it achieved in treaties and what it failed to change in the arms race.
[hist-eur4-s2.25.0] Achievements: arms limits in SALT I, the German treaties such as the Moscow Treaty and the Basic Treaty, and Helsinki's human rights standard.
[hist-eur4-s2.25.1] Limits: arms building continued despite the agreements, and each side read détente differently, since the West hoped for restraint and the Soviet Union for recognition of its gains.
[hist-eur4-s2.25.2] Détente ended in 1979 with Afghanistan and the Euromissiles, which suggests it had never settled the basic rivalry.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.b] Khrushchev's record is judged by contrasting his bold moves with the setbacks that ended his rule, and a stated verdict such as 'partly successful' scores better than a narrative.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.0] At home, his achievements were de-Stalinisation, the release of prisoners, the housing programme and the space successes of Sputnik and Gagarin.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.1] At home, his failures were the Virgin Lands crisis, the reversed reorganisations, Novocherkassk and promises that went unkept.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.2] Abroad, his achievements were peaceful coexistence, the Austrian State Treaty, the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.3] Abroad, his failures were Hungary, the failed Berlin ultimatum, the Wall (a propaganda defeat), the Cuban retreat and the split with China.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.4] Compared with Gorbachev: both were reformers from above who lost power. Khrushchev kept the one-party system and the bloc by force, while Gorbachev let both go.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.5] Contemporary Western views often saw a bully and gambler, and his Soviet colleagues attacked a schemer whose 'hare-brained schemes' and boasting were reckless.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.6] Later historians judge him as a reformer who made the system less brutal and opened it, but never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s9: 9. Marriage, the family and the changing role of women; affluence, leisure time and the consumer society
[hist-eur4-s9.0.b] To understand the changes after 1945, you need to know where families and women started from. Europe was damaged and short of homes, but its economy was about to grow fast, and that growth paid for most of what follows.
[hist-eur4-s9.0.0] The Second World War left Europe devastated, with serious housing shortages and millions of men returning from military service, so families had to be rebuilt and homes found for them.
[hist-eur4-s9.0.1] Western Europe's post-war economic growth is what paid for rising incomes, welfare states (governments providing benefits such as family allowances) and the consumer goods that filled homes in the 1950s and 1960s.
[hist-eur4-s9.0.2] Before 1945 most European women had few legal rights in marriage and were paid less than men, which is why later reforms of family law and pay mattered so much.
[hist-eur4-s9.0.3] Women had gained the vote only recently in many countries: French women voted for the first time in 1945, the right having been granted by ordinance in 1944, and Italian women voted in 1946, the right having been granted in 1945.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.b] De Beauvoir was a French philosopher and writer whose 1949 study The Second Sex became a founding text of modern feminism. Feminism is the argument for equal rights and opportunities for women, and a challenge to the roles society assigns them.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.0] She was born in Paris on 9 January 1908 and died there on 14 April 1986. She was a philosopher and writer and the partner of Jean-Paul Sartre, with whom she shared a close intellectual life for decades.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.1] She passed the highly competitive agrégation (the French state examination for teaching posts) in philosophy in 1929, placed second to Sartre and, at 21, the youngest candidate to have passed. She then taught for some years.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.2] The Second Sex (Le Deuxième Sexe) was published in two volumes in 1949 and translated into English in 1953. It argued that women were treated as the 'Other' while men were taken as the norm by a male-centred society.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.3] She argued that women's inferior position was created by history, law, religion and education, not by biology, so it could be changed, and that economic independence was the key for women.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.4] Her best-known line, from The Second Sex, is "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." It separated biological sex from the roles society gives women, which is the idea behind the word gender.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.5] She lived through the Second World War and the Occupation of Paris as a witness. Later she became a participant in the feminist movement of the 1970s, joining the Women's Liberation Movement (MLF) in France and campaigning for the legalisation of abortion.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.6] In April 1971 she signed the Manifesto of the 343, in which women declared that they had had abortions, and she co-founded the pro-choice group Choisir with the lawyer Gisèle Halimi. She did not sponsor the 1975 abortion law.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.7] Contemporaries were divided. The Vatican placed her works, including The Second Sex, on the Index of Forbidden Books in 1956, and male intellectuals on both left and right attacked or mocked it, yet younger women read it as a guide to their own position.
[hist-eur4-s9.1.8] Historians now treat the book as a foundational text of second-wave feminism. Later critics questioned its assumption of a universal experience of womanhood, pointing to differences of class and race.
[hist-eur4-s9.2.b] After 1945 marriage and childbirth rose, and a domestic model of family life became the norm in much of Western Europe. Governments, churches and the media all backed it.
[hist-eur4-s9.2.0] The baby boom (a sharp rise in the number of births) in Western Europe ran from about 1946 to the mid-1960s, with birth rates higher than in the 1930s. Marriage was also earlier and more common than before the war.
[hist-eur4-s9.2.1] The nuclear family, meaning two parents and their children, with a male breadwinner and a housewife-mother, became the ideal.
[hist-eur4-s9.2.2] Housing shortages after the war meant that many young couples lived with relatives, so public housing programmes were used to increase family accommodation.
[hist-eur4-s9.2.3] Welfare states paid family allowances (regular payments to families with children).
[hist-eur4-s9.2.4] The emphasis on the housewife and mother reflected a wish to restore stability after the war, so conservative parties and churches promoted the family as the basis of society.
[hist-eur4-s9.2.5] Cinema, magazines, television and advertising presented the ideal of the happy housewife surrounded by labour-saving appliances, which reinforced the idea that her place was in the home.
[hist-eur4-s9.2.6] Inside marriage, women were legally unequal. In France a law of 13 July 1965 allowed a married woman to work and open a bank account without her husband's permission, which shows that she needed it until then.
[hist-eur4-s9.2.7] In West Germany the marriage law kept a clause until 1977 that a wife was to run the household and could work only if that was compatible with her family duties.
[hist-eur4-s9.3.b] From the 1950s more married women took paid jobs, but they were concentrated in low-paid work. Equal pay laws were the first real attempt to close the gap.
[hist-eur4-s9.3.0] The number of married women in paid employment rose steadily from the 1950s, especially in service work and clerical (office) work, because these sectors grew as economies expanded.
[hist-eur4-s9.3.1] Women's work was mostly in low-paid jobs such as teaching, nursing, secretarial work and factory assembly, and women were paid less than men and barred from many professions.
[hist-eur4-s9.3.2] Article 119 of the Treaty of Rome (1957) required equal pay for equal work for men and women, but at first it was weakly enforced.
[hist-eur4-s9.3.3] In the second Defrenne case (Defrenne v Sabena), decided on 8 April 1976, the European Court of Justice ruled that Article 119 had direct effect, which means individual women could rely on it in their national courts.
[hist-eur4-s9.3.4] In Britain the Equal Pay Act 1970 came into force on 29 December 1975, alongside the Sex Discrimination Act 1975.
[hist-eur4-s9.4.b] Second-wave feminism of the 1960s and 1970s demanded equal pay, nurseries and an end to confinement in the home. A central demand was control over childbearing, and the pill and new laws made this possible. This is different from first-wave feminism, which campaigned mainly for the vote.
[hist-eur4-s9.4.0] The contraceptive pill was approved in the USA in 1960 and became widely used in Western Europe in the 1960s, so women could now plan whether and when to have children.
[hist-eur4-s9.4.1] France legalised contraception with the Neuwirth Law of December 1967, named after Lucien Neuwirth. Do not confuse it with the Veil Law on abortion.
[hist-eur4-s9.4.2] The Veil Law of 17 January 1975 legalised abortion in France within the first ten weeks of pregnancy. It was sponsored by the health minister Simone Veil.
[hist-eur4-s9.4.3] Elsewhere, Britain's Abortion Act of 1967 legalised abortion on medical grounds in England, Scotland and Wales, but not Northern Ireland. Italy legalised abortion in 1978, and West Germany reformed its abortion law in the 1970s.
[hist-eur4-s9.4.4] The Catholic Church opposed contraception and abortion. Pope Paul VI's encyclical (a formal papal letter) Humanae Vitae in 1968 reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception.
[hist-eur4-s9.4.5] Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963), an American book, helped start second-wave feminism by arguing that confinement to the home left women unfulfilled. The Mouvement de libération des femmes (MLF) was formed in France in 1970.
[hist-eur4-s9.4.6] The Manifesto of the 343, published in Le Nouvel Observateur in April 1971, carried the names of 343 women who declared that they had had abortions, so it called publicly for legalisation. Choisir, a pro-choice group founded in 1971, continued the campaign.
[hist-eur4-s9.5.b] From 1969 divorce became easier in several Western European countries, and family law moved from the husband as head of the household towards equality between spouses. Change came at very different speeds.
[hist-eur4-s9.5.0] The Divorce Reform Act of 1969 in England and Wales took effect in 1971 and made irretrievable breakdown of the marriage the sole ground for divorce.
[hist-eur4-s9.5.1] Italy legalised divorce in December 1970 under the Fortuna-Baslini law. In a referendum on 12 May 1974 voters chose to keep it, with about 59 per cent against repeal.
[hist-eur4-s9.5.2] Italy's family-law reform of 1975 gave spouses equal rights, replacing the idea of the husband as head of household.
[hist-eur4-s9.5.3] West Germany's marriage and family law reform of 1 July 1977 introduced divorce by breakdown instead of fault, so one partner no longer had to be proved guilty. It also dropped the clause that a wife was to run the household.
[hist-eur4-s9.5.4] Later and slower cases include Spain, which legalised divorce in 1981, and Ireland, whose constitution banned it, approved divorce only by a narrow referendum vote in November 1995.
[hist-eur4-s9.5.5] Divorce rates rose across the region in the 1970s, and single-parent households became a growing category.
[hist-eur4-s9.5.6] The rise in divorce and in working mothers led to debate about the future of the family, with conservatives blaming the changes on permissiveness.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.b] Economic growth raised incomes in the 1950s and 1960s. Families could now buy goods once thought luxuries, and this created a consumer society, one built around buying and owning goods, which critics soon attacked.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.0] Households acquired consumer durables (goods that last for years), such as refrigerators, washing machines and televisions. These saved housework and changed daily routines.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.1] Cars became affordable: the Volkswagen Beetle, mass-produced in West Germany from the late 1940s, was a huge success in the 1950s and 1960s, the Citroën 2CV was launched in 1948, the Fiat 500 in 1957 and the Mini in 1959.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.2] Self-service supermarkets spread across Western Europe in the 1960s, and they changed how families shopped and the goods available to them.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.3] Hire purchase (paying for goods in instalments) and consumer credit made it possible for families to buy goods before they could afford them outright.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.4] Advertising and television encouraged consumption. Television spread rapidly in the 1950s and 1960s, becoming the centre of the home and the main form of evening leisure.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.5] Vance Packard in The Hidden Persuaders (1957) argued that advertising manipulated consumers.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.6] John Kenneth Galbraith in The Affluent Society (1958) argued that private affluence went with public squalor, meaning rich households but poor public services.
[hist-eur4-s9.6.7] Herbert Marcuse in One-Dimensional Man (1964) argued that consumer society encouraged conformity and suppressed critical thought. The French Situationists, whose leader Guy Debord wrote The Society of the Spectacle (1967), also attacked the 'spectacle' of consumption.
[hist-eur4-s9.7.b] Higher incomes came with more free time. Together they created mass leisure industries, above all mass tourism to the Mediterranean.
[hist-eur4-s9.7.0] Working hours fell and paid holidays grew. In France paid holiday was two weeks from 1936, a third week was added in 1956 and a fourth in 1969, so workers had more time away from work.
[hist-eur4-s9.7.1] Package holidays (flights, hotel and transfers sold as one price) and charter flights to the Spanish coast (the Costa Brava and Costa del Sol), the Balearic islands, Italy and Greece boomed from the 1960s and brought the Mediterranean within reach of workers from northern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s9.7.2] Club Méditerranée (Club Med), founded in 1950, offered all-inclusive holidays.
[hist-eur4-s9.7.3] Car ownership made domestic holidays and weekend trips possible, and camping and caravanning became popular in Britain and Scandinavia.
[hist-eur4-s9.7.4] Leisure at home and nearby included spectator sport, cinema, television, record-buying and home improvement, which show the rise of mass leisure industries.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.b] In the communist states of Eastern Europe, women's employment and legal equality were promoted by the state, but there was little consumer choice and no free feminist movement. Comparing East and West is a good way to show what these changes meant.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.0] In the East many more women went out to work, backed by state childcare and the expectation that they would work. Communist states promoted equal rights in law.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.1] Domestic responsibilities for housework and childcare remained largely with women, who therefore carried a double burden of paid work and housework.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.2] East Germany offered state nurseries, paid maternity leave and a 'baby year' from 1976. Abortion was legalised there in 1972, and the divorce rate was high.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.3] In the Soviet Union abortion was legalised again in 1955, and women formed a large part of the workforce, including doctors and engineers. Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space in June 1963, an achievement, but women still did most of the housework.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.4] There was no independent feminist movement in the East, so the Western movement of the 1960s and 1970s had no equivalent there.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.5] Consumer goods in the East were scarcer and of lower quality, so the consumer society was far less developed than in the West. East German cars such as the Trabant and Wartburg had waiting lists of many years.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.6] East German holidays were organised by the trade union federation in union holiday homes and Baltic resorts, and Black Sea resorts served the Soviet bloc, while travel to the West was restricted.
[hist-eur4-s9.8.7] East Germans could watch West German television, so they were conscious of the gap in goods.
[hist-eur4-s9.9.b] Historians debate whether the changes of the period liberated women and families or extended consumer pressures into private life. Good answers set achievements against limits and give a judgement.
[hist-eur4-s9.9.0] Achievements: legal and employment equality, access to contraception and abortion in many states, easier divorce and more choice in marriage.
[hist-eur4-s9.9.1] Limits: women still bore most domestic work, a pay gap persisted, many women worked in low-paid jobs, and gains were uneven across countries and social classes. In the East there was equality in law but not at home.
[hist-eur4-s9.9.2] Consumerism brought higher living standards and more leisure, but critics argued that it encouraged conformity, debt through credit and pressure to buy, and that it tied family life to paid work.
[hist-eur4-s9.9.3] Contemporaries disagreed: churches and conservatives defended the traditional family, while feminists demanded new rights.
[hist-eur4-s9.9.4] Later historians see the 1960s and 1970s as a turning point, but they stress that the gains were uneven.
