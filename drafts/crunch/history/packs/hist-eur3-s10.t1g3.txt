TARGET hist-eur3-s10: 10. Society during World War II: the Home Front, rationing, evacuees, refugees, collaboration and resistance
[hist-eur3-s10.0.b] In the Second World War the fighting was not confined to soldiers on distant fronts. Whole populations and economies were put to work for the war, and civilians became targets, so what ordinary people did and endured is part of the history of the war itself.
[hist-eur3-s10.0.0] Total war means a war in which a state mobilises its whole population and economy, and in which civilians become targets. This is why the Second World War, more than the First, made the Home Front decisive: factories, farms, food supplies and morale mattered alongside the fighting.
[hist-eur3-s10.0.1] For civilians the war ran in stages. Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939. The Phoney War followed (months with little fighting), then the fall of France (armistice 22 June 1940).
[hist-eur3-s10.0.2] The Battle of Britain and the Blitz came next, and by 1942 most of Europe was occupied. The Allied bombing offensive and liberation in 1944-45 came last.
[hist-eur3-s10.0.3] Before 1939 governments expected air attack on cities to cause huge casualties and panic. This fear is why Britain planned evacuation, shelters and the blackout in advance.
[hist-eur3-s10.0.4] Nazi racial ideology ranked peoples, so occupation in the east was far harsher than in the west. This shaped rationing, forced labour and resistance, as the later blocks show.
[hist-eur3-s10.1.b] Britain's government expected German bombers to devastate the cities, so it planned to move the people it thought most at risk, above all children, out to the countryside before the war began. The first movement took place in the first days of September 1939.
[hist-eur3-s10.1.0] Operation Pied Piper, the government's evacuation scheme from London, Birmingham, Liverpool, Manchester and other big cities, began on 1 September 1939. It moved schoolchildren with their teachers, mothers with young children, expectant mothers and the disabled, because these groups were judged most at risk and least able to protect themselves.
[hist-eur3-s10.1.1] Evacuees travelled by train,  with luggage labels on their coats and gas masks in boxes. The government organised billeting (the placing of evacuees in private homes), and householders could be required to accept them.
[hist-eur3-s10.1.2] About 1.5 million people were evacuated in the first few days, most of them children. This was a vast movement of people, planned in advance because of the expectation of air raids.
[hist-eur3-s10.1.3] Operation Pied Piper, the evacuation of civilians in September 1939, is not Operation Dynamo, the Dunkirk evacuation of troops in May-June 1940.
[hist-eur3-s10.2.b] Evacuation did not go smoothly, because it threw together families from very different backgrounds. Its results ran from lasting friendships to a new awareness of how poor much of urban Britain was.
[hist-eur3-s10.2.0] Reception was mixed. Some hosts and evacuees formed lasting bonds, while others met homesickness, culture clash and hostility. Evacuees were received unevenly in the countryside.
[hist-eur3-s10.2.1] Some middle-class hosts were shocked by lice, poor clothing, poor hygiene and unfamiliar behaviour, and by the poor health of children from poor urban homes.
[hist-eur3-s10.2.2] When the expected bombing did not come during the Phoney War, many evacuees drifted back to the cities, and most had gone home by early 1940.
[hist-eur3-s10.2.3] There were further waves of evacuation in 1940, after the fall of France and when the Blitz began, and again in 1944 against the V-1 flying bombs.
[hist-eur3-s10.2.4] Overseas evacuation was a separate scheme, run by the Children's Overseas Reception Board (CORB) in 1940, to send children to the Dominions and the USA.
[hist-eur3-s10.2.5] The overseas scheme ended after the liner City of Benares was sunk by a U-boat in September 1940, because 77 of the 90 CORB children on board died.
[hist-eur3-s10.2.6] Evacuation showed the comfortable classes the poverty of the inner cities. Reformers used its social findings to argue for better health and education services, so it strengthened the case for social reform after the war.
[hist-eur3-s10.3.b] Britain imported much of its food, and German U-boats sank the merchant ships that carried it. Rationing, a fixed allowance for each person, shared scarce food fairly and helped keep prices down.
[hist-eur3-s10.3.0] Each household registered with a shopkeeper and received ration books. Shoppers handed in coupons as well as money, so everyone had a fixed weekly allowance whatever their income.
[hist-eur3-s10.3.1] Petrol was rationed from September 1939. Bacon, butter and sugar were rationed on 8 January 1940, and meat followed in March 1940, each with a fixed weekly allowance.
[hist-eur3-s10.3.2] Tea and margarine were rationed in July 1940, and clothing on 1 June 1941, by coupons. A points system for tinned and dried goods began in December 1941, and sweets were rationed in 1942.
[hist-eur3-s10.3.3] Rationing lasted beyond the war because shortages continued. Bread was rationed in 1946-48, though never during the war, and all rationing ended in 1954, with meat last in July 1954.
[hist-eur3-s10.4.b] The government did more than ration. It organised food supply, standardised everyday goods and asked people to grow their own, so that a nation short of imports could stay healthy.
[hist-eur3-s10.4.0] The Ministry of Food, led by Lord Woolton from April 1940, controlled food imports, supply and prices. It also ran publicity and recipes, and basic foods included National Dried Milk and the wholemeal National Loaf (from 1942).
[hist-eur3-s10.4.1] Children and mothers received extra milk and vitamins, such as orange juice and cod liver oil. British Restaurants were subsidised communal restaurants serving cheap meals.
[hist-eur3-s10.4.2] Utility goods were standard designs of clothing (from 1941-42) and furniture (from 1943) sold at controlled prices, with minimum standards of design and quality. The Make Do and Mend campaign urged people to repair clothes rather than buy new.
[hist-eur3-s10.4.3] The Dig for Victory campaign, from 1939, encouraged families to grow vegetables in gardens, allotments and parks, so that less food had to be imported and more land was ploughed.
[hist-eur3-s10.4.4] The Women's Land Army, re-formed in 1939, recruited women, called Land Girls, to work on farms. This raised home food production.
[hist-eur3-s10.4.5] Most people accepted rationing as fair. The diet of the poorest improved, and children's health held up or improved. There were still shortages, queues and a black market, run by dealers known as spivs.
[hist-eur3-s10.5.b] From September 1940 German bombers attacked British cities by night. The Blitz tested the country, and the shelters and the blackout were the everyday defences against it.
[hist-eur3-s10.5.0] The blackout, enforced from 1 September 1939 by ARP (Air Raid Precautions) wardens, hid cities from bombers. It also raised road deaths sharply in the first winter,.
[hist-eur3-s10.5.1] Anderson shelters were garden shelters, issued from 1939 and free to households below an income limit, while others paid. Morrison shelters were indoor shelters from 1941. Public shelters and London Underground stations were also used, and people took over the Underground despite official reluctance.
[hist-eur3-s10.5.2] The Blitz began on 7 September 1940 with raids on London's docks and the East End. London was bombed on 57 consecutive nights, and raids continued until May 1941, the heaviest on London being 10-11 May 1941.
[hist-eur3-s10.5.3] Other cities suffered too. Coventry was bombed on 14 November 1940, which destroyed the city centre and cathedral and killed about 550 to 600 people. Clydebank was hit in March 1941, and Belfast in April and May 1941, with about 900 killed in the raid of 15-16 April 1941.
[hist-eur3-s10.5.4] Liverpool, Birmingham, Glasgow, Bristol, Plymouth and Hull were also heavily bombed.
[hist-eur3-s10.5.5] The Blitz of September 1940 to May 1941 killed over 40,000 civilians, about half of them in London. About 60,000 civilians were killed by bombing in Britain in the whole war, though sources give 60,000-70,000.
[hist-eur3-s10.5.6] Many homes were destroyed, which left people homeless and dependent on rest centres, places where bombed-out families were given shelter and help.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.b] Surviving the Blitz depended on thousands of volunteers and on keeping up spirits. The government used propaganda and the media, and later historians have questioned how united the people really were.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.0] Civil defence was staffed by ARP wardens, the Auxiliary Fire Service, fire-watchers, and ambulance and rescue workers, many of them part-time volunteers. The Women's Voluntary Service (WVS), founded in 1938 and led by Lady Reading, provided welfare work, including meals and clothing for bombed-out families.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.1] The Local Defence Volunteers were formed in May 1940 after Anthony Eden's broadcast appeal, so that ordinary men could help resist invasion, and renamed the Home Guard in July 1940. Mostly older men and those in reserved jobs, they guarded against invasion and supported civil defence.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.2] The Home Guard stood down in 1944.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.3] The Ministry of Information used posters and slogans such as Careless Talk Costs Lives, and censorship, to shape opinion. BBC news, Churchill's broadcasts, comedy such as ITMA (It's That Man Again, starring Tommy Handley) and cinema newsreels and films carried official messages.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.4] Mass Observation, a social research organisation founded in 1937, was independent of the government. Its reports on morale and public opinion were used by the Ministry of Information.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.5] From June 1944 V-1 flying bombs, and from September 1944 V-2 rockets, fell on London and the south-east, killing thousands of civilians. About 6,000 were killed by V-1s and about 2,700 by V-2s.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.6] The 'Blitz spirit' is the idea of a united, cheerful people under the bombs, and it was contemporary propaganda. Historians such as Angus Calder (The People's War, 1969; The Myth of the Blitz, 1991) show fear, looting, class anger (especially at the East End's suffering) and complaints alongside real endurance.
[hist-eur3-s10.6.7] In short, morale bent but did not break. The shared danger and rationing also encouraged support for government planning and the welfare reforms after 1945.
[hist-eur3-s10.7.b] Fighting a total war meant directing labour. Men were conscripted, workers were steered into war industry, and women entered work and service on a new scale.
[hist-eur3-s10.7.0] Ernest Bevin, a trade union leader, was Minister of Labour and National Service from May 1940 to 1945 in Churchill's coalition. He had power to direct workers into war industries, and reserved occupations kept key workers out of the forces.
[hist-eur3-s10.7.1] Men were conscripted (called up by law) under the National Service (Armed Forces) Act of 3 September 1939. From December 1943 some conscripts, the Bevin Boys, were chosen by ballot to work in the coal mines.
[hist-eur3-s10.7.2] Women's war work grew greatly, so women worked in factories making munitions and aircraft, and in transport, farms and offices. Women also served as nurses and in anti-aircraft batteries, but not in front-line combat.
[hist-eur3-s10.7.3] The women's auxiliary services were the ATS (Auxiliary Territorial Service, the army), the WAAF (Women's Auxiliary Air Force) and the WRNS (Women's Royal Naval Service, 'Wrens'). The Women's Land Army was a civilian service for farms.
[hist-eur3-s10.7.4] The National Service (No. 2) Act of December 1941 made unmarried women and childless widows aged 20 to 30 liable to conscription (call-up) into the auxiliary services, civil defence or industry. It was later extended, though married women with young children were exempt.
[hist-eur3-s10.7.5] Change had limits. Women were usually paid less than men for the same work, and nurseries were limited. Most women expected, or were expected, to leave paid work after the war, but attitudes had begun to change.
[hist-eur3-s10.7.6] Northern Ireland was part of the UK Home Front, with Belfast's Harland and Wolff shipyard, Short Brothers aircraft works and the 1941 Blitz, but conscription was not applied there.
[hist-eur3-s10.8.b] The war made ordinary people want a better country afterwards. Beveridge's report gave that wish a plan, and the governments of 1944-48 turned the plan into law.
[hist-eur3-s10.8.0] Sir William Beveridge's report, Social Insurance and Allied Services, was published on 1 December 1942. It named five giants to be defeated: Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness.
[hist-eur3-s10.8.1] It proposed a single national insurance scheme, in return for weekly contributions, often summed up as 'from the cradle to the grave'. It also proposed family allowances, a national health service and the aim of full employment.
[hist-eur3-s10.8.2] The report sold hundreds of thousands of copies, which shows huge public interest. Churchill's coalition was cautious, while Labour was enthusiastic.
[hist-eur3-s10.8.3] The report was a plan, not law, and it did not itself create the welfare state. The coalition's Education Act 1944 (R.A. Butler) came first, then the Family Allowances Act 1945.
[hist-eur3-s10.8.4] Labour won the general election of July 1945, and Clement Attlee became Prime Minister. The National Insurance Act 1946 followed, and the National Health Service began on 5 July 1948 under Aneurin Bevan, Minister of Health.
[hist-eur3-s10.8.5] The war had made state planning and 'fair shares' acceptable, which helps explain why the reforms had such wide support.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.b] Germany's civilians faced the same demands of war as Britain's, but the Nazi regime handled them differently. It took food and labour from occupied Europe and was cautious about asking Germans to sacrifice, until defeat at Stalingrad.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.0] Rationing began at the end of August 1939, just before the war. Rations were relatively adequate until the later war years because Germany took food from occupied Europe, but they were cut in 1942 and there was severe shortage in 1944-45.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.1] Ration scales were graded by work and by race, so that German civilians received higher rations than foreign workers and occupied populations, and Jews and eastern workers received far less.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.2] Germany entered the war with a shortage of labour, yet Nazi ideology kept women's call-up limited in 1939-42, and the regime preferred foreign labour.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.3] Many German women already worked in 1939, so the number in paid work rose little in the war. Historians such as Richard Overy stress this; the older claim that German women hardly worked is wrong.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.4] After Stalingrad, where the German Sixth Army surrendered in early February 1943, Goebbels made his total war speech at the Berlin Sportpalast on 18 February 1943.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.5] The regime then, because manpower was running out, registered women for labour service and closed non-essential businesses. Albert Speer, Minister of Armaments from 1942, drove up armaments output, and the Volkssturm (a home militia of men aged 16 to 60) was formed in October 1944.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.6] Women's employment rose less than in Britain, so Britain mobilised its own people, women included, more fully.
[hist-eur3-s10.9.7] Control and terror kept order at home. The SD (the SS security service) reported on public mood, 'defeatism' was punished harshly and listening to foreign radio was a crime, and terror rose as defeat neared. The July 1944 plot was part of the opposition.
[hist-eur3-s10.10.b] Because the regime held back its own women and drafted its men, Germany filled factories and farms with foreign workers. From 1942 its cities were also bombed, and civilians suffered both ways.
[hist-eur3-s10.10.0] By 1944 about seven to eight million foreign civilian workers and prisoners of war, from all over occupied Europe, were in the German economy. Fritz Sauckel was in charge of labour recruitment from 1942.
[hist-eur3-s10.10.1] Western workers were treated better than eastern ones, because Nazi racial ideology ranked eastern peoples lower. Ostarbeiter ('eastern workers') from the USSR, and Poles, wore badges (Ostarbeiter an 'OST' badge), lived in camps and suffered hunger, beatings and high death rates from disease, overwork and mistreatment.
[hist-eur3-s10.10.2] Firms also used concentration-camp prisoners as labour.
[hist-eur3-s10.10.3] RAF area bombing of cities began in 1942 under Arthur Harris, head of Bomber Command, and was joined by American daylight raids. The Hamburg firestorm of late July 1943, killed about 40,000 (estimates 35,000 to 45,000).
[hist-eur3-s10.10.4] Dresden was bombed on 13-15 February 1945, killing up to about 25,000. German civilian deaths from bombing in the whole war are estimated at about 350,000 to about 600,000, with great loss of housing and many left homeless.
[hist-eur3-s10.10.5] Morale was damaged but the regime did not collapse, and production rose until 1944. The effect on production is debated.
[hist-eur3-s10.10.6] The Kinderlandverschickung (KLV), run with the Hitler Youth from 1940, evacuated millions of children and families from bombed cities to the countryside. It is not the Kindertransport or British evacuation.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.b] War and occupation uprooted millions of people. Whether they were fleeing, interned, exiled or freed, their fate depended on the policies of the governments they met.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.0] Before the war, about 10,000 mainly Jewish children reached Britain on the Kindertransport in 1938-39, fleeing Nazi persecution.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.1] In May and June 1940, millions of Belgians, Dutch and French fled the German advance, the French 'exodus'. They crowded and blocked the roads of northern and central France and were strafed from the air.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.2] After the fall of France, Britain interned about 27,000 German, Austrian and Italian 'enemy aliens' in 1940, many of them Jewish refugees from Nazism. Most were held on the Isle of Man, and some were shipped overseas to Canada and Australia. Most were released by 1941.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.3] The Arandora Star, carrying German and Italian internees to Canada, was sunk by a U-boat on 2 July 1940, and over 800 died.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.4] London hosted governments in exile: Poland (from 1940), Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium and Czechoslovakia. De Gaulle's Free French were in London from June 1940, and exiles served in the Allied forces.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.5] In the east, Germans were settled in annexed Polish lands and Poles were expelled, and in 1941 the Soviet Union moved people and industry eastward.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.6] In 1945 millions of displaced persons (DPs) were found: former forced labourers, prisoners of war, camp survivors and people who had fled the Red Army.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.7] UNRRA (the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration, set up in November 1943) and the Allied armies ran DP camps and repatriation, though many eastern Europeans and Jewish survivors would not or could not go home.
[hist-eur3-s10.11.8] More than ten million Germans fled or were expelled from eastern Europe in 1944-47.
[hist-eur3-s10.12.b] Most occupied countries were run, at least in part, with the help of some of their own people. To judge collaboration fairly we need to know the forms it took and the reasons people gave.
[hist-eur3-s10.12.0] Collaboration means cooperation with the occupier. It ranged from state collaboration by governments and officials, through economic collaboration by firms and workers, to collaborationism, which is ideological support for Nazism, including joining German units.
[hist-eur3-s10.12.1] Collaboration forced by circumstance must be kept apart from willing collaboration, because a worker obeying orders is not the same as a volunteer for the SS. Attentisme (waiting to see what happens) is neither collaboration nor resistance.
[hist-eur3-s10.12.2] People collaborated out of belief in Nazism or fascism, anti-communism, ambition and profit, a wish to protect their country or job, fear, and the belief in 1940 that Germany had won.
[hist-eur3-s10.12.3] Waffen-SS units recruited volunteers in western Europe, including Norwegians, Dutch, Belgians and French. Auxiliaries also served in the east.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.b] The occupied states show collaboration in different forms, from a puppet government in Norway to a 'model protectorate' in Denmark. In each case it carried consequences for Jews and for the country after liberation.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.0] Norway: Vidkun Quisling led the fascist party Nasjonal Samling (founded 1933). He proclaimed himself head of government on 9 April 1940, the day of the German invasion, but the Germans soon set him aside.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.1] Quisling was Minister-President from 1 February 1942 under the real ruler, Reichskommissar Josef Terboven, with little popular support. His name became a word for traitor.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.2] The Netherlands: the civil service and police kept working under Reichskommissar Arthur Seyss-Inquart. Anton Mussert's NSB (National Socialist Movement) cooperated, and Dutch police helped round up Jews. About three-quarters of the Jews of the Netherlands were killed.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.3] Belgium: King Leopold III surrendered the Belgian army on 28 May 1940 and stayed in Belgium. Léon Degrelle's Rexists (Rex) and Flemish nationalists collaborated, and Degrelle fought on the Eastern Front in the Waffen-SS.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.4] Denmark was occupied on 9 April 1940 and was a 'model protectorate'. The Danish government and parliament carried on until the government resigned in August 1943. This collaboration by negotiation spared Danes the worst until then.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.5] Vichy France was the largest case of state collaboration. Pétain met Hitler at Montoire in October 1940, and Laval headed the Vichy government from April 1942.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.6] Vichy helped in the deportation of Jews (the Vel' d'Hiv round-up in Paris, July 1942) and supplied labour to Germany. The Milice, formed in January 1943, carried out reprisals against resisters.
[hist-eur3-s10.13.7] After liberation there were trials and executions, purges and summary revenge. Laval and Quisling were executed in October 1945, and Mussert in 1946.
[hist-eur3-s10.14.b] Resistance was not only armed. Most of it was small acts of defiance, and it depended on help from outside that kept it supplied and connected.
[hist-eur3-s10.14.0] Resistance ranged from passive and symbolic acts, such as go-slows, jokes and refusing to cooperate, to underground newspapers, intelligence, escape lines for airmen, hiding Jews, strikes, sabotage and armed partisan war.
[hist-eur3-s10.14.1] Britain's Special Operations Executive (SOE, set up in July 1940) and later American agents supplied arms, radios and agents, because resisters could not make these themselves. The BBC carried messages to resisters, and the resistance supplied intelligence in return.
[hist-eur3-s10.14.2] The French Resistance and the Polish Home Army were both linked to Allied intelligence and special operations, which provided weapons, radio equipment and training.
[hist-eur3-s10.14.3] The cost was high. Reprisals and hostage shootings, the destruction of villages, and the torture and execution of captured resisters followed.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.b] The strength, politics and risks of resistance depended on the country and on the occupier. These four cases are the ones examiners most often name.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.0] France: the Free French (de Gaulle's forces abroad) and the internal Resistance, including the maquis (rural armed groups, swollen by evaders of the STO, the compulsory labour service of February 1943), carried out sabotage before D-Day. Jean Moulin united the movements and died in 1943.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.1] Reprisals followed, for example at Oradour-sur-Glane on 10 June 1944, where the SS killed more than 600 villagers.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.2] Poland: the Home Army (Armia Krajowa, AK), formed in February 1942, was usually described as the largest underground army in occupied Europe. It was loyal to the Polish government in exile in London, and part of a Polish Underground State with underground schools and courts.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.3] The Warsaw Uprising ran from 1 August to 2 October 1944. About 150,000 to 200,000 civilians were killed. The Red Army stood on the far bank of the Vistula and did not help, and the Germans then largely destroyed the city.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.4] Yugoslavia: Josip Broz Tito's Communist partisans fought the Germans, Italians and the Croatian Ustaša (the fascist regime under Ante Pavelić) from 1941, after Germany invaded in April 1941. They also fought a civil war, because of rivalry, against Draža Mihailović's royalist Chetniks.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.5] Britain switched its support to Tito in 1943. The partisans liberated most of the country, with the Red Army helping take Belgrade in October 1944, and Tito then ran a post-war Communist state.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.6] Germany: the White Rose was a group of Munich University students who distributed six leaflets against the regime in 1942-43. Hans and Sophie Scholl were arrested on 18 February 1943 and executed on 22 February 1943.
[hist-eur3-s10.15.7] Other German opposition included Communist groups, youth gangs such as the Edelweiss Pirates (working-class youths, some executed in Cologne in 1944), the churches, and the July 1944 plot (20 July 1944). Resistance was hard in a police state among one's own people.
[hist-eur3-s10.16.b] Some of the best-known acts of resistance came from small countries and happened in a single moment. Taken together they show both what resistance achieved and why historians weigh its limits.
[hist-eur3-s10.16.0] Norway: Norwegian commandos trained by SOE sabotaged the Vemork heavy-water plant in February 1943, and teachers refused to join a Nazi union.
[hist-eur3-s10.16.1] The Netherlands: the February Strike in Amsterdam on 25-26 February 1941 was a protest against the round-up of Jews, for example a rare mass act of public defiance.
[hist-eur3-s10.16.2] Czechoslovakia: Czech and Slovak parachutists trained in Britain attacked Reinhard Heydrich in Prague on 27 May 1942, and he died in June. In reprisal, the village of Lidice was destroyed in June 1942, with men shot and women and children deported.
[hist-eur3-s10.16.3] Denmark: after a warning of a planned round-up in early October 1943, ordinary Danes ferried about 7,000 Jews, most of the community, across the Øresund to neutral Sweden. The story that the Danish king wore the yellow star is a legend.
[hist-eur3-s10.16.4] Soviet partisans fought behind German lines. Italian partisans fought the Germans and Mussolini's Salò Republic from September 1943, and partisans shot Mussolini in April 1945. The Italian resistance became a major political force after liberation.
[hist-eur3-s10.16.5] Resistance had military value in intelligence, sabotage and tying down German troops, especially in Yugoslavia and the USSR. It also had political value: national self-respect, post-war governments formed by resistance movements, and prestige for the Communists.
[hist-eur3-s10.16.6] Its limits were small numbers until 1943-44 and heavy losses from reprisals.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.b] For French civilians, occupation meant shortage, fear of reprisal and divided loyalties. Comparing this with the British experience is a standard essay.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.0] France was divided into two zones, and the Germans occupied the southern zone as well on 11 November 1942. German soldiers requisitioned buildings and food, and curfews, identity checks and the taking of hostages made daily life uneasy.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.1] Ration cards were issued by age and work category, and bread, meat, fat and fuel were short. There were queues and substitutes, prices rose and city dwellers went hungry, so many made trips to the countryside to buy food on the black market.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.2] About 1.8 million French soldiers were taken prisoner in 1940, and well over a million were still in Germany years later.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.3] Many women had to take over family farms and businesses because so many men were away. The STO (February 1943) sent young men to work in Germany or into the maquis.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.4] Jews were persecuted and deported, which Vichy helped carry out.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.5] Allied bombing of French industry and railways before and after D-Day, and the battles of 1944, killed tens of thousands of French civilians.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.6] Attitudes changed over time. Most people accepted Pétain in 1940, but the compulsory labour service and the German occupation of the south in November 1942 turned many against Vichy and the Germans from 1942-43.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.7] Attentisme ('wait and see') was the commonest response, and only minorities actively resisted or collaborated.
[hist-eur3-s10.17.8] French women were given the vote by de Gaulle's provisional government in 1944 and first voted in 1945.
[hist-eur3-s10.18.b] Examiners expect a judgement, not a list. The three societies shared rationing and bombing but not their fate, because Britain was never occupied.
[hist-eur3-s10.18.0] Britain was bombed but never occupied, so it kept democracy and a free (if censored) press, and the war united society and led to reform.
[hist-eur3-s10.18.1] France was defeated, occupied and divided by collaboration and resistance, so it emerged from the war with bitter memories.
[hist-eur3-s10.18.2] Britain and Germany both rationed, evacuated and were bombed. Britain mobilised its own people, women included, more fully, while Germany relied on forced foreign labour and the exploitation of occupied Europe.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.b] Judgements of wartime society balance the solidarity and sacrifice of civilians against fear, division and the wide scale of collaboration. The balance has changed as historians have looked beyond wartime propaganda.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.0] Achievement: British society sustained the war effort through rationing, evacuation and civil defence, and the Beveridge Report reshaped post-war social policy. Morale held and production rose.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.1] Limit: the British Home Front had class tensions and a black market, and women were paid less than men, so change for women was limited. The 'Blitz spirit' was partly a myth.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.2] Limit: Germany's home front relied on forced foreign labour, so its civilians were protected by the systematic exploitation of occupied peoples.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.3] Achievement: resistance movements kept political opposition alive, so they gave national self-respect and provided the bases for post-war governments in France, Italy and Yugoslavia.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.4] Limit: most people in occupied Europe neither resisted nor collaborated actively. Resistance remained a small minority until 1943-44, and it brought heavy reprisals.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.5] Contemporary view: the British public generally accepted the sacrifices of the war and supported the welfare plans that followed. In Britain, the 'people's war' was a widely shared picture.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.6] Later view: Angus Calder challenged the Blitz spirit, and Robert Paxton (Vichy France, 1972) revised the post-war myth of a nation in resistance in France. Historians have since found widespread accommodation elsewhere too, and research since the 1970s shows a more complex and divided record.
[hist-eur3-s10.19.7] In short, resistance and collaboration were two ends of a range in which most people lay in between.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s6: 6. France: the Third Republic, 1920-1940, and the Vichy state
[hist-eur3-s6.0.b] The Third Republic was France's parliamentary democracy from 1870 to 1940. Its institutions and parties explain why so much of the politics of the 1920s and 1930s looked the way it did.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.0] The Republic was proclaimed in 1870, after France's defeat by Prussia, and its constitutional laws date from 1875. Democracy here means government by elected representatives answerable to the voters, with free elections, parties and civil rights, although only men could vote.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.1] Parliament had two houses: an elected Chamber of Deputies and an indirectly elected Senate, which could block laws, so reforms needed to pass both. Women had no vote until 1944-45.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.2] The President, elected for seven years by the Chamber and Senate meeting together, had a largely ceremonial role. Real power lay with the Prime Minister (called the President of the Council) and the ministers.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.3] Governments needed a majority in the Chamber and fell when they lost a vote, so ministers lived at the mercy of the deputies.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.4] No single party ever won a majority, so every government was a coalition. The right was made up of conservative and Catholic groups, and the Radical Party stood in the centre-left.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.5] The Radical Party, anticlerical (opposed to the Church's influence) and strong among small-town and rural voters, was the hinge of most coalitions because governments needed its votes whether they leaned left or right. It was the largest force in most governments of the 1920s and early 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.6] The left consisted of the Socialists (the SFIO) and, from 1920, the Communists (the PCF), founded at the Congress of Tours in December 1920 after a split in the Socialist party.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.7] French society was deeply divided: left against right, Catholic against secular (anticlerical) France, workers against employers, Paris against the provinces. These divisions shaped every crisis of the period, because each camp distrusted the other's government.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.b] France won the First World War but paid heavily for it, and its politics after 1919 were dominated by the demand that Germany pay. The first post-war government, the Bloc National, was built around that aim.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.0] About 1.4 million French soldiers were killed in 1914-1918, and the north-east, where much of the fighting took place, was devastated, with many villages and farms destroyed. A falling birth rate and heavy war debts added to the damage.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.1] Alsace-Lorraine returned to France by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919. Even so, France felt a deep fear of Germany, so it wanted the treaty enforced in full.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.2] Reconstruction of the devastated areas was financed by borrowing, in the expectation that German reparations (payments for war damage) would repay the loans. The slogan of the early 1920s, 'Germany will pay' ('L'Allemagne paiera'), summed up this hope.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.3] The Bloc National, a coalition of conservative, centre and Catholic parties, won the November 1919 election and governed until 1924. It took a firm line on Germany and against the left.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.4] Clemenceau left politics, his career ended, after he was defeated in the presidential election of January 1920 by Paul Deschanel.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.5] Budget deficits, large debts and a falling franc marked the early and mid 1920s. This made the government's reliance on German payments a financial gamble as well as a political one.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.b] When Germany fell behind with reparations, France chose force. The result showed the limits of French power and left it short of friends.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.0] Raymond Poincaré was Prime Minister from January 1922 to June 1924 and took a hard line towards Germany, which suited the parties of the Bloc National.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.1] In January 1923, after Germany defaulted on reparations (failed to pay what was due), French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, a German industrial area.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.2] Germany answered with passive resistance, refusing to co-operate with the occupiers. Britain disapproved openly and refused to take part.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.3] The Dawes Plan of 1924 reorganised German payments, so the French troops were no longer needed and were withdrawn by 1925.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.4] The occupation brought some payments, but also isolation, cost and a weaker franc, so it ended without securing full reparations.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.b] While governments argued about money, French foreign policy pursued one aim: protection against a revival of German power.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.0] France made an alliance with Poland in 1921 and a treaty with Czechoslovakia in 1924, to gain friends against Germany in eastern Europe.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.1] Aristide Briand was Foreign Minister for most of the years 1925 to 1932 and favoured agreement with Germany rather than force, in contrast to Poincaré's hard line.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.2] The Locarno treaties of 1925, which aimed to keep the peace in western Europe, and the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, in which states renounced war, were his best-known achievements.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.3] Briand shared the 1926 Nobel Peace Prize with the German statesman Stresemann.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.4] France also decided in this period to build a fortified line along the German border, the Maginot Line, as a further guarantee of security.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.b] Voters turned left in 1924, but a financial crisis undid the new government. Poincaré's return then restored confidence and gave France a few calm, prosperous years.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.0] The Cartel des Gauches, a coalition of Radicals and Socialists, won the May 1924 election, and Édouard Herriot, a Radical, was Prime Minister from June 1924 to April 1925.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.1] The Cartel fell in April 1925 after a run on the franc (people rushing to sell francs) and a financial crisis. It was called the 'wall of money': banks and investors had lost confidence in a left-wing government.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.2] A run of short governments followed in the next years, and the franc crisis of July 1926 brought Poincaré back at the head of a Government of National Union.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.3] Poincaré raised taxes and cut spending, which restored confidence, so the franc was stabilised in practice by late 1926.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.4] The 'Poincaré franc' was fixed in law in June 1928 at about one-fifth of its 1914 gold value. The late 1920s were prosperous, and Poincaré retired in July 1929 because of ill health.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.b] Governments in the Third Republic were short-lived, which made steady policy difficult. Several features of the system, working together, explain why.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.0] There were many parties and no majority party, so governments were fragile coalitions that lasted only as long as every partner agreed.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.1] The Radicals switched between left and right partners, so withdrawing their support could topple a government quickly.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.2] The Chamber held frequent votes of no confidence, and governments changed very often, many lasting only months. Ministers were often replaced after a few months in office.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.3] The President had the power to dissolve the Chamber, but it was not used after 1877, so deputies could bring down governments without facing an election themselves.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.4] The Senate blocked reforms, so reforming governments lost momentum, and financial crises, especially in 1924-26, brought governments down.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.5] The President's role was largely ceremonial, so policy was decided by the Prime Minister and the parliamentary majority, and when the majority broke up there was no one else to hold the government together.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.b] France's economy was less affected by the Depression at first than Britain's or Germany's, but the slump arrived later and was prolonged by government policy.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.0] France's economy rested on small family farms and small family firms, and the country held large gold reserves, which cushioned it against the immediate effects of the Wall Street Crash.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.1] The slump hit in earnest from 1931, with industrial production falling sharply. It then lasted longer than in Britain or Germany, and recovery was slow until the late 1930s. Farmers and small businesses suffered most.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.2] Official unemployment stayed lower than in Britain or Germany, partly because foreign workers left and some unemployed returned to the land. It still remained a serious problem.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.3] Governments kept the franc at its 1928 gold value, and France led the 'gold bloc' of countries that stayed on gold. To defend the currency they used deflation, cutting spending, wages and prices, which deepened the slump and hurt industry and farming.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.4] Pierre Laval, Prime Minister from June 1935 to January 1936, issued decree-laws in 1935 that cut public spending and salaries, so the policy of deflation continued.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.5] The franc was devalued (its gold value lowered) only under the Popular Front in autumn 1936, after which exports recovered slightly.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.6] The left (Radicals and Socialists) won the May 1932 election and Herriot was Prime Minister again, but governments fell in quick succession. Contempt for parliament and 'the politicians' grew, which prepared the ground for the leagues.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.b] As faith in parliament fell, leagues offered an alternative. These were anti-parliamentary, nationalist and mostly right-wing movements.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.0] Action française was founded in 1899 and led by Charles Maurras. It was monarchist and anti-Semitic, had opposed the republic since before 1914, and its street militants were called the Camelots du Roi.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.1] The Jeunesses Patriotes were led by Pierre Taittinger, and the Solidarité française was funded by the perfume maker François Coty, so the leagues had wealthy backers as well as street militants.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.2] The Croix-de-Feu was a veterans' league led by Colonel François de La Rocque. It was the largest league, with hundreds of thousands of members by 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.3] Francisme, led by Marcel Bucard, was a smaller group that was openly fascist, which made it different from the larger leagues that claimed only to defend the nation.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.4] Economic hardship and fear of war split French opinion into left and right camps, so the leagues found support on the right while Communists and Socialists gained from the unity forged after February 1934.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.5] Historians disagree on how far the leagues threatened democracy. René Rémond argued that they belonged to France's traditional right, while Zeev Sternhell (Neither Right nor Left, 1983) argued that fascist ideas had deep roots in France.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.6] After the leagues were dissolved in 1936, the Croix-de-Feu re-formed as the Parti Social Français, which grew into a large party.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.b] A financial scandal in January 1934 gave the leagues their chance. The riot that followed brought down a government and pushed the left to unite.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.0] Alexandre Stavisky was a swindler with political protection who sold worthless bonds through the municipal pawnshop (the Crédit municipal) of Bayonne.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.1] He was found shot dead in January 1934. The police called it suicide, but left and right both suspected he had been silenced, and the affair suggested corruption reaching into the Radical Party.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.2] The scandal forced the resignation of the Radical Prime Minister Camille Chautemps in January 1934, and the leagues used it to attack the republic as corrupt.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.3] The new Prime Minister, Édouard Daladier, dismissed Jean Chiappe, the Paris Prefect of Police, who was favoured by the right. This angered the right further.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.4] On 6 February 1934 the leagues, including the Croix-de-Feu, Action française and the Solidarité française, and veterans' groups marched on the Chamber of Deputies. Police opened fire and about 15 people were killed and over a thousand injured.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.5] Daladier resigned on 7 February, the first government brought down by street violence. A government of national union under Gaston Doumergue, with Pétain as War Minister, was formed to restore order.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.6] The left read 6 February as an attempted fascist coup. It responded with a general strike and mass demonstration on 12 February 1934, which led to left unity.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.b] Fear of fascism did what years of argument had not, and brought Socialists, Communists and Radicals together. Their alliance, the Popular Front, won power in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.0] The Socialist-Communist unity-of-action pact of July 1934 was possible because the Comintern, the international body of Communist parties directed from Moscow, had reversed the Communists' hostility to other left parties. Its Seventh Congress confirmed the popular front line in 1935.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.1] The Radicals joined in 1935, shown at the mass rally of 14 July 1935, and a joint programme followed in January 1936. The slogan was 'Bread, Peace and Liberty' ('Pain, Paix, Liberté').
[hist-eur3-s6.9.2] The Popular Front won a majority in the elections of 26 April and 3 May 1936, and Léon Blum became Prime Minister on 4 June 1936. Socialists and Radicals sat in the cabinet.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.3] The Communists supported the government in parliament but took no ministries, so they should not be listed as ministers.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.4] Blum was the first Socialist and the first Jewish Prime Minister, but not the first left-of-centre one, because Herriot, a Radical, had led the Cartel in 1924.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.5] Blum was attacked by the anti-Semitic right and was beaten up by Action française supporters in February 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.6] Three women became under-secretaries, Cécile Brunschvicg, Irène Joliot-Curie and Suzanne Lacore, although women could not yet vote.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.b] Workers expected a Popular Front government to improve their lives, and they pressed the point with strikes. The employers gave way, and the Matignon Agreements and the June laws were the biggest social gains of the period.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.0] The strikes began in May 1936 (from about 11 May), before Blum took office. The main wave and the factory occupations came after he formed his government on 4 June, and they peaked in early-to-mid June.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.1] Many were sit-down strikes, in which workers occupied their factories instead of leaving them, and about two million workers were out at the height of the wave.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.2] The pressure of the wave pushed the employers into the Matignon Agreements, reached on 7-8 June 1936 in the middle of the wave to end it. They were signed at the Hôtel Matignon, the Prime Minister's residence, with Blum chairing the talks between employers and unions.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.3] The agreements gave wage rises of about 7 to 15 per cent, union recognition (the right of unions to represent workers), collective agreements and elected workers' delegates.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.4] Laws that month brought in the 40-hour week and two weeks' paid holiday, which meant that for the first time many workers could take a holiday.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.b] Beyond Matignon, the government used its majority to reshape the economy, farming, schools and leisure, and to deal with the leagues.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.0] The leagues were dissolved in June 1936, because the government feared the threat they had shown on 6 February 1934.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.1] The Wheat Office was set up in 1936 to stabilise farm prices, which helped farmers hurt by deflation, for example wheat growers.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.2] The state gained greater control of the Bank of France, and the arms industry was part-nationalised (partly taken into state ownership).
[hist-eur3-s6.11.3] The school-leaving age was raised to 14 in 1936 by the education minister Jean Zay.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.4] Léo Lagrange, under-secretary for sport and leisure, developed a leisure and sport policy that included cheap rail tickets for holidays.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.5] A rearmament programme began in September 1936, and the franc was devalued in autumn 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.6] The plans for public works spending were limited by financial pressure and capital flight.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.b] The Popular Front's gains were soon eroded by economic trouble, and the Spanish Civil War split the coalition itself.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.0] Capital fled abroad because investors distrusted the government, and rising prices ate up the wage gains of workers.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.1] Industrial output was slow to recover, and the 40-hour week limited production, including the production of arms.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.2] Blum adopted a policy of non-intervention in the Spanish Civil War in August 1936. This angered the Communists and much of his own party, but Britain backed non-intervention and the Radicals opposed intervening, so he feared splitting the coalition and the country.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.3] Roger Salengro, the Interior Minister, killed himself in November 1936 after a campaign against him in the right-wing press.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.4] The anti-Semitic right hated Blum, and a phrase attributed to parts of the right said they preferred Hitler to Blum. The origin of the phrase is unclear, so do not treat it as anyone's recorded words.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.b] Economic pressure and the Senate's opposition ended the Popular Front's reforming period. Its social gains were then undone one by one.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.0] Blum announced a pause in reform in February 1937, because financial pressure made further spending impossible.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.1] He resigned in June 1937 when the Senate refused him emergency financial powers. Governments under Chautemps followed.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.2] Blum led a brief second government in March and April 1938, and Daladier became Prime Minister in April 1938 (he held office until March 1940), which ended the coalition.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.3] The finance minister Paul Reynaud's decree-laws of November 1938 undid the 40-hour week.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.4] The general strike of 30 November 1938 failed, and its failure ended the Popular Front era.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.b] France's defences in the 1930s reflected memories of the First World War and a fear of losing another generation of young men. The result was a defensive strategy with fatal weaknesses.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.0] The Maginot Line was named after André Maginot, the War Minister. A law funding it passed in January 1930, and it was built mainly in the 1930s, so it was funded from 1930 and built mainly in the 1930s. It was very expensive.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.1] It was a system of concrete forts and underground works along the Franco-German border, designed to hold an invader while the army mobilised.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.2] It reflected defensive thinking after Verdun and the shortage of manpower, because France could not afford the huge casualties of attacking again.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.3] It did not extend along the Belgian border, because Belgium was an ally until it declared neutrality in 1936. It was also weak opposite the Ardennes, a wooded region thought unsuitable for tanks.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.4] The army's doctrine was slow, methodical and defensive. Tanks were spread among infantry units instead of being concentrated.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.5] De Gaulle's book of 1934, Vers l'armée de métier (in English The Army of the Future), argued for a professional armoured force. It was ignored.
[hist-eur3-s6.15.b] As Hitler's Germany rearmed, France looked for allies but did not act alone. Its caution helped to make war more likely.
[hist-eur3-s6.15.0] France signed the Franco-Soviet Pact in May 1935, because it hoped for an ally against Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.15.1] When Hitler remilitarised the Rhineland on 7 March 1936, France took no action, and its reliance on Britain grew.
[hist-eur3-s6.15.2] Daladier signed the Munich Agreement on 29-30 September 1938, which showed how far France followed Britain's policy of appeasement.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.b] France declared war in September 1939 but did little fighting for eight months. When the German attack came in May 1940, the French plan collapsed within weeks.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.0] France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939, after the invasion of Poland. During the Phoney War (the drôle de guerre) there was little fighting, and the army under General Maurice Gamelin, the commander-in-chief, waited behind the Maginot Line.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.1] The Communist Party was banned in September 1939 after the Nazi-Soviet Pact, which the Communists had to defend. Paul Reynaud replaced Daladier as Prime Minister in March 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.2] The German attack began on 10 May 1940. Gamelin and Pétain thought the Ardennes forest difficult terrain for tanks, so the main French and British forces moved into Belgium on the Dyle plan.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.3] German armoured divisions came through the Ardennes and crossed the Meuse at Sedan on 13 May and reached the Channel coast at Abbeville on 20 May, cutting off the northern armies.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.4] Maxime Weygand replaced Gamelin on 19 May. The British Expeditionary Force and many French troops, about 338,000 Allied troops in all, were evacuated from Dunkirk between 26 May and 4 June.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.b] Once the northern armies were cut off, resistance collapsed. Within six weeks the government had asked for peace and the Republic was at an end.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.0] Italy declared war on France on 10 June 1940. Paris was declared an open city (undefended) and occupied on 14 June, and millions of civilians fled south in the 'exodus'.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.1] The government left Paris for Bordeaux, and Reynaud resigned on 16 June. Pétain became Prime Minister and asked Germany for an armistice on 17 June.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.2] The armistice was signed with Germany on 22 June 1940 at Compiègne, in the same railway carriage as the 1918 armistice, so that Germany's humiliation of 1918 was reversed. A separate armistice with Italy followed on 24 June.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.3] France was divided into an occupied zone, the north and the Atlantic coast, about three-fifths of the country, and an unoccupied zone in the south. Alsace-Lorraine was in practice annexed by Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.4] France had to pay the costs of occupation, at first 400 million francs a day, and prisoners of war were to stay in Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.5] The armistice army was limited to 100,000 men, and the fleet was to be disarmed but kept by France.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.6] About 1.8 million French soldiers were taken prisoner in 1940, and well over a million were still in Germany years later.
[hist-eur3-s6.18.b] The Third Republic did not fall to a coup. Its own parliament voted away its powers, which makes 10 July 1940 a key date in the story of democracy in France.
[hist-eur3-s6.18.0] After the armistice the government settled at Vichy, a spa town in the unoccupied zone, in July 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.18.1] On 10 July 1940 the National Assembly, the Chamber and Senate meeting together in the Grand Casino at Vichy, voted by 569 to 80, with about 20 abstentions, to give full powers to Marshal Philippe Pétain to draw up a new constitution.
[hist-eur3-s6.18.2] The vote did not itself create the Vichy regime. The next day, 11 July, Pétain's constitutional acts made him Head of the French State.
[hist-eur3-s6.18.3] The order of events is therefore armistice (22 June), vote (10 July), Head of State (11 July). The Third Republic had survived the Depression and the leagues, but it ended by its own parliament's vote.
[hist-eur3-s6.19.b] The speed of the defeat shocked contemporaries and has been argued over ever since. Explanations fall into two schools, military and political.
[hist-eur3-s6.19.0] Military explanations stress the army's slow defensive doctrine, slow command and communications, and the gamble that the Ardennes was unsuitable for tanks. German air power and surprise also mattered.
[hist-eur3-s6.19.1] France had roughly as many tanks as Germany, but it spread many of them among infantry units instead of concentrating them in armoured divisions as Germany did, so the Germans could break through at one point.
[hist-eur3-s6.19.2] The Maginot Line did not extend along the Belgian frontier or through the Ardennes, so the Germans simply went round it.
[hist-eur3-s6.19.3] Ernest May (Strange Victory, 2000) stresses poor intelligence, slow command and German skill rather than moral failure.
[hist-eur3-s6.19.4] Political and moral explanations stress instability, division, defeatism and 'decadence' (a decline in the nation's will). The historian Jean-Baptiste Duroselle took this view in La Décadence (1979).
[hist-eur3-s6.19.5] Marc Bloch, a historian and army officer, wrote the contemporary witness account Strange Defeat in 1940 (published 1946). He later joined the Resistance and was shot by the Germans in 1944.
[hist-eur3-s6.19.6] Contemporaries blamed the political decadence of the Third Republic, and Vichy used that explanation. Today most historians stress the military failures of May 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.20.b] Pétain was the most admired soldier in France in 1940 and became the ruler of the Vichy state. His reputation has fallen from saviour to collaborator.
[hist-eur3-s6.20.0] Pétain was born in 1856 and became known as the 'victor of Verdun' for his part in the battle of 1916, which gave him huge prestige.
[hist-eur3-s6.20.1] He was War Minister in 1934, in Doumergue's government, and ambassador to Franco's Spain in 1939.
[hist-eur3-s6.20.2] He became Prime Minister in June 1940 at the age of 84, asked for the armistice, and was Head of the French State from 1940 to 1944.
[hist-eur3-s6.20.3] He was taken to Sigmaringen in Germany in 1944 and was later tried in France. He was sentenced to death in August 1945, but de Gaulle commuted the sentence to life imprisonment, and he died in 1951.
[hist-eur3-s6.20.4] Many saw him in 1940 as a saviour who would shield France, because he had been a hero in 1916.
[hist-eur3-s6.20.5] Historians now judge him a collaborator who bore responsibility for persecution under his regime.
[hist-eur3-s6.21.b] De Gaulle began 1940 as a little-known general and ended the war as the leader of liberated France. He also built the image of France that lasted for decades.
[hist-eur3-s6.21.0] De Gaulle was born in 1890 and argued in the 1930s for armoured warfare, which was later shown to be the German method, but his ideas were ignored.
[hist-eur3-s6.21.1] He commanded the 4th Armoured Division in May 1940, and became Under-Secretary of State for War in Reynaud's government from early June 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.21.2] His appeal of 18 June 1940 led to the Free French, and he led the Provisional Government from 1944. A Vichy court sentenced him to death in his absence in 1940, so his regime was a rival to Vichy.
[hist-eur3-s6.21.3] He resigned in January 1946, and the Fourth Republic's constitution was approved by referendum in October 1946.
[hist-eur3-s6.21.4] He built the post-war image of a France united in resistance, an image later historians challenged.
[hist-eur3-s6.22.b] Blum led the Popular Front government of 1936 and was the target of the anti-Semitic right. Vichy then put him on trial for the defeat.
[hist-eur3-s6.22.0] Blum was born in 1872 and was a Socialist leader. He was Prime Minister in 1936-37 and briefly in 1938.
[hist-eur3-s6.22.1] He was the first Socialist and the first Jewish Prime Minister, and was attacked by the anti-Semitic right, which beat him up in February 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.22.2] Vichy tried him at Riom, with Daladier and Gamelin, in a trial that opened in 1942 and was suspended when it embarrassed the regime.
[hist-eur3-s6.22.3] He was held by the Germans from 1943 to 1945, near Buchenwald, and survived.
[hist-eur3-s6.23.b] Laval held office under both the Third Republic and Vichy, and he is remembered above all as the man of collaboration.
[hist-eur3-s6.23.0] Laval was born in 1883 and was Prime Minister in 1931-32 and 1935-36, so he led governments under the Third Republic as well as under Vichy.
[hist-eur3-s6.23.1] His 1935 decree-laws cut spending and salaries, and the Hoare-Laval Pact of December 1935 concerned Abyssinia.
[hist-eur3-s6.23.2] He became Pétain's deputy in 1940, but he was dismissed in December 1940. Admiral Darlan, not Laval, led the Vichy government in 1941.
[hist-eur3-s6.23.3] He returned as head of government from April 1942 until 1944, and was executed in October 1945 after being convicted of treason.
[hist-eur3-s6.24.b] Pétain's regime rejected the Republic and its values. It called itself the French State and set out to remake France through what it called the National Revolution.
[hist-eur3-s6.24.0] The regime renamed the state the French State (the État français). Pétain, aged 84, held executive and legislative power, and there was no parliament and there were no elections.
[hist-eur3-s6.24.1] The capital was the spa town of Vichy. The regime governed all France in law, except Alsace-Lorraine, and was based in the unoccupied zone, but the Germans ruled the occupied zone directly through their military administration.
[hist-eur3-s6.24.2] Instead of a single party, Vichy relied on the Legion of French Combatants, a veterans' organisation founded in August 1940. A cult of Pétain grew, with portraits and the song 'Maréchal, nous voilà!'.
[hist-eur3-s6.24.3] Vichy's own press and radio supported the National Revolution, and its youth organisations, including the youth camps (Chantiers de la jeunesse), aimed to instil discipline and loyalty to Pétain.
[hist-eur3-s6.24.4] The National Revolution blamed the defeat on the Republic, the left, the Popular Front and 'anti-French' groups, so it presented defeat as a punishment for decadence.
[hist-eur3-s6.24.5] The motto 'Travail, Famille, Patrie' (Work, Family, Fatherland) replaced the republican 'Liberté, Égalité, Fraternite'.
[hist-eur3-s6.24.6] It favoured the Catholic Church, the family and large families, women's place in the home, and a return to the land and rural life.
[hist-eur3-s6.25.b] Vichy was an authoritarian regime that silenced opposition and punished groups it blamed for the defeat.
[hist-eur3-s6.25.0] The trade union confederations were dissolved and strikes were banned. The Labour Charter of October 1941 replaced the unions, so workers lost the rights won in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.25.1] Freemasons, Communists and Jews were excluded from public life or persecuted, because the regime held them responsible for France's decline.
[hist-eur3-s6.25.2] De Gaulle was sentenced to death in his absence in 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.25.3] The Riom trial of Blum, Daladier and Gamelin opened in February 1942, to blame the Republic's leaders for the defeat, but it was suspended in April 1942 when it embarrassed the regime.
[hist-eur3-s6.26.b] Collaboration means co-operation with the occupying power. In France there was state collaboration by the Vichy government, and collaborationism, the open support for Nazism of fascist groups, was a separate phenomenon.
[hist-eur3-s6.26.0] On 24 October 1940 Pétain met Hitler at Montoire, and on 30 October he announced in a broadcast: 'I enter today on the path of collaboration.'
[hist-eur3-s6.26.1] Laval was dismissed on 13 December 1940, and Admiral François Darlan headed the government from February 1941 to April 1942.
[hist-eur3-s6.26.2] Laval returned as head of government in April 1942 and in a broadcast in June 1942 said: 'I wish for a German victory.' This led to ever closer co-operation with Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.26.3] The British attack on the French fleet at Mers-el-Kébir in Algeria on 3 July 1940, to stop it falling into German hands, killed about 1,300 French sailors. It fed anti-British feeling and helped Vichy.
[hist-eur3-s6.26.4] Vichy hoped for a better place in a German-dominated Europe, wanted the release of prisoners, saw a chance to carry out the National Revolution, and was anti-communist.
[hist-eur3-s6.26.5] Defenders argued that Pétain acted as a 'shield' protecting the French from harsher German rule, whereas critics said Vichy collaborated willingly.
[hist-eur3-s6.27.b] The occupation touched daily life in every part of France. It brought hardship and then, in 1942, ended Vichy's limited independence.
[hist-eur3-s6.27.0] France had to pay the costs of the German occupation, set at 400 million francs a day at first, and its food, raw materials and industrial output were directed to Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.27.1] Rationing, queues, shortages and the black market (illegal trading at high prices) affected daily life, because so much was sent to Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.27.2] Well over a million French prisoners of war remained in Germany for years, so thousands of families lacked fathers and sons.
[hist-eur3-s6.27.3] In 1942 the relève was announced in June: skilled workers went to Germany in return for the release of prisoners. It was voluntary and a failure.
[hist-eur3-s6.27.4] After the Allied landings in French North Africa on 8 November 1942 (Operation Torch), the Germans occupied the southern zone on 11 November, so the 'free' zone ended.
[hist-eur3-s6.27.5] The fleet was scuttled (sunk by its own crews) at Toulon on 27 November 1942, so that Germany could not seize it. Vichy lost what independence it had left.
[hist-eur3-s6.28.b] Some French people went further than the Vichy government, and wanted a fascist France allied to Nazi Germany. They are called collaborationists.
[hist-eur3-s6.28.0] These were Paris-based fascist parties, more pro-Nazi than Vichy itself.
[hist-eur3-s6.28.1] Jacques Doriot, an ex-Communist turned fascist, led the Parti Populaire Français, founded in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.28.2] Marcel Deat led the Rassemblement National Populaire, founded in 1941.
[hist-eur3-s6.28.3] The Legion of French Volunteers against Bolshevism, founded in 1941, fought on the Eastern Front, so it was an armed force of collaboration.
[hist-eur3-s6.29.b] Vichy persecuted Jews on its own initiative, and later its police helped to deport them. French people also saved many.
[hist-eur3-s6.29.0] The first Statute on Jews of October 1940 was issued without any German demand. It excluded Jews from public service and many professions, so thousands lost their livelihoods.
[hist-eur3-s6.29.1] A second statute followed in June 1941. The Commissariat-General for Jewish Questions was set up in March 1941, first led by Xavier Vallat, and Jewish businesses were 'aryanised', that is taken over. Foreign Jews were interned in camps.
[hist-eur3-s6.29.2] From 1942 Vichy's police, under Rene Bousquet's agreements with the SS, carried out arrests in both zones. Foreign Jews in the southern zone were handed over in August 1942.
[hist-eur3-s6.29.3] On 16-17 July 1942, in the Vel' d'Hiv round-up, French police arrested about 13,000 Jews in Paris, including about 4,000 children.
[hist-eur3-s6.29.4] Families were held in the Vélodrome d'Hiver cycling stadium and then sent through camps such as Drancy, a transit camp near Paris, to Auschwitz.
[hist-eur3-s6.29.5] About 76,000 Jews were deported from France and about 2,500 survived. About three-quarters of the Jews in France survived the war, many helped by ordinary people and churches.
[hist-eur3-s6.30.b] By 1943 Vichy was becoming a police state. It created its own armed force and sent French workers to Germany.
