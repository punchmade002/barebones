[hist-ire1-s5.0.b] The Famine left fewer, larger and more commercial farms, with livestock replacing tillage and a more prosperous stronger-farmer class.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.0] Population fell from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.5 million in 1851, about 5.8 million in 1861 and about 5.4 million in 1871.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.1] Tillage declined and pasture expanded, because cattle, sheep and dairy produce rose in price relative to grain and needed less labour.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.2] Cattle were exported live to Britain, a trade carried by the new railways and steamships, and the growing British towns were the market.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.3] Small holdings disappeared as cottiers died or emigrated and landlords consolidated plots; farms above fifteen acres became far more common.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.4] The stronger farmers, holding large farms on long tenure, were the main gainers; they dominated parish life and later supplied much of the support for land and nationalist movements.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.5] Agricultural labourers fell in number and remained poor, and the small farmers of the west stayed close to subsistence.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.6] The Encumbered Estates Act (1849) and the Landed Estates Court (1858) sold bankrupt estates; some new owners were commercial investors who raised rents and cleared tenants.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.7] Landlordism survived: the landlord class still owned almost all the land, and tenants had no legal security of tenure until the Land Act of 1870 gave limited protection.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.8] Evictions fell from their Famine peak but remained a threat, so tenant right stayed a central grievance of the 1850s and 1860s.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.b] The Famine hardened a pattern of late marriage, celibacy and single-heir inheritance that kept farms intact and pushed other children abroad.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.0] A dowry was money or land that a bride's family gave on her marriage; farmers used it to portion a sister, to buy out siblings or to pay debts.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.1] Farms usually passed intact to one son, so a farmer could marry only when his father handed over the holding and a bride with a dowry was found.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.2] Matchmaking was widespread, and the match depended on land and money more than on affection.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.3] Marriage was late, often in the thirties, and a large share of men and women never married, especially in the west.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.4] Younger sons and daughters without a dowry had to choose between emigration, farm labour, the priesthood or religious life, and domestic service.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.5] Late marriage and celibacy kept the birth rate down, so the population did not recover after the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.b] Large-scale emigration continued long after the Famine and became part of normal life; the diaspora is covered in detail in the notes on emigration and the Irish abroad.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.0] About a million people left Ireland in the 1850s, and emigration stayed high through the 1860s.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.1] The United States was the main destination, followed by Britain, Canada and Australia; Liverpool and Cork (Queenstown) were major ports of departure.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.2] Emigrants were young and unmarried, and women left in numbers roughly equal to men.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.3] Chain migration meant that relatives and neighbours already abroad sent news and prepaid passages, so people followed established routes to particular towns.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.4] Remittances sent home helped families pay rent and passage costs for further relatives.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.5] Emigration relieved pressure on land, but it drained the countryside of young people and fed the idea of exile in nationalist politics.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.b] The national school system, set up in 1831, became the main provider of primary education and a major influence on literacy and language.

[hist-ire1-s5.3.1] Its aim was combined literary and moral education for children of all denominations, with religious instruction given separately by each church.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.2] The state paid grants, supplied textbooks and inspected schools, and a training college for teachers opened in Dublin.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.3] The churches contested the plan: the Church of Ireland set up its own Church Education Society (1839), Presbyterians objected at first, and the Catholic bishops were divided between Archbishop Daniel Murray, who co-operated, and Archbishop John MacHale of Tuam, who opposed.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.4] In practice most schools became denominational in character, because local clergy managed them and most pupils in each district were of one faith.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.5] After 1850 Cardinal Cullen pressed for greater Catholic control of education.

[hist-ire1-s5.3.7] Lessons were in English, and the system reinforced the decline of the Irish language by teaching through English and treating Irish as no help to a career.

[hist-ire1-s5.4.b] Higher education divided on religious lines, and the dispute fed into the Synod of Thurles.

[hist-ire1-s5.4.1] They were non-denominational and had no chapel or chair of theology, so critics called them godless colleges.

[hist-ire1-s5.4.3] The Catholic University opened in Dublin in 1854 with John Henry Newman as first rector; it gave no state-recognised degrees and had few students, and Newman resigned in 1858.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.4] Trinity College Dublin, an Anglican institution, remained the main university for Protestants, and Catholics were admitted to degrees but not to fellowships.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.5] The Queen's College in Belfast served the Presbyterian middle class of Ulster and was the most successful of the three.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.b] The railway network was built from 1834 and was largely complete by about 1860, changing trade, towns and communications.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.0] The Dublin and Kingstown Railway opened in December 1834 and ran about six miles from Westland Row in Dublin to Kingstown (now Dún Laoghaire); it was the first railway in Ireland.

[hist-ire1-s5.5.2] The Great Southern and Western reached Cork in 1849, the Midland Great Western reached Galway in 1851, and Belfast was linked to Dublin in the 1850s.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.3] Ireland adopted a broad gauge of 5 feet 3 inches, a decision of the 1840s that set a different gauge from Britain.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.4] Railways cut the cost and time of moving cattle, butter, grain and fish, and tied farmers to British markets through the ports.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.5] Fairs and markets shifted to railway towns, and junctions such as Mallow, Limerick Junction and Athlone grew in importance.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.6] Newspapers, mail and ideas travelled faster, so national newspapers reached the provinces and political movements could organise nationally.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.7] Tourism grew, with the line to Killarney opening in 1853, and the rail and steamer link to Britain eased travel and emigration.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.8] Some small ports lost trade as goods moved inland by rail.

[hist-ire1-s4.0.0] Government relief was limited by laissez-faire and by the rule that Irish property should pay, so charity filled gaps in food, clothing, seed and medical care.

[hist-ire1-s4.0.3] The worst-hit western districts had the fewest resident gentry and merchants, so the areas that most needed local charity could raise the least.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.4] Newspaper reports and drawings from places such as Skibbereen, Co. Cork, in the winter of 1846–1847, especially in the Illustrated London News, made the Famine known in Britain and America and set off the great appeals of 1847.

[hist-ire1-s4.1.3] The Friends opened soup kitchens in Cork and elsewhere in the winter of 1846–1847, and gave boilers, food and money to local committees regardless of religion.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.4] American Quakers and other donors sent food shipments to the Committee, which distributed them through its network of local correspondents.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.5] The Friends refused to proselytise and kept careful accounts, which earned them trust among Catholics and lasting gratitude.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.6] From mid-1847 they withdrew from mass feeding, concluding that only the government had the resources, and turned to longer-term projects: seed distribution, fisheries and spinning schemes.
