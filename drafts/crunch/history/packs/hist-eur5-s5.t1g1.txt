TARGET hist-eur5-s5: 5. Africa: Nigeria, the Congo, Tanzania and Angola
[hist-eur5-s5.0.b] To understand what went wrong and right after independence, you need three starting facts: how the colonial borders were drawn, why the colonial powers let go, and how the Cold War shaped the new states.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.0] Colonial rule means control of a territory by an external power, governed for the benefit of the imperial state rather than of the people who lived there.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.1] European powers partitioned Africa in the later nineteenth century, at the Berlin Conference of 1884-85. They drew borders that cut across peoples and joined rival ones together, which is the root of later ethnic tension.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.2] The Second World War weakened the standing of Britain, France, Belgium and Portugal, and African nationalism (the demand for self-government) grew after 1945. Ghana became independent on 6 March 1957 and led the wave.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.3] About seventeen African states became independent in 1960 alone, so independence came to many territories in a very short time.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.4] The Cold War (the rivalry between the USA and the USSR) meant both superpowers competed for influence in the new states. This shaped events in the Congo and Angola in particular.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.b] Several terms recur in every case in this subsection. Knowing exactly what each means as historians use it lets you write precise answers.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.0] Tribalism was the colonial officials' term for ethnic loyalty (loyalty to your own ethnic group). Colonial policy often defined, fixed and exploited ethnic identities, so the label described something that colonial rule itself had hardened.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.1] The (British) Commonwealth is an association of Britain and its former colonies, and many of them joined on independence, which kept a link with Britain after rule had ended.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.2] Indirect rule meant governing through existing local rulers instead of replacing them. Lord Lugard used it in Nigeria, where the colonial government ruled much of the north through its emirs (Muslim rulers).
[hist-eur5-s5.1.3] A federation is a state in which regions keep their own governments and powers under a central government. Nigeria's regions are the main example.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.4] Secession means a region breaking away from a state to form its own. Katanga seceded from the Congo in 1960 and Biafra from Nigeria in 1967.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.5] The Force Publique was the Belgian Congo's colonial army, and ONUC was the UN operation in the Congo. The Estado Novo was Portugal's authoritarian regime under Salazar, in place from the 1930s until 1974.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.6] The MPLA was one of Angola's three nationalist movements. The Carnation Revolution was the 1974 overthrow of the Portuguese regime in Lisbon, and Zairianisation was Mobutu's 1973 transfer of foreign-owned businesses to Zairian owners.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.b] Nigeria began as a British creation that joined very different peoples. How Britain governed it, and how the regions were later organised, set up the rivalries that would break the country in the 1960s.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.0] Lord Lugard united the Northern and Southern territories of Nigeria into one colony in 1914. Under indirect rule, the colonial government ruled much of the north through its existing emirs.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.1] Nigeria's three main groups were the Hausa-Fulani in the north, the Yoruba in the west and the Igbo in the east. This mattered because each group was concentrated in one part of the country.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.2] The north was predominantly Muslim and the south had large Christian populations, so religion deepened the divide between north and south.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.3] The 1954 federal constitution created three regions, Northern, Western and Eastern, with Lagos as a federal territory.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.4] The regions had their own parliaments and revenue, and the regional parties drew their support on ethnic lines, which made federal politics tense.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.5] The main parties were the Northern People's Congress (NPC), the Action Group (Yoruba west) and the National Council of Nigeria and the Cameroons (NCNC, led by Azikiwe, Igbo east), each rooted in one region.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.6] Nigeria's oil came from Oloibiri in the Niger Delta, discovered by Shell-BP in 1956, and exports began in 1958. The oil lay in the Eastern Region, so it later became a source of dispute.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.b] Independence in 1960 handed power to a federation whose largest party came from the north. The first years showed both how the system was meant to work and where it was already under strain.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.0] In the federal elections of December 1959 the NPC won the most seats and formed a coalition with the NCNC, and its leader Abubakar Tafawa Balewa became federal prime minister.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.1] Nigeria became independent on 1 October 1960 as a member of the British Commonwealth, with Balewa as prime minister. The Queen remained head of state, represented by a governor-general, Nnamdi Azikiwe from November 1960.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.2] Nigeria became a republic on 1 October 1963 with Azikiwe as president, so the Queen was no longer head of state. A fourth region, the Mid-West, was also created in 1963.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.3] Regional tension grew in the early 1960s. A political crisis in the Western Region began in 1962, and the elections of 1965 were violently disputed, which weakened trust in the federal system before the army acted.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.b] In 1966 the army took over, and ethnic violence turned a political crisis into a break-up. The order of events matters, because each step pushed the Igbo-dominated east closer to leaving.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.0] 1. On 15 January 1966 army officers led by Major Chukwuma Nzeogwu killed Prime Minister Balewa, the Northern premier Ahmadu Bello and the Western premier Samuel Akintola. The coup was seen as dominated by Igbo officers, which made northerners suspicious of the Igbo.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.1] 2. Major-General Johnson Aguiyi-Ironsi became the first military head of state in January 1966. He was killed in a counter-coup in July 1966, and Lieutenant-Colonel Yakubu Gowon became head of state.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.2] 3. Massacres of Igbo people in the north in 1966, in May and again in September-October, killed many thousands. Large numbers fled to the east, which hardened Igbo feeling against the federation.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.3] 4. At Aburi in Ghana in January 1967 an attempt to settle the crisis was made, but it failed.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.4] 5. On 27 May 1967 Gowon decreed twelve states. This broke up the Eastern Region and cut the Igbo off from most of the oil.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.5] 6. Three days later, on 30 May 1967, Lieutenant-Colonel Chukwuemeka Odumegwu Ojukwu (later called Colonel) declared the east the Republic of Biafra. The federal government responded with a blockade of the east.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.b] The Biafran War was a secession and a famine crisis, not simply Nigeria against itself. Biafra had very little international recognition, but the suffering of its people drew worldwide sympathy.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.0] The war lasted from July 1967 to 15 January 1970. It caused widespread famine in Biafra, because the blockade cut off supplies.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.1] Estimates of the death toll vary widely, from several hundred thousand to over a million, most of them from starvation and disease. No single figure is reliable.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.2] Only five states recognised Biafra: Tanzania, Gabon, the Ivory Coast, Zambia and Haiti. Recognition was limited to a few states, which left Biafra isolated.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.3] Britain and the Soviet Union supplied arms to the federal government, while France gave covert (secret) support to Biafra.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.4] Many people outside Nigeria were moved by images of starving Biafran children. Joint Church Aid flew relief into Biafra, and Irish missionaries (Holy Ghost Fathers) were prominent, so supplies kept reaching the enclave.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.5] Gowon announced reconciliation, rehabilitation and reconstruction at the end of the war on 15 January 1970. He said there was "no victor, no vanquished", which aimed to bring the Igbo back into the federation.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.b] Belgium ran the Congo as a colony for profit and gave Congolese people almost no training for government. That is why independence in 1960 arrived so suddenly and so badly prepared.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.0] King Leopold II ruled the Congo Free State from 1885 as his personal possession. Forced rubber collection caused widespread abuses, which Roger Casement and E. D. Morel exposed, and this led to international protest.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.1] The Belgian government took over the colony in 1908. It was ruled by a paternalistic administration (one that treated Congolese like children to be looked after), which gave them little political role.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.2] The colony's wealth lay in minerals including copper, cobalt and diamonds. The Belgian company Union Minière du Haut-Katanga ran the mines of Katanga, mining copper and cobalt.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.3] By 1960 only a handful of Congolese had a university degree. The Force Publique had no Congolese officers, so the new state would have almost no trained administrators or commanders.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.4] Congolese political parties emerged in the late 1950s, including Patrice Lumumba's National Congolese Movement (MNC), which won the most seats in the May 1960 elections.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.5] Riots in Léopoldville in January 1959 shocked Belgian opinion, and King Baudouin soon promised independence but gave no date.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.6] The Round Table at Brussels in January-February 1960 fixed independence for 30 June 1960, only months later, which left almost no time to prepare.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.b] Within weeks of independence the new state had a mutiny, two secessions and a UN intervention. The speed of the collapse is the main evidence for how unprepared it was.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.0] On 30 June 1960 Joseph Kasa-Vubu became head of state and Patrice Lumumba became prime minister.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.1] At the independence ceremony King Baudouin praised Belgium's colonial work. Lumumba replied with a speech attacking the humiliations of colonial rule, which offended Belgian opinion.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.2] Soldiers of the Force Publique mutinied on 5-6 July 1960. Attacks on Europeans followed, so Belgium sent troops back to the Congo in July 1960.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.3] Katanga, led by Moïse Tshombe, seceded on 11 July 1960 with Belgian backing, and South Kasai declared its own state in August 1960.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.4] The UN Security Council authorised the UN operation in the Congo (ONUC) in July 1960. It had about 20,000 troops at its height and withdrew in 1964.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.5] Kasa-Vubu dismissed Lumumba on 5 September 1960 and Lumumba tried to dismiss Kasa-Vubu. Colonel Joseph Mobutu, the army chief of staff, took power on 14 September 1960.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.6] Lumumba, who had appealed to the Soviet Union for help, was confined to his residence under UN guard. He left it in late November 1960 and was captured in December.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.7] Lumumba was flown to Katanga and murdered there on 17 January 1961 with Belgian officers present. Belgium later accepted moral responsibility.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.b] Mobutu first took power in 1960 and then seized it again in 1965, this time to rule for over thirty years. His rule shows how a Cold War ally could keep a weak state together while ruining its economy.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.0] Mobutu seized power in a second coup on 24 November 1965, deposing Kasa-Vubu and putting the army in charge of the state.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.1] The country was renamed Zaire in 1971. Mobutu's authenticité campaign banned Christian first names and replaced European suits with the abacost, to stress African identity.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.2] In 1973 Zairianisation transferred foreign-owned plantations and businesses to Zairian owners, mostly Mobutu's associates. The economy collapsed as the copper price fell in 1974-75.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.3] The Popular Movement of the Revolution (MPR) was the only legal party, so political opposition was suppressed.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.4] The United States, Belgium and France supported Mobutu as a barrier to communism in central Africa. Their help repelled the Shaba invasions of 1977 and 1978 by Katangan exiles from Angola.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.5] Mobutu announced the end of the one-party state in April 1990, under pressure from protests and the end of Cold War support. He stayed in power until he fell in 1997.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.b] Tanganyika moved to independence without armed conflict under Julius Nyerere and then united with Zanzibar. It is the main example of the opportunities of independence.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.0] Tanganyika was German East Africa until 1918, then a British mandate and, from 1946, a UN trust territory. In both cases Britain governed it on behalf of an international body.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.1] Julius Nyerere founded the Tanganyika African National Union (TANU) in 1954 and led it to a majority in the 1960 elections.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.2] Tanganyika became independent on 9 December 1961 with Nyerere as prime minister. It became a republic with Nyerere as president in December 1962.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.3] Zanzibar became independent in December 1963, but a revolution in January 1964 overthrew its Arab-led government. The Afro-Shirazi Party dominated the new regime.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.4] Tanganyika and Zanzibar united on 26 April 1964, and the union was renamed Tanzania in October 1964. Zanzibar kept its own government and its president, Abeid Karume.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.5] In January 1964 the Tanganyika Rifles mutinied against British officers. Nyerere asked Britain for help, Royal Marines put down the mutiny, and the army was then rebuilt.
[hist-eur5-s5.10.b] With no dominant ethnic group, Nyerere built a national identity instead of letting ethnic politics take hold. In foreign policy he was willing to quarrel with Britain over principle.
[hist-eur5-s5.10.0] Tanzania was a multi-ethnic state with more than a hundred ethnic groups and no dominant majority, so no single group could claim to speak for the nation.
[hist-eur5-s5.10.1] Nyerere made Swahili the national language and avoided ethnic parties. A shared language and a national identity reduced ethnic conflict after independence.
[hist-eur5-s5.10.2] Nyerere broke off diplomatic relations with Britain in December 1965 over its failure to act against the white minority regime in Southern Rhodesia. The break was over Rhodesia, not over Tanzania's own affairs.
[hist-eur5-s5.10.3] Tanzania nevertheless stayed in the Commonwealth, so it kept the association while criticising British policy.
[hist-eur5-s5.11.b] Portugal refused to decolonise, so Angola's nationalists had to fight. The nationalists were divided among three movements (the MPLA, founded in 1956, the FNLA and UNITA, founded in 1966) with different regional and ethnic bases, and this division later fed a civil war.
[hist-eur5-s5.11.0] Portugal under the Estado Novo regime of António de Oliveira Salazar regarded Angola as an overseas province rather than a colony, and it refused to grant independence.
[hist-eur5-s5.11.1] Armed attacks in Luanda on 4 February 1961 began the Angolan war of independence. A rising in the north followed on 15 March 1961, in which many Europeans and Africans were killed and Portuguese reprisals were severe. The war lasted until 1974.
[hist-eur5-s5.11.2] The MPLA, led by Agostinho Neto and founded in 1956, was strongest among the Mbundu and in Luanda.
[hist-eur5-s5.11.3] The FNLA, led by Holden Roberto, was based among the Bakongo in the north.
[hist-eur5-s5.11.4] UNITA, founded in 1966 and led by Jonas Savimbi, was based among the Ovimbundu.
[hist-eur5-s5.11.5] The Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 in Lisbon overthrew the Portuguese regime, and the new government agreed to grant independence to Angola.
[hist-eur5-s5.12.b] Independence came in 1975 without agreement between the movements, so the anti-colonial war turned straight into a civil war that the Cold War powers fed.
[hist-eur5-s5.12.0] The Alvor Agreement of January 1975 set up a transitional government in which the three movements shared power, but it broke down into fighting.
[hist-eur5-s5.12.1] Angola became independent on 11 November 1975, with the MPLA under Neto holding Luanda.
[hist-eur5-s5.12.2] The civil war between the MPLA and UNITA and the FNLA lasted until 2002. Cuban troops and Soviet aid supported the MPLA, while South African and American support went to its opponents.
[hist-eur5-s5.12.3] Most of the roughly 300,000 Portuguese settlers left Angola in 1975, taking skills and capital with them. This left the new state short of skilled people.
[hist-eur5-s5.12.4] Oil from Cabinda and diamonds became the main sources of revenue.
[hist-eur5-s5.13.b] Independence did not end the link with the old rulers. Each power kept a different kind of influence, and this is what examiners mean when they ask whether the colonial powers helped or hindered.
[hist-eur5-s5.13.0] Britain and Nigeria: Nigeria kept the Queen as head of state until becoming a republic in 1963 and stayed in the Commonwealth. British companies such as Shell-BP dominated the oil industry, and Britain supplied arms to the federal side in the Biafran War.
[hist-eur5-s5.13.1] Belgium and the Congo: Belgium kept large economic interests through the Union Minière and backed Katanga's secession in 1960. Belgium later supported Mobutu.
[hist-eur5-s5.13.2] Mobutu nationalised the Union Minière's Katanga assets in 1966-67, before reaching a settlement with it in 1969.
[hist-eur5-s5.13.3] Portugal and Angola: Portugal's refusal to decolonise until 1974 meant Angola faced a long war, and Portugal's exit left Angola with few trained administrators.
[hist-eur5-s5.13.4] Tanzania and Britain: Tanzania remained in the Commonwealth and received British aid, but Nyerere pursued non-alignment (not taking sides in the Cold War) and criticised Western economic influence. He also broke relations over Rhodesia in 1965.
[hist-eur5-s5.13.5] France kept influence in its former colonies through co-operation accords, the franc zone and military agreements. This pattern was later called Françafrique.
[hist-eur5-s5.14.b] Ethnic loyalty did not simply survive from the past. Colonial rule shaped it, and after independence politicians in several states built their support on it.
[hist-eur5-s5.14.0] Colonial rulers defined and fixed ethnic identities through census categories, languages and indirect rule. Colonial officials then used the term tribalism to describe the ethnic loyalty that this policy had helped to harden.
[hist-eur5-s5.14.1] In Nigeria, each of the three main parties drew its support from one region and one ethnic group. The army's officer corps also divided on regional lines, which contributed to the coups of 1966 and the massacres that followed.
[hist-eur5-s5.14.2] In the Congo, the leading politicians drew support from particular regions and ethnic communities. Lumumba's base was in the east, Kasa-Vubu's party ABAKO drew on the Kongo people in the Léopoldville region in the west, and Tshombe's Conakat drew on the Lunda of Katanga.
[hist-eur5-s5.14.3] Katanga's secession was partly based on the claim that the Katangan people had a separate identity from the rest of the Congo. The national government rejected this claim.
[hist-eur5-s5.14.4] Tanzania's single language, Swahili, and the policy of building a national identity were among the factors that reduced ethnic conflict after independence. This gives a contrast to Nigeria and the Congo.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.b] Independence brought real gains and serious dangers, and the balance differed from country to country. The best answers state a judgement and support it with a paired example on each side.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.0] Opportunity: independence gave Africans control of their own governments. Nyerere's Tanzania pursued national unity and African development without a civil war.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.1] Problem: the Belgian Congo's rapid independence left the new state with almost no trained administrators or officers, and the army's mutiny and the secession of Katanga followed within weeks.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.2] Opportunity: Nigeria's oil revenue, which grew rapidly after the 1973 oil price rise, gave the federal government resources for reconstruction after the Biafran War.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.3] Problem: the Biafran War and the 1966 coups showed how the federal structure could fail to hold ethnic groups together.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.4] Problem: Angola's long war of independence ended in civil war because the three movements had different ethnic bases and different foreign backers.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.5] Contemporary view: many African leaders saw independence as the end of colonial domination, while the former colonial powers often saw it as the start of a new relationship of trade and aid.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.6] Later view: historians stress that colonial borders, ethnic policies and the Cold War constrained the new states, but they also stress African agency in shaping the outcomes.
[hist-eur5-s5.15.7] Overall judgement: outcomes differed by country, with Tanzania stable, Nigeria and Angola at war and the Congo in crisis, so a balanced answer compares them.

CANDIDATES hist-eur5-s4: 4. Africa and the “winds of change”: Algeria and de Gaulle
[hist-eur5-s4.0.b] After the Second World War the old empires were harder to hold than before. Britain and France had to decide whether to fight for their colonies or let them go, and France's own recent history shaped its choices.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.0] Empires lost both the power and the will to continue: war-weariness at home, the weakness of Britain and France after the war, and hostility to empire from both the United States and the Soviet Union all made colonial rule harder to defend.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.1] The new United Nations gave colonial peoples a public place to raise their grievances, and nationalist movements (organised groups demanding self-rule) grew stronger, so every colony became a potential problem for its ruler.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.2] France was defeated in 1940 and the Vichy regime governed France afterwards, which damaged French prestige. De Gaulle's appeal of 18 June 1940, made from London, began the Free French movement that fought on.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.3] The Fourth Republic (1946–58) was unstable, with weak governments that rarely lasted long, so France found it hard to settle on a firm colonial policy.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.4] Britain's withdrawal from India in 1947 and France's defeat in Indochina in 1954 had already shown that empires could be ended, so nationalists in Africa could point to real examples.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.5] The French Union, created by the 1946 constitution, kept the empire under Paris. It was replaced in the 1958 constitution by the French Community, which gave territories internal self-government but left foreign affairs, defence and currency with France.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.b] Before the Algerian war ended, change had already begun elsewhere. Reform in French Africa and independence for Ghana and Guinea showed that independence was becoming possible.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.0] The Brazzaville Conference of January–February 1944, a Free French gathering, promised reforms for the empire but ruled out self-government, so it admitted that change was needed while keeping French rule intact.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.1] Tunisia and Morocco, France's neighbours to Algeria's east and west, became independent in March 1956. Their independence mattered to the Algerian war because both later sheltered FLN forces.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.2] The Loi-cadre (framework law) of June 1956, introduced by the minister Gaston Defferre, gave the French African territories elected assemblies and governments with wider powers, an early step towards self-government.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.3] Ghana (the former Gold Coast, a British colony) became independent on 6 March 1957 under Kwame Nkrumah, the first sub-Saharan colony to do so, which gave other African nationalists an example to point to.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.4] During an African tour de Gaulle spoke at Brazzaville on 24 August 1958 and offered the territories a choice between the Community and independence.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.5] Guinea, under Sékou Touré, rejected the 1958 constitution (and so the Community) in the referendum of 28 September 1958 and became independent on 2 October 1958, the first French colony in sub-Saharan Africa to leave. Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, Tunisia and Morocco had already left French rule.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.b] In 1960 independence came in a rush. A British prime minister gave the movement its best-known phrase, and France's Community broke up in practice.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.0] Harold Macmillan, the British prime minister, told the South African Parliament in Cape Town on 3 February 1960 that 'the wind of change is blowing through this continent'.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.1] Seventeen African states became independent in 1960, so it is known as the Year of Africa. Examples include the Ivory Coast, Nigeria and the Congo.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.2] Most French West and Equatorial African territories, along with Madagascar, became independent by negotiation in 1960 and signed co-operation accords with France on defence, currency and aid, so independence did not mean a clean break.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.3] In practice the French Community therefore broke up within two years of its creation, because its members chose independence instead of internal self-government.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.4] Britain chose negotiated transfers of power in most of its colonies. France's policy in Algeria was contested by about a million settlers and an armed nationalist movement, which made a negotiated settlement far harder.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.b] Algeria was not an ordinary colony in French eyes. It had been conquered long before, was treated as part of France itself, and was home to a large settler community, which is why giving it up was so painful.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.0] France began its conquest of Algeria in 1830. By the 1848 constitution Algeria was divided into three départements (administrative districts like those of mainland France) that were treated as part of metropolitan France.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.1] The settlers, known as pieds-noirs, numbered about one million out of about nine million people by 1954. They dominated the economy, land and local administration, so they had much to lose from any change.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.2] The French aim was assimilation: making colonial subjects French in language, law and culture. Few Muslim Algerians accepted this in practice, and most never won full equality.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.3] Most Muslim Algerians were subjects rather than full citizens. Those who wanted full French citizenship had to give up their Islamic personal status (Muslim family law), a condition few chose to meet before 1954.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.4] The Sétif and Guelma uprising of 8 May 1945 killed about 100 Europeans. French repression killed thousands of Algerians: the official French figure is about 1,500, Algerian nationalists claim 45,000, and historians generally accept a figure in between.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.5] Messali Hadj founded the Movement for the Triumph of Democratic Liberties (MTLD) in 1946 to campaign for independence. It split in 1954 between moderates and the armed nationalists.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.6] The Statute of 1947 created an Algerian Assembly with two colleges (voting groups) of equal size. Europeans, with a small number of Muslims, elected the first and the Muslim majority the second, which favoured the settlers because far fewer people elected as many members.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.b] On 1 November 1954 a new nationalist movement chose armed force. France answered with its army, and by 1957 the war was being fought in the cities, the countryside and along the borders.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.0] The National Liberation Front (FLN) launched a rising on 1 November 1954 with attacks on police and military targets across Algeria, which began the Algerian war.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.1] The French government sent conscripts (young men called up for compulsory service), reaching about 400,000 troops at the peak. Under Guy Mollet's government in 1956 the Special Powers law gave the army wide powers.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.2] The FLN held the Soummam Valley Congress in August 1956, which set up its political leadership, so the movement had an organised direction beyond its fighting units.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.3] The Morice Line, built from 1957, was a barrier of wire, mines and fencing along the Tunisian border, intended to stop arms and fighters crossing from Tunisia, where the FLN found shelter.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.4] In the Battle of Algiers (January to October 1957) French paratroopers under General Jacques Massu broke the FLN's network in the city through arrests, interrogation and torture. It was a military success won by methods that damaged France's reputation.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.b] As the war grew, both sides used methods that shocked observers. How the war was fought turned it into a political problem for France at home and abroad.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.0] French forces used torture. Henri Alleg's La Question, an account of his own torture, was banned in France in 1958, but as a first-hand account it brought the issue before French opinion.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.1] Terrorism means violence against civilians to spread fear and win a political aim. The FLN used it, with bombings in Algiers cafés from September 1956 onward, and it also killed Algerian rivals and Muslim opponents.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.2] French forces also killed civilians in reprisal operations (revenge attacks), so civilians suffered at the hands of both sides.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.3] From 1955, and especially after 1957, the French army moved more than a million rural Algerians into fenced regroupement camps as part of its counter-insurgency strategy (the effort to defeat a guerrilla rising).
[hist-eur5-s4.5.4] French military deaths numbered about 25,000. Estimates of Algerian deaths range from about 300,000 to the Algerian official figure of 1.5 million, so historians still debate the figures.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.5] Support for the FLN abroad grew. Tunisia and Morocco sheltered its fighters, Egypt supplied arms, and the UN General Assembly debated Algeria from 1955, so the war was never only a French matter.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.6] On 8 February 1958 French aircraft raided the Tunisian village of Sakiet, which damaged France's reputation with the US and the UN.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.b] By 1958 the Fourth Republic could neither win the war nor settle on a policy. A revolt by settlers and army officers in Algiers gave Charles de Gaulle his chance to return to power.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.0] Between 1946 and 1958 the Fourth Republic had more than twenty governments, which made it hard to sustain a consistent policy on Algeria and helped bring about its fall.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.1] On 13 May 1958 settlers and army officers seized government buildings in Algiers and demanded that Algeria stay French. They formed a Committee of Public Safety.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.2] The government of Pierre Pflimlin, the last prime minister of the Fourth Republic, lost the confidence of the army, and President René Coty then called on de Gaulle to form a government.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.3] The National Assembly invested de Gaulle on 1 June 1958 as prime minister, not president, because he became president only in January 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.4] Speaking to the crowd in Algiers on 4 June 1958 de Gaulle said 'Je vous ai compris' (I have understood you). At Mostaganem on 6 June he cried 'Vive l'Algérie française'.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.5] These words led the settlers to believe he would keep Algeria French, which is why they later felt betrayed when he changed course.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.b] De Gaulle's first task was to rebuild the state. The new constitution gave France the strong government the Fourth Republic had lacked, and it set out a new relationship with the colonies.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.0] A new constitution was approved by referendum on 28 September 1958, establishing the Fifth Republic with a strong presidency, which gave France stable government after the Fourth Republic's constant changes of government.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.1] The constitution also set up the French Community, which gave the colonies internal self-government but kept foreign affairs, defence and currency with France.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.2] De Gaulle was elected president in December 1958 (on 21 December) by an electoral college, a limited group of electors rather than a popular vote, and he took office on 8 January 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.b] Once in office de Gaulle moved step by step away from the settlers' hopes. Each step provoked a violent reaction from those who wanted Algeria to stay French.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.0] On 16 September 1959 de Gaulle announced that Algerians would be offered self-determination (the right to decide their own future), with a choice among independence, close association with France and full integration.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.1] Settler militants staged Barricades Week in Algiers from 24 January to 1 February 1960, a revolt against this policy that de Gaulle put down with the army.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.2] Talks with the FLN at Melun in June 1960 failed, so the war went on.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.3] On 4 November 1960 de Gaulle spoke of an Algerian Republic in the future, a clear sign that independence was now a real prospect.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.4] In the referendum of 8 January 1961 about 75 per cent of votes cast overall approved self-determination, and about 70 per cent in Algeria itself, which gave de Gaulle a clear mandate (authority from the voters).
[hist-eur5-s4.8.5] In the Algiers putsch (a seizure of power by force) of 21–26 April 1961, four generals who opposed independence, Challe, Salan, Jouhaud and Zeller, took Algiers. It collapsed in about four days because conscripts and most of the army stayed loyal to de Gaulle.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.6] The Secret Army Organisation (OAS), formed in early 1961 by settlers set on stopping independence, carried out bombings and assassinations in Algeria and in France, and it attacked de Gaulle himself.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.b] After years of war France and the FLN finally negotiated a settlement. Independence came in stages, which is why several dates are given for it.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.0] The Évian Accords were signed on 18 March 1962 after negotiations between the French government and the FLN, and a ceasefire took effect on 19 March.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.1] The Accords guaranteed the rights of the French minority (the settlers), kept the Mers-el-Kébir naval base for France for a time, and gave France access to Saharan oil and gas.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.2] The Accords were approved in a referendum in France on 8 April 1962 (about 91 per cent yes) and in Algeria on 1 July 1962 (an overwhelming yes), so signing them was not the end of the process.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.3] Algeria became independent after the 1 July vote: France recognised it on 3 July, and 5 July is the date celebrated as independence.
[hist-eur5-s4.9.4] After a struggle with rival nationalist leaders in the summer of 1962, Ahmed Ben Bella became prime minister in September 1962 and president in 1963.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.b] The OAS tried to wreck the settlement by violence, and the settlers' anger peaked in the weeks after Évian. Its defeat ended the settlers' armed resistance.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.0] On 26 March 1962 French troops fired on pro-French demonstrators in the rue d'Isly in Algiers, killing about 50 people, which deepened the settlers' sense of betrayal because the shots came from French soldiers.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.1] The OAS was defeated in Algiers and in France, and its leaders were tried. General Raoul Salan, a leader of the 1961 putsch, was arrested in April 1962 and received a life sentence.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.2] The OAS ambush of de Gaulle's car at Petit-Clamart on 22 August 1962 failed. Its organiser, Jean Bastien-Thiry, was executed in March 1963, the last person executed by firing squad in France for a political offence.
[hist-eur5-s4.10.3] A general amnesty in 1968 freed those OAS members still in prison.
[hist-eur5-s4.11.b] Independence did not end the links between the two countries, but the new state steadily took control of what France had kept.
[hist-eur5-s4.11.0] Ben Bella was overthrown by his defence minister Houari Boumediene in June 1965.
[hist-eur5-s4.11.1] France handed over the Mers-el-Kébir naval base in 1968, ending the military presence that the Évian Accords had allowed it to keep for a time.
[hist-eur5-s4.11.2] In 1971 Algeria nationalised (took into state ownership) French oil and gas holdings, reversing the access to Saharan oil and gas that the Accords had given France.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.b] Independence turned into flight for the European settlers and tragedy for many of the Algerians who had served France.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.0] On 5 July 1962, the day celebrated as independence, violence against Europeans broke out in Oran. Estimates vary, but hundreds were killed or went missing, and many settlers fled the city.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.1] About 800,000 settlers left Algeria in 1962, many within a few months and most for France, which had to absorb them into an already strained economy. Nearly a million had gone by the mid-1960s.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.2] The departure of the settlers ended the French presence in Algerian agriculture and administration, and many farms were taken over by the new state.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.3] The harkis were Algerians who had served as auxiliaries (helpers) in the French forces.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.4] The French government refused or obstructed organised rescue of the harkis, and officers who helped them were punished. About 90,000 reached France with their families.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.5] Tens of thousands of harkis were killed in reprisals (revenge killings) after independence, with estimates ranging widely from about 30,000 to 150,000.
[hist-eur5-s4.12.6] Most harkis who reached France were placed in camps and forest hamlets. Recognition and compensation remained a political issue: President Chirac paid national tribute to them in 2001 and President Macron asked their forgiveness in 2021.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.b] De Gaulle led the Free French in the Second World War and then ended the Algerian war as president of the Fifth Republic. His career shows both a leader shaping events and one pushed by them.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.0] Born on 22 November 1890 in Lille, he was a career army officer who became undersecretary for war in June 1940. His appeal of 18 June 1940 from London made him leader of the Free French.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.1] He headed the provisional government from 1944 to January 1946, resigned, founded the Rally of the French People (RPF) in 1947, and withdrew from politics in the 1950s before returning to power in 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.2] As the key decision-maker he took the major steps himself: the return in 1958, the Fifth Republic, the 1959 offer of self-determination, putting down Barricades Week and the putsch, and the path to Évian.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.3] In 1958 the settlers welcomed him because they believed he would keep Algeria French, but his 1959 turn showed events shaping him too. He moved from 'Je vous ai compris' in 1958 to self-determination in 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.4] Contemporaries split sharply: the settlers, the army and the OAS called him a traitor, while many in France and Algeria saw him as the leader who ended a costly war.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.5] Some historians argue that he planned withdrawal from early on and moved slowly to avoid civil war. Others argue that he moved step by step as the war's costs, the army's attitude and international pressure became clear.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.6] He used the Community and, after 1960, bilateral co-operation accords (agreements between two countries) to keep French influence in sub-Saharan Africa, working through Jacques Foccart, his adviser on African affairs.
[hist-eur5-s4.13.7] He resigned as president in April 1969 after losing a referendum, and died on 9 November 1970.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.b] De Gaulle's record and the wider retreat from empire can be judged by pairing achievements against limits, and contemporary views against later ones.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.0] Achievement: de Gaulle ended a war that the Fourth Republic had failed to win, and he gave Algerians the right of self-determination.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.1] Limit: the war cost many lives, caused the exodus of about a million people and divided the French army and political class. His policy was also delayed and ambiguous, which left the settlers feeling deceived.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.2] Achievement: the Fifth Republic's constitution gave France a stable government that could take the decision to withdraw.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.3] Limit: the French Community did not hold the colonies together, yet most independent states of West and Equatorial Africa kept close ties with France through co-operation accords, with French troops stationed in several of them.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.4] Explanations for withdrawal: the cost and unpopularity of the Algerian war, the independence of Tunisia and Morocco in 1956, UN General Assembly debates on Algeria from 1955, American pressure, world opinion and the growth of African nationalist parties.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.5] The British and French cases differed: Britain's transfers were mostly negotiated, while France fought a war in Algeria before accepting independence.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.6] Later view: the war divided the army, the political class and French society, and many judge that de Gaulle's greatest achievement was to end it without a collapse of the Republic.
[hist-eur5-s4.14.7] Contemporary view: settlers, the OAS and the generals of the 1961 putsch saw withdrawal as betrayal, while about 91 per cent of French voters approved the Évian Accords in April 1962.
