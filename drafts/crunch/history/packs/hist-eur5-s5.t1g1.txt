[hist-eur5-s4.0.0] Ghana (the former Gold Coast) became independent on 6 March 1957, the first sub-Saharan African colony to do so.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.1] Guinea voted against joining the French Community in the referendum of September 1958 and became independent on 2 October 1958, the first French colony in Africa to leave the community.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.2] Harold Macmillan told the South African Parliament in Cape Town on 3 February 1960 that a ‘wind of change’ was blowing through Africa.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.3] Seventeen African states gained independence in 1960, a year that became known as the Year of Africa.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.4] France's neighbours Tunisia and Morocco became independent in March 1956, and Britain chose negotiated transfers of power in most of its colonies, while France's policy in Algeria was contested by about a million settlers and an armed nationalist movement.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.5] The Loi-cadre of June 1956, introduced by the minister Gaston Defferre, gave the African territories elected assemblies and governments with wider powers, as an early step towards self-government.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.6] De Gaulle toured Africa in August 1958 and at Brazzaville on 24 August offered the territories a choice between the Community and independence; the Brazzaville Conference of 1944 had earlier promised reforms but ruled out self-government.
[hist-eur5-s4.0.7] In 1960 the Community broke up in practice: most French West and Equatorial African territories, along with Madagascar, became independent by negotiation and signed co-operation accords with France on defence, currency and aid.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.0] France conquered Algeria from 1830, and by the 1848 constitution Algeria was divided into three départements that were treated as part of metropolitan France.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.1] The settler population, known as pieds-noirs, numbered about one million by 1954 out of about nine million inhabitants, and dominated the economy, land and local administration.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.2] Most Muslim Algerians were subjects rather than full citizens; the Statute of 1947 created an Algerian Assembly with two colleges of equal size, one elected by about a million Europeans and one by about nine million Muslims, which favoured the settlers.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.3] Messali Hadj's Movement for the Triumph of Democratic Liberties (MTLD), founded in 1946, campaigned for independence, and it split in 1954 between moderates and the armed nationalists.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.4] The French government's policy was assimilation: the French state aimed to make Algerians French in language, law and culture, a goal few Muslim Algerians accepted in practice.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.5] The Sétif and Guelma uprising of 8 May 1945 killed about a hundred Europeans and was met by French repression that killed many thousands of Algerians; official French figures say about 1,500 and Algerian nationalists claim 45,000, and historians generally accept a figure in between.
[hist-eur5-s4.1.6] Muslim Algerians who wanted full French citizenship had to give up their Islamic personal status, a condition that few chose to meet before 1954.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.0] The National Liberation Front (FLN) launched a rising on 1 November 1954 with attacks on police and military targets across Algeria.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.1] The French government sent conscripts in large numbers, reaching about 400,000 troops at the peak, and the army was given wide powers to restore order in the cities and countryside.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.2] The FLN held the Soummam Valley Congress in August 1956, which created its political leadership and confirmed the primacy of the armed struggle.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.3] The Morice Line, a barrier of wire, mines and electrified fencing along the Tunisian frontier from 1957, was built to stop arms and fighters crossing the border.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.4] The Battle of Algiers of 1957 saw French paratroopers under General Jacques Massu break the FLN's urban network in the city through arrests, interrogation and torture.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.5] The use of torture by French forces was reported in Henri Alleg's book La Question, which was banned in France in 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.6] The FLN used terrorism against civilians, including bombings in Algiers, and it killed Algerians who opposed it; the French forces also killed civilians in reprisal operations.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.7] The French army moved large numbers of rural Algerians, probably more than a million, into fenced regroupement camps from 1955 and especially after 1957, as part of its counter-insurgency strategy.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.8] French military deaths numbered about 25,000; estimates of Algerian deaths range from about 300,000 to the Algerian official figure of 1.5 million, and historians still debate the figures.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.9] Support for the FLN abroad grew: Tunisia and Morocco sheltered its fighters, Egypt supplied arms, and the French bombing of the Tunisian village of Sakiet in February 1958 damaged France's reputation with the US and UN.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.b] A revolt by settlers and army officers in Algiers brought Charles de Gaulle back to power.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.0] On 13 May 1958 settlers and army officers seized government buildings in Algiers and demanded that Algeria stay French, and they formed a Committee of Public Safety.

[hist-eur5-s4.3.2] A new constitution was approved by referendum on 28 September 1958, establishing the Fifth Republic with a strong presidency.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.3] The constitution set up the French Community, which gave the colonies internal self-government, but kept foreign affairs, defence and currency with France.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.4] De Gaulle was elected president in December 1958 by an electoral college, and he took office in January 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.5] Between 1946 and 1958 the Fourth Republic had some two dozen governments, which made it hard to sustain a consistent policy on Algeria.
