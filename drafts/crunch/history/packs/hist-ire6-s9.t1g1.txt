[hist-ire6-s9.0.b] To judge how much changed between 1949 and 1989, you need to know what the starting point was: a state built around the Irish language and the Catholic Church, and cut off from much of the outside world.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.0] The 1937 Constitution (Article 8) made Irish the national language and first official language, with English recognised as a second official language. State policy was to revive Irish through schools, the civil service and the Gaeltacht, the mainly western and south-western districts where Irish was still spoken daily.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.1] The Catholic Church shaped schools, health and social policy, giving the state a strongly Catholic ethos, and Mass attendance was very high.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.2] The Mother and Child dispute of 1951, in which the bishops opposed Noel Browne's health scheme and the government dropped it, showed the church at the peak of its influence over policy.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.3] Ireland in the 1950s was isolated and suffered heavy emigration, which drained rural areas and the Gaeltacht. From 1958 and through the 1960s, economic expansion, urbanisation and rising living standards opened the country to outside influences, which is why old certainties came under pressure.
[hist-ire6-s9.1.b] Irish was the first official language of the state, but it was losing ground as a community language, and by the 1960s public attitudes were turning against compulsion.
[hist-ire6-s9.1.0] Official policy rested on compulsion. Irish was a compulsory school subject, a pass in Irish was required for the Leaving Certificate (the final secondary school exam) and Irish was needed for entry to the civil service.
[hist-ire6-s9.1.1] Gael Linn, founded in 1953, tried to promote the language positively through popular means, including records, the Amharc Éireann newsreel from 1956 and the feature film Mise Éire in 1959, a documentary on the years before 1916.
[hist-ire6-s9.1.2] The Gaeltacht was in decline in the 1950s because of emigration, so the state set up Gaeltarra Éireann in 1957 to encourage industry in Irish-speaking areas and give people a reason to stay.
[hist-ire6-s9.1.3] The Commission on the Restoration of the Irish Language reported in 1963, and a government White Paper of 1965 accepted the aim of restoration but proposed a more flexible approach, showing that policy was softening.
[hist-ire6-s9.1.4] The Language Freedom Movement, founded in 1966, campaigned against compulsory Irish, arguing that compulsion turned pupils against the language, and it provoked counter-campaigns from defenders of the language.
[hist-ire6-s9.1.5] In 1973 the Fine Gael-Labour government, with Richard Burke as Minister for Education, ended the rule that students must pass Irish to gain the Leaving Certificate. The requirement for civil service entry was also relaxed later.
[hist-ire6-s9.1.6] Do not overstate the 1973 change. Only the compulsory pass at the Leaving Certificate ended, and Irish itself stayed a compulsory school subject.
[hist-ire6-s9.2.b] From the late 1960s, Irish speakers themselves pressed for services in their own language, and the state responded by creating new broadcasting and development bodies.
[hist-ire6-s9.2.0] The Gaeltacht Civil Rights Movement, formed in 1969, demanded better services and Irish-language radio because the state offered none. In 1970 it ran a pirate station (an unlicensed broadcaster), Saor Raidió Chonamara.
[hist-ire6-s9.2.1] Raidió na Gaeltachta began broadcasting from Connemara on Easter Sunday, 2 April 1972, giving Irish speakers a regular, official service in their own language, so they no longer needed pirate broadcasts. Do not confuse it with the pirate Saor Raidió Chonamara of 1970.
[hist-ire6-s9.2.2] Bord na Gaeilge was set up in 1975 to coordinate efforts to promote Irish.
[hist-ire6-s9.2.3] Údarás na Gaeltachta, created in 1980, took over responsibility for the economic and social development of the Gaeltacht. It later absorbed the work of Gaeltarra Éireann, set up in 1957.
[hist-ire6-s9.2.4] The Irish language retained its symbolic status, but the number of everyday speakers continued to decline outside the Gaeltacht, which itself was shrinking. Compulsion had also left many pupils indifferent.
[hist-ire6-s9.3.b] Ó hEithir was a writer, journalist and broadcaster whose work in Irish and English brought the culture of the Gaeltacht and the Irish language to a modern audience.
[hist-ire6-s9.3.0] Breandán Ó hEithir was born in 1930 on Inis Mór, one of the Aran Islands, and died in 1990.

[hist-ire6-s9.3.3] His Over the Bar (1984) was a personal account of the GAA, and The Begrudger's Guide to Irish Politics (1986) was a witty account of political life, written in English.
[hist-ire6-s9.3.4] He was a participant in the cultural change of the period rather than a leader of a movement. He presented Irish as a living medium for modern subjects, not as a duty, which fitted the move away from compulsion.
[hist-ire6-s9.3.5] Contemporaries valued him as a bridge between the Gaeltacht and the English-speaking world and as a rare Irish-language writer with a wide readership.
[hist-ire6-s9.3.6] Later critics regard him as one of the most accomplished bilingual writers and broadcasters of his generation.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.b] Alongside the debate about the language, traditional music enjoyed a revival that made Irish culture feel modern and attractive rather than a duty.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.0] Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann was founded in Mullingar in 1951, and the fleadh cheoil (music festival and competition) held from 1952 helped to spread traditional music and dance.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.1] Seán Ó Riada composed the score for Mise Éire (1959). From 1961 his group Ceoltóirí Chualann played traditional music in a concert style, which brought it to a wider audience. Do not confuse the group with Comhaltas.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.2] Ó Riada's Ceol an Aifrinn (1968), the Mass in Irish set to sean-nós-style music (the old unaccompanied singing style) for the church at Cúil Aodha, linked the music revival to the changes brought by Vatican II.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.3] The Chieftains were formed in 1962, and the Clancy Brothers, The Dubliners and later Planxty made Irish ballads and traditional music popular at home and abroad.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.4] The revival gave Irish culture a modern commercial and international dimension, and it reduced the old divide between a rural traditional culture and urban popular culture.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.b] The Second Vatican Council, the worldwide meeting of Catholic bishops in Rome, changed Catholic worship and the church's relationship with other Christians and the modern world.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.0] Pope John XXIII opened the council on 11 October 1962 and it closed on 8 December 1965 under Paul VI. Irish bishops, including McQuaid, attended.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.1] The council allowed the Mass to be said in the vernacular (the everyday language) instead of Latin, so people could follow it. The first Masses in English in Ireland were celebrated in 1965.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.2] Priests now faced the congregation, laypeople could be readers and distribute communion, and parish councils and lay organisations were encouraged, giving ordinary Catholics a bigger role.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.3] The council promoted ecumenism, closer relations and dialogue between Christian churches. The Catholic ban on Catholics attending Trinity College Dublin was lifted in 1970, and the first formal inter-church talks with the main Protestant churches in Ireland were held at Ballymascanlon, Co. Louth, in 1973.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.4] It declared in 1965 that religious freedom was a right, which weakened the claim that the Catholic Church should have a special position in law.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.5] Many Irish Catholics welcomed the changes, but others, mostly older, felt unsettled by the loss of Latin and old devotions, because these had been part of their lifelong habits of worship.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.6] Vatican II also encouraged people to think for themselves on moral questions and to follow their conscience, which contributed to a questioning of church authority.
[hist-ire6-s9.6.b] McQuaid, Archbishop of Dublin from 1940 to 1972, was the most powerful churchman in Ireland, and he was cautious about the changes brought by the council. His reaction shows the gap between the council's openness and his conservative style.
[hist-ire6-s9.6.0] McQuaid was born in 1895 in Cootehill, Co. Cavan. He was a participant in church-state relations, with direct access to ministers, and his influence had peaked in the Mother and Child dispute of 1951, when the bishops opposed a state health scheme.

[hist-ire6-s9.6.4] He was also involved in education, social work and the building of new churches and schools in the Dublin suburbs, and the Catholic Social Service Conference, set up in 1941, was one of his charitable bodies.

[hist-ire6-s9.6.6] Contemporaries admired his efficiency and devotion to the church, but critics complained of his authoritarian style and his readiness to intervene in politics and in people's lives.

[hist-ire6-s9.6.8] Other historians stress his building of schools and welfare services. They argue that the decline of church power came from social change as much as from his policies, so he cannot be blamed alone.
[hist-ire6-s9.7.b] Secularisation is the decline of religion's influence over public life, institutions and personal behaviour. The evidence for it in Ireland points both ways, which is why historians treat the period as a slow shift rather than a sudden break.
[hist-ire6-s9.7.0] A survey in 1973 and 1974 found about nine in ten Catholics attending Mass weekly, so practice remained high even as other signs of change appeared.
[hist-ire6-s9.7.1] Pope Paul VI's encyclical (a formal papal letter) Humanae Vitae in 1968 reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception, and many Irish married Catholics found it difficult to accept. It was a papal decision after the council, not a Vatican II reform.

[hist-ire6-s9.8.0] The Censorship of Publications Act 1929 set up a board that could ban books for being indecent or obscene, and the Act of 1946 added an appeals board. Thousands of books were banned, including works by leading Irish writers.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s1: 1. Alternating governments and their economic and social policies, 1948–1959
[hist-ire6-s1.0.b] To understand why governments changed so often in the 1950s, you first need to know what Ireland inherited from the 1930s and the war years. The country was poor, politically divided and closely tied to Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.0] Fianna Fáil under de Valera governed from 1932 to February 1948 and built a protected, self-sufficiency economy, using tariffs (taxes on imports) and the Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934, which required Irish citizens to hold a majority of shares in most firms.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.1] The party system was Fianna Fáil against Fine Gael and Labour, and the Civil War still shaped loyalties. Proportional representation (voters rank candidates, so smaller parties win seats) made coalitions possible when no party had a majority.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.2] Ireland was neutral in the Second World War, which it called 'the Emergency'. It came out of the war poor, isolated and tied to the British market and to sterling (the British pound).
[hist-ire6-s1.0.3] The Catholic Church had strong influence on social policy, and its position was recognised in the Constitution of 1937. This is why a clash between a minister and the bishops, as in 1951, could decide government policy.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.b] Two economic terms run through the whole decade. Between them they explain why governments of every party kept cutting spending, even when it made life harder.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.0] The balance of payments is the record of a country's payments to and from the rest of the world. If more money goes out (mainly to buy imports) than comes in (mainly from exports), the country has a deficit.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.1] A deficit drained Ireland's reserves of foreign currency, because the money to pay for imports had to come from those reserves. Since the Irish pound was linked to sterling, the government could not simply let its currency fall.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.2] The usual cure was deflation: raising taxes and cutting government spending or credit to reduce demand, so that people bought fewer imports. The cost was jobs, because falling demand led firms to cut staff.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.3] This is why MacEntee's 1952 budget was unpopular, and why deflation (including the measures of 1955–56) helped drive both unemployment and emigration.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.b] After sixteen years of Fianna Fáil rule, a coalition of the opposition parties took office in 1948. An Inter-Party Government is simply a coalition of several parties that agreed to govern together; there were two, in 1948–51 and 1954–57.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.0] In the February 1948 general election Fianna Fáil, in power since 1932, remained the largest party but lost its majority, so it could no longer govern alone.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.1] Clann na Poblachta, a republican party founded in 1946 by Seán MacBride, won only 10 seats in 1948 but held the balance of power in the Dáil, meaning the coalition needed its votes.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.2] The government that took office on 18 February 1948 united Fine Gael, Labour, the National Labour Party, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan and some independents.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.3] Richard Mulcahy, the Fine Gael leader, stood aside because of his Civil War record, so John A. Costello became Taoiseach. William Norton of Labour was Tánaiste (deputy prime minister).
[hist-ire6-s1.2.4] Seán MacBride was Minister for External Affairs, and Noël Browne of Clann na Poblachta became Minister for Health in 1948.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.5] The coalition formed to end Fianna Fáil's long hold on office and had no common economic programme. It contained both conservative and radical members, which made bold policy hard.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.b] The government's best-known act was constitutional, but it also made economic and health decisions that mattered for the rest of the decade.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.0] Costello announced in Ottawa, Canada, in September 1948 that the government would repeal the External Relations Act of 1936 and declare a republic.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.1] The Republic of Ireland Act, passed in 1948, came into force on 18 April 1949 (Easter Monday). It ended the state's last formal link with the Crown and its Commonwealth membership.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.2] Britain replied with the Ireland Act 1949, which guaranteed that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of its parliament.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.3] The Ireland Act also said the Republic was not a foreign country and its citizens were not aliens in Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.4] Ireland declined an invitation to join NATO (the Western military alliance) in 1949, with MacBride citing partition as the obstacle.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.5] The Industrial Development Authority (IDA) was set up in 1949 to encourage new industry, including investment from abroad, though in the 1950s it had little money or power.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.6] Ireland took part in Marshall Aid (the European Recovery Programme, American aid for rebuilding after the war) and received about 146 million dollars, mostly as loans. Much went on land reclamation and agriculture rather than industry, which later critics saw as a missed chance.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.7] Noël Browne led a campaign against tuberculosis (TB), a major killer. New sanatoria (hospitals for TB patients) were paid for partly from the Irish Hospitals' Sweepstake, and a BCG vaccination programme began. TB deaths fell steeply during the 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.8] The government also expanded house building.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.b] Browne's plan for free health care for mothers and children became the clearest example of the Catholic hierarchy's power over social policy in the 1950s. Its defeat also helped end the government.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.0] Browne's scheme of 1950 used the Health Act 1947 to offer free care for mothers before and after childbirth and for children up to the age of 16, available to all without a means test (a check on income).
[hist-ire6-s1.4.1] The Irish Medical Association opposed it as a threat to doctors' private practice and independence.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.2] The Catholic hierarchy (the bishops) objected that the scheme gave the state too much power over family life, that it might lead to state-provided education in sexual matters, and that it undermined the rights of parents.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.3] The hierarchy wrote to Costello in late 1950 and again in April 1951, saying the scheme was contrary to Catholic social teaching.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.4] MacBride, who wanted to protect his government from a clash with the church, asked Browne to resign. Browne resigned in April 1951 and then published the correspondence, which made the church's role public.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.5] Costello and the cabinet accepted the bishops' view, so the scheme was dropped.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.6] The affair helped bring down the government. Costello called a general election for 30 May 1951, in which Clann na Poblachta fell from 10 seats to 2.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.7] The Fianna Fáil government that took office replaced the scheme with the Health Act 1953, which offered means-tested maternity, child and hospital services, so many people were left without free care.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.b] McQuaid was Archbishop of Dublin from 1940 to 1972 and the most influential churchman in the state in the 1950s. Here the focus is only his part in the crisis of 1950–51.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.0] John Charles McQuaid was born in 1895 in Cootehill, Co. Cavan, joined the Holy Ghost Fathers and was president of Blackrock College before he became Archbishop of Dublin in 1940.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.1] His influence rested on close personal contact with ministers, notably de Valera, whom he had advised on the religious article of the Constitution of 1937.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.2] In 1950–51 he was a participant: he advised Browne and the government on the church's objections and was the most influential voice in arguing that the scheme conflicted with Catholic social teaching. [hist-ire6-s1.5.3] His principle was that the family and the church, not the state, should direct the care of mothers and children. He objected to the principle, not only to medical detail.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.4] Contemporaries who agreed with him saw a defender of Catholic social teaching. Browne and his supporters saw an interfering prelate who used his power to defeat a good scheme.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.5] John Whyte, in Church and State in Modern Ireland, 1923–1979 (1971), concluded that the government deferred to the hierarchy. Later work using archives stresses Browne's weak political position and the doctors' opposition, while still treating the bishops' stance as decisive.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.b] De Valera returned as Taoiseach in June 1951, but without a majority and with an economy short of foreign currency. The government's answer to the balance of payments problem was deflation.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.0] Fianna Fáil formed a minority government with de Valera as Taoiseach, Seán Lemass as Tánaiste and Minister for Industry and Commerce, and Seán MacEntee as Minister for Finance.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.1] The minority government depended on the votes of independent deputies, which limited how far it could go with unpopular measures.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.2] MacEntee's 1952 budget was deflationary: it cut subsidies on basic foods and raised duties, which pushed prices up, in order to reduce imports and the balance of payments deficit. It was unpopular.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.3] The Underdeveloped Areas Act 1952 gave grants for new industry in the poorer western counties.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.4] The Health Act 1953 provided a framework of means-tested maternity, child and hospital care in place of the Mother and Child Scheme.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.5] Emigration continued at a high level and unemployment stayed high. In the 1954 general election Fianna Fáil lost seats.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.b] Costello returned as Taoiseach in 1954 at the head of a coalition that again had no programme for growth. It then faced a balance of payments crisis.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.0] The second coalition contained Fine Gael, Labour and Clann na Talmhan, with Clann na Poblachta supporting it from outside government, meaning it voted for the government but held no ministries.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.1] Gerard Sweetman, the Minister for Finance, was a strong believer in private enterprise and low state spending.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.2] A balance of payments crisis built up in 1955 and 1956 because imports ran well ahead of exports and foreign currency reserves fell.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.3] The government responded with higher taxes, credit restrictions and cuts in current spending. This reduced demand and pushed up unemployment.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.4] The Capital Investment Advisory Committee, set up in 1956, reviewed proposals for public capital spending. It was an early step towards planning public investment.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.5] In December 1956 the IRA began its Border Campaign, and the government faced pressure from Clann na Poblachta over its handling of the North and its response to the campaign.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.6] Clann na Poblachta withdrew its support in January 1957, which left Costello without a majority, and he called an election.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.b] While most of western Europe boomed, the Irish economy grew only slowly and people left in large numbers. Unemployment stayed high throughout the decade.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.0] The census showed a population of 2,960,593 in 1951 and 2,818,341 in 1961, a fall of about 142,000 despite a high birth rate, because so many people emigrated.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.1] Net emigration (people leaving minus people arriving) in the 1950s was about 400,000, roughly 200,000 in each half of the decade. Most of those who left were young people heading for Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.2] Britain attracted them because it offered work in construction, factories, the health service and transport, and its economy was growing faster than the Irish.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.3] Agriculture still employed about two-fifths of the workforce, mostly on small farms of low productivity, and cattle exports to Britain were the main earner.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.4] Industry sheltered behind protective tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts, which required that most shares in new firms be held by Irish citizens and so discouraged foreign investment.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.5] Irish growth was a fraction of the growth in western Europe, so the gap with other countries widened.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.6] The Irish pound was linked to sterling, so Irish interest rates and trade followed British conditions, including the British government's alternating cycles of expansion and restriction, known as 'stop-go'.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.b] The 1957 election ended the second coalition. Historians use the phrase 'wasted years' for the 1950s, a decade they see as one of missed opportunity, and they disagree about who or what was to blame.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.0] In the March 1957 general election Fianna Fáil won 78 seats and an overall majority, and de Valera became Taoiseach for the last time (to 1959).
[hist-ire6-s1.9.1] Lemass returned to Industry and Commerce and also served as Tánaiste, and James Ryan became Minister for Finance.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.2] T.K. Whitaker had become Secretary of the Department of Finance in 1956 and began the work that led to Economic Development.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.3] View 1: historians such as J.J. Lee (Ireland 1912–1985) and Tom Garvin (Preventing the Future, 2004) criticise the leaders of the 1950s for lacking ambition and for preferring stability to change, which gave rise to the term 'wasted years'.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.4] View 2: others point to constraints, such as a small economy that depended on the British market and the sterling link, and weak foreign currency reserves.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.5] View 3: the Inter-Party governments did lay groundwork, through the IDA, the Underdeveloped Areas Act and public investment, that Fianna Fáil later built on. Planning tools were developed only late in the decade, so policy-makers earlier had little to work with.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.b] Examiners reward a clear judgement that pairs successes with failures and explains why governments kept changing. Instability and economic failure were linked: poor results brought defeat, and short-lived governments could not plan.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.0] Achievements: the Republic of Ireland Act completed the move to a republic, while the IDA, the Underdeveloped Areas Act and Marshall Aid laid foundations for later industrial policy. The anti-tuberculosis campaign saved lives.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.1] Limits: no government drew up a long-term plan, and each balance of payments crisis led to deflation, which made unemployment and emigration worse.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.2] Limits: the Mother and Child affair showed the Church's power over social policy, since the state could not act when the church and the medical profession opposed it.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.3] Contemporary view: voters punished each government in turn in 1951, 1954 and 1957 for high taxes and emigration, and the sharp swings between coalition and Fianna Fáil governments reflected that dissatisfaction.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.4] Later view: historians generally accept that the decade was one of slow growth and missed opportunities, but they differ about how far this was caused by policy mistakes rather than by Ireland's dependence on Britain.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s3: 3. Changes in education, health and social welfare provision
[hist-ire6-s3.0.b] In the 1950s the Irish state did little to plan or run schools, hospitals or welfare. The reforms of the 1960s and 1970s changed that, and the idea behind them is called equality of opportunity.
[hist-ire6-s3.0.0] Equality of opportunity means that access to education, health care and welfare should not depend on wealth, class or where a person lives. It is the standard against which historians judge these reforms.
[hist-ire6-s3.0.1] The 1950s were a decade of stagnation and emigration, and the state did little to plan. After 1958 the turn to economic planning (the Programme for Economic Expansion) made the government ask whether the country had the skilled people it needed.
[hist-ire6-s3.0.2] The churches, above all the Catholic Church, owned and managed most schools and many hospitals. The state paid for much of this but did not run it, which is why church control appears later as a limit on reform.
[hist-ire6-s3.0.3] Much help was means-tested, meaning a person had to prove a low income to qualify. This shaped the health and welfare schemes and explains why better-off people often paid for services themselves.
[hist-ire6-s3.1.b] Until the 1960s the state left most second-level education to the churches and to parents who could pay. Economic planning after 1958 made this a problem the government could no longer ignore.
[hist-ire6-s3.1.0] Before the 1960s most children left school at 14 with only a primary education, because secondary schools charged fees and were mostly owned by religious orders, which put them out of reach of many families.
[hist-ire6-s3.1.1] Vocational schools, set up under the Vocational Education Act 1930, were run by local vocational education committees. They taught practical subjects and had lower status than the fee-charging secondary schools.
[hist-ire6-s3.1.2] Ireland had no state policy for expanding education, and the Department of Education was seen as a passive administrator, in other words it managed what existed rather than planning for change.
[hist-ire6-s3.1.3] Planning for economic growth after 1958 raised the question of whether the country was educating enough people with the skills it needed. Patrick Hillery, Minister for Education from 1959 to 1965, was in office as this question arose.
[hist-ire6-s3.1.4] The government commissioned a survey under Hillery, carried out in the early 1960s with the help of the OECD (the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, an international body of mainly Western countries).
[hist-ire6-s3.1.5] Investment in Education, published in 1965, showed wide differences in participation by social class and region, and a shortage of trained teachers and of technical skills.
[hist-ire6-s3.1.6] The report argued that education was an investment in economic growth and not only a cultural or religious matter. It therefore recommended expanding second-level and technical education.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.b] Two ministers, Hillery and then Donogh O'Malley, turned the report's findings into reforms. Hillery planned a new kind of school, and O'Malley made the political decision to remove fees.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.0] In 1963 Hillery announced a new type of second-level school, the comprehensive school. It would offer both academic and practical subjects to pupils of all abilities and be funded by the state.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.1] The first comprehensive schools opened in 1966, among them schools at Carraroe, Co. Galway, and Cootehill, Co. Cavan, which were in areas that lacked second-level schools.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.2] Donogh O'Malley became Minister for Education in July 1966 and announced on 10 September 1966 that free second-level education would be introduced the following year.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.3] O'Malley is remembered for announcing the scheme boldly, and it is often said that he did so without having settled the cost with the Department of Finance.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.4] The scheme began in September 1967 and abolished fees in the schools that joined it.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.5] It was accompanied by free school transport in 1967, which allowed rural pupils who lived far from a school to reach one.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.6] Most secondary schools joined, and enrolment at second level rose steeply in the years that followed.
[hist-ire6-s3.2.7] O'Malley died in March 1968 aged 47, and Brian Lenihan and then Pádraig Faulkner as Ministers for Education continued the work.
[hist-ire6-s3.3.b] Alongside free fees, other changes removed old barriers and kept pupils in school for longer.
[hist-ire6-s3.3.0] The Primary Certificate examination, which pupils had taken at the end of primary school, was abolished in 1967.
[hist-ire6-s3.3.1] The school-leaving age was raised from 14 to 15 in 1972, so that every child had to stay in school for an extra year.
[hist-ire6-s3.4.b] Reform did not stop at second level. In the late 1960s and 1970s the state also built new kinds of school and college and opened university education to more people.
[hist-ire6-s3.4.0] Community schools were introduced from the early 1970s, usually by merging a local secondary school and a vocational school, so as to offer a full range of subjects to all pupils and to serve adults in the area.
[hist-ire6-s3.4.1] Regional Technical Colleges, which offered certificate and diploma courses in technology and business, opened from 1970 in towns such as Carlow, Dundalk, Athlone and Waterford.
[hist-ire6-s3.4.2] The Higher Education Authority was set up in 1968 to advise the government on university funding and to plan expansion.
[hist-ire6-s3.4.3] A higher education grants scheme was introduced in 1968 to help students from poorer homes to attend university or college.
[hist-ire6-s3.4.4] The National Institute for Higher Education in Limerick opened in 1972 with an emphasis on technology and business.
[hist-ire6-s3.4.5] The number of students in third-level education (university and college) grew strongly through the 1970s, and more women and more students from rural and working-class homes reached university than before.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.b] The reforms also changed what was taught and how in primary school, moving from drilling facts to developing the child.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.0] A new curriculum for primary schools was introduced in 1971 after years of planning by a committee of teachers, inspectors and others.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.1] It replaced a curriculum built on memorisation and examination with one that emphasised the needs and interests of the individual child, which is called a child-centred approach.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.2] It gave more attention to activity-based learning (learning by doing), to art, music and drama, and to the teaching of Irish through speaking rather than drill.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.3] Teachers needed retraining, and some, along with some parents, found the change difficult in large classes and poor buildings.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.b] The two ministers are easily confused, but they did different jobs. Hillery prepared the ground, and O'Malley made the public breakthrough.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.0] Patrick Hillery, from Miltown Malbay, Co. Clare, was Minister for Education from 1959 to 1965. He was later Minister for Industry and Commerce, for Labour and, from 1969 to 1973, for External Affairs, before becoming President in 1976.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.1] Hillery's contribution was the planning: he commissioned the survey that became Investment in Education and announced the comprehensive schools in 1963.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.2] Donogh O'Malley, a Limerick TD, was Minister for Health in 1965 and 1966 and Minister for Education from July 1966 until his death in March 1968.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.3] O'Malley's contribution was the political decision: his announcement of free second-level education turned a long-term plan into an immediate promise.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.4] Historians generally credit Hillery with the groundwork and O'Malley with the public breakthrough. They note that the headline of free education was a simplification, because books, uniforms and travel costs remained.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.b] The reforms widened access, but they ran into practical and political limits, and they did not end inequality.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.0] The churches kept control of management in most primary and secondary schools. The hierarchy (the bishops) was wary of comprehensive and community schools, so compromises gave the bishops representation on boards of management.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.1] Many schools were small, overcrowded and short of qualified teachers when enrolment rose after 1967.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.2] Tension between the academic secondary tradition and the practical vocational tradition continued, since the Leaving Certificate points for college places favoured academic subjects.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.3] Free fees did not remove other costs such as books, uniforms and travel, so poorer families still faced barriers.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.4] Rural areas, especially in the west, still had fewer second-level and third-level places than the east.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.b] Health policy moved gradually from a means-tested service towards wider public provision, with continuing disputes over who should provide it. The first steps came in the 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.0] The Health Act 1953 replaced the Mother and Child Scheme (a free scheme that had collapsed in 1951) with means-tested maternity and child services. It also set out eligibility for public hospital care according to income.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.1] Before the health boards, rural primary care was provided mainly through the dispensary system run by the county authorities, with dispensary doctors treating medical card holders.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.2] Medical cards, which gave lower-income families free GP care and medicines, remained the main way of providing free primary care, while better-off patients paid for visits.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.3] The Voluntary Health Insurance Board was set up in 1957 to offer private health insurance, mainly to people who earned too much to qualify for free hospital care.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.b] By the late 1960s the government wanted a more organised hospital system, and it created new bodies to run and regulate it.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.0] The Fitzgerald Report of 1968 on hospital services recommended fewer, larger regional hospitals, a change that proved controversial in local communities.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.1] The Health Act 1970 created eight regional health boards, which began work in 1971 and took over the functions of the old county health authorities.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.2] Comhairle na nOspidéal, set up in 1972, regulated the appointment of consultants and the structure of hospital services.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.3] Hospital building was funded partly by the Hospitals Sweepstake, and large projects such as Beaumont Hospital in Dublin, which opened in 1987, were delayed for years by cost and planning disputes.
[hist-ire6-s3.10.b] Welfare payments grew in range and level, especially after 1965, as the economy grew and the state's role in family support expanded.
[hist-ire6-s3.10.0] Children's allowance, first introduced in 1944, was paid to families, and its value and coverage were raised over the following decades.
[hist-ire6-s3.10.1] The Social Welfare Act 1952 gathered the existing social insurance schemes (payments funded by workers' contributions) into a single system covering unemployment, illness, widowhood and old age.
[hist-ire6-s3.10.2] Contributory old age pensions were introduced in 1961 for insured workers, in addition to the non-contributory pension, which did not depend on contributions.
[hist-ire6-s3.10.3] Free travel for old age pensioners was introduced in 1967 and an electricity allowance followed in 1968.
[hist-ire6-s3.10.4] Deserted wife's benefit was introduced in 1970, and an unmarried mother's allowance followed in 1973, recognising mothers who had children outside marriage as claimants in their own right.
[hist-ire6-s3.10.5] Pay-related benefit for unemployed and sick workers was introduced in 1974, tying payments to previous earnings.
[hist-ire6-s3.10.6] Welfare spending rose as a share of national income in the 1970s, partly because unemployment grew after the oil crises.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.b] Housing and basic services changed where and how people lived. Towns grew outwards, and electricity and water reached the countryside.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.0] Local authorities built large numbers of houses and flats from the 1950s onwards, replacing city-centre tenements and slums and housing families on new estates at the edge of the cities.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.1] The Housing Act 1966 allowed tenants to buy their local authority houses, which increased home ownership.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.2] Ballymun, in north Dublin, was built by Dublin Corporation between 1966 and 1969 as a large estate of flats, including seven fifteen-storey towers named after the leaders of 1916.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.3] Poor facilities, unemployment and decay later gave Ballymun a bad name, and it was demolished and rebuilt from the late 1990s.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.4] Planners in the late 1960s recommended new towns at Tallaght, Clondalkin and Blanchardstown around Dublin, and suburban growth transformed the edges of Dublin, Cork and Limerick.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.5] The ESB's rural electrification scheme, begun in 1946, had connected nearly all rural homes by the mid-1970s, bringing lighting, radio, television and appliances to farms.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.6] Group water schemes, supported by state grants from the 1960s, brought piped water to rural homes that had relied on wells and pumps.
[hist-ire6-s3.11.7] Electricity and running water changed daily life, especially for women. They also made it easier to keep young people on the land or in rural towns.
[hist-ire6-s3.12.b] The judgement examiners reward is how far the state changed things and for whom. Set achievements against limits, and contemporary views against later ones.
[hist-ire6-s3.12.0] Achievements: free post-primary education and transport widened access, a state-wide hospital and health board structure was created, and social welfare protected more families, including lone mothers.
[hist-ire6-s3.12.1] Limits: class differences in educational attainment persisted, the VHI and the dual system left health care unequal, and some large housing schemes, notably Ballymun, created new social problems.
[hist-ire6-s3.12.2] Contemporary view: parents, teachers and unions welcomed the reforms, while the churches accepted the expansion but were concerned to keep control of their schools.
[hist-ire6-s3.12.3] Later view: historians stress that the reforms were driven as much by the economic need for skills as by social ideals, and that the benefits went first to the middle classes, but they accept that they were a major change of direction.
