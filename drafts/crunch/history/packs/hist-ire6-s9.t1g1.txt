[hist-ire6-s3.4.6] Medical cards, which gave lower-income families free GP care and medicines, remained the main way of providing free primary care, while better-off patients paid for visits.
[hist-ire6-s3.4.7] Hospital building was funded partly by the Hospitals Sweepstake, and large projects such as Beaumont Hospital in Dublin were delayed for years by cost and planning disputes.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.b] Welfare payments grew in range and level, especially after 1965, as the economy grew and the state's role in family support expanded.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.0] Children's allowance, first introduced in 1944, was paid to families and its value and coverage were raised over the following decades.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.1] The Social Welfare Act 1952 gathered the existing social insurance schemes into a single system covering unemployment, illness, widowhood and old age.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.2] Contributory old age pensions were introduced in 1961 for insured workers, in addition to the non-contributory pension.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.3] Free travel for old age pensioners was introduced in 1967 and an electricity allowance followed in 1968.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.4] Deserted wife's benefit was introduced in 1970, and an unmarried mother's allowance followed in 1973, recognising mothers who had children outside marriage as claimants in their own right.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.5] Pay-related benefit for unemployed and sick workers was introduced in 1974, tying payments to previous earnings.
[hist-ire6-s3.5.6] Welfare spending rose as a share of national income in the 1970s, partly because unemployment grew after the oil crises (see the notes on the economic challenges of the 1970s and 1980s).
[hist-ire6-s3.6.0] Local authorities built large numbers of houses and flats from the 1950s onwards, replacing city-centre tenements and slums and housing families on new estates at the edge of the cities.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.1] The Housing Act 1966 allowed tenants to buy their local authority houses, which increased home ownership.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.2] Ballymun, in north Dublin, was built by Dublin Corporation between 1966 and 1969 as a large estate of flats, including seven fifteen-storey towers named after the leaders of 1916; poor facilities, unemployment and decay later gave it a bad name, and it was demolished and rebuilt from the late 1990s.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.3] Planners in the late 1960s recommended new towns at Tallaght, Clondalkin and Blanchardstown around Dublin, and suburban growth transformed the edges of Dublin, Cork and Limerick.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.4] The ESB's rural electrification scheme, begun in 1946, had connected nearly all rural homes by the mid-1970s, bringing lighting, radio, television and appliances to farms.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.5] Group water schemes, supported by state grants from the 1960s, brought piped water to rural homes that had relied on wells and pumps.
[hist-ire6-s3.6.6] Electricity and running water changed daily life, especially for women, and they made it easier to keep young people on the land or in rural towns.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.0] Patrick Hillery, from Miltown Malbay, Co. Clare, was Minister for Education from 1959 to 1965, then Minister for Industry and Commerce, for Labour and from 1969 to 1973 for External Affairs, before becoming President in 1976.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.1] Hillery's contribution was the planning: he commissioned the survey that became Investment in Education and announced the comprehensive schools in 1963.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.2] Donogh O'Malley, a Limerick TD, was Minister for Health in 1965 and 1966 and Minister for Education from 1966 until his death in March 1968.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.3] O'Malley's contribution was the political decision: his announcement of free second-level education turned a long-term plan into an immediate promise.
[hist-ire6-s3.7.4] Historians generally credit Hillery with the groundwork and O'Malley with the public breakthrough, and note that the headline of 'free education' was a simplification because some costs remained.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.0] The churches kept control of management in most primary and secondary schools, and the hierarchy was wary of comprehensive and community schools, so compromises gave the bishops representation on boards of management.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.1] Many schools were small, overcrowded and short of qualified teachers when enrolment rose after 1967.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.2] Tension between the academic secondary tradition and the practical vocational tradition continued, since the Leaving Certificate points for college places favoured academic subjects.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.3] Free fees did not remove other costs such as books, uniforms and travel, so poorer families still faced barriers.
[hist-ire6-s3.8.4] Rural areas, especially in the west, still had fewer second-level and third-level places than the east.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.0] Achievements: free post-primary education and transport widened access, a state-wide hospital and health board structure was created, and social welfare protected more families, including lone mothers.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.1] Limits: class differences in educational attainment persisted, the VHI and the dual system left health care unequal, and some large housing schemes, notably Ballymun, created new social problems.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.2] Contemporary view: parents, teachers and unions welcomed the reforms, while the churches accepted the expansion but were concerned to keep control of their schools.
[hist-ire6-s3.9.3] Later view: historians stress that the reforms were driven as much by the economic need for skills as by social ideals, and that the benefits went first to the middle classes, but they accept that they were a major change of direction.
