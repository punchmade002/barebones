[hist-eur2-s10.0.b] Nationalism is loyalty to the nation and the belief that each nation should be united, strong and self-governing. Between 1871 and 1914 artists turned that belief into music, buildings and poetry, which is why culture matters in the story of the nation states.
[hist-eur2-s10.0.0] Cultural nationalism used history, myth, folk tradition (the songs and tales of ordinary country people), language and landscape to prove that a people had a distinct identity, so a nation's art became evidence for its right to exist.
[hist-eur2-s10.0.1] There were two kinds. Established, imperial states such as Germany and Britain used culture to express pride and power. Peoples without their own state, such as the Czechs, Finns and Norwegians, used it to assert an identity that their rulers did not recognise.
[hist-eur2-s10.0.2] Across Europe, governments and nationalist societies spread national identity by founding national museums, galleries and theatres, teaching national history in schools and fostering national languages, so ordinary people absorbed it as well as artists.
[hist-eur2-s10.0.3] Pre-1914 cultural nationalism fed the patriotic mood of 1914, because a generation had grown up taught that the nation deserved loyalty and sacrifice. It also shaped the idealistic early war poetry.
[hist-eur2-s10.1.b] In Germany and Britain, nationalist culture celebrated a state that already existed and was powerful. It turned myth, history and empire into sources of pride.
[hist-eur2-s10.1.0] Richard Wagner built his operas on Germanic myth, so they presented the Germans as heirs to an ancient heroic tradition. His Ring cycle (Der Ring des Nibelungen) was first staged in full at his own Bayreuth theatre in August 1876.
[hist-eur2-s10.1.1] Wagner died in Venice in 1883, but the Bayreuth Festival continued under his widow Cosima as a shrine of German culture. His anti-Jewish essay 'Judaism in Music' (1850, republished under his name in 1869) links him to the anti-Semitism of the age.
[hist-eur2-s10.1.2] The Hermann Monument in the Teutoburg Forest, completed in 1875, honours Arminius (Hermann), the Germanic leader who defeated three Roman legions in AD 9. The new Reich thus claimed a heroic ancestry of resistance to foreign rule.
[hist-eur2-s10.1.3] The Monument to the Battle of the Nations at Leipzig was unveiled in 1913, the centenary of Napoleon's defeat there in 1813, so it tied German identity to the memory of a national victory.
[hist-eur2-s10.1.4] Edward Elgar's Pomp and Circumstance March No. 1 (1901) supplied the tune for 'Land of Hope and Glory' in the Coronation Ode (1902), with words by A. C. Benson. Together they expressed British imperial confidence, for example in the praise of a great Empire.
[hist-eur2-s10.1.5] Rudyard Kipling, who won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1907, celebrated British soldiers and the Empire in Recessional (1897, refrain 'Lest we forget') and The White Man's Burden (1899).
[hist-eur2-s10.1.6] Kipling's only son John was killed at the Battle of Loos in 1915. Kipling later worked for the Imperial War Graves Commission and chose the inscription 'Their Name Liveth for Evermore', so his life links imperial pride to the grief of the war.
[hist-eur2-s10.2.b] For peoples ruled by an empire, music could say what politics forbade. Composers drew on their own history, landscape and folk tunes to show that the nation existed.
[hist-eur2-s10.2.0] Bedřich Smetana's Má vlast ('My Country'), six symphonic poems (orchestral pieces that tell a story or paint a scene) composed in 1874-79, includes Vltava ('The Moldau'). It celebrated Bohemian history and landscape when Bohemia was part of Austria-Hungary and the Czechs sought more autonomy.
[hist-eur2-s10.2.1] Jean Sibelius's Finlandia (1899, revised 1900) was written for a protest against Russian censorship of the Finnish press. Finland was a grand duchy of the Russian Empire, so the piece became a symbol of Finnish resistance to Russification (imposing Russian language and control on minorities).
[hist-eur2-s10.2.2] Edvard Grieg wrote incidental music (music played during a play) for Henrik Ibsen's Peer Gynt, first performed in 1876, using the Norwegian folk idiom. Norway was in union with Sweden until 1905, so the music expressed a distinct Norwegian identity.
[hist-eur2-s10.2.3] Because these peoples had no state of their own, culture was often their main means of proving they were a nation, which made art a form of political resistance.
[hist-eur2-s10.3.b] War writing is best understood as a story of changing attitudes: patriotic idealism in 1914-15, then disillusionment, anger and pity, then a disenchanted post-war view. The reasons lie in how the war was fought.
[hist-eur2-s10.3.0] On the Western Front (the line of trenches across France and Belgium), trench deadlock, gas, shelling and huge casualties from 1915 turned the hope of a short heroic war into years of mass death.
[hist-eur2-s10.3.1] The Battle of the Somme (1 July to November 1916) shows the scale: on its first day the British army suffered about 57,000 casualties, about 19,000 of them dead.
[hist-eur2-s10.3.2] Writers also felt a gap between home-front propaganda, which kept praising glory, and what soldiers experienced, so much later writing is angry at those who had not been there.
[hist-eur2-s10.3.3] Censorship limited what soldiers could say in letters and the press. It did not stop protest being published during the war, because Le Feu appeared in 1916 and Sassoon's statement and The Old Huntsman in 1917.
[hist-eur2-s10.3.4] In short, there are three phases: patriotic idealism (1914-15), disillusionment, anger and pity (1916-18), and the post-war 'disenchanted' view of the late 1920s, when a boom in war books ran from 1928 to 1933.
[hist-eur2-s10.4.b] At first many writers saw the war as a noble cause and death for one's country as a fulfilment. This mood drew on the cultural nationalism of the pre-war years.
[hist-eur2-s10.4.0] Rupert Brooke's 1914 sonnets, published in 1915, include The Soldier, in which he imagines that if he dies, some corner of a foreign field will be 'for ever England'. They expressed idealism about dying for England.
[hist-eur2-s10.4.1] Brooke died of blood poisoning on a hospital ship off the Greek island of Skyros on 23 April 1915, on the way to Gallipoli. He never saw major combat, but his early death made him celebrated as the ideal patriot.
[hist-eur2-s10.4.2] Julian Grenfell (Into Battle, 1915; died of wounds in May 1915) and John McCrae, a Canadian army doctor (In Flanders Fields, written in 1915), wrote in a similar spirit.
[hist-eur2-s10.4.3] Contemporaries preferred this patriotic verse, because it matched the mood of the time. The protest poets were read widely only after 1918.
[hist-eur2-s10.5.b] By 1917 officers who had survived the trenches began to write against the war. Siegfried Sassoon was the first to turn protest into a public act.
[hist-eur2-s10.5.0] Siegfried Sassoon won the Military Cross in 1916, so when he protested he did so as a decorated officer, not a shirker.
[hist-eur2-s10.5.1] In July 1917 he made a statement against the war, read out in the House of Commons and printed in The Times. It said the war was being 'deliberately prolonged' by those who had the power to end it.
[hist-eur2-s10.5.2] Instead of a court martial he was sent to Craiglockhart War Hospital in Edinburgh in July 1917, after Robert Graves arranged a medical board. The army thus treated a decorated officer as ill rather than as a rebel.
[hist-eur2-s10.5.3] His satire in The Old Huntsman (1917) and Counter-Attack (1918) attacked generals and the home front, which is why he is the model of the angry protest poet.
[hist-eur2-s10.5.4] Other soldier-poets included Isaac Rosenberg (killed in April 1918), Edmund Blunden, who wrote Undertones of War (1928), and Ivor Gurney.
[hist-eur2-s10.5.5] Sassoon survived the war and died in 1967, whereas Owen was killed in 1918.
[hist-eur2-s10.6.b] Prose writers carried the argument beyond the poets, from a French infantryman's view written during the war to German and British bestsellers of the late 1920s and early 1930s.
[hist-eur2-s10.6.0] Henri Barbusse, who served as a private soldier, wrote Le Feu (Under Fire, 1916) during the war. It won the Prix Goncourt, showed the suffering of ordinary French infantrymen in the trenches and turned against the war.
[hist-eur2-s10.6.1] Ernst Jünger's In Stahlgewittern (Storm of Steel, 1920) gave the viewpoint of a German officer who found meaning in combat. It contrasts with the protest writers, so war literature did not speak with one voice.
[hist-eur2-s10.6.2] Erich Maria Remarque served on the Western Front and published Im Westen nichts Neues (All Quiet on the Western Front) in 1929. This post-war German novel showed a lost generation of young soldiers ruined by the war and became a worldwide bestseller. The Nazis burned it in 1933.
[hist-eur2-s10.6.3] In the boom in war books of 1928-33, Robert Graves published the memoir Goodbye to All That (1929). Vera Brittain published Testament of Youth (1933), a woman's view of the war.
[hist-eur2-s10.6.4] Together, Remarque, Graves and Brittain shaped later memory of the war. This is why the post-war view is called disenchanted: it looked back on the war as a loss rather than a cause.
[hist-eur2-s10.7.b] Historians use war literature as a source, but it is not a simple record of what soldiers thought. Its value and its limits both matter when you judge it.
[hist-eur2-s10.7.0] Its strengths are eyewitness detail and strong feeling, so poems and memoirs convey the sights, sounds and emotions of the trenches in a way official reports do not.
[hist-eur2-s10.7.1] Its limits are that the best-known writers were mostly educated officers and wrote for effect, so they may not speak for ordinary soldiers.
[hist-eur2-s10.7.2] Many soldiers kept their belief that the war was justified. This is why revisionist historians such as Dan Todman, in The Great War: Myth and Memory (2005), argue that the poets' view became the 'myth' of a futile war which most soldiers did not share.
[hist-eur2-s10.8.b] Wilfred Owen fought as an officer and wrote as a witness for the men he led. He became the English poet most closely linked with the suffering of the war, yet almost nothing he wrote was published in his lifetime.
[hist-eur2-s10.8.0] Owen was born on 18 March 1893 in Oswestry, Shropshire (in England, near the Welsh border, not in Wales), the eldest of four children of a railway worker. He was lay assistant to the vicar of Dunsden, Oxfordshire (1911-13), then taught English in Bordeaux from 1913.
[hist-eur2-s10.8.1] He enlisted in the Artists Rifles in October 1915 and was commissioned in the Manchester Regiment in June 1916. In January 1917 he joined the 2nd Manchesters on the Somme sector, near Beaumont-Hamel, in severe winter conditions.
[hist-eur2-s10.8.2] In March 1917 he suffered concussion. In April a shell blew him into the air and he lay for several days near the remains of a fellow officer. He was diagnosed with neurasthenia (shell shock, a nervous breakdown caused by the strain of bombardment).
[hist-eur2-s10.8.3] He arrived at Craiglockhart War Hospital in Edinburgh in June 1917 and edited its magazine, The Hydra. He met Siegfried Sassoon there in August 1917, and Sassoon encouraged him. He met Robert Graves there in October 1917.
[hist-eur2-s10.8.4] Between autumn 1917 and autumn 1918 he wrote or revised his best poems: Dulce et Decorum Est (drafted October 1917), Anthem for Doomed Youth (autumn 1917, opening 'What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?'), Strange Meeting, Futility and Exposure.
[hist-eur2-s10.8.5] He was both participant and witness: an officer who fought, and who wrote on behalf of the men he led, so his poems carry the authority of experience.
[hist-eur2-s10.9.b] Owen's poems attack the idea that war is glorious, using the detail of what he had witnessed. His fame came after his death, and historians still debate what he represents.
[hist-eur2-s10.9.0] Dulce et Decorum Est attacks 'The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est / Pro patria mori' (from Horace: it is sweet and fitting to die for one's country). Owen aims it at those at home who spread the idea.
[hist-eur2-s10.9.1] His method was graphic detail of gas, mud and death, and pararhyme (matching consonants with a changed vowel instead of full rhyme, as in Strange Meeting), which sounds uneasy and unresolved. His draft preface says 'My subject is War, and the pity of War. The Poetry is in the pity.'
[hist-eur2-s10.9.2] He returned to France in late summer 1918 and won the Military Cross for action at Joncourt on 1 October 1918. It was announced only after his death.
[hist-eur2-s10.9.3] He was killed on 4 November 1918 crossing the Sambre-Oise canal near Ors, aged 25, a week before the armistice. The telegram reached his parents in Shrewsbury on 11 November 1918.
[hist-eur2-s10.9.4] Owen was almost unknown in his lifetime, with only about five poems published. Poems (1920) was edited by Sassoon with Edith Sitwell, and Edmund Blunden produced a fuller edition in 1931.
[hist-eur2-s10.9.5] Contemporaries: in the 1920s he was one of several war poets, and the public were drawn at first to Brooke. In 1936 W. B. Yeats excluded him from The Oxford Book of Modern Verse. Interest grew after the 1930s as pacifist feeling spread.
[hist-eur2-s10.9.6] Later reputation: Benjamin Britten's War Requiem (1962), written for the consecration of the new Coventry Cathedral, sets nine Owen poems, and Owen became the best-known English poet of the war, taught in schools.
[hist-eur2-s10.9.7] Historians: the biographers Jon Stallworthy (1974) and Dominic Hibberd (2002) stress his craft and development. Revisionists such as Dan Todman argue that the war poets' view became a myth of futile war not shared by most soldiers, and the poets' view came from an educated officer class.
[hist-eur2-s10.10.b] The new German Reich was divided by religion. Chancellor Bismarck came to see the Catholic Church as a rival loyalty, and the conflict that followed was called the Kulturkampf.
[hist-eur2-s10.10.0] The Reich, proclaimed in January 1871, was a Protestant-led state under Prussia. Protestants were about two-thirds of its people and Catholics about a third, living mainly in the south, the Rhineland and the Polish east.
[hist-eur2-s10.10.1] In July 1870 the First Vatican Council (a gathering of the Church's bishops) declared papal infallibility, the doctrine that the Pope cannot err when defining Church teaching. Bismarck feared that German Catholics now owed their loyalty to Rome rather than to the Reich.
[hist-eur2-s10.10.2] The Catholic Centre Party, founded in December 1870 and led by Ludwig Windthorst, defended Catholic interests. Bismarck called Catholics, socialists and national minorities 'Reichsfeinde' (enemies of the Reich) and saw the Centre as an enemy of the new state.
[hist-eur2-s10.10.3] He also feared Polish Catholics in the east, because as a national minority whose faith set them apart from Protestant Prussia they seemed disloyal.
[hist-eur2-s10.10.4] The National Liberals, his allies in the Reichstag, were anticlerical (opposed to Church influence in public life). They welcomed an attack on the Church as a defence of modern secular society, so the campaign had liberal support.
[hist-eur2-s10.10.5] The conflict took its name in 1873 from Rudolf Virchow, a Progressive (liberal) deputy and scientist, who called it a Kulturkampf ('struggle for civilisation' or culture).
[hist-eur2-s10.11.b] Between 1871 and 1875 the Prussian and Reich governments passed laws that put schools, clergy and marriage under state control. The order matters, because each step escalated the conflict.
[hist-eur2-s10.11.0] In July 1871 the Catholic department of the Prussian Ministry of Culture was abolished, and in December 1871 the Reich's Pulpit Law (Kanzelparagraph) made it an offence for clergy to criticise the state in sermons.
[hist-eur2-s10.11.1] The School Supervision Law (Prussia, March 1872) put schools under state inspection, so the state rather than the clergy checked them. The Jesuit Law (July 1872) expelled the Jesuits (a Catholic religious order seen as loyal to the Pope) from Germany.
[hist-eur2-s10.11.2] In May 1872 Bismarck told the Reichstag, 'We will not go to Canossa' (Nach Canossa gehen wir nicht), a vow not to give way to the papacy.
[hist-eur2-s10.11.3] The May Laws of 1873 (Prussia), piloted by Adalbert Falk, Prussian Minister of Culture 1872-79, required priests to be educated at state universities and pass state exams, and made state approval necessary for church appointments.
[hist-eur2-s10.11.4] Further laws followed. Civil marriage (a state ceremony instead of a church one) became compulsory in Prussia in 1874 and in the whole Reich in 1875. The Breadbasket Law of 1875 stopped state payments to the clergy, and most religious orders were dissolved in 1875.
[hist-eur2-s10.11.5] Take care with names, because several laws share a label: these are the Prussian May Laws of 1873, not the Russian anti-Jewish May Laws of 1882 or the Anti-Socialist Law of 1878.
[hist-eur2-s10.12.b] The laws did not break Catholic loyalty to the Church. They drove Catholics together, and within a few years Bismarck needed peace.
[hist-eur2-s10.12.0] Pope Pius IX's encyclical Quod Nunquam (February 1875) declared the Prussian laws invalid. An encyclical is a formal letter from the Pope to the Church, known by its opening Latin words.
[hist-eur2-s10.12.1] By 1876 every Prussian bishop was in prison or exile, and many parishes had no priest, because priests could not be appointed without state approval.
[hist-eur2-s10.12.2] Catholics rallied behind the Centre under Ludwig Windthorst. In the Reichstag election of 1874 the Centre's vote roughly doubled, to about 1.4 million, and it won about 90 seats, making it the second-largest party.
[hist-eur2-s10.12.3] Bismarck had reasons to end it. The policy had failed and strengthened the Centre. He needed Centre votes for the tariff law of 1879 and against the socialists, the new enemy (the Anti-Socialist Law of 1878), and he had broken with the National Liberals.
[hist-eur2-s10.12.4] Pius IX died in February 1878 and Leo XIII was elected on 20 February 1878. The new pope was ready to negotiate, and Falk resigned in July 1879.
[hist-eur2-s10.12.5] After negotiations with Leo XIII, mitigation laws (1880-83) and peace laws (1886-87) repealed or relaxed most of the measures.
[hist-eur2-s10.12.6] Civil marriage, state school inspection and the Pulpit Law stayed. The Jesuit Law was relaxed in 1904 and repealed in 1917.
[hist-eur2-s10.13.b] The Kulturkampf began as an attempt to subdue the Church and ended with the state giving way. Its results show the limits of Bismarck's power.
[hist-eur2-s10.13.0] It was a political defeat for Bismarck, so he had to compromise with the Church and rely on the Centre Party he had set out to crush.
[hist-eur2-s10.13.1] The Centre became a permanent, large party in the Reichstag, because the attack had united Catholics behind it.
[hist-eur2-s10.13.2] Catholics felt alienated from the Protestant-led Reich, which left a lasting religious division in German politics.
[hist-eur2-s10.13.3] Many people saw it as a model of state-church conflict and as a failure of liberalism, because liberals had welcomed state force against a religious minority.
[hist-eur2-s10.14.b] In Italy the quarrel ran the other way round. The state was not attacking the Church; the Pope refused to accept a state that had taken his territory.
[hist-eur2-s10.14.0] Until 1870 the Pope ruled central Italy as a temporal (worldly) ruler over the Papal States. The Kingdom of Italy, proclaimed in March 1861, took most of them, and Italian troops entered Rome on 20 September 1870 (the breach at Porta Pia). Rome became the capital in 1871.
[hist-eur2-s10.14.1] The First Vatican Council had declared papal infallibility only in July 1870, just before Rome was lost, so the Pope lost his territory at the moment he claimed greater spiritual authority.
[hist-eur2-s10.14.2] The Law of Guarantees (May 1871) declared the Pope's person inviolable (he could not be attacked or arrested), gave him use of the Vatican, the Lateran and Castel Gandolfo, and offered a yearly payment.
[hist-eur2-s10.14.3] Pius IX rejected the law and refused the payment, because accepting a law made by the Italian parliament would have meant recognising the state that had taken Rome. He described himself as a prisoner in the Vatican.
[hist-eur2-s10.14.4] This unresolved dispute over the Pope's loss of Rome and his status was the Roman Question. The papacy's refusal to accept the loss divided Italians between the liberal, anticlerical state and the Catholic population.
[hist-eur2-s10.15.b] The Pope's refusal also shaped Italian politics, because he told Catholics to keep away from the state's elections. Catholics responded by building a separate world of their own.
[hist-eur2-s10.15.0] Non expedit ('it is not expedient') was an instruction of 1868, confirmed in 1874, that Catholics should neither vote in nor stand for parliamentary elections.
[hist-eur2-s10.15.1] As a result most Catholics stayed outside national politics, which left a narrow liberal ruling class in control of parliament.
[hist-eur2-s10.15.2] Catholics remained active in local government and in social organisations: the Opera dei Congressi (founded 1874), Catholic Action, banks, newspapers and cooperatives.
[hist-eur2-s10.15.3] The liberal state meanwhile expanded civil marriage and secular state schooling and closed religious houses, which deepened the divide.
[hist-eur2-s10.15.4] Leo XIII kept the hard line on Rome. Pius X relaxed non expedit from 1905, and in the Gentiloni Pact of 1913 Catholic voters backed the liberal candidates of Giovanni Giolitti who promised to defend Catholic interests.
[hist-eur2-s10.15.5] In January 1919, for example, Don Luigi Sturzo founded the Italian Popular Party (Partito Popolare Italiano). The Roman Question was settled only by the Lateran Treaty of 11 February 1929, beyond this period.
[hist-eur2-s10.15.6] Compare the two countries. Germany's conflict was an attack by the state on the Church and was ended by compromise by 1887. Italy's was the Church's refusal to accept the state, and it lasted until 1929.
[hist-eur2-s10.16.b] France's own quarrel between church and state is the setting for both Leo XIII's ralliement and the Dreyfus Affair, so its background has to be clear.
[hist-eur2-s10.16.0] After defeat by Prussia in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-71, France lost Alsace and part of Lorraine to Germany (Treaty of Frankfurt, 1871) and the Third Republic began in 1870. The army became the symbol of national pride and revenge.
[hist-eur2-s10.16.1] The Third Republic was divided between republicans, who were often anticlerical (hostile to the Church's role in public life), and monarchist Catholics, who distrusted the republic.
[hist-eur2-s10.16.2] The republic passed anticlerical laws: Jules Ferry's education laws of 1881-82 (free, compulsory, secular primary schooling), the Law of Associations (1901) and the Law of Separation of Church and State (December 1905). The Dreyfus Affair sped them up, because much of the Church sided against Dreyfus's defenders.
[hist-eur2-s10.17.b] Leo XIII was pope from 1878 to 1903. After the confrontational reign of Pius IX he tried to renew the Church's relations with governments and the modern world.
[hist-eur2-s10.17.0] Gioacchino (Vincenzo) Pecci was born on 2 March 1810 at Carpineto Romano. He was nuncio (the Pope's ambassador) to Belgium in 1843-46, Archbishop of Perugia 1846-78 and a cardinal from 1853, and was a diplomat and scholar.
[hist-eur2-s10.17.1] He was elected pope on 20 February 1878, after Pius IX's reign (1846-78, marked by infallibility, the 'prisoner in the Vatican' stance and the Kulturkampf at its height). He remained pope until his death on 20 July 1903, aged 93.
[hist-eur2-s10.17.2] He ended the Kulturkampf by negotiation with Bismarck in the 1880s, and the Centre Party's cooperation with the government helped to repeal most of the laws. In 1885 Bismarck referred the Caroline Islands dispute between Germany and Spain to him for arbitration.
[hist-eur2-s10.17.3] He encouraged scholarship. His encyclical Aeterni Patris (1879) urged the study of Thomas Aquinas, and in 1881 he opened the Vatican archives to researchers.
[hist-eur2-s10.17.4] In April 1888 the Holy Office (a Vatican department) issued a decree condemning the Plan of Campaign and boycotting in Ireland, which gives his papacy an Irish link.
[hist-eur2-s10.17.5] He refused to accept the loss of the Papal States and kept the 'prisoner in the Vatican' stance and non expedit in Italy, so the Roman Question remained open. He was also conservative on doctrine.
[hist-eur2-s10.18.b] Leo XIII is best known for two initiatives, a statement on workers' rights and a call for French Catholics to accept the republic. Both show how he tried to move the papacy from lost territory to moral influence.
[hist-eur2-s10.18.0] Rerum Novarum (15 May 1891), an encyclical on 'the condition of the working classes' (the rights and duties of capital and labour), answered the social question in general terms. It rejected socialism and class war and defended private property.
[hist-eur2-s10.18.1] It upheld the right of workers to a just wage, decent conditions and trade unions, and called on states to protect workers and the poor. It thus offered Catholics a route between socialism and unregulated capitalism.
[hist-eur2-s10.18.2] Rerum Novarum became the foundation of modern Catholic social teaching and encouraged Catholic trade unions and Christian democratic parties in Germany, Belgium, Italy and elsewhere. Pius XI marked its fortieth anniversary with Quadragesimo Anno (1931), which shows its lasting influence.
[hist-eur2-s10.18.3] The ralliement ('rallying') of 1890-92 urged French Catholics to accept the republic and work within it. It began with Cardinal Lavigerie's toast to the republic at Algiers in November 1890 and was set out in the encyclical Au milieu des sollicitudes (February 1892).
[hist-eur2-s10.18.4] Monarchist Catholics largely refused. The Dreyfus Affair and the anticlerical laws of the early 1900s, ending in the 1905 separation of church and state, wrecked the policy, because much of the Church sided with the anti-Dreyfusards.
[hist-eur2-s10.18.5] Contemporaries: governments and many workers respected him as a moderate reformer. Conservative Catholics distrusted his accommodation with republics, and socialists opposed his rejection of socialism.
[hist-eur2-s10.18.6] Historians regard him as the first modern pope, who moved the papacy from lost temporal power to moral and social influence. Critics regard Rerum Novarum as cautious and paternalist, and the ralliement as a failure.
[hist-eur2-s10.19.b] Anti-Semitism is hostility to Jews as a people. After 1870 older religious prejudice was joined by political and racial forms, which is why it matters in this period.
[hist-eur2-s10.19.0] Anti-Jewish prejudice was old in Europe and had been mainly religious. After 1870 political and racial anti-Semitism spread. Wilhelm Marr popularised the word 'anti-Semitism' and founded the League of Antisemites in 1879; the term existed before him.
[hist-eur2-s10.19.1] Economic depression from the stock-market crash of 1873 made many people resent Jewish success in finance, trade and the professions, and look for scapegoats.
[hist-eur2-s10.19.2] Nationalism that defined the nation by blood or religion treated Jews as outsiders, and pseudo-scientific race theory (claims, without scientific basis, that humans divide into biologically different races) gave prejudice a modern look.
[hist-eur2-s10.19.3] Jews were blamed at once for modernity, capitalism and socialism, so anti-Semites of very different politics could all find a target.
[hist-eur2-s10.20.b] French Jews were a small, assimilated minority, yet after 1870 nationalists and journalists made them scapegoats. The Dreyfus Affair grew out of this mood.
[hist-eur2-s10.20.0] French Jews had been full citizens since 1791. Defeat in 1870-71, the Panama Canal Company scandal of 1892 and economic fears gave anti-Semites targets, and nationalists blamed Jews and Freemasons for the country's weakness.
[hist-eur2-s10.20.1] Édouard Drumont's La France juive (1886) was a bestseller that portrayed Jews as a threat to France. In 1892 he founded the newspaper La Libre Parole to carry on the campaign.
[hist-eur2-s10.20.2] Captain Alfred Dreyfus, born in Mulhouse, Alsace, in 1859, was a Jewish artillery officer on the General Staff. Arrested on 15 October 1894, he was accused of passing secrets to Germany; the evidence was a memorandum, the bordereau.
[hist-eur2-s10.20.3] A court martial sitting in closed session convicted him in December 1894, after the judges were shown a secret file not given to the defence. He was publicly degraded on 5 January 1895 and sent to Devil's Island in French Guiana.
[hist-eur2-s10.21.b] The case became an Affair when evidence of Dreyfus's innocence emerged and the army refused to admit its mistake. Public opinion then split France in two.
[hist-eur2-s10.21.0] In 1896 Lieutenant-Colonel Georges Picquart, head of army intelligence, identified Major Ferdinand Walsin-Esterhazy as the real author of the bordereau. The army suppressed this: Picquart was posted to Tunisia, and Esterhazy was acquitted by court martial on 11 January 1898.
[hist-eur2-s10.21.1] On 13 January 1898 Émile Zola published the open letter 'J'accuse...!' to President Félix Faure in the newspaper L'Aurore (its editor Georges Clemenceau chose the headline), accusing the army of covering up the truth. Zola was convicted of libel in February and fled to England in July 1898.
[hist-eur2-s10.21.2] France divided. Dreyfusards, who defended Dreyfus, included Zola, Clemenceau, the socialist leader Jean Jaurès, many republicans and intellectuals, and the League of the Rights of Man (founded 1898). Anti-Dreyfusards included army leaders, much of the Catholic press, nationalists, monarchists and anti-Semitic leagues.
[hist-eur2-s10.21.3] Anti-Jewish riots broke out in French cities and in Algeria in 1898, which shows how far the prejudice had spread.
[hist-eur2-s10.21.4] In August 1898 the forgery by Lieutenant-Colonel Henry was exposed, and he cut his throat in prison.
[hist-eur2-s10.21.5] A second court martial at Rennes (August-September 1899) again found Dreyfus guilty, 'with extenuating circumstances', and gave him ten years. President Émile Loubet pardoned him in September 1899.
[hist-eur2-s10.21.6] In July 1906 the Cour de cassation (France's highest appeal court) quashed the Rennes verdict, and Dreyfus was reinstated in the army as a major and given the Legion of Honour.
[hist-eur2-s10.22.b] The Affair changed French politics, and it also had an effect beyond France through the birth of political Zionism.
[hist-eur2-s10.22.0] It was a republican victory and ended in justice for one man, because the courts and a free press could finally correct the army's error.
[hist-eur2-s10.22.1] It discredited the army, whose leaders had covered up the truth, and the Church, much of whose press had sided with the anti-Dreyfusards. It led to the anticlerical laws of the early 1900s and the 1905 separation of church and state.
[hist-eur2-s10.22.2] A new nationalist right emerged, such as Action Française (founded 1899), and anti-Semitism survived the Affair.
[hist-eur2-s10.22.3] Theodor Herzl reported on the case for the Vienna Neue Freie Presse and later said it convinced him that Jews needed a state of their own. Historians debate how much it mattered.
[hist-eur2-s10.22.4] Herzl published Der Judenstaat (The Jewish State) in 1896, and the First Zionist Congress, the first meeting of the movement for a Jewish national home (Zionism), met at Basel in August 1897.
[hist-eur2-s10.23.b] In the Russian Empire anti-Jewish prejudice was backed by the state. Laws confined Jews and kept many poor, and violent attacks on them were tolerated.
[hist-eur2-s10.23.0] The Empire was a tsarist autocracy (rule by a tsar with unlimited power) with the Orthodox Church as the state church and a policy of Russification (imposing Russian language and control on minorities).
[hist-eur2-s10.23.1] About five million Jews lived in the Empire in the late 1800s (the 1897 census), the largest Jewish population in the world. Many were poor artisans and traders.
[hist-eur2-s10.23.2] Most were confined to the Pale of Settlement, the western provinces where Jews were allowed to live, created in 1791 under Catherine the Great and abolished in 1917.
[hist-eur2-s10.23.3] Alexander II was assassinated on 13 March 1881 (1 March in the Russian calendar), and rumours blamed Jews. Pogroms (violent attacks on Jewish communities) began at Elizavetgrad in April 1881 and spread across the south, including Kiev and Odessa, continuing to 1884.
[hist-eur2-s10.23.4] Officials were slow to act or blamed the victims, so the violence spread unchecked.
[hist-eur2-s10.23.5] The May Laws (Temporary Regulations) of May 1882 banned new Jewish settlement in rural areas and restricted Jews from acquiring land and property outside towns. Though called temporary, they remained in force until 1917.
[hist-eur2-s10.24.b] Under Alexander III and Nicholas II discrimination became settled government policy, and violence continued.
[hist-eur2-s10.24.0] From 1887 quotas (numerus clausus) limited the number of Jewish students in secondary schools and universities. About 20,000 Jews were expelled from Moscow in 1891-92, and there were limits on the professions.
[hist-eur2-s10.24.1] Konstantin Pobedonostsev, Over-Procurator of the Holy Synod (the state official in charge of the Orthodox Church) from 1880 to 1905 and tutor and adviser to Alexander III and Nicholas II, supported discrimination and Russification. Alexander III and Nicholas II were themselves personally hostile to Jews.
[hist-eur2-s10.24.2] Some clergy spread anti-Jewish propaganda. From 1905 the Union of the Russian People, whose street gangs were called the Black Hundreds, did the same.
[hist-eur2-s10.24.3] The Kishinev pogrom in Bessarabia, at Easter on 6-7 April 1903 (19-20 April in the Western calendar), killed about 49 Jews and injured hundreds, and caused international outrage. Hayim Nahman Bialik wrote his poem 'In the City of Slaughter' in response.
[hist-eur2-s10.24.4] More pogroms followed in 1905-06 during the revolution, after the October Manifesto of 1905, and were worst in Odessa.
[hist-eur2-s10.24.5] For example, in the Beilis case Menahem Mendel Beilis, a Kiev Jew, was arrested in 1911 and tried for 'ritual murder' (the false claim that Jews murder for religious rites). He was acquitted in 1913.
[hist-eur2-s10.25.b] Forged propaganda spread the myth of a Jewish plot, while Jews responded by leaving, organising or joining revolution. The legal system ended only in 1917.
[hist-eur2-s10.25.0] The Protocols of the Elders of Zion, which claimed a Jewish plot to dominate the world, was forged in Russia. It was first serialised in the St Petersburg paper Znamya in 1903 and appeared in book form in 1905 (Sergei Nilus).
[hist-eur2-s10.25.1] In 1921 The Times showed that it was plagiarised from Maurice Joly's satire of Napoleon III (1864). It spread worldwide after 1917.
[hist-eur2-s10.25.2] About two million Jews left the Russian Empire between 1881 and 1914, most to the USA and others to Britain and Palestine, because persecution and poverty made life there hard.
[hist-eur2-s10.25.3] The General Jewish Labour Bund, a Jewish socialist party, was founded in Vilna in 1897. Zionism, the movement for a Jewish national home, was separate: it was a national movement, not a socialist one.
[hist-eur2-s10.25.4] Many young Jews also joined revolutionary parties, so persecution pushed some towards revolution as well as emigration.
[hist-eur2-s10.25.5] The Provisional Government removed all legal restrictions on Jews in March and April 1917, ending the Pale and the legal disabilities. Pogroms returned in the civil war.
[hist-eur2-s10.26.b] Examiners reward judgement, so each theme from national identity to anti-Semitism needs both a success and a limit. Each point pairs them.
[hist-eur2-s10.26.0] National identity in the arts: it liberated peoples without a state, such as the Czechs, Finns and Norwegians, but in Germany and Britain it fed imperial pride, exclusive nationalism and the war spirit of 1914.
[hist-eur2-s10.26.1] War literature: 1914-15 patriotism against 1917-18 protest. Contemporaries preferred Brooke, later generations Owen and Sassoon. The literature is valuable as eyewitness evidence, but the 'myth' argument reminds us that most soldiers did not share the protest view.
[hist-eur2-s10.26.2] Kulturkampf: a success in state control of schools and marriage, but a political failure, because the Centre was strengthened and Bismarck was forced to compromise.
[hist-eur2-s10.26.3] Italy: the liberal state survived and kept Rome, but there was a lasting split from Catholic Italy until 1929.
[hist-eur2-s10.26.4] Leo XIII: achievements in ending the Kulturkampf, Rerum Novarum and papal prestige, against limits in the failed ralliement and the unresolved Roman Question.
[hist-eur2-s10.26.5] Anti-Semitism compared: in France a popular and press movement that a republic finally defeated in the courts, giving justice to Dreyfus. In Russia a state-backed policy of laws and tolerated violence that drove mass emigration. Prejudice survived in both.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s8: 8. Industrialisation, economic crisis and revolution in Tsarist Russia, 1871–1920
[hist-eur2-s8.0.b] Russia was the largest state in Europe, but it was poor, overwhelmingly rural and ruled by an autocratic tsar. To see why it later faced crisis and revolution, you need to know how it was governed and how far behind the West it had fallen.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.0] Autocracy means the tsar ruled with no parliament and no constitution before 1905. He was backed by the Orthodox Church, the nobility, the army, the bureaucracy and the secret police (the Okhrana), so there was no legal way to challenge him.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.1] Defeat in the Crimean War (1853-56) convinced the government that Russia must modernise, because its army and economy could not match Britain and France. This led to the reforms of Alexander II.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.2] The emancipation of the serfs (peasants bound to their landlords) in 1861 was Alexander II's greatest reform. Peasants had to make redemption payments to the state over 49 years for the land they received, which left many in debt.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.3] Most peasants held land in the mir (the village commune), which periodically redistributed strips among households. This discouraged investment, because no one kept the same land long enough to improve it, so farming methods stayed backward.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.4] The census of 1897 showed about 126 million people, about four-fifths of them peasants. Russia was a multinational empire, and ethnic Russians were under half the population.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.5] In 1871 Russia had little heavy industry, few railways for its size and a weak banking system. It exported grain and relied on foreign capital (money invested from abroad) and technology.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.6] Alexander II's other reforms, such as the zemstva (elected local councils, from 1864), the courts and the army, were limited. He was assassinated in St Petersburg on 13 March 1881 (1 March in the Russian calendar) by the revolutionary group People's Will (Narodnaya Volya).
[hist-eur2-s8.0.7] Alexander III (1881-94) and Nicholas II (1894-1917) upheld autocracy and pursued Russification, forcing the Russian language and officials on minorities such as Poles and Finns. In January 1895 Nicholas told zemstvo delegates that hopes of a share in government were 'senseless dreams'.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.b] Russian dates and names can confuse you, because Russia ran on a different calendar and its capital changed names. Getting these right explains why the 'February' and 'October' revolutions happened in March and November.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.0] Russia used the old (Julian) calendar, which was 12 days behind the Western (Gregorian) calendar in the nineteenth century and 13 days behind in the twentieth. The Bolsheviks adopted the Western calendar in February 1918, when 31 January was followed by 14 February.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.1] Western dates come first with the Russian date in brackets. This is why the February Revolution took place in March 1917 and the October Revolution in November 1917.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.2] St Petersburg was renamed Petrograd in 1914, so use the name that is correct for the date. The capital moved from Petrograd to Moscow in March 1918.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.b] With no parliament and no legal opposition, those who wanted change had to organise in secret. By 1905 three main groups had formed, and they shaped everything that followed.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.0] Marxism sees history as a class struggle. Capitalism creates an industrial working class (the proletariat) that will overthrow it, which is why Russian Marxists welcomed industrialisation: it was creating the class that would make revolution.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.1] The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party was Marxist and was founded in 1898. At its second congress in 1903 it split into the Bolsheviks under Lenin and the Mensheviks under Martov.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.2] The Socialist Revolutionary Party (SRs), founded in 1901-02, was based on the peasantry, because its members believed Russia's revolution would rest on the land-hungry peasants rather than on factory workers.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.3] The Union of Liberation, founded in 1903-04, was made up of liberals who wanted a constitution, meaning written rules limiting the tsar's power.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.b] Sergei Witte, Finance Minister from 1892 to 1903, led a state-directed 'great spurt' of industrial growth. Russia had little private capital, so the state had to find the money and direct the effort itself.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.0] Witte believed industry was needed for Russia to remain a Great Power, because military strength rested on factories and railways. The state built railways, placed orders with factories and protected industry with tariffs (taxes on imports), including the high tariff of 1891.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.1] Foreign loans and investment paid for mines, ironworks and railways. They came chiefly from France, also from Belgium, Britain and Germany. The Franco-Russian Alliance of 1894 was the channel that made French money flow.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.2] Russia put the rouble on the gold standard in 1897. This meant the rouble could be exchanged for a fixed amount of gold, which reassured foreign investors that their money would keep its value.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.3] Heavy indirect taxes (taxes on goods such as sugar and vodka, paid by everyone) and grain exports provided the rest of the money. Peasants bore most of this burden.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.4] Witte's predecessor, Ivan Vyshnegradsky (Finance Minister 1887-92), had already pushed grain exports. The remark 'We shall go hungry, but we shall export' is usually attributed to him.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.b] Railways were the heart of the spurt. They tied a vast country together, carried raw materials and goods, and created demand for iron, coal and steel.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.0] The Trans-Siberian Railway was begun in 1891. A through route to Vladivostok was open by about 1904, running partly across Manchuria, and the all-Russian route along the Amur was completed in 1916.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.1] The railway network nearly doubled in the 1890s, from about 30,000 km to about 53,000 km, which is why iron and coal industries grew so fast.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.2] Coal and iron grew in the Donbas (the Donets basin) in Ukraine, oil in Baku in the Caucasus, and engineering and textiles in St Petersburg and Moscow.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.3] Industrial output grew at about 8 per cent a year in the 1890s. Coal and pig-iron output (iron fresh from the furnace, the raw material for steel) roughly tripled between 1890 and 1900.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.b] Rapid growth brought real power but also real strain. The same policies that built factories squeezed the peasants and gathered discontented workers together.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.0] Industry was concentrated in huge factories such as the Putilov works in St Petersburg, which had about 12,000 workers in 1905 and tens of thousands during the war. Large crowds of workers made organisation and protest easier.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.1] The peasantry carried the burden through taxes, and grain was exported even in hungry years, so the countryside paid for the towns.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.2] The industrial depression of 1900-03 caused unemployment and strikes. Witte moved out of the Finance Ministry in August 1903.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.3] Growth was rapid again from about 1908 to 1914. By 1914 Russia was often ranked the fifth industrial power in the world by total output, which is an estimate.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.4] Output per head was still low, because the huge peasant population produced little, and dependence on foreign capital was high. Germany's industry, by contrast, grew through private banks and firms rather than the state.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.b] Most Russians were peasants, and their lives were getting harder. In 1891 a harvest failure turned long-running poverty into a national disaster that damaged the regime's standing.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.0] Peasants faced small plots, old methods, rapid population growth and redemption payments. Land hunger (too many people for too little land) grew as families increased.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.1] The harvest failed in 1891 across the Volga and the central black-earth provinces (the rich soil belt), causing famine in 1891-92. Cholera and typhus followed in 1892, and deaths are estimated at about 375,000 to 500,000.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.2] Government relief was slow. Zemstvo and private volunteers, including Leo Tolstoy and Anton Chekhov, organised famine relief, which showed that educated Russians were doing what the state failed to do.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.3] The famine discredited the regime and revived opposition among educated Russians, such as the liberals of the Union of Liberation (1903-04) and the SRs (1901-02).
[hist-eur2-s8.6.4] Peasant unrest grew, with attacks on estates in Kharkov and Poltava provinces in 1902 and far wider violence in 1905-06.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.5] Workers were mostly first-generation peasants who kept links with their villages. This spread discontent between town and country.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.b] Russia's rivalry with Japan in the Far East led to a war it was confident of winning. Defeat instead exposed the regime's weakness and set off revolution at home.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.0] Russia and Japan were rivals over Manchuria and Korea. Russia had leased Port Arthur from China in 1898, and the Trans-Siberian Railway gave it a route east.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.1] Vyacheslav von Plehve, Minister of the Interior from 1902 until he was assassinated in July 1904, is reported to have wanted 'a short victorious war'. Instead, defeat in the war helped trigger the 1905 revolution.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.2] Japan attacked the Russian fleet at Port Arthur on the night of 8-9 February 1904, before declaring war. Port Arthur surrendered on 2 January 1905.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.3] Russia lost the land battle of Mukden in February-March 1905. The Baltic Fleet, which had sailed right around the world, was destroyed at Tsushima on 27-28 May 1905.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.4] The Treaty of Portsmouth (New Hampshire, USA) of 5 September 1905 was mediated by President Theodore Roosevelt. Japan gained southern Sakhalin, the Port Arthur lease and recognition of its control of Korea.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.5] Defeat humiliated the autocracy and exposed the weakness of the army, transport and administration, so it was the trigger of the 1905 revolution.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.b] Defeat abroad and hardship at home produced a year of revolt. The tsar's response moved from bullets to concessions in a matter of months.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.0] On 22 January 1905 (9 January in the Russian calendar) Father Georgy Gapon led a procession to the Winter Palace to petition the tsar. Troops fired on it. The official figure was about 130 dead, though other estimates run to several hundred or more.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.1] Bloody Sunday broke the people's faith in the tsar as their protector, the 'little father'. Strikes, peasant risings, the mutiny on the battleship Potemkin (Black Sea Fleet, June 1905) and national movements in Poland and the Caucasus followed.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.2] In October 1905 a general strike paralysed the railways and cities. The St Petersburg Soviet (council) of Workers' Deputies was formed, with Trotsky as its leading figure.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.3] On Witte's advice the tsar issued the October Manifesto on 30 October 1905 (17 October in the Russian calendar). It promised civil liberties, an elected Duma (parliament) with power over laws, and a wider franchise (more people able to vote).
[hist-eur2-s8.8.4] Witte then became the first chairman of the Council of Ministers, in effect prime minister, serving from October 1905 to April 1906.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.b] Despite a year of revolt, the regime held on. It split its enemies, kept the army, found money abroad and then took back much of what it had promised.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.0] The Manifesto split the opposition. The Octobrists (led by Alexander Guchkov) accepted it. The Constitutional Democrats or Kadets (led by Pavel Milyukov) wanted a constituent assembly and more power for the Duma, and the socialists rejected it.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.1] The army stayed loyal once peace had been made with Japan, so the tsar could use force. The Soviet's leaders were arrested in December 1905 and the Moscow armed rising was crushed that month.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.2] Redemption payments were halved for 1906 and abolished from 1 January 1907, a concession to the peasants announced in November 1905.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.3] A very large French-led international loan in April 1906, about 2,250 million francs, let the government survive without depending on the Duma for money.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.4] The Fundamental Laws of 6 May 1906 (23 April in the Russian calendar) were published shortly before the First Duma met. The tsar remained 'Supreme Autocrat', since the word 'unlimited' was dropped.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.5] He appointed ministers, controlled the army and foreign policy and had an absolute veto over laws. He could dissolve the Duma and issue emergency decrees when it was not sitting (Article 87).
[hist-eur2-s8.9.6] An upper house, the State Council, was half appointed, so it balanced the elected Duma in the tsar's favour.
[hist-eur2-s8.10.b] Peter Stolypin, Prime Minister from July 1906 to September 1911, combined repression with reform. The Dumas show how little real power the elected parliament had.
[hist-eur2-s8.10.0] The First Duma (May-July 1906) demanded land reform and ministers responsible to it. It was dissolved after about 72 days. The Second Duma (February-June 1907) was also dissolved.
[hist-eur2-s8.10.1] In June 1907 the electoral law was changed by decree (the 'coup of 3 June'). Landowners and the wealthy then dominated the Third Duma (1907-12) and the Fourth Duma (1912-17).
[hist-eur2-s8.10.2] The Duma became a place for criticism but not control, because the tsar kept his veto and could still dissolve it.
[hist-eur2-s8.10.3] Stolypin, also Minister of the Interior, crushed disorder with field courts-martial from August 1906. More than a thousand people were executed, and the hangman's noose became known as 'Stolypin's necktie'.
[hist-eur2-s8.11.b] Stolypin wanted to win the peasants over by making them owners. He needed time, and he did not get it.
[hist-eur2-s8.11.0] The land decree of November 1906 let peasants leave the commune, own land privately and consolidate their strips into single farms. The Peasant Land Bank lent them money to buy land.
[hist-eur2-s8.11.1] Resettlement of millions of peasants in Siberia was encouraged. The aim was a loyal class of prosperous peasants, often called a 'wager on the strong', meaning the sober and hardworking peasants.
[hist-eur2-s8.11.2] By 1914 about two million households, roughly a fifth to a quarter depending on how they are counted, had left the commune. Most peasants stayed in it.
[hist-eur2-s8.11.3] Stolypin said that if the state gave him twenty years of peace, Russia would be transformed. This is a paraphrase, not a quotation.
[hist-eur2-s8.11.4] Dmitri Bogrov, a revolutionary who was also a police informer, shot Stolypin at the Kiev opera in September 1911 with Nicholas II present. Stolypin died a few days later.
[hist-eur2-s8.11.5] Protest revived after 1912. Troops shot striking miners at the Lena goldfields in Siberia in April 1912, killing well over a hundred, and large strikes broke out in St Petersburg in July 1914.
[hist-eur2-s8.12.b] A world war is a war between great alliances that spreads across continents and draws on a country's whole economy and population. Russia's economy and government could not stand that strain, and the war pushed an already weak regime towards collapse.
[hist-eur2-s8.12.0] Russia mobilised a huge army, but it was poorly equipped. It lost at Tannenberg in August 1914 and the Masurian Lakes in September 1914, both German victories in East Prussia. The great retreat of 1915 lost Russian Poland.
[hist-eur2-s8.12.1] The Brusilov offensive of June-September 1916 against Austria-Hungary succeeded but at heavy cost. Over the war Russia suffered millions of dead, wounded and prisoners.
[hist-eur2-s8.12.2] About 15 million men were mobilised between 1914 and 1917, which took labour from the farms and cut food output. Railways were overloaded, so food and fuel could not reach the cities.
[hist-eur2-s8.12.3] Inflation rose as the government printed money. The state also lost revenue when the sale of vodka was banned in 1914, because the vodka monopoly had been a major source of income. Food and fuel were short in the cities by winter 1916-17.
[hist-eur2-s8.12.4] Nicholas II took personal command at army headquarters in September 1915. This left Empress Alexandra, who was German-born and so suspected, and Rasputin influential in government, and ministers changed rapidly.
[hist-eur2-s8.12.5] The Progressive Bloc, formed in the Duma in August 1915, demanded a government with public confidence.
[hist-eur2-s8.12.6] Grigori Rasputin was a peasant mystic whom the empress trusted because he seemed to ease the haemophilia of the heir, Alexei. Prince Felix Yusupov and other aristocrats murdered him in December 1916, a sign of the regime's isolation.
[hist-eur2-s8.13.b] The regime that had survived 1905 fell in a few days in 1917. The key difference was that this time the soldiers refused to fire on the crowds.
[hist-eur2-s8.13.0] On 8 March 1917 (23 February in the Russian calendar), International Women's Day, women textile workers in Petrograd demanded bread. Their strikes grew into a general strike in the city.
[hist-eur2-s8.13.1] The garrison regiments mutinied on 12 March (27 February) and joined the crowds instead of firing on them. This was the key difference from 1905, when the army stayed loyal.
[hist-eur2-s8.13.2] On the same day the Duma set up a Provisional Committee and the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies was revived.
[hist-eur2-s8.13.3] The generals advised abdication. Nicholas II abdicated on 15 March (2 March) at Pskov, for himself and Alexei, and his brother Grand Duke Michael declined the throne the next day.
[hist-eur2-s8.13.4] This ended about 300 years of Romanov rule, which had begun in 1613.
[hist-eur2-s8.14.b] After the tsar fell, two bodies claimed authority. The new government's decisions on war and land gave the Bolsheviks their chance.
[hist-eur2-s8.14.0] Prince Georgy Lvov headed the Provisional Government from March to July 1917, and Alexander Kerensky from July. It shared authority with the Petrograd Soviet, an arrangement called 'dual power' (dvoevlastie).
[hist-eur2-s8.14.1] The Soviet's Order No. 1, issued in March 1917, told soldiers to obey the Provisional Government's military orders only if the Soviet agreed. This weakened the government's control of the army.
[hist-eur2-s8.14.2] The government granted civil liberties but kept fighting the war and postponed land reform until a Constituent Assembly could meet. The failed July (Kerensky) offensive of 1917 brought more desertions, and peasants began to seize land.
[hist-eur2-s8.14.3] Lenin returned to Petrograd's Finland Station in April 1917 (16 April; 3 April in the Russian calendar), crossing Germany by train with German permission. He issued the April Theses.
[hist-eur2-s8.14.4] The Theses demanded 'All power to the Soviets' and promised 'Peace, land and bread'. These slogans appealed to soldiers, peasants and hungry workers.
[hist-eur2-s8.15.b] Within months of the April Theses the Bolsheviks went from a small party to the rulers of Petrograd. Failure by their opponents and careful organisation by Trotsky made this possible.
[hist-eur2-s8.15.0] The July Days of July 1917 were a failed Bolshevik-backed rising, and Lenin went into hiding in Finland.
[hist-eur2-s8.15.1] General Lavr Kornilov, commander-in-chief, moved troops towards Petrograd in mid-September 1917 (late August / early September in the Russian calendar). Kerensky armed the Petrograd workers (Red Guards) and released Bolshevik prisoners. The Kornilov affair thus armed the Red Guards and boosted the Bolsheviks.
[hist-eur2-s8.15.2] The Bolsheviks won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow Soviets in September. Trotsky became chairman of the Petrograd Soviet from October (late September) and organised its Military Revolutionary Committee.
[hist-eur2-s8.15.3] The rising began on 6-7 November (24-25 October in the Russian calendar). Bolshevik forces seized the stations, bridges and telegraph, and the Winter Palace was taken in the early hours of 8 November (26 October).
[hist-eur2-s8.15.4] The Second All-Russian Congress of Soviets met on 7-8 November (25-26 October). It approved the Council of People's Commissars (Sovnarkom), with Lenin as chairman.
[hist-eur2-s8.15.5] The Decree on Peace and the Decree on Land followed on 8 November (26 October), together with the Declaration of the Rights of the Peoples of Russia. The Cheka (the Bolshevik secret police) was founded in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky.
[hist-eur2-s8.15.6] In the Constituent Assembly election of November 1917 the SRs won about 40 per cent of the vote and the Bolsheviks about 24 per cent, about a quarter. The Assembly met once, on 18-19 January 1918 (5-6 January), and was dispersed by Red Guards.
[hist-eur2-s8.16.b] The Bolsheviks had promised peace, but Germany demanded a high price. The treaty shows how weak the new government was.
[hist-eur2-s8.16.0] An armistice (a halt in fighting) with Germany was agreed in December 1917. Trotsky, Commissar for Foreign Affairs, stalled at Brest-Litovsk with a policy of 'neither war nor peace', hoping revolution would spread.
[hist-eur2-s8.16.1] Germany advanced again in February 1918, so the Bolsheviks gave in. A Soviet delegation signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918.
[hist-eur2-s8.16.2] Russia lost Poland, the Baltic provinces (Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia), Finland and Ukraine. This was about a third of the population of European Russia, with much of its coal, industry and farmland.
[hist-eur2-s8.16.3] The treaty was cancelled after Germany's defeat in November 1918.
[hist-eur2-s8.16.4] The capital moved from Petrograd to Moscow in March 1918, away from the German threat.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.b] Within months the Bolsheviks faced armed enemies at home and abroad. They won, and the reasons why are a favourite exam question.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.0] The Romanovs, Nicholas II, Alexandra and their children, were shot at Ekaterinburg on 16-17 July 1918, so no tsar could become a rallying point for the Whites.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.1] The civil war was fought between the Reds (the Bolsheviks) and the Whites, a loose mix of generals, monarchists and liberals: Admiral Kolchak in Siberia, General Denikin in the south, General Yudenich in the north-west against Petrograd and General Wrangel in the Crimea in 1920.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.2] Trotsky, Commissar for War from March 1918, built the Red Army and used tens of thousands of former tsarist officers, watched by political commissars. The Czech Legion, former prisoners of war, revolted along the Trans-Siberian Railway in May 1918.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.3] Britain (at Murmansk and Archangel), Japan and the USA (at Vladivostok) and France in the south landed troops from 1918. Their effect was limited.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.4] War Communism (1918-21) meant seizing grain from peasants, nationalising industry and rationing. The Red Terror was launched in September 1918 after an attempt on Lenin's life in August 1918.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.5] Kolchak was defeated in 1919 and executed in February 1920. Yudenich was turned back near Petrograd in October 1919. Denikin's advance on Moscow was defeated in autumn 1919 and he resigned in 1920. Wrangel was evacuated from the Crimea in November 1920.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.6] The Reds held the central core with its railways, industry, the capitals and the largest population, so they could move troops. They had one command and one aim.
[hist-eur2-s8.17.7] The Whites were divided and scattered, and were linked to landlords and foreign powers, so many peasants feared their return. In the Polish-Soviet war of 1919-20 the Red Army's advance on Warsaw was defeated in August 1920.
[hist-eur2-s8.18.b] Self-determination was one of the most powerful ideas of the period, and the revolution gave it a particular Russian shape. Examiners ask how it was applied and how it was denied.
[hist-eur2-s8.18.0] Self-determination is the right of a people to choose its own government and state.
[hist-eur2-s8.18.1] The Bolsheviks applied it in the Declaration of the Rights of the Peoples of Russia in November 1917, which recognised the right of peoples to self-determination. Finland declared independence in December 1917 and the Soviet government recognised it at the end of December 1917 or January 1918.
[hist-eur2-s8.18.2] Poland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania became independent in 1918, confirmed by treaties with Soviet Russia in 1920. Poland's border was settled in 1921.
[hist-eur2-s8.18.3] It was denied elsewhere: the Red Army brought Ukraine and other borderlands back under Bolshevik rule. For Lenin, self-determination came second to the revolution.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.b] Tsarist Russia was changing fast, but its political system could not keep up. Judgement questions reward answers that weigh achievements against limits and long-term against short-term causes.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.0] Achievement: Witte built railways and heavy industry, growth resumed after 1908, and Stolypin began to create a class of independent farmers.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.1] Limit: growth rested on foreign capital and a poor peasantry, and it created a discontented working class in huge factories.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.2] Limit: political reform stopped short, because the Dumas were weak. Nicholas II mistrusted reformers, with Witte let go in 1906 and Stolypin's position weak by 1911, and he wasted the chance of 1905-06.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.3] Why the regime survived 1905 but fell in 1917: in 1905 the army stayed loyal and the opposition was split. In 1917 the army was exhausted, the cities were starving and the soldiers joined the strikers.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.4] Long-term causes (economic backwardness, autocracy, the peasantry) set against the short-term cause (the war) is the central judgement. Decide which mattered most and show how one led to the other.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.5] Historians: the 'optimists' argue Russia was modernising and the war caused the collapse. The 'pessimists' argue revolution was likely anyway. Leopold Haimson pointed to the 1912-14 strike wave, and Orlando Figes stresses the regime's failure to reform.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.6] Alexander Gerschenkron argued that in a backward country the state substitutes for missing private capital, with Witte as the example. Richard Pipes sees October as a coup by a minority party, while Soviet historians saw a popular workers' revolution.
[hist-eur2-s8.19.7] Contemporary views: liberals blamed the tsar's obstinacy, and the Bolsheviks presented October as a workers' revolution. Opponents called it a seizure of power by a minority party.
