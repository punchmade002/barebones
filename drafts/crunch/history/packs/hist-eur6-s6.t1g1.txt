[hist-eur6-s6.0.b] The Great Depression of the 1930s left the United States with high unemployment, and it was the Second World War that finally ended it. The war then left the country with an industrial economy far stronger than any rival's, and that was the platform for the postwar boom.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.0] The United States entered the war after the attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941. Between 1941 and 1945 its factories turned out vast numbers of aircraft, ships, tanks and munitions, so jobs were plentiful.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.1] Unemployment fell to very low levels, about 1 to 2 per cent at its lowest, in 1944, which ended the Depression.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.2] The United States was never bombed or invaded, so its factories survived intact. In 1945 it produced about half of the world's manufactured goods, while its European and Japanese rivals had been devastated by the war.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.3] Rationing, wage and price controls and war bonds meant households could not spend freely during the war, so they saved heavily. When rationing and controls ended in 1945-46 families had large sums to spend.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.4] Wartime shortages of consumer goods also built up a pent-up demand (buying that had been held back and was now released) for cars, houses and household appliances after 1945, which kept factories busy once war orders fell.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.5] The Servicemen's Readjustment Act, known as the GI Bill and signed in June 1944, paid tuition and living allowances for veterans, offered government-guaranteed loans for homes, farms and businesses, and gave unemployment allowances for a year.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.6] The GI Bill's home loans helped to create a mass market for housing, which fed demand for consumer goods and for the construction industry. In practice black veterans often met discrimination in access to loans and colleges, especially in the South.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.7] The wartime government became the largest customer in the economy and built many plants itself, in industries such as aluminium, synthetic rubber and aircraft. The Surplus Property Act of 1944 arranged their sale after 1945, and large firms bought most of them cheaply.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.b] The United States also designed the postwar international money system and paid for much of Western Europe's recovery. Both made the dollar the central currency and gave American firms customers abroad. This setting was the Cold War, in which the US aimed to contain Soviet influence and wanted strong trading partners.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.0] The Bretton Woods conference of July 1944 in New Hampshire created the International Monetary Fund (IMF), which supports exchange rates with short-term loans, and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (the World Bank), which lends for reconstruction and development.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.1] Under the Bretton Woods system the US dollar was convertible to gold at 35 dollars an ounce and other currencies were pegged (fixed) to the dollar. This made the dollar the reserve currency, the one other countries held to settle international trade.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.2] War-damaged Europe could not earn enough dollars to buy what it needed, a shortage known as the dollar gap. American loans and aid filled it, and they were spent on US goods, so the dollar's role helped US exporters. It also let American companies invest abroad cheaply.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.3] The Marshall Plan (European Recovery Program) of 1948 to 1952 gave about 13 billion dollars to Western European states, after George Marshall announced it in June 1947. Much of it was spent on American goods, which boosted US exports and revived trading partners.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.4] The Korean War began in June 1950, and NSC-68 (a policy paper of April 1950) urged a large rearmament. Defence spending roughly quadrupled between 1950 and 1953, so rearmament added a great deal of demand to the economy.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.5] The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), signed in 1947 and in force from 1948, committed the United States and other states to lower trade barriers (limits such as tariffs on imports). This suited the world's biggest exporter.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.b] The federal government did not leave growth to the market alone. Spending on roads, defence, science and education built up whole industries in the 1950s and 1960s.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.0] The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, signed by Eisenhower, funded an Interstate Highway System of about 41,000 miles. It opened up suburban development and boosted car production and steel.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.1] The Interstate programme was paid for from a Highway Trust Fund financed by fuel taxes. It was also justified on defence grounds, such as evacuating cities and moving troops, and Eisenhower had been impressed by the German autobahns.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.2] Federal defence spending stayed high through the 1950s and 1960s, which supplied a large and steady market for aircraft, electronics and missile manufacturers.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.3] Defence research paid for the development of jet aircraft, radar, nuclear power for submarines and later computers. These spin-offs (civilian uses of military technology) then spread into civilian industry.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.4] The National Science Foundation was created in 1950, and federal research funding grew steadily, with universities acting as partners to government research.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.5] The Soviet Union launched Sputnik in October 1957, and the United States founded NASA in 1958. The space programme then employed thousands of engineers and contractors in the 1960s.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.6] The National Defense Education Act of 1958 funded science, mathematics and foreign-language teaching and student loans, because Sputnik made Americans fear they were falling behind in science.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.b] Large, lasting defence budgets created a network of armed forces, contractors and politicians with a shared interest in keeping spending high. The military-industrial complex is the name given to that alliance, and Eisenhower warned of it as he left office.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.0] Eisenhower's farewell address was broadcast on 17 January 1961, three days before he left office. He said: "we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex."
[hist-eur6-s6.3.1] He also noted a "conjunction of an immense military establishment and a large arms industry" that was new in American experience. His point was about influence over policy, not an attack on defence spending in general.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.2] Major defence contractors such as Lockheed, Boeing, General Dynamics and Douglas received large government contracts, and many companies depended on them for much of their revenue, so they had a strong interest in high defence budgets.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.3] The warning came from a former general whose own administration had built up the nuclear arsenal and relied on it. Critics raised this irony to argue that he had helped create the problem.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.4] Defence and space contracts fuelled growth in California and later Silicon Valley (the electronics and semiconductor centre). A stable source of contracts encouraged high-technology industries, including computing.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.5] Critics argued that the complex distorted national priorities, because money and skilled scientists were drawn to weapons rather than to schools, housing or civilian needs. C. Wright Mills's The Power Elite was an earlier critical study of a small group of leaders.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.b] The Organization Man is not a single real person but a type: a salaried corporate manager or professional who moved with the company. William H. Whyte named the type in 1956. It shows corporate capitalism, an economy dominated by large corporations run by salaried managers.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.0] William H. Whyte, an editor at Fortune magazine, published The Organization Man in 1956 as a study of white-collar workers (office and managerial employees) in large corporations and suburban communities.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.1] Whyte argued that the corporate employee had accepted a social ethic that valued belonging, teamwork and conformity over individualism and personal conscience.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.2] The social ethic had three beliefs: that the group is the source of creativity, that belonging is the individual's greatest need, and that science can be applied to achieve belonging.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.3] Whyte studied Park Forest, Illinois, a planned suburb outside Chicago of young corporate families who moved often and valued being sociable. He also criticised the personality tests companies used in hiring, which rewarded fitting in.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.4] The Organization Man was a salaried manager or professional, often moving between cities and suburbs as the company required. His career depended on loyalty to the corporation and on fitting in with its expectations.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.5] Other writers shared the worry about conformity. David Riesman's The Lonely Crowd (1950) described the other-directed person, guided by the approval of others, and C. Wright Mills's White Collar (1951) described anxious salaried workers.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.6] Sloan Wilson's novel The Man in the Gray Flannel Suit (1955) portrayed a similar figure, an executive torn between career success and family life.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.7] Contemporaries praised the corporation for stability, security and rising living standards, but many critics saw the Organization Man as a threat to individual creativity and independence.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.8] Historians use the figure as a useful portrait of 1950s corporate culture and conformity, though later scholars argue that the book overstated the conformity of American corporate life.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.b] A multinational corporation is a company that owns and runs operations in several countries. From the 1950s American multinationals expanded overseas, which is a key part of globalisation, the growing integration of national economies through trade, investment and multinationals.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.0] General Motors, IBM, Coca-Cola and the International Telephone and Telegraph Corporation (ITT) were large US multinationals with big overseas operations by the 1960s. They spread by direct investment, subsidiaries, franchises and local bottlers, and the purchase of existing firms.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.1] IBM's World Trade Corporation, created in 1949 to run IBM's overseas business, shows how a company could organise itself for the world market. By the 1960s a large share of IBM's revenue came from outside the United States.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.2] Coca-Cola grew abroad through franchised local bottlers, who bought the syrup and sold the drink in their own market. The system was built up from the 1920s and extended widely after 1945.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.3] General Motors owned Opel in Germany (since 1929) and Vauxhall in Britain (since 1925), and Ford also had European subsidiaries, so American carmakers built and sold cars across Europe.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.4] ITT under Harold Geneen, chief executive from 1959, became a conglomerate (a company owning unrelated businesses) by buying firms in many industries and countries in the 1960s. It later became notorious for its involvement in Chile.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.5] US firms invested heavily in Western Europe after the Treaty of Rome of 1957 created the European Economic Community (in force 1958), a large single market behind a common external tariff. Building plants inside it let them avoid the tariff.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.6] US foreign policy supported the expansion by encouraging open trade and investment, and the strong dollar made it cheap for American companies to buy assets abroad.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.7] Critics in Europe, such as Jean-Jacques Servan-Schreiber in The American Challenge (1967), warned that US firms were taking over key European industries. The Fortune 500 list of the largest American industrial companies, first published in 1955, showed how far a few big firms dominated the economy.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.b] The boom was shared with many industrial workers because trade unions were strong in its core industries. Their agreements with large firms tied pay to the growth the corporations enjoyed.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.0] Union members reached about a third of non-farm workers in the mid-1950s, concentrated in steel, car manufacturing, mining and transport.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.1] The United Auto Workers and General Motors signed a five-year contract in 1950, often called the Treaty of Detroit. It linked wages to productivity and the cost of living and provided pensions and health insurance, while management kept control of production decisions.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.2] Such agreements gave many manual workers rising real wages and benefits, which means pay that bought more over time, and they became a model for other unions in the core industries.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.3] The Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 restricted union tactics such as secondary boycotts (action against a firm's customers or suppliers) and the closed shop (only hiring union members), and it allowed states to pass right-to-work laws. It also required union leaders to sign non-communist affidavits.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.4] The American Federation of Labor and the Congress of Industrial Organizations merged in 1955 under George Meany, which created a single labour federation.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.5] Unions were weakest in the South and among service and farm workers, who were often excluded from the postwar bargain.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.b] Government economists believed that spending and tax policy could keep growth going, and the 1964 tax cut was the major test of that belief. The idea comes from John Maynard Keynes, who argued that governments can manage demand (the total spending in the economy) through spending and taxation.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.0] The economic advisers of Kennedy and Johnson accepted Keynes's ideas. In practice this meant cutting taxes or raising spending to boost demand when growth slowed.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.1] Walter Heller, chairman of the Council of Economic Advisers under Kennedy, argued for a tax cut to stimulate demand and growth, because lower taxes leave households and firms with more to spend and invest.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.2] The Revenue Act of 1964, signed by Johnson, cut taxes and reduced the top income tax rate from 91 per cent to 70 per cent.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.3] Strong economic growth followed in the mid-1960s, so the tax cut is often cited as a successful example of demand management before the costs of the Vietnam War appeared.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.4] Milton Friedman, a monetarist (someone who stresses controlling the money supply), argued that governments could not fine-tune the economy.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.b] A strong judgement weighs the scale of the boom against its dependence on temporary US dominance and on government spending. It also compares what contemporaries praised with what later critics noticed.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.0] Achievement: between 1945 and 1968 living standards rose steadily, home ownership became widespread and a large middle class emerged, helped by the GI Bill and rising union wages.

[hist-eur6-s6.8.2] Limit: critics argue the boom rested on defence spending and large public investment, such as the Interstate system and research funding, and not only on the free market.

[hist-eur6-s6.8.4] John Kenneth Galbraith's The Affluent Society (1958) argued that the boom created private wealth but left public services such as schools and parks neglected, an idea summed up as private affluence and public squalor.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.5] Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) showed that tens of millions still lived in poverty despite the boom.

[hist-eur6-s1.0.b] The US Constitution (drafted 1787) divides power between the federal government in Washington and the fifty state governments. That division, called federalism, shaped the civil rights and social policy fights of the period because southern states defended segregation as their own business.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.0] The Bill of Rights (1791) added the Tenth Amendment, which reserves powers not given to the federal government to the states, so states controlled schools, policing, voting rules and marriage law.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.1] Before the 1960s most southern states enforced segregation (the separation of black and white people, often called Jim Crow) by state law, and federal action against it was met with the argument that such matters belonged to the states. This argument is known as states' rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.2] In 1948 southern Democrats angry at Truman's civil rights programme left the party and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party (the Dixiecrats) with Strom Thurmond as candidate. It carried four Deep South states: Alabama, Louisiana, Mississippi and South Carolina.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.3] The Civil Rights Act 1957 was the first federal civil rights law since Reconstruction (the years after the Civil War when the North tried to protect black rights in the South). It set up a Civil Rights Commission and let the Justice Department act over voting rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.4] In September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus of Arkansas used the National Guard to stop nine black students entering Little Rock Central High School. Eisenhower replied by sending federal troops, including the 101st Airborne Division, to enforce integration, so the federal government overruled a state governor.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.5] The Civil Rights Act, signed on 2 July 1964, rested on the federal power to regulate interstate commerce (trade between states), which allowed Congress to ban segregation in hotels, restaurants and other public places even though they were privately owned.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.6] The Voting Rights Act, signed on 6 August 1965, suspended literacy tests used to keep black people off the voting lists and sent federal examiners to register voters. It put federal law above state practice, and it is a separate law from the 1957 and 1964 Acts.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.b] The Constitution gives law-making, executing and judging to three different bodies, so that no one can rule alone. Each can also limit the others, a system called checks and balances, and the period from 1945 to 1989 shows each check being used.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.0] Congress (the Senate and House of Representatives) makes laws, raises taxes and declares war; the President executes the laws, commands the armed forces as commander in chief and conducts foreign policy; the Supreme Court interprets the Constitution.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.1] The President can veto (refuse to sign) bills, but Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds majority in both houses. The Senate must also confirm senior appointments and ratify treaties by a two-thirds vote, which stops a President acting alone.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.2] Republicans won both houses of Congress in the November 1946 midterm elections (held halfway through a presidential term), using the slogan "Had enough?". It was the first Republican control since the 1928 elections, so Truman faced a hostile Congress until January 1949.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.3] That Congress passed the Taft-Hartley Act in 1947, restricting trade union activity such as the closed shop (a workplace where only union members can be hired), and overrode Truman's veto in June 1947. Truman did not simply give in: he campaigned against that Congress and won in 1948.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.4] Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer: Truman seized the steel mills in April 1952 during a strike without congressional authority. In June 1952 the Supreme Court ruled that he had exceeded his powers, a check on the President by the courts.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.5] The Twenty-Second Amendment, passed by Congress in March 1947 and ratified in 1951, limits a president to two elected terms. It was a direct response to Roosevelt winning four elections, so it is a check on the length of a President's power, not on a single act.

[hist-eur6-s1.2.0] The Warren Court (led by Chief Justice Earl Warren, appointed by Eisenhower in 1953) used judicial review, the power to strike down acts that break the Constitution, to extend civil rights. Brown v. Board of Education (1954) rested on the Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.1] Miranda v. Arizona (1966) on the rights of arrested suspects was another Warren Court ruling, so the Court was shaping social policy as well as checking the President.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.2] United States v Nixon (July 1974): the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Nixon must hand over the White House tapes, and his resignation followed on 9 August 1974.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.3] Earlier, in November 1973, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto to limit the President's power to commit troops without congressional approval, so Congress took back a power the President had been using.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.4] The Boland Amendments (1982 to 1984) restricted US aid to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua. Officials in the Reagan administration kept the Contras supplied in breach of them, which is the core of the Iran-Contra affair.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.b] From the 1930s the President's staff and agencies grew steadily, a development called the presidential bureaucracy. It meant that more policy was shaped inside the White House and its agencies rather than in Congress, which is why people began to worry about presidential power.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.0] The Executive Office of the President was created in 1939 under Roosevelt, giving the President a personal staff separate from the government departments, so the President no longer depended only on cabinet departments.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.1] The National Security Act of July 1947, signed by Truman, created the National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and the National Military Establishment. It was renamed the Department of Defense in 1949.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.2] The National Security Council gave the President a forum of advisers on foreign and defence policy, so decisions could be made without going through the State Department and Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.3] The Council of Economic Advisers was created by the Employment Act of 1946, which committed the federal government to promote maximum employment, production and purchasing power. This made the President responsible for the health of the economy.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.4] Public opinion, meaning what ordinary voters think, increasingly shaped presidents as polls and media grew. Kennedy held live televised press conferences from January 1961, using them as a direct channel to the public that bypassed newspapers and Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.5] Arthur Schlesinger Jr. described the expanded executive in The Imperial Presidency (1973). The phrase became the standard label for concern over presidential power after Watergate,, which also led Congress to reassert its oversight of presidents.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.b] Roosevelt led the US through the Depression and most of the Second World War, and his coalition and the wartime economy shaped the politics that Truman inherited in 1945. Roosevelt's New Deal (from 1933) had expanded federal power and welfare, and 'liberalism' in the US sense came to mean an active federal government promoting welfare and civil rights.

[hist-eur6-s1.4.1] The New Deal coalition combined urban workers, trade unions, Catholics and Jews, farmers, southern whites and black voters who moved to the Democrats from the 1930s. It gave the Democrats a majority, but it held together very different groups.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.2] Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945, and Harry Truman, vice president since January 1945, became president while the Second World War and the start of the Cold War were still in progress.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.3] Truman inherited a wave of strikes in 1945 and 1946 as the wartime economy was converted to peacetime production (reconversion), when workers wanted higher pay while prices were rising.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.4] Truman's party was divided between northern liberals and conservative southern Democrats. The southerners often voted with Republicans in Congress (the conservative coalition) to block reform, so a Democratic president could not rely on a Democratic Congress.

[hist-eur6-s1.6.5] Historians since the 1970s, and David McCullough's biography Truman (1992), have generally ranked him among the stronger presidents for his decisiveness and his civil rights initiatives.

[hist-eur6-s1.7.b] After Truman, the Republican Eisenhower accepted the New Deal's main structures rather than undoing them, and Kennedy then promised a more active government. Both presidents faced the federal-state conflict over civil rights, and Kennedy's death left his bills to Johnson.

[hist-eur6-s1.8.8] The cost of the Vietnam War from 1965 drained money and attention from the Great Society, and Republican gains in the 1966 midterms slowed new legislation.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.b] Between 1969 and 1981 three presidents faced scandal, recession and inflation, and the public's trust in the presidency fell. Watergate was the turning point, because it forced a resignation and showed Congress and the Court could check a President.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.0] Richard Nixon won the 1968 election narrowly and was re-elected in 1972 by carrying 49 states, so his fall in 1974 came soon after a landslide.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.1] Nixon's domestic record included the Environmental Protection Agency (1970), wage and price controls (August 1971) to fight inflation, and the expansion of affirmative action (favouring minorities) in federal contracts.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.2] Five men were arrested for burgling the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate complex on 17 June 1972, and the Washington Post's reporting kept the scandal in the news. [hist-eur6-s1.9.5] The Camp David Accords of September 1978, brokered by Carter, led to a peace treaty between Egypt and Israel signed in March 1979. Carter also created the Departments of Energy (1977) and Education (1979).
[hist-eur6-s1.9.6] High inflation and the 1979 oil shock undermined Carter. His televised speech of July 1979 on a "crisis of confidence", nicknamed the malaise speech, was seen by critics as blaming the public.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.7] The Iran hostage crisis held 52 American diplomats and citizens in Iran from 4 November 1979 to 20 January 1981, 444 days. An attempted rescue in April 1980 failed, and the crisis was a major cause of Carter's defeat.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.b] Ronald Reagan won in 1980 promising smaller government and tax cuts, reversing the liberal direction of the New Deal and Great Society. His first term set the conservative agenda, and his second was clouded by Iran-Contra.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.0] Ronald Reagan defeated Carter in 1980 and was re-elected in 1984, carrying 49 states against Walter Mondale, which shows how popular he stayed.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.1] In his first inaugural address (20 January 1981) Reagan said that "government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem", which summed up his case against federal power.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.2] The Economic Recovery Tax Act of August 1981 cut income tax rates by about 25 per cent over three years and lowered the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.3] Reagan's first term combined cuts to many domestic social programmes with a large rise in defence spending, a pattern known as Reaganomics.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.4] When air traffic controllers in the PATCO union struck in August 1981, Reagan dismissed the strikers, signalling a hard line on public sector unions, because the strikers worked for the federal government.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.5] Iran-Contra was revealed in November 1986: the administration had sold arms to Iran and diverted the money to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua despite congressional restrictions, the Boland Amendments.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.6] The Tower Commission (February 1987) criticised Reagan's detached management style. National Security Adviser John Poindexter and his aide Oliver North were among those prosecuted.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.7] Reagan's popularity survived the scandal and he left office in January 1989 with high approval ratings, earning the nickname "the Teflon president" because scandals did not stick to him.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.b] Judging presidents means weighing what each achieved against where each failed, and contemporary verdicts often differ from later ones. A good answer gives a stated judgement with dated evidence on each side.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.0] Truman: his civil rights initiative, including desegregating the armed forces, and his leadership in the early Cold War (the Marshall Plan and NATO) are usually seen as successes.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.1] Truman's critics point to the failure to pass national health insurance, because a hostile Congress blocked it, and to the unpopularity of the Korean War.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.2] Johnson: the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts and Medicare are counted as major achievements, while his Vietnam policy is widely judged a failure that drained the Great Society.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.3] Nixon and Ford: Nixon's opening to China and his arms control agreements are credited as achievements, but Watergate and the secret bombing of Cambodia are judged serious failings, and Ford's pardon of Nixon cost him public trust.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.4] Yet the Court's ruling and the War Powers Resolution show that Congress and the Court acted as effective checks on the President.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.5] Carter: the Camp David Accords are judged a success, while the Iran hostage crisis, inflation and his 1980 defeat are the main charges against him.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.6] Reagan: supporters credit his tax cuts and conservative revival, and they claim he helped to end the Cold War, which is their view and not settled fact. Critics point to the rising deficit, the 1981 to 1982 recession and Iran-Contra.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.7] Public opinion polls made presidential popularity visible from the 1930s onwards, and the Watergate and Iran-Contra scandals led Congress to reassert its oversight of presidents.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.8] Contemporary verdicts often differed from later ones: Truman left office in 1953 deeply unpopular, yet his reputation rose sharply in later historians' rankings, as did Eisenhower's, while Johnson's soared in 1964 and collapsed by 1968.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s2: 2. US foreign policy, 1945–1972: Berlin, Korea, Cuba and Vietnam
[hist-eur6-s2.0.b] Before 1941 the United States kept out of most quarrels in Europe and Asia. The Second World War ended that, and by 1945 it faced a rival superpower, the Soviet Union, whose system was the opposite of its own.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.0] Pre-war US isolationism (staying out of other countries' conflicts) was ended by Pearl Harbor and the war, so after 1945 the United States followed internationalism: active involvement in world affairs instead of withdrawal.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.1] The war left the United States the richest industrial power and, until the Soviet test in 1949, the only atomic power, which gave it the means to lead a worldwide policy.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.2] The wartime alliance with the Soviet Union broke down because Soviet forces held Eastern Europe at the war's end; the strain showed at the Yalta conference (February 1945) and the Potsdam conference (July to August 1945).
[hist-eur6-s2.0.3] Churchill's "iron curtain" speech at Fulton, Missouri, in March 1946 gave the division of Europe between East and West its best-known image.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.4] The Cold War rested on a clash of systems: communism, with the state controlling the economy under one party, against capitalism, with private ownership and markets.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.5] In China a civil war was being fought between Mao Zedong's Communists and Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists, so Asia was drawn into the same contest as Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.6] Imperialism means domination of one country by another through political, military or economic control. In this topic it is applied to US influence in Cuba and to Soviet control of Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.7] Germany, and Berlin within it, was divided into four occupation zones or sectors (US, British, French and Soviet). Berlin lay deep inside the Soviet zone, so the three Western sectors, which became West Berlin, were cut off from the West by Soviet-held territory.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.b] Containment means checking Soviet and communist expansion by firm resistance. It grew out of a new weapon and one diplomat's analysis of how the Soviet Union behaved.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.0] The atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, on Truman's authority, which left the United States as the only country with atomic weapons.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.1] The Soviet Union tested its own bomb on 29 August 1949, which ended the American monopoly, so the United States could no longer rely on the bomb alone to hold Soviet power back.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.2] George Kennan, a US diplomat in Moscow, sent his Long Telegram in February 1946, arguing that Soviet expansion could be checked by firm, long-term resistance rather than by negotiation.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.3] Kennan's article "The Sources of Soviet Conduct", published in Foreign Affairs in July 1947 under the signature X, made the same case in public and gave containment its name.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.4] Keep four terms apart: containment is the idea (Kennan), the Truman Doctrine is political and military aid to Greece and Turkey, the Marshall Plan is economic aid to Western Europe, and NATO is the alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.b] Between 1947 and 1950 a run of crises turned Kennan's idea into American policy and open commitments.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.0] In February 1947 Britain told the United States it could no longer support Greece and Turkey, so Washington decided to take over that burden.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.1] In the Truman Doctrine speech to Congress on 12 March 1947 Truman asked for about 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey and committed the United States to support free peoples resisting armed minorities or outside pressure.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.2] The Communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 convinced many in Washington and Western Europe that the Soviet threat was immediate, so it made them readier to act.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.3] Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949. Republicans asked "Who lost China?" and used the question to attack Truman's Asia policy, which made it harder for him to look weak abroad.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.4] NSC-68, a paper by the National Security Council (the President's foreign-policy advisory body), called in April 1950 for a large rise in defence spending to meet the Soviet threat. It was approved as policy in autumn 1950, after the Korean War began.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.b] Containment in Western Europe had an economic side. American money would rebuild damaged economies so that poverty and unrest did not push voters towards communism.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.0] George Marshall, the Secretary of State, proposed a European recovery programme in a speech at Harvard University on 5 June 1947.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.1] The European Recovery Program, passed by Congress in April 1948, provided about 13 billion dollars of aid to Western European countries between 1948 and 1952.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.2] The plan had three aims: economic recovery in Western Europe, stopping the spread of communism, and rebuilding European markets for American goods.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.3] Most aid was given as grants of food, fuel and machinery rather than as loans, so recipients received supplies they did not have to repay as ordinary debt.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.4] The Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC) was set up in April 1948 to allocate the aid, and Britain, France and West Germany were the largest recipients.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.5] The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and pressed its satellite states (the Eastern European countries it controlled) to refuse too. Czechoslovakia first accepted, then withdrew in July 1947 under Soviet pressure, so Europe settled more firmly into two blocs.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.b] Aid was matched by a military guarantee. In 1949 the defence of Western Europe and the future of Germany were both settled in ways that made the division of Europe harder to undo.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.0] The North Atlantic Treaty was signed on 4 April 1949 by twelve countries, including the United States, Britain, France, Canada and Italy.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.1] NATO, founded in April 1949 on the Article 5 principle of collective defence, was the first peacetime alliance of its kind that the United States had joined, a clear break from pre-war isolationism and a sign that internationalism now meant permanent commitments.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.2] Article 5 of the treaty stated that an armed attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, which is the basis of collective defence: any attacker would face the whole alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.3] Germany was divided as well: the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) was founded in May 1949 in the Western zones, and the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) followed in October 1949 in the Soviet zone.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.b] Berlin lay inside the Soviet zone, so the Soviet Union could squeeze the Western sectors without invading. The crisis of 1948 to 1949 tested whether the West would stay.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.0] The Soviet Union began blocking road, rail and canal access to West Berlin on 24 June 1948, aiming to force the Western powers out of the city.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.1] The Western powers responded with an airlift that flew food, coal and other supplies into West Berlin. More than two million tons were carried before the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.2] Truman chose the airlift rather than an armed convoy.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.3] The result was a Western success: the Soviet Union lifted the blockade without achieving its aim, and the West held Berlin without firing a shot.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.b] Berlin remained a flashpoint after the airlift. From 1958 the Soviet leader pressed the West to leave, and in 1961 the border between East and West Berlin was sealed.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.0] In November 1958 Khrushchev demanded that the Western powers withdraw from Berlin within six months, and the issue was debated at the Vienna summit with Kennedy in June 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.1] More than two million East Germans had fled to the West between 1949 and 1961, mostly by crossing from East to West Berlin, which drained the East's skilled workforce.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.2] The Berlin Wall was built from 13 August 1961, closing the border between East and West Berlin and stopping the flow of refugees.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.3] In October 1961 American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, the crossing point for foreigners, in a tense standoff that ended without shots.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.4] The Wall was a propaganda defeat for the East, because it had to wall in its own people, but the West accepted it rather than tearing it down, so the division of the city became permanent.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.5] Kennedy visited West Berlin on 26 June 1963 and declared in German "Ich bin ein Berliner" (I am a Berliner), a statement of solidarity that was widely praised in the West.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.b] Korea was split by the superpowers in 1945. In 1950 the split turned into war, and the United States fought under the flag of the United Nations.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.0] Korea was divided at the 38th parallel in 1945. The Republic of Korea in the South under Syngman Rhee and the Democratic People's Republic in the North under Kim Il Sung were both founded in 1948.

[hist-eur6-s2.7.3] The Council could act because the Soviet delegate was absent (the Soviet Union was boycotting it), so Security Council resolutions could be passed.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.4] Truman sent American forces without asking Congress for a declaration of war and described the operation as a police action, so the war was never formally declared.
