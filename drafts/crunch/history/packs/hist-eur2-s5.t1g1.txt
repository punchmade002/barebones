TARGET hist-eur2-s5: 5. The conduct of war, 1914–1918
[hist-eur2-s5.0.b] All the Great Powers expected a short war. Germany's plan was to defeat France in about six weeks and then turn on Russia, but the plan failed and the Western Front became a stalemate by the end of the year.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.0] Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August 1914 and took Liège, then swept through Belgium and northern France under the Schlieffen Plan, modified by the younger Moltke, who weakened the right wing.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.1] The British Expeditionary Force, a small professional army, fought its first major action at Mons on 23 August 1914 and retreated with the French.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.2] The First Battle of the Marne (5-12 September 1914) halted the German advance about 40 kilometres from Paris. The Germans withdrew to the River Aisne and dug in.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.3] Both sides then tried to outflank each other in the 'race to the sea', and the line of trenches reached the North Sea and the Swiss frontier by late 1914.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.4] The First Battle of Ypres (October-November 1914) was a hard-fought defensive success for the British and French; the BEF lost much of its regular army and Britain had to rely on volunteers.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.5] At Christmas 1914 troops along parts of the line took part in unofficial truces, which the high commands then forbade.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.6] In the east, Russia invaded East Prussia, but General Hindenburg and his chief of staff Ludendorff destroyed the Russian Second Army at Tannenberg (26-30 August 1914), capturing about 90,000 men, and defeated the First Army at the Masurian Lakes in September.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.b] Defensive weapons were stronger than the means of attack, so battles on the Western Front produced very high casualties for small gains.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.0] Trench systems had a front line, support and reserve trenches linked by communication trenches, with barbed wire and the shell-torn ground of no man's land between the opposing lines.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.1] Soldiers lived with mud, rats, lice, cold and the constant danger of shelling and snipers. Trench foot and shell shock (what we now call combat stress) were common.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.2] The machine gun and quick-firing artillery made frontal attacks costly; most battle casualties were caused by shellfire.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.3] Attackers relied on long preliminary bombardments, which warned the enemy and churned up the ground. From 1916 the 'creeping barrage' moved ahead of infantry as they advanced.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.4] Poison gas was first used on a large scale by Germany at the Second Battle of Ypres (22 April 1915), when chlorine was released against French colonial and Canadian troops. Phosgene and mustard gas followed, and gas masks reduced its effect.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.5] Tanks were first used by Britain at Flers-Courcelette on 15 September 1916. They were slow and unreliable, but at Cambrai (November 1917) hundreds were used together with artillery and infantry.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.6] Aircraft were used for reconnaissance, artillery spotting and, later, fighter combat and bombing. Germany used Zeppelins and Gotha bombers against British cities.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.7] By 1918 both sides used combined arms: Germany developed stormtrooper infiltration tactics, and the Allies combined tanks, aircraft, artillery and infantry.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.b] In a war of attrition each side tried to wear down the other's manpower and resources. Generals on both sides believed a breakthrough was possible, and they continued costly offensives.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.0] In 1915 the Allies launched offensives at Neuve Chapelle, Artois and Loos, and the Germans used gas at Ypres. None broke the line, and shell shortages in Britain caused a political crisis in May 1915.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.1] Verdun (21 February to 18 December 1916): the German chief of staff Falkenhayn attacked the symbolically important French fortress, intending, according to his later account, to bleed the French army white. Both sides lost hundreds of thousands of men.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.2] The French commander at Verdun, General Pétain, rotated divisions through the battle, and the road known as the Sacred Way supplied the front. Verdun became a symbol of French endurance.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.3] The Somme (1 July to 18 November 1916) was a joint British-French offensive planned partly to relieve Verdun. On the first day the British Army lost about 57,000 casualties, of whom about 19,000 were killed, the worst single day in its history.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.4] The Somme advanced the Allied line only a few kilometres and cost over a million casualties in total across all armies. It was the first big battle for Kitchener's volunteer 'Pals' battalions, and wiped out the male population of some local communities.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.5] Nivelle's offensive in April 1917 along the Chemin des Dames failed with heavy losses, causing mutinies in the French army; Pétain replaced Nivelle and restored morale by improving conditions.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.6] Passchendaele (the Third Battle of Ypres, July to November 1917) was fought in the Ypres salient, partly to clear German submarine bases in Belgium. Heavy rain and shell damage turned the ground to mud, and the final gain was only about 8 kilometres.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.7] At Cambrai (20 November 1917) Britain used hundreds of tanks in a surprise attack; the gains were mostly lost to a German counter-attack.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.0] The Central Powers broke the Russian line at Gorlice-Tarnow in May 1915, forcing a great Russian retreat from Poland and Galicia.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.1] The Brusilov Offensive (June 1916), led by General Brusilov against Austria-Hungary, was the most successful Allied offensive of the year. It took many prisoners and forced Germany to move troops east, but it cost Russia about a million casualties and could not be sustained.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.2] Romania joined the Allies in August 1916 and was largely overrun by Germany and its allies by the end of the year.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.3] Defeats, losses, shortages and the failure of the Tsar's government led to the February Revolution of 1917 (see the notes on Tsarist Russia). The Provisional Government continued the war, and the Kerensky Offensive of July 1917 collapsed.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.4] After the Bolsheviks seized power in October 1917, they signed an armistice in December 1917 and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918, giving up large parts of Poland, the Baltic lands, Ukraine and Finland.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.5] The treaty let Germany move over a million men west for the offensives of 1918, but it also tied down troops in the occupied east.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.0] Gallipoli: after Ottoman entry into the war in October-November 1914, Britain and France tried to force the Dardanelles in March 1915. The land campaign began on 25 April 1915 with landings of British, French, Australian and New Zealand (ANZAC) troops. The campaign was stalemated and the troops were evacuated by 9 January 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.1] Gallipoli damaged the reputation of Churchill, who had promoted it, but helped to build the sense of national identity of Australia and New Zealand.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.2] Italy joined the Allies in May 1915 under the Treaty of London, promised territory from Austria-Hungary. Eleven battles on the Isonzo brought little gain, and Italy suffered a disastrous defeat at Caporetto in October 1917 before recovering at Vittorio Veneto (October-November 1918).
[hist-eur2-s5.4.3] In the Middle East, the British fought the Ottomans in Mesopotamia (surrender at Kut in April 1916) and in Palestine, where General Allenby captured Jerusalem in December 1917 and won at Megiddo in September 1918. The Arab Revolt was supported by T.E. Lawrence.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.4] In the Balkans, Serbia was overrun by the Central Powers in 1915; the Allies maintained a front at Salonika. Bulgaria asked for an armistice on 29 September 1918, and the Ottomans and Austria-Hungary followed in October and November.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.5] Germany's colonies were captured by the Allies, though German East Africa held out until the end of the war.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.0] Britain used its fleet to blockade Germany, stopping imports of food and raw materials. Germany suffered shortages, and the winter of 1916-17, known as the turnip winter, was especially severe.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.1] The German High Seas Fleet was cautious. The only major fleet engagement, Jutland (31 May to 1 June 1916), cost Britain 14 ships and about 6,000 men, and Germany 11 ships and about 2,500 men. The British fleet kept control of the North Sea and the German fleet remained mostly in port.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.2] Germany turned to submarines. The sinking of the liner Lusitania on 7 May 1915, with almost 1,200 dead including 128 Americans, outraged American opinion, and Germany limited its campaign.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.3] On 1 February 1917 Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, hoping to starve Britain into surrender within months. British shipping losses were very high in spring 1917, and Britain had food stocks for only weeks.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.4] The adoption of the convoy system in 1917 and improved anti-submarine methods reduced losses, and the campaign failed to knock Britain out of the war while bringing the USA in.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.b] Mass war needed mass armies. Continental states already had conscription, but Britain had relied on volunteers and had to change.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.0] Germany and France called up all fit men under the pre-war system of conscription. Russia did the same.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.1] In Britain, Kitchener's recruiting campaign raised over two million volunteers by the end of 1915. As losses grew and numbers fell, the Military Service Act of January 1916 introduced conscription for unmarried men aged 18 to 41; it was extended to married men in May 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.2] Conscientious objectors could appear before tribunals, and about 16,000 men claimed that status. Some were jailed or sent to non-combatant units.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.3] The Military Service Act of 1916 did not apply to Ireland. A proposal in April 1918 to extend conscription to Ireland provoked the Conscription Crisis, a nationwide campaign of opposition, and was never put into effect.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.4] The USA introduced conscription through the Selective Service Act of May 1917. Germany passed the Auxiliary Service Law in December 1916, which required all men aged 17 to 60 to work for the war effort.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.5] Conscription and casualties raised the question of 'reserved occupations': governments had to keep skilled workers in munitions, mines and railways, and sometimes replaced them with women (see the notes on women in the workforce).
[hist-eur2-s5.7.0] The Zimmermann Telegram, a secret German offer to Mexico of an alliance against the USA, was intercepted by British intelligence and published in the American press in March 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.1] The USA declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917, after the submarine campaign sank American ships and the Zimmermann Telegram, as an 'associated power' rather than a formal ally. Its troops under General Pershing arrived in strength during 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.2] With Russia out, Ludendorff planned the Spring Offensive (Kaiserschlacht), launched on 21 March 1918 with 'Operation Michael'. It made large gains and came close to Amiens, but did not break the Allied line.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.3] In response, the Allies set up a unified command under Marshal Foch in April 1918. German troops were exhausted and short of supplies, and a counter-attack at the Second Battle of the Marne (July 1918) turned the tide.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.4] The Hundred Days began with the Battle of Amiens on 8 August 1918, which Ludendorff called the 'black day of the German army'. Allied tanks, aircraft and artillery pushed the Germans back to the Hindenburg Line, which was broken in late September.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.5] On 29 September 1918 Ludendorff told the Kaiser that Germany needed an armistice at once. Germany asked for terms using Wilson's Fourteen Points, and the armistice was signed in a railway carriage at Compiègne at 5 a.m. on 11 November, taking effect at 11 a.m.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.6] Military deaths in the war are usually put at about nine to ten million, with many more wounded, and civilian deaths in the millions.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.b] Douglas Haig commanded the British Expeditionary Force in France from December 1915 until the end of the war. He is the most controversial British commander of the war.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.0] Born in Edinburgh in 1861 into a whisky-distilling family, Haig attended Sandhurst, joined the cavalry and served in the Sudan and the Boer War before becoming a corps commander in 1914.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.1] He succeeded Sir John French as commander-in-chief of the BEF in December 1915 and was promoted field marshal in January 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.2] He commanded the Somme offensive of 1916, the Arras offensive and Passchendaele in 1917, and the defensive battles of 1918. On 11 April 1918 he issued his 'backs to the wall' order.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.3] He led the Allied advance in the Hundred Days of 1918, in which British forces played the leading part and took many prisoners.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.4] After the war he was made an earl and given a grant of money. He founded the British Legion in 1921 and supported the sale of poppies. He died in 1928, and his funeral was a national occasion.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.5] Contemporaries: King George V and the army trusted him; Lloyd George distrusted him, thought his battles wasteful, and withheld reinforcements in early 1918. Many soldiers, and the public after the war, mourned him.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.6] From the 1920s memoirs, notably Lloyd George's, criticised him. In the 1960s Alan Clark's The Donkeys popularised the phrase 'lions led by donkeys', which Clark misattributed to German generals.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.7] Historians such as Denis Winter and Gerard De Groot depicted Haig as a callous and unimaginative butcher; John Terraine, Gary Sheffield and others argue that he adapted, learned from experience and led the army to victory in 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.8] Many historians now accept that his attritional strategy was shaped by circumstances: the strength of defences, the need to support France, and the poor communications that limited control over battles.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.0] Allied success: the blockade, attrition and the arrival of American troops in 1918 wore down Germany's capacity to fight, and 1918's coordinated tank, artillery and air attacks broke its army.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.1] Limit: the Western Front battles of 1915-17 produced casualties out of proportion to gains, and Passchendaele and the Somme became symbols of waste.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.2] German success: Germany held the Western Front for four years, knocked Russia out of the war and nearly won in spring 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.3] German failure: the Schlieffen Plan failed in 1914, unrestricted submarine warfare brought in the USA, and the army was exhausted by 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.4] Contemporary view: the troops themselves, and many officers, criticised the high command; later views range from Clark's 'donkeys' to Sheffield's 'Forgotten Victory'.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s4: 4. Serbia, the Second International and the outbreak of war in 1914
[hist-eur2-s4.0.b] Serbia became fully independent in 1878. Its drive to unite the South Slavs threatened Austria-Hungary, and its link with Russia made a Serbian quarrel a Great Power matter.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.0] The Congress of Berlin (1878) recognised Serbia's full independence from the Ottoman Empire and enlarged its territory. Serbia became a kingdom in 1882 under the Obrenović dynasty.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.1] Austria-Hungary dominated Serbia's economy, which depended on exporting pigs and other livestock to the Habsburg market, and it kept Serbia in its sphere until the early 1900s.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.2] Serbian nationalists dreamed of uniting the South Slavs (Serbs, Croats, Slovenes and the Slavs of Bosnia) in a single state. This threatened a Habsburg empire with millions of South Slavs.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.3] In June 1903 a group of army officers murdered King Alexander Obrenović and Queen Draga in Belgrade. Peter I of the Karadjordjević dynasty became king.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.4] The new regime turned away from Vienna and towards Russia and France. Russia, the great Slav and Orthodox power, saw itself as protector of the Balkan Slavs and wanted to prevent Austrian control of the Balkans.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.5] The Pig War began in 1906, when Austria-Hungary closed its market to Serbian livestock to punish its trade links with France. Serbia found other markets, including Germany, Italy and France, and the war weakened Austrian influence.
[hist-eur2-s4.0.6] Austria-Hungary feared that a strong Serbia could encourage the Slav nationalities of the empire to leave, and Chief of Staff Conrad von Hötzendorf repeatedly urged a preventive war against it.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.0] Austria-Hungary had occupied and administered Bosnia-Herzegovina since 1878, though it remained under nominal Ottoman rule.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.1] The Young Turk revolution of July 1908 in the Ottoman Empire raised the possibility that Bosnia would be reclaimed, so Austria acted.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.2] In September 1908 the Austrian foreign minister Aehrenthal and the Russian foreign minister Izvolsky met at Buchlau and discussed a swap: Austria would annex Bosnia, and Russia would gain support for opening the Straits to its warships.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.3] Austria announced the annexation on 6 October 1908, before Izvolsky had won agreement on the Straits, and Russia felt cheated.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.4] Serbia, which regarded Bosnia as the heartland of Serb population, mobilised and demanded compensation; Serbian nationalism and anti-Austrian feeling grew, and the Narodna Odbrana society was formed to resist Austrian rule.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.5] In March 1909 Germany told Russia to accept the annexation and drop its support for Serbia, or face consequences. Russia, still weak after the war with Japan and revolution of 1905, gave way; Serbia also had to accept.
[hist-eur2-s4.1.6] The crisis humiliated Russia and Serbia, and left both resolved not to back down again. Russia accelerated its rearmament, and Austria-Hungary and Germany drew closer.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.0] In 1912 Russia helped to build the Balkan League of Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro, aimed at the Ottoman Empire. Italy had just taken Libya from the Ottomans in 1911–12.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.1] The First Balkan War (October 1912 to May 1913) saw the League defeat the Ottomans and take most of their European territory.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.2] The Great Powers meeting at the London Conference (1912–13) created an independent Albania, supported by Austria-Hungary and Italy, which denied Serbia the Adriatic port it wanted.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.3] The League then quarrelled over the spoils. In the Second Balkan War (June to August 1913) Bulgaria attacked Serbia and Greece and was beaten by them, with Romania and the Ottomans joining in.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.4] By the Treaty of Bucharest (August 1913) Serbia doubled in size and took much of Macedonia, including Kosovo.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.5] A stronger Serbia, backed by Russia and unsatisfied over the Adriatic, alarmed Austria-Hungary further, and Conrad pressed for war against it.
[hist-eur2-s4.2.6] The Balkan Wars showed that a regional conflict could be contained by Great Power conference diplomacy (London 1912–13); but they left Austria-Hungary feeling that the next time it would have to fight.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.0] The Black Hand (Unification or Death) was a secret society of Serbian army officers founded in May 1911. Its leader was Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, chief of Serbian military intelligence.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.1] It sought a Greater Serbia and trained and armed Bosnian nationalists, particularly those of the Young Bosnia movement, who wanted to end Habsburg rule over the South Slavs.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.2] Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, went to Sarajevo to inspect army manoeuvres, on 28 June 1914. It was St Vitus's Day (Vidovdan), the anniversary of the Serbian defeat at Kosovo in 1389.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.3] A first attempt in the morning, a bomb thrown by Nedeljko Čabrinović, failed to kill the Archduke. Later his driver took a wrong turn, and the car stopped near Gavrilo Princip, who shot Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.4] Princip was a 19-year-old Bosnian Serb. He and the other conspirators had been given weapons and training in Serbia, but how much the Serbian government knew of the plot is disputed.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.5] The assassins were arrested; Princip was too young for the death penalty and died of tuberculosis in prison in 1918.
[hist-eur2-s4.3.6] Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and decided to use the murder to destroy it, but first needed to know how Germany would respond.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.0] On 5 and 6 July Germany gave Austria-Hungary its 'blank cheque', a promise of unconditional support in whatever action it took against Serbia. The Kaiser and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg hoped for a short, localised war.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.1] French President Poincaré and his prime minister visited St Petersburg from 20 to 23 July, reaffirming French support for Russia.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.2] On 23 July Austria-Hungary presented an ultimatum to Serbia with ten demands and a 48-hour deadline. They included the suppression of anti-Austrian propaganda and a role for Austrian officials in the investigation within Serbia.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.3] Serbia accepted most demands on 25 July but refused Austrian officials a role on its soil. Austria broke off relations and declared war on Serbia on 28 July.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.4] Russia ordered partial mobilisation on 29 July and general mobilisation on 30 July. Germany demanded that Russia stop; when it did not, Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.5] Germany declared war on France on 3 August. Its Schlieffen Plan, which required a rapid defeat of France before Russia mobilised, meant it would attack France through Belgium whether or not France was the aggressor.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.6] Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August after Belgium rejected an ultimatum. Britain, bound by the Treaty of London (1839) guaranteeing Belgian neutrality and concerned for the balance of power, declared war that day.
[hist-eur2-s4.4.7] Bethmann Hollweg dismissed the treaty to the British ambassador as 'a scrap of paper'. Britain's entry made it a European war and, through the empires, a world war.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.b] The Second International (1889) was a federation of socialist and labour parties. Its members believed that workers had more in common across borders than with their own ruling classes, but in 1914 national loyalty proved stronger.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.0] It was founded in Paris in 1889, with a permanent International Socialist Bureau in Brussels from 1900. The largest member was the German SPD, which in 1912 won 110 seats and became the biggest party in the Reichstag.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.1] Its parties disagreed over reform or revolution, and over how to act in a war. Rosa Luxemburg and Lenin argued for revolutionary action; Jean Jaurès of France backed a general strike against war.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.2] The Stuttgart Congress (1907) resolved that socialists should do everything to prevent war, and if it came, to end it quickly and use the crisis to hasten the fall of capitalism. The last part was added after amendments by Luxemburg, Lenin and Martov.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.3] The Basel Congress (24-25 November 1912), called during the first Balkan War, issued a manifesto against war and threatened that governments would face revolution if they started one.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.4] Jaurès, the main French pacifist, was assassinated in Paris by a nationalist on 31 July 1914.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.5] On 4 August 1914 the SPD Reichstag group voted for war credits. French socialists backed the 'sacred union' and joined the government, and British Labour largely supported the war.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.6] The International collapsed. Socialists divided into those who backed their governments, pacifists, and a revolutionary minority (including Luxemburg, Liebknecht and Lenin) who opposed it as an imperialist war.
[hist-eur2-s4.5.7] Anti-war socialists met at Zimmerwald (September 1915) and Kienthal (April 1916); the split led directly to the communist parties and the Third International (Comintern) in 1919.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.b] Luxemburg was a Marxist theorist, speaker and journalist on the left of the SPD, a participant in the International's debates and a leading opponent of the war.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.0] She was born into a Jewish family on 5 March 1871 in Zamość in Russian-ruled Poland, and studied at Zurich. Her disability, a limp from childhood illness, did not prevent her from speaking in public.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.1] She helped found a Polish socialist party (later the SDKPiL) in 1893 and moved to Germany in 1898, where she gained German citizenship by marriage and joined the SPD.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.2] In Reform or Revolution (1899) she attacked the revisionism of Eduard Bernstein, who wanted socialism by gradual reform. In 1905-06 she travelled to Warsaw to take part in the Russian revolution.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.3] She taught at the SPD's party school in Berlin (1907-14) and wrote The Accumulation of Capital (1913), an economic study of imperialism.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.4] She spoke against war before 1914 and was jailed in 1914 for urging soldiers to disobey orders. When the SPD voted for war credits on 4 August 1914 she was shocked by its capitulation.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.5] With Karl Liebknecht she formed the International Group, later the Spartacus League (1916). From prison she wrote the Junius pamphlet (1915, published 1916), condemning the war as imperialist and the SPD's stance as betrayal.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.6] She spent most of the war in prison or protective custody, from 1915 to 1916 and from July 1916 until November 1918, and was freed by the German revolution.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.7] On the Russian Revolution of 1917 she welcomed the Bolsheviks' seizure of power but criticised their suppression of democracy and the Constituent Assembly.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.8] She helped found the Communist Party of Germany (KPD) at the end of December 1918, but opposed the Spartacist rising of January 1919, which she thought premature. She was murdered by Freikorps soldiers on 15 January 1919 and her body thrown into a Berlin canal.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.9] Contemporaries: the SPD leaders and conservatives viewed her as a dangerous agitator, 'Red Rosa'; to supporters, she was a courageous principled critic.
[hist-eur2-s4.6.10] Historians now see her as a major Marxist thinker and a critic of both militarism and dictatorship, although they argue over whether her ideas would have worked in practice.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.0] Alliances: Europe was divided into the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente, so a local quarrel could bring in all the Great Powers.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.1] Militarism and plans: the powers built up large armies and conscription, and had mobilisation plans that left little time for diplomacy; the Schlieffen Plan required a swift attack on France.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.2] Naval and colonial rivalry: the Anglo-German naval race and the Moroccan crises had poisoned relations between the powers.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.3] Nationalism: South Slav nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary, French revanche aimed at Alsace-Lorraine, and pan-Slavism and pan-Germanism pushed Russia and Germany towards confrontation.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.4] Short-term: the Bosnian crisis, the Balkan Wars, the assassination at Sarajevo, the blank cheque, the ultimatum and the sequence of mobilisations.
[hist-eur2-s4.7.5] The Balkans were where the interests of Austria-Hungary and Russia collided, and each feared that backing down would cost it Great Power status.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.0] Contemporary German view: Germany had been encircled and was forced into a defensive war by Russian mobilisation; this became the basis of German resistance to the war guilt clause later.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.1] Contemporary Entente view: Germany and Austria-Hungary were aggressors, shown by the blank cheque, the ultimatum, and the invasion of Belgium.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.2] Fritz Fischer (1961) argued that German leaders deliberately risked war in July 1914 to achieve world-power aims; his view raised an enduring debate about German war aims.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.3] Christopher Clark, in The Sleepwalkers (2012), argues that the leaders of all the powers, including Serbia, Russia and France, stumbled into a war none fully intended, stressing the Balkans and contingency.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.4] Austria-Hungary's decision to crush Serbia, Serbian-backed nationalism, Russian backing for Serbia and Germany's blank cheque were all necessary links in the chain; historians differ on the weight of each.
[hist-eur2-s4.8.5] The war-guilt question became part of the peace settlement (see the notes on the Peace Settlement).

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s2: 2. The structures of European diplomacy, the New Imperialism and colonial rivalries, 1890–1914
[hist-eur2-s2.0.b] After Bismarck's fall, Germany's new leaders let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse. Russia and France, both isolated, drew together and ended France's isolation.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.0] Chancellor Caprivi and the Foreign Office official Holstein decided not to renew the Reinsurance Treaty in 1890, believing it was incompatible with the Dual Alliance and with good relations with Britain.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.1] Russia then turned to France: French banks began large loans to Russia from 1888 and the powers agreed to consult each other in a political understanding in 1891, after a French naval squadron visited Kronstadt.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.2] A military convention was agreed in August 1892 and the Franco-Russian Alliance was formally ratified in January 1894. It was a defensive alliance directed at the Triple Alliance powers.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.3] If France was attacked by Germany, or by Italy supported by Germany, Russia would attack Germany; if Russia was attacked by Germany, or by Austria-Hungary supported by Germany, France would attack Germany. Both would mobilise if any Triple Alliance member mobilised.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.4] The alliance gave Germany the risk of a war on two fronts, the danger Bismarck had tried to avoid, and it later shaped the Schlieffen Plan.
[hist-eur2-s2.0.5] The alliance was cemented by French investment in Russian railways and industry, and lasted until 1917.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.b] Britain stayed outside permanent alliances until the early twentieth century. The Boer War showed it had few friends, and it began to settle quarrels with its imperial rivals instead.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.0] Britain's policy of 'splendid isolation' lost its value after the Boer War, when most European opinion was hostile, and after Germany began to build a large navy.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.1] The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902) gave Britain support in the Far East against Russia and allowed it to concentrate its navy nearer home; it was renewed in 1905 and 1911.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.2] The Entente Cordiale (8 April 1904) settled colonial disputes: Britain was recognised in Egypt, France in Morocco, and the two settled questions over Siam, Madagascar, and the Newfoundland fisheries.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.3] The Entente was not an alliance, but after the first Moroccan crisis British and French military staffs began secret talks in 1906, and in 1912 their navies agreed to divide responsibilities in the Channel and the Mediterranean.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.4] The Anglo-Russian Entente (31 August 1907) settled rivalries in Asia: Persia was divided into a Russian northern zone, a British southern zone and a neutral middle; Afghanistan was recognised as a British sphere; both promised to stay out of Tibet.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.5] The Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia was therefore a loose understanding, without a common military commitment, facing the formal Triple Alliance.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.6] The Triple Alliance was weaker than it seemed: Italy signed a secret agreement with France in 1902 (the Prinetti-Barrère agreement) promising neutrality if France was attacked, and attacked the Ottoman Empire for Libya in 1911–12.
[hist-eur2-s2.1.7] From 1906 to 1914 each bloc grew more fearful of the other, and Germany spoke of 'encirclement' while Britain and France feared German ambitions; this made each crisis seem part of a struggle between the blocs.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.b] From about 1880 the European powers competed to acquire territory in Africa, Asia and the Pacific. Historians group the causes into economic, strategic, nationalist, missionary and ideological motives.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.0] Economic: industrial states needed raw materials such as rubber, cotton, palm oil, copper and later oil, and wanted protected markets when tariffs rose in Europe from the 1870s.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.1] Economic theory: the British writer J.A. Hobson argued in Imperialism: A Study (1902) that capitalists invested overseas because of surplus capital at home; Lenin built on this in Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1916). Historians dispute it, since most trade and investment went to areas outside the empires.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.2] Strategic: states sought naval bases, coaling stations and secure routes. Britain occupied Egypt in 1882 to protect the Suez Canal, the route to India, and then moved to control the Nile valley.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.3] Nationalist: colonies were seen as a mark of Great Power status, and newspapers, colonial societies and the public cheered each acquisition. After defeat in 1871, France sought compensation overseas under Jules Ferry.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.4] Missionary and humanitarian: churches wanted to spread Christianity and end the slave trade, and explorers such as David Livingstone and H.M. Stanley made Africa known to the public.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.5] Ideological: ideas of racial and cultural superiority, including Social Darwinism, and a belief in a duty to 'civilise' other peoples (France's mission civilisatrice; Kipling's poem The White Man's Burden, 1899) were used to justify rule.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.6] Technology made conquest easy: quinine against malaria, steamships, the telegraph and rifles and machine guns such as the Maxim gun gave Europeans overwhelming military advantage.
[hist-eur2-s2.2.7] Competition fed on itself: once one power claimed territory, others felt that they had to do the same or be shut out.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.0] In 1880 only about a tenth of Africa was under European control, mostly coastal areas such as Algeria, the Cape and Senegal; by 1914 about nine tenths of the continent had been taken.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.1] The scramble was triggered by Leopold II's claims on the Congo, France's advance in West Africa and Tunisia (1881), and Britain's occupation of Egypt in 1882.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.2] The Berlin Conference (November 1884 to February 1885), called by Bismarck and attended by fourteen powers including the United States, set rules for claims: notice to other powers, effective occupation, and freedom of trade on the Congo and Niger rivers.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.3] No African ruler was represented at the Conference, and it did not itself divide Africa, but it made the partition faster and gave it a legal shape.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.4] Britain's aim was a belt of territory from the Cape to Cairo, promoted by Cecil Rhodes, Prime Minister of the Cape Colony (1890–96); France sought to link its territories from Senegal to the Red Sea.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.5] These two ambitions crossed at Fashoda in 1898. Germany took South West Africa, Togo, Cameroon and East Africa; Italy took Eritrea and Somaliland and, in 1911–12, Libya.
[hist-eur2-s2.3.6] Africans resisted: Ethiopia defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa (1 March 1896), and only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent by 1914.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.0] Leopold II, King of the Belgians, founded the International African Association in 1876 and used Stanley to claim the Congo basin for himself.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.1] The Berlin Conference recognised the Congo Free State in 1885 as Leopold's personal possession, on the promise that he would end slavery and keep free trade.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.2] From the 1890s the state forced Africans to collect wild rubber under quotas, as demand rose with the bicycle and the motor car; failure was punished by hostage-taking, beatings, mutilation and the burning of villages.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.3] The Force Publique, the colonial army, enforced the system. Mortality rose sharply from violence, disease and starvation; the population fell by millions, though estimates vary widely.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.4] E.D. Morel, a shipping clerk who noticed the Congo's trade figures, and the British diplomat Roger Casement (whose 1904 report documented the abuses) led the Congo Reform Association (1904).
[hist-eur2-s2.4.5] Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness (1899) was based on his own time in the Congo, and Mark Twain wrote a satire on Leopold's rule.
[hist-eur2-s2.4.6] International pressure forced the Belgian parliament to take over the colony in 1908, ending Leopold's personal rule.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.0] France created French Indochina from Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos (1880s–1890s); Britain added Burma and Malaya; the Dutch held the East Indies.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.1] China was defeated by Japan in the Sino-Japanese War (1894–95). The powers then forced concessions: Germany leased Kiaochow (Tsingtao), Russia Port Arthur, Britain Weihaiwei and France Guangzhouwan, and each claimed a sphere of influence.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.2] The United States, which had defeated Spain in 1898 and taken the Philippines, Guam and Puerto Rico, proposed an 'Open Door' for trade in China (1899).
[hist-eur2-s2.5.3] The Boxer Rising (1899–1901) was a Chinese uprising against foreigners and Christians, which besieged the foreign legations in Beijing in 1900; an eight-nation force relieved them.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.4] Japan modernised after 1868, built a strong army and navy and defeated Russia in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05), the first time an Asian power beat a European Great Power in modern times.
[hist-eur2-s2.5.5] The defeat turned Russia's attention back towards the Balkans and Europe, and contributed to the revolution of 1905 (see the notes on Tsarist Russia).
[hist-eur2-s2.6.0] Fashoda: in July 1898 a small French expedition under Captain Marchand reached Fashoda on the Upper Nile in Sudan, hoping to claim a French East-West belt of Africa.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.1] In September 1898, after his victory at Omdurman, General Kitchener arrived with a far stronger force. Both sides refused to withdraw and war was feared.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.2] France, isolated and facing the Dreyfus Affair at home, backed down in November 1898, and an agreement in March 1899 set the boundary between British and French spheres.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.3] Fashoda poisoned Anglo-French relations until the settlement of 1904; but it also showed the danger of colonial clashes and encouraged both powers to solve their disputes.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.4] The Boer War began in October 1899 after disputes over British claims on the Boer republics, the Transvaal and the Orange Free State, whose gold and rights of foreign settlers were at issue; the failed Jameson Raid of 1895–96 had already soured relations.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.5] The Boers won early successes (Black Week, December 1899). Britain sent large reinforcements and, under Kitchener, burned farms and interned Boer civilians in camps, where over 20,000 died.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.6] The Treaty of Vereeniging (31 May 1902) ended the war and the two republics were absorbed into the British Empire.
[hist-eur2-s2.6.7] The war damaged British prestige, aroused hostility in Europe (Germany's Kruger telegram of 1896 had already caused outrage), and persuaded Britain to end its isolation.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.0] After the Entente Cordiale of 1904, France moved to control Morocco, one of the last independent states in Africa.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.1] Wilhelm II landed at Tangier on 31 March 1905 and spoke in support of Moroccan independence. Germany hoped to test the Entente and to win concessions from France.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.2] German pressure forced the resignation of the French foreign minister Delcassé in June 1905, but the crisis ended at the Algeciras Conference (January to April 1906).
[hist-eur2-s2.7.3] Algeciras was a diplomatic defeat for Germany: only Austria-Hungary supported it, Britain, Russia, Italy and the United States backed France, and France and Spain received the policing of Morocco's ports.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.4] The crisis strengthened the Entente: British and French generals began secret staff talks.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.5] In May 1911 France sent troops to Fez. Germany, in July 1911, sent the gunboat Panther to Agadir, demanding compensation, and Lloyd George warned in his Mansion House speech (21 July) that Britain would not be ignored.
[hist-eur2-s2.7.6] The Agadir crisis was settled in November 1911: Germany accepted French control of Morocco in return for part of the French Congo (the 'New Cameroon' strip).
[hist-eur2-s2.7.7] German opinion saw the settlement as a humiliation and Britain and France drew closer; the crisis led directly to the naval talks of 1912 between Britain and France.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.0] Imperial rivalry created crises (Fashoda, the Boer War and Morocco) that raised tension and made Britain and Germany suspicious of each other.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.1] Imperial agreements also reduced tension, as the Entente Cordiale and the Anglo-Russian Entente show; the powers were often able to settle colonial quarrels by compromise.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.2] Imperialism made the alliance blocs more rigid: the Moroccan crises turned the Entente Cordiale from a colonial settlement into a quasi-alliance.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.3] Imperial rivalries were mostly settled peacefully, so the war in 1914 came from the Balkans and Europe's own alliance system rather than from colonial clashes.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.4] Contemporaries in Germany blamed British and French encirclement for their sense of insecurity, while the British and French blamed German pressure for the Moroccan crises.
[hist-eur2-s2.8.5] Lenin and Hobson, writing from different positions, saw capitalism and imperialism as an underlying cause of the war; many later historians see imperialism as one factor alongside nationalism, militarism and alliances.
