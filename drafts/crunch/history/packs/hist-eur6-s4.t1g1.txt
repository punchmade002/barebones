TARGET hist-eur6-s4: 4. Domestic factors in US foreign policy: McCarthyism, the anti-war movement and race relations
[hist-eur6-s4.0.b] US foreign policy was not made in a vacuum: presidents had to carry voters, Congress and the press with them. After 1945, fear of communism abroad turned into fear of subversion (secret undermining of the government from within) at home, and later protest and racial injustice put pressure on policy too.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.0] Fear of subversion grew out of Cold War events: the Truman Doctrine of March 1947 (a pledge to resist communist expansion, called containment) and the Soviet atomic test of August 1949.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.1] The communist victory in China (People's Republic proclaimed 1 October 1949) and the Korean War (from 25 June 1950) added to the fear, so Americans began to suspect spies and traitors at home.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.2] Congressional committees could summon witnesses and demand answers. The Fifth Amendment of the Constitution allowed a witness to refuse to answer questions that might incriminate them. The Senate and the House of Representatives are separate chambers, each running its own investigations.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.3] Segregation in the South, known as Jim Crow, was a system of laws keeping Black and white Americans apart in schools, transport and public places. Black Americans were fighting it in the Black freedom struggle, and the Vietnam War escalated under President Johnson from 1965.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.4] Public opinion means what ordinary Americans thought, as measured by polls and the press. It set limits on what a president could do abroad, because a policy that voters rejected cost votes and support in Congress.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.5] Mass media means newspapers, radio and above all television. Television carried McCarthy, the Vietnam War and the civil rights struggle into homes across the country, so that millions saw events at the same moment.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.6] Liberalism, in US usage, means support for civil liberties (such as free speech), civil rights and an active federal government. McCarthyism attacked it, while civil rights and anti-war campaigners defended it.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.7] McCarthyism means reckless accusation of communist subversion, made without proof. The Red Scare is the wider wave of fear of communists that began before McCarthy and outlasted him.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.b] The panic about communists did not start with Joseph McCarthy. By 1947 the government and Congress had already set up machinery to hunt for disloyal people, and Hollywood was an early target.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.0] Truman's loyalty programme (Executive Order 9835, March 1947) set up loyalty review boards to investigate federal employees, and the Attorney General kept a list of 'subversive' organisations. Many federal employees feared losing their jobs over any past political association.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.1] The House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) held hearings on communist influence in Hollywood in October 1947. The Hollywood Ten, writers and directors including Dalton Trumbo, refused to say whether they were communists, and Congress cited them for contempt in November 1947.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.2] The Ten were convicted of contempt of Congress in 1948. Their appeals failed, so they served prison terms of up to a year from 1950. This mattered because it showed that refusing to answer a congressional committee could end in prison.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.3] The studio heads, meeting at the Waldorf-Astoria hotel in New York in November 1947, issued the Waldorf Statement. They promised not to employ the Ten or anyone suspected of communism, and this created the Hollywood blacklist (a secret ban on employing named people).
[hist-eur6-s4.1.4] The blacklist ran for about a decade, and it began to break in 1960 when Trumbo was credited for a film. This shows how long a fear of association could shut people out of their careers.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.5] HUAC (House, from 1938, Hollywood hearings in 1947) must not be confused with McCarthy's later Senate subcommittee. McCarthy was never a member of HUAC, and his own investigations did not concern Hollywood.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.b] Real and alleged spy cases between 1948 and 1953 convinced many Americans that traitors were at work in government. Laws and prosecutions followed, and the fear spread into everyday working life.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.0] Whittaker Chambers, an ex-communist who had been a courier and later a Time editor, accused Alger Hiss, a former State Department official, before HUAC in August 1948. Chambers said Hiss was a communist who had passed documents to him; Hiss denied it.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.1] A time-bar (a legal limit on how long after an act charges may be brought) prevented a spy charge, so Hiss was tried for perjury. The first trial in 1949 ended in a hung jury; he was convicted in January 1950 and served about three and a half years.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.2] Richard Nixon, a California congressman on HUAC, pressed the Hiss case, and it made his national name. Historians still debate Hiss's guilt, though most think he was guilty. The case was highly publicised.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.3] Klaus Fuchs, a German-born British physicist who had worked on the Manhattan Project (the wartime atomic bomb programme), confessed in January 1950 and was arrested in February 1950. His confession led to the Rosenbergs' ring and so strengthened fears of atomic spies.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.4] Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were arrested in 1950 and convicted in 1951 of conspiracy to commit espionage, for passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union. They were executed at Sing Sing on 19 June 1953.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.5] Eleven Communist Party leaders were tried in 1949 under the Smith Act (1940), and the Supreme Court upheld their convictions in Dennis v. United States (1951). The McCarran Internal Security Act of September 1950 forced Communist organisations to register, and Congress overrode Truman's veto of it.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.6] The Red Scare reached teachers, trade unionists and entertainers. Loyalty oaths were widely required of public employees, including teachers, and many people lost jobs for refusing to name former associates, so dissent itself became dangerous.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.b] McCarthy was a little-known senator until early 1950. He then seized on a moment when Americans were frightened, and he made accusation his political weapon.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.0] On 9 February 1950, in a Lincoln Day speech to a Republican women's club at Wheeling, West Virginia, McCarthy claimed to hold a list of communists working in the State Department. The numbers he gave later varied, and the speech text is disputed.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.1] The speech became a national sensation because of the timing: the Hiss conviction in January 1950, the arrest of Fuchs, the Soviet bomb and the fall of China had left Americans ready to believe in traitors.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.2] The Tydings Committee, a Senate subcommittee chaired by Millard Tydings, reported in July 1950 and called the charges a fraud and a hoax. This was the committee's verdict. Tydings then lost his Maryland seat in November 1950 after a campaign in which McCarthy's allies attacked him.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.3] McCarthy's methods were guilt by association (damning people for the company they had kept), accusations without evidence, unnamed lists, shifting numbers, bullying of witnesses and personal smears. The cartoonist Herblock (Herbert Block of the Washington Post) coined the word McCarthyism in March 1950.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.4] In June 1950 Margaret Chase Smith, a Republican senator from Maine, delivered her Declaration of Conscience. She criticised smear tactics without naming McCarthy, showing that some in his own party were uneasy. This mattered because criticism from inside his party was harder to dismiss.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.5] In 1951 McCarthy attacked General George Marshall, a respected wartime commander. Eisenhower, campaigning in 1952, did not defend Marshall in McCarthy's presence in Wisconsin, a sign of how much Republicans feared to cross him. This is an example of how far fear of McCarthy reached.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.b] The Republican victory of 1952 gave McCarthy an official platform, but the same publicity that built him also destroyed him once television and Senate colleagues turned on him.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.0] Republicans won the presidency and Congress in 1952. From January 1953 McCarthy chaired the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, which gave him the power to summon officials and hold hearings.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.1] In 1953 he investigated the Voice of America (the government's overseas radio service) and the State Department's overseas libraries. His aides Roy Cohn, chief counsel, and G. David Schine toured State Department libraries in Europe checking them.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.2] On 9 March 1954 Edward R. Murrow's CBS programme See It Now used McCarthy's own speeches and film to criticise him. It was effective because viewers saw his methods in his own words. This is why it hurt him more than press criticism.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.3] McCarthy then turned his inquiries on the army, partly over the treatment of Schine, who had been drafted. The resulting Army-McCarthy hearings, televised from 22 April to 17 June 1954, showed him to a national audience as a bully.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.4] On 9 June 1954 Joseph Welch, the army's counsel, rebuked McCarthy for attacking a young lawyer in Welch's firm, asking 'Have you no sense of decency, sir?' This became a turning point in public opinion against him.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.5] On 2 December 1954 the Senate voted 67 to 22 to censure him, for conduct that tended to bring the Senate into dishonour, or loosely conduct unbecoming a senator. A committee, the Watkins Committee, had recommended the censure, and after the vote his influence vanished.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.b] McCarthy was a direct participant who used the Red Scare to build a national following, and he was also shaped by the events around him. Contemporaries and historians have judged him very differently.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.0] Joseph McCarthy was born on 14 November 1908 in Grand Chute, Wisconsin, studied law at Marquette University and became a circuit judge in 1939. He served in the Marines from 1942 to 1945, later calling himself 'Tail-Gunner Joe', a claim that was exaggerated.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.1] He won the Republican primary against the incumbent Robert La Follette Jr and was elected to the Senate in 1946 (he took his seat in January 1947). He was re-elected in 1952. This is why he could claim a mandate from Wisconsin voters.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.2] As a participant he made the Wheeling speech of February 1950 and the investigations of 1953 to 1954, chairing the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations. The Red Scare existed before him, and he rode it, exploiting the Hiss conviction, the fall of China, the Soviet bomb, Fuchs and Korea.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.3] His fall was also shaped by events. Television exposure, the Army-McCarthy hearings, the turning of Senate colleagues and Eisenhower's refusal to help him brought him down, and the term McCarthyism outlived him as a word for smear and witch-hunt.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.4] McCarthy died on 2 May 1957, aged 48, of acute hepatitis linked to heavy drinking. His early death at 48 ended his career as well as his influence.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.5] Contemporaries split. Many Republicans and conservatives, and much of the Catholic and Midwest public, backed him, while liberals, most of the press and many Senate colleagues denounced his methods as reckless smears.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.6] Historians since Richard Rovere (Senator Joe McCarthy, 1959) have judged him a demagogue (a leader who stirs up fear for personal gain).
[hist-eur6-s4.5.7] Ellen Schrecker (Many Are the Crimes, 1998) argues that McCarthyism was a wider system, including the FBI under J. Edgar Hoover, HUAC and both parties, which he exploited rather than created, so removing him did not end the Red Scare.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.8] M. Stanton Evans (Blacklisted by History, 2007) offered a minority defence of him. The Venona decrypts (Soviet cables made public from 1995) show a number of Soviet agents in the US government, but they proved real Soviet spying without vindicating McCarthy's methods or lists.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.b] When the Republicans took office in 1953, the loyalty rules of Truman were replaced, not abandoned. The new president kept the anti-communist climate in place and avoided a public fight with McCarthy.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.0] Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10450 in April 1953. It replaced Truman's loyalty standard with 'security risk' criteria for federal employment, so many dismissals followed.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.1] Under the new criteria, past membership in suspect organisations could count against a person as well as proven disloyalty, which made it easy to dismiss employees on suspicion. For example, an old membership could cost someone a federal post.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.2] Eisenhower avoided naming McCarthy in public for most of 1953 and 1954, and kept his criticism private or indirect. This public caution meant the charges spread until the Army hearings of 1954.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.3] Richard Nixon, Eisenhower's vice president, had built his national reputation on the Hiss investigation. This made anti-communism a bipartisan position (shared by both parties) in 1950s politics.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.b] The climate of suspicion changed what officials said and did about China, the Soviet Union and Asia, because anyone who looked soft on communism risked a political attack.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.0] Republicans charged from late 1949 that Truman and the State Department had lost China, after the communist victory. McCarthy's attacks on State Department officials amplified the charge.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.1] Owen Lattimore, a Johns Hopkins China scholar, was accused by McCarthy in March 1950 of being a leading Soviet agent. He was never charged with espionage; he was indicted for perjury in 1952, and the charges were dropped in 1955.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.2] State Department China specialists John Paton Davies and John S. Service were removed from their posts: Service was dismissed in 1951 and Davies in 1954. This weakened the government's expert knowledge of Asia.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.3] The campaign made it politically risky for Democrats to show any willingness to negotiate with communist states, which constrained diplomacy in the Korean and Chinese crises.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.4] John Foster Dulles was secretary of state from 1953 to 1959, and his department, with security chief Scott McLeod, applied the new security standards. The climate pressed the administration to avoid any open conflict with McCarthy.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.5] Soviet propaganda and allied critics used McCarthyism, and images of racial violence and discrimination such as the Little Rock crisis of 1957, to argue that American democracy was a sham, which damaged America's image abroad.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.b] Opposition to the Vietnam War started on university campuses in 1965 and grew into a mass movement. Students had a personal stake because the draft could send them to fight.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.0] The first major teach-in (an all-night meeting of lectures and debate) was held at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, on 24 and 25 March 1965. Several thousand attended, and teach-ins spread to many campuses in the following weeks.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.1] Students for a Democratic Society (SDS) emerged in 1960 and was famous for the Port Huron Statement of 1962, largely written by Tom Hayden. Its Washington march of April 1965 drew about 15,000 to 25,000 people.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.2] By 1969 SDS had split, with the Weathermen emerging from it. This fragmentation shows that the movement was divided as well as large.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.3] The Selective Service System (the draft) called up men aged 18 to 26. Student deferments favoured the better-off, so poorer and Black men were more likely to be sent to Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.4] Many young men resisted by burning draft cards or going to Canada. The first draft lottery, which selected men for call-up by birthdate, was held on 1 December 1969 and made young men more aware of the war's impact on their lives.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.5] Muhammad Ali, formerly Cassius Clay, refused induction in Houston on 28 April 1967, citing his religious beliefs as a Nation of Islam minister. He was stripped of the world title and boxing licence.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.6] He was convicted of draft evasion in June 1967 (five years and a fine; he stayed free on appeal), and the Supreme Court overturned the conviction in June 1971. As a famous champion, his refusal made the draft a matter of conscience for a mass audience.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.b] From 1967 protest moved from the campuses into churches, the streets and the national conventions. Television brought each event to a mass audience, which made it hard for the government to ignore.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.0] On 4 April 1967, at Riverside Church in New York, Martin Luther King gave his 'Beyond Vietnam' speech. He called the US government 'the greatest purveyor of violence in the world today', and argued that the war drained resources from the war on poverty.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.1] King was criticised by some allies and by the press for linking civil rights to the war, but his speech linked civil rights to peace.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.2] The March on the Pentagon on 21 October 1967 drew tens of thousands and many arrests. Norman Mailer took part and wrote The Armies of the Night (1968), which won the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.3] The Democratic National Convention met in Chicago from 26 to 29 August 1968. Police and the National Guard clashed with protesters in Grant Park and on Michigan Avenue, under Mayor Richard Daley, in front of television cameras.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.4] The protesters chanted that the whole world was watching, a chant that was televised. Hubert Humphrey was nominated.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.5] The Vietnam Moratorium of 15 October 1969 brought demonstrations across the country. A separate large march followed in Washington on 15 November 1969, and it must not be called a Moratorium.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.6] Nixon replied with a televised speech on 3 November 1969, appealing to the silent majority of Americans who did not protest. This shows that many Americans disapproved of the protesters.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.b] In 1970 and 1971 anger over the widening war led to deaths on campuses and a leak that damaged the government's case. Together they pressed Nixon to bring troops home.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.0] Nixon announced the Cambodian incursion on 30 April 1970, and campus protests and a student strike followed. This is why the war's widening, not only its length, sparked protest.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.1] On 4 May 1970 Ohio National Guardsmen fired on students at Kent State University, killing four and wounding nine. At Jackson State in Mississippi, on 15 May 1970, police killed two. Kent State was in Ohio and Jackson State in Mississippi.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.2] The Pentagon Papers were a secret Defense Department history of US involvement in Vietnam, leaked by Daniel Ellsberg. The New York Times began publishing on 13 June 1971, the government obtained a halt, and the Supreme Court ruled for the press on 30 June 1971.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.3] Congress repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, the August 1964 vote that had given the president wide war powers, in January 1971, and in 1973 it cut off funds for US combat in Indochina, so Congress itself began to act against the war.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.4] Protest and public opinion put pressure on Nixon to withdraw troops and to Vietnamise the war (hand more fighting to South Vietnamese forces). Johnson's decision of March 1968 not to seek re-election was also a response to public opinion.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.b] Racial discrimination at home was a serious problem for US foreign policy, because it weakened America's claim to lead the free world. The audience that mattered was the newly independent states of Asia and Africa.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.0] President Truman's Executive Order 9981, of 26 July 1948, ordered equality of treatment in the armed services. It was partly motivated by the need to answer communist criticism of US racism; Truman also faced the 1948 election and the Black vote. Full integration took years, until the Korean War.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.1] In Brown v. Board of Education, on 17 May 1954, the Supreme Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional. The ruling was reported abroad as evidence of progress, though desegregation was slow in the South.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.2] The Justice Department's brief in Brown urged the Court to remember the effect abroad, since segregation was a Cold War embarrassment, a point Mary Dudziak later stressed.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.3] At Little Rock Central High School in September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus resisted the entry of nine Black students. Eisenhower sent federal troops (the 101st Airborne) to enforce it, and Soviet media used the images around the world.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.4] The Freedom Rides of May 1961 (buses attacked in Alabama) and the Birmingham campaign of spring 1963 (police dogs and fire hoses used on children, widely televised) were international news. They undermined the US claim to be a model of democracy.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.5] Soviet and Chinese propaganda broadcast stories of segregation and racial violence to the Third World, which is why US diplomats argued that events like Little Rock damaged the country's standing in newly independent Asian and African states.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.6] Black leaders linked race to foreign policy. King tied the cost of the war in Vietnam to the funds needed for the poor. Malcolm X toured Africa in 1964 and argued that the American struggle was an international human rights matter.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.7] The Civil Rights Act of July 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of August 1965 were landmark reforms. Historians such as Mary Dudziak (Cold War Civil Rights, 2000) argue that foreign opinion was one factor, though not the main one.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.b] Judgements that weigh the pressure of domestic fears, protest and race against the Cold War and strategic aims earn the most credit. Each force had real effects and clear limits.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.0] McCarthyism was the strongest constraint in the early 1950s: it damaged civil liberties, weakened expert advice on China and Asia, and made negotiation with communists risky. But the Red Scare predated McCarthy and outlasted him, and some Soviet spying was real, as the Venona decrypts show.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.1] The anti-war movement shaped public opinion, Johnson's decision of 1968 and the pace of Nixon's withdrawals. But it was divided, often unpopular with the silent majority, and the war continued for years after Johnson's decision.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.2] Historians disagree about whether protests changed the outcome of the war or simply sharpened a decision the government had already reached.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.3] Race relations were an embarrassment that pushed leaders towards reform, because Soviet propaganda used Little Rock, and the Birmingham campaign was also international news. But reform was driven mainly by the Black freedom movement itself and by domestic politics.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.4] Overall, domestic opinion limited and shaped policy, but strategic and Cold War considerations stayed the main drivers of US foreign policy.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.5] Contemporaries often saw McCarthy and the protesters through party and class lines. Later historians such as Rovere and Schrecker judge McCarthyism harshly, while Evans defends it using Venona, so the verdict depends on the view taken.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s2: 2. US foreign policy, 1945–1972: Berlin, Korea, Cuba and Vietnam
[hist-eur6-s2.0.b] Before 1941 the United States kept out of most quarrels in Europe and Asia. The Second World War ended that, and by 1945 it faced a rival superpower, the Soviet Union, whose system was the opposite of its own.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.0] Pre-war US isolationism (staying out of other countries' conflicts) was ended by Pearl Harbor and the war, so after 1945 the United States followed internationalism: active involvement in world affairs instead of withdrawal.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.1] The war left the United States the richest industrial power and, until the Soviet test in 1949, the only atomic power, which gave it the means to lead a worldwide policy.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.2] The wartime alliance with the Soviet Union broke down because Soviet forces held Eastern Europe at the war's end; the strain showed at the Yalta conference (February 1945) and the Potsdam conference (July to August 1945).
[hist-eur6-s2.0.3] Churchill's "iron curtain" speech at Fulton, Missouri, in March 1946 gave the division of Europe between East and West its best-known image.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.4] The Cold War rested on a clash of systems: communism, with the state controlling the economy under one party, against capitalism, with private ownership and markets.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.5] In China a civil war was being fought between Mao Zedong's Communists and Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists, so Asia was drawn into the same contest as Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.6] Imperialism means domination of one country by another through political, military or economic control. In this topic it is applied to US influence in Cuba and to Soviet control of Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.7] Germany, and Berlin within it, was divided into four occupation zones or sectors (US, British, French and Soviet). Berlin lay deep inside the Soviet zone, so the three Western sectors, which became West Berlin, were cut off from the West by Soviet-held territory.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.b] Containment means checking Soviet and communist expansion by firm resistance. It grew out of a new weapon and one diplomat's analysis of how the Soviet Union behaved.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.0] The atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, on Truman's authority, which left the United States as the only country with atomic weapons.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.1] The Soviet Union tested its own bomb on 29 August 1949, which ended the American monopoly, so the United States could no longer rely on the bomb alone to hold Soviet power back.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.2] George Kennan, a US diplomat in Moscow, sent his Long Telegram in February 1946, arguing that Soviet expansion could be checked by firm, long-term resistance rather than by negotiation.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.3] Kennan's article "The Sources of Soviet Conduct", published in Foreign Affairs in July 1947 under the signature X, made the same case in public and gave containment its name.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.4] Keep four terms apart: containment is the idea (Kennan), the Truman Doctrine is political and military aid to Greece and Turkey, the Marshall Plan is economic aid to Western Europe, and NATO is the alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.b] Between 1947 and 1950 a run of crises turned Kennan's idea into American policy and open commitments.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.0] In February 1947 Britain told the United States it could no longer support Greece and Turkey, so Washington decided to take over that burden.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.1] In the Truman Doctrine speech to Congress on 12 March 1947 Truman asked for about 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey and committed the United States to support free peoples resisting armed minorities or outside pressure.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.2] The Communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 convinced many in Washington and Western Europe that the Soviet threat was immediate, so it made them readier to act.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.3] Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949. Republicans asked "Who lost China?" and used the question to attack Truman's Asia policy, which made it harder for him to look weak abroad.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.4] NSC-68, a paper by the National Security Council (the President's foreign-policy advisory body), called in April 1950 for a large rise in defence spending to meet the Soviet threat. It was approved as policy in autumn 1950, after the Korean War began.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.b] Containment in Western Europe had an economic side. American money would rebuild damaged economies so that poverty and unrest did not push voters towards communism.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.0] George Marshall, the Secretary of State, proposed a European recovery programme in a speech at Harvard University on 5 June 1947.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.1] The European Recovery Program, passed by Congress in April 1948, provided about 13 billion dollars of aid to Western European countries between 1948 and 1952.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.2] The plan had three aims: economic recovery in Western Europe, stopping the spread of communism, and rebuilding European markets for American goods.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.3] Most aid was given as grants of food, fuel and machinery rather than as loans, so recipients received supplies they did not have to repay as ordinary debt.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.4] The Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC) was set up in April 1948 to allocate the aid, and Britain, France and West Germany were the largest recipients.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.5] The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and pressed its satellite states (the Eastern European countries it controlled) to refuse too. Czechoslovakia first accepted, then withdrew in July 1947 under Soviet pressure, so Europe settled more firmly into two blocs.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.b] Aid was matched by a military guarantee. In 1949 the defence of Western Europe and the future of Germany were both settled in ways that made the division of Europe harder to undo.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.0] The North Atlantic Treaty was signed on 4 April 1949 by twelve countries, including the United States, Britain, France, Canada and Italy.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.1] NATO, founded in April 1949 on the Article 5 principle of collective defence, was the first peacetime alliance of its kind that the United States had joined, a clear break from pre-war isolationism and a sign that internationalism now meant permanent commitments.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.2] Article 5 of the treaty stated that an armed attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, which is the basis of collective defence: any attacker would face the whole alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.3] Germany was divided as well: the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) was founded in May 1949 in the Western zones, and the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) followed in October 1949 in the Soviet zone.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.b] Berlin lay inside the Soviet zone, so the Soviet Union could squeeze the Western sectors without invading. The crisis of 1948 to 1949 tested whether the West would stay.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.0] The Soviet Union began blocking road, rail and canal access to West Berlin on 24 June 1948, aiming to force the Western powers out of the city.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.1] The Western powers responded with an airlift that flew food, coal and other supplies into West Berlin. More than two million tons were carried before the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.2] Truman chose the airlift rather than an armed convoy.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.3] The result was a Western success: the Soviet Union lifted the blockade without achieving its aim, and the West held Berlin without firing a shot.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.b] Berlin remained a flashpoint after the airlift. From 1958 the Soviet leader pressed the West to leave, and in 1961 the border between East and West Berlin was sealed.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.0] In November 1958 Khrushchev demanded that the Western powers withdraw from Berlin within six months, and the issue was debated at the Vienna summit with Kennedy in June 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.1] More than two million East Germans had fled to the West between 1949 and 1961, mostly by crossing from East to West Berlin, which drained the East's skilled workforce.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.2] The Berlin Wall was built from 13 August 1961, closing the border between East and West Berlin and stopping the flow of refugees.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.3] In October 1961 American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, the crossing point for foreigners, in a tense standoff that ended without shots.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.4] The Wall was a propaganda defeat for the East, because it had to wall in its own people, but the West accepted it rather than tearing it down, so the division of the city became permanent.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.5] Kennedy visited West Berlin on 26 June 1963 and declared in German "Ich bin ein Berliner" (I am a Berliner), a statement of solidarity that was widely praised in the West.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.b] Korea was split by the superpowers in 1945. In 1950 the split turned into war, and the United States fought under the flag of the United Nations.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.0] Korea was divided at the 38th parallel in 1945. The Republic of Korea in the South under Syngman Rhee and the Democratic People's Republic in the North under Kim Il Sung were both founded in 1948.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.1] US troops left South Korea in 1949, and Secretary of State Dean Acheson's speech of January 1950 placed Korea outside the American defence perimeter in the Pacific. It may have encouraged the North, though historians disagree how far.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.2] North Korean forces invaded the South on 25 June 1950. The UN Security Council condemned the attack and recommended that members help South Korea repel it.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.3] The Council could act because the Soviet delegate was absent (the Soviet Union was boycotting it), so Security Council resolutions could be passed.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.4] Truman sent American forces without asking Congress for a declaration of war and described the operation as a police action, so the war was never formally declared.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.5] Korea applied containment to Asia: Washington saw the invasion as communist expansion that had to be stopped, as the domino theory (if one country falls to communism, its neighbours follow) later argued in Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.b] The war swung up and down the peninsula before settling into a stalemate close to where it began.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.0] General Douglas MacArthur led the UN forces. His landing at Inchon on 15 September 1950 cut North Korean supply lines and led to the recapture of Seoul in late September 1950.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.1] UN forces crossed the 38th parallel in October 1950. Chinese forces entered the war in large numbers from late October and November 1950 and drove them back.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.2] Truman dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 after the general publicly argued for attacking China, because Truman wanted the war kept limited. The decision caused a major domestic row.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.3] An armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953 and the frontier stayed close to the 38th parallel. No peace treaty was ever signed, so the two Koreas remained technically at war.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.4] About 36,000 American servicemen died in the war in all. This figure counts non-battle deaths too, so battle deaths were lower.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.5] The results were a stalemate and a limited war. US troops remained in South Korea after the armistice, and the build-up in defence spending urged by NSC-68 went ahead.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.b] Cuba lay about ninety miles from Florida, and the United States had long treated it as within its sphere of influence. Castro's revolution ended that, and the quarrel pushed Cuba towards the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.0] Under the Platt Amendment of 1901 the United States had the right to intervene in Cuba. It was repealed in 1934 but left a legacy of US economic dominance, an example of the imperialism Cubans resented.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.1] Fidel Castro's revolution took power on 1 January 1959 after Batista fled, and the new government nationalised (took into state ownership) US-owned sugar, oil and other property in 1960.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.2] Eisenhower retaliated by cutting the Cuban sugar quota in July 1960, imposing a partial trade embargo in October 1960 and breaking off diplomatic relations in January 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.3] Cut off from its main market, Cuba turned to the Soviet Union for trade and oil, which brought Cold War rivalry to the Caribbean.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.4] CIA-trained Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs on 17 April 1961 and were defeated within three days, a humiliation for Kennedy.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.5] Operation Mongoose, a covert campaign against Castro's government, was authorised in November 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.b] In 1962 the Cold War came closest to nuclear war. It runs from the shipment of the missiles to Kennedy's decision on how to respond.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.0] Soviet medium-range missiles were shipped to Cuba from September 1962. Photographs taken by a US U-2 spy plane on 14 October 1962 revealed the launch sites, and Kennedy was shown them on 16 October.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.1] Khrushchev's motives were to defend Cuba from another invasion, to answer US Jupiter missiles in Turkey and Italy, and to offset Soviet weakness in long-range missiles.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.2] Kennedy and his advisers, the Executive Committee (ExComm), rejected an air strike or invasion and chose a naval measure instead.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.3] On 22 October 1962 Kennedy announced on television a naval quarantine of Cuba, meaning US ships would stop and search vessels heading there.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.4] Kennedy said "quarantine" and not "blockade" because a blockade is an act of war in law, so the word kept the move short of war.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.b] Within a week the crisis reached its most dangerous days and was settled, but only part of the settlement was made public.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.0] On 24 October Soviet ships turned back from the quarantine line, so a clash at sea was avoided.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.1] On 27 October ("Black Saturday") a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.2] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed on Moscow radio to remove the missiles in return for a public US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.3] Kennedy also secretly agreed to withdraw US Jupiter missiles from Turkey. The withdrawal came in 1963, and the deal was kept private for years.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.4] A hotline between Washington and Moscow was installed in 1963, so leaders could talk directly in a future crisis instead of by slow diplomatic messages.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.5] Both sides could claim success. Kennedy got the missiles out and a public victory, while Khrushchev gained a public US pledge not to invade Cuba and the secret removal of the Jupiters.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.b] US involvement in Vietnam grew out of the containment of communism in Asia. Johnson's escalation of 1964 to 1968 is a separate case study. There is more on this in the case study on Lyndon Johnson and Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.0] Vietnam was part of French Indochina, and the Viet Minh under Ho Chi Minh fought the French, who were defeated at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.1] From 1950 Truman gave aid to the French war, and by 1954 the United States was paying about three quarters of its cost, which shows how early the commitment began.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.2] In April 1954 Eisenhower described the domino theory: if Vietnam fell to communism, the rest of Southeast Asia would follow, like a row of falling dominoes.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.3] The Geneva Accords of July 1954 divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with elections planned for 1956 that were never held.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.4] The United States backed Ngo Dinh Diem in South Vietnam and joined the Southeast Asia Treaty Organisation (SEATO, an anti-communist alliance for the region) in 1954.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.5] Under Eisenhower the United States trained the South Vietnamese army with a military advisory group, and the first American advisers killed in the conflict died in 1959.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.6] The National Liberation Front, known to the Americans as the Viet Cong, was formed in December 1960 to fight the government in the South.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.b] Kennedy deepened the commitment that Eisenhower had begun, and the weakness of the South Vietnamese government became plain.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.0] Kennedy increased US military advisers in South Vietnam to about 16,000 by late 1963, whereas Eisenhower had sent advisers but not combat troops.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.1] Kennedy backed the Strategic Hamlet programme (1962), which moved peasants into fortified villages. It was widely resented.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.2] In 1963 Diem's persecution of Buddhists provoked protests, including the self-immolation (burning himself to death) of the monk Thich Quang Duc in Saigon in June 1963.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.3] Diem was overthrown and killed in a coup on 2 November 1963, which US officials had tacitly encouraged. After it the South suffered a series of unstable governments.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.4] Diem's fall came three weeks before Kennedy's assassination, so it is easy to mix the two events up.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.b] Nixon wanted to end US ground fighting without abandoning South Vietnam. His method, Vietnamisation, withdrew American troops but widened the war into neighbouring countries.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.0] The Nixon Doctrine, announced at Guam in July 1969, stated that Asian nations should take the main responsibility for their own defence, so the United States would reduce its direct role.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.1] Vietnamisation handed the ground war to the South Vietnamese army while US troops were withdrawn.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.2] US troops numbered about 540,000 at the 1969 peak and had fallen to under 30,000 by late 1972.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.3] Secret bombing of Cambodia, which communist forces used as a base, began in March 1969.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.4] In April 1970 Nixon sent US and South Vietnamese troops into Cambodia to attack communist bases, which sparked protests at home, including Kent State.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.5] In 1971 South Vietnamese forces invaded Laos with US air support and were driven back, which showed the limits of Vietnamisation.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.6] In 1972 Nixon answered North Vietnam's Easter Offensive by mining Haiphong harbour and launching the Linebacker bombing raids, then ordered the Christmas bombing of Hanoi in December 1972.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.7] The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973 and the last US combat troops were out by late March 1973. The fall of Saigon in 1975 came later, and
[hist-eur6-s2.15.b] Presidents also had to decide how to use nuclear weapons to support containment, and Eisenhower and Kennedy chose very different strategies.
[hist-eur6-s2.15.0] Eisenhower's Secretary of State John Foster Dulles relied on massive retaliation (1954): the threat of nuclear attack to deter communist aggression, which was cheaper than large conventional forces.
[hist-eur6-s2.15.1] Eisenhower is said to have signalled through intermediaries in 1953 that he might use nuclear weapons if the Korean talks failed, though historians dispute how far this forced the armistice.
[hist-eur6-s2.15.2] Kennedy replaced massive retaliation with flexible response, building up conventional and special forces (such as the Green Berets) so the United States could fight limited wars instead of choosing between doing nothing and nuclear war.
[hist-eur6-s2.15.3] After the missile crisis Kennedy and Khrushchev signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty (August 1963), banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.b] Harry Truman became President on Roosevelt's death in April 1945 and faced the start of the Cold War. His domestic record is a separate topic.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.0] At the Potsdam conference of July and August 1945 Truman met Stalin for the first time, and he authorised the atomic bombings shortly afterwards.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.1] His main achievements were the Truman Doctrine speech to Congress (12 March 1947), the Marshall Plan, NATO and the Berlin airlift, which together contained Soviet power in Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.2] He recognised the new state of Israel on 14 May 1948, within minutes of its declaration of independence, against the advice of parts of his State Department.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.3] He decided in June 1950 to send American forces to defend South Korea without a congressional vote, a decision that set the pattern for later presidential war-making, and he dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 to keep the war limited.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.4] He took the decisions himself: he chose the airlift rather than an armed convoy to Berlin and accepted a limited war in Korea rather than MacArthur's wider war with China. A sign on his desk read "The buck stops here".
[hist-eur6-s2.16.5] Contemporaries were divided: Republicans attacked him over China and spy cases, and by 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.6] Revisionist historians from the 1960s, such as William Appleman Williams, argued that US economic expansion and the atomic bomb helped cause the Cold War. Post-revisionists such as John Lewis Gaddis stressed that Stalin bore great responsibility and that containment worked in Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.7] His reputation has risen since he left office.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.b] Each president from Truman to Nixon had successes and failures, and judging them means weighing the two against each other.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.0] Truman was strongest in Europe, where the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO and the airlift contained Soviet power, but weaker in Asia, where the Communists took power in China, prompting the "Who lost China?" attack by Republicans, and Korea ended in stalemate.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.1] Eisenhower relied on massive retaliation, which was cheaper than large conventional forces, but he also began the commitment in Vietnam with aid to France, support for Diem and US advisers.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.2] Kennedy suffered the Bay of Pigs failure and then handled the missile crisis with ExComm and the quarantine, yet he raised the number of advisers in Vietnam to about 16,000.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.3] Nixon cut US troops in Vietnam from about 540,000 at the 1969 peak to under 30,000 by late 1972, but he also widened the war into Cambodia and Laos.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.b] Judging US policy means setting its successes against its failures, episode by episode, and then deciding how much containment achieved overall.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.0] Success in Western Europe: the Marshall Plan, NATO and the Berlin airlift contained Soviet power in Europe and helped to create a prosperous Western alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.1] Failure in Asia: the Korean War ended in a stalemate after about three years, with heavy American losses and no victory.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.2] Korea can also be judged a limited success: South Korea survived, and Truman kept the war from spreading to China or becoming nuclear.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.3] In Cuba the Bay of Pigs was a clear failure, but the missile crisis avoided nuclear war and removed the missiles, although the Jupiters in Turkey were withdrawn as a secret concession alongside the public no-invasion pledge.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.4] Vietnam showed the limits of containment: a commitment that began with aid and advisers had escalated to about 500,000 troops by 1968.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.5] Supporters argue that US commitments in Asia deterred further Soviet and Chinese expansion, while critics say they drew the United States into costly wars in places of little strategic importance.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.6] Contemporaries and later historians differ over whether the Cold War could have been managed by diplomacy earlier, and the debate shapes judgements of Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy and Nixon.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.7] Korea and Vietnam both came from containment and the domino theory, but the methods differed: a UN force with a declared limit in Korea, and a gradual commitment in Vietnam.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s3: 3. Case study: Lyndon Johnson and Vietnam, 1963–1968
[hist-eur6-s3.0.b] Johnson did not start the American involvement in Vietnam. He inherited a commitment built up over two earlier presidencies, driven by Cold War fear of communist expansion.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.0] Containment was the Cold War policy of stopping communism spreading. In 1954 Eisenhower put forward the domino theory: if one country fell to communism its neighbours would fall one after another, like a row of dominoes. This is why Americans thought South Vietnam mattered so much.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.1] The United States gave aid to France in its war in Vietnam and then to South Vietnam directly. Vietnam was divided in 1954 at the 17th parallel (a line of latitude), which left a communist state in the North and a US-backed state in the South.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.2] Ho Chi Minh, a communist, led North Vietnam, and Ngo Dinh Diem led South Vietnam with American support. Diem's government was weak, which is why it needed outside help to survive.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.3] The Viet Cong were communist guerrillas (fighters who use ambush and hit-and-run rather than open battle) operating inside South Vietnam against Diem's government. They were separate from the North Vietnamese Army, the regular army of the North.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.4] Kennedy raised the number of US military advisers from under 1,000 in 1961 to about 16,000 by late 1963, but he sent no combat troops. So the United States was committed to South Vietnam's survival without yet fighting the war itself.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.5] The Great Society was Johnson's domestic programme of civil rights, the War on Poverty, health and education. He announced it in May 1964, and it is the reason he wanted Vietnam kept in the background.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.b] Johnson came to the presidency as one of the most skilful legislators in American history. His methods worked on Congress but not on an enemy abroad, which helps to explain why Vietnam beat him.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.0] Lyndon Baines Johnson was born on 27 August 1908 near Stonewall, Texas. He became Texas director of the National Youth Administration (a New Deal agency that gave work to young people) in 1935 and was elected to the House of Representatives in 1937.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.1] He won the 1948 Democratic Senate runoff in Texas by only 87 votes, which earned him the mocking nickname Landslide Lyndon. He took his Senate seat in January 1949.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.2] He became Democratic leader in the Senate in 1953 and Majority Leader from 1955 to 1961. After losing the 1960 Democratic nomination to Kennedy he accepted the vice presidency (1961 to 1963).
[hist-eur6-s3.1.3] He was a master of Senate politics and persuasion, using personal pressure on colleagues (the Johnson treatment) to pass legislation. This worked because colleagues needed his favour.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.4] He could not apply those methods to a war against an enemy who would not bargain on his terms, because Hanoi had nothing to gain from his favour and could simply refuse to negotiate.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.5] Kennedy was shot in Dallas on 22 November 1963, and Johnson was sworn in as president the same day. He remained president until January 1969.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.b] Johnson took over a war that was going badly, in a country with an unstable government. He chose continuity, and his own fears of political damage made him hold on.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.0] Diem was overthrown by a military coup on 1 to 2 November 1963 and killed, so the Saigon government was unstable when Johnson took office. The Viet Cong controlled large parts of the countryside, and about 16,000 US advisers were in the country.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.1] Within days Johnson told his ambassador in Saigon that he would not lose Vietnam. National Security Action Memorandum 273 (26 November 1963) confirmed the policy of supporting South Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.2] He kept Kennedy's advisers: Robert McNamara (Secretary of Defense), Dean Rusk (Secretary of State) and McGeorge Bundy (national security adviser, replaced by Walt Rostow in 1966). So the same thinking about the war carried on.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.3] Johnson feared that losing Vietnam would repeat the loss of China, when Truman's party had been attacked as soft on communism. He feared a right-wing backlash that would wreck his domestic programme.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.4] He also believed that losing South Vietnam would damage US credibility worldwide, because other allies would doubt American promises. He was a firm anti-communist who was also aware of the domestic cost of war.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.b] Two incidents in the Gulf of Tonkin gave Johnson congressional authority for a larger war, and the 1964 election confirmed his mandate while he presented himself as the candidate of restraint.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.0] On 2 August 1964 North Vietnamese torpedo boats attacked the US destroyer USS Maddox, which was on a naval intelligence patrol near the North Vietnamese coast. South Vietnamese raids on that coast (Operation 34A) had taken place days before, and Hanoi linked them to the patrol.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.1] A second attack reported on 4 August 1964, on the Maddox and the destroyer USS Turner Joy, is now believed not to have happened. Sonar and radar readings in bad weather were misread, and later documents showed that officials doubted the report even as it was used.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.2] On the evening of 4 August Johnson announced retaliation, and air strikes hit North Vietnamese naval bases on 5 August.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.3] The Tonkin Gulf Resolution, passed on 7 August 1964 by 416 votes to 0 in the House and 88 to 2 in the Senate, authorised the President to take 'all necessary measures'. This gave Johnson a free hand without a declaration of war.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.4] Senator J. William Fulbright helped steer it through the Senate. Senators Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening voted against it. A draft resolution had been prepared before the incidents, and the second incident was doubtful, which later made the resolution a target of criticism.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.5] In the election of 3 November 1964 Johnson won about 61 per cent of the popular vote against Barry Goldwater, who had called for stronger action in Vietnam and was portrayed as reckless. Johnson presented himself as the candidate of restraint.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.6] The restraint was a political strategy. Johnson publicly promised not to widen the war while privately preparing for a larger one, which is the gap between campaign promises and later action that critics later seized on.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.b] In the first months of 1965 Johnson moved from reprisal to a sustained bombing campaign and then to ground troops. This turned an advisers' war into an American war.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.0] On 7 February 1965 a Viet Cong attack on the American base at Pleiku killed eight Americans. Johnson ordered reprisal air raids (Flaming Dart), which led on to sustained bombing.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.1] Operation Rolling Thunder, a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam, began on 2 March 1965 and continued in stages until 2 November 1968. Its aims were to put pressure on Hanoi and to support the Saigon government.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.2] Its limit was that bombing did not stop the war. North Vietnam refused to negotiate while the bombing continued, and the Viet Cong kept fighting in the South, supplied through the Ho Chi Minh Trail (a network of routes through Laos and Cambodia).
[hist-eur6-s3.4.3] About 3,500 US Marines landed at Da Nang on 8 March 1965, the first American ground combat troops in Vietnam. They were sent to protect the air base, but they began the build-up of ground forces.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.4] Johnson's strategy was to hold the South while signalling willingness to negotiate. In his Johns Hopkins University speech in Baltimore on 7 April 1965 he offered unconditional discussions and a development plan for the Mekong region (about one billion dollars).
[hist-eur6-s3.4.5] Hanoi did not accept, so the offer of talks was not followed up, and the build-up went on.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.b] Johnson committed large ground forces in the summer of 1965, but he did it quietly and against some advice. He wanted to protect the Great Society legislation then passing through Congress.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.0] General William Westmoreland, commander of US forces from June 1964, argued that a war of attrition with large forces would wear down the North Vietnamese.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.1] Johnson's advisers were not united. Under Secretary of State George Ball argued in memos in 1964 and 1965 that the United States would be dragged into an unwinnable land war, but Johnson, McNamara, Rusk and Bundy chose escalation.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.2] On 28 July 1965 Johnson announced that US forces would rise from 75,000 to 125,000 at once and that more would follow as commanders requested. He asked for no declaration of war and did not call up the reserves.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.3] He kept the escalation low-key and avoided a formal declaration of war because he did not want to endanger his Great Society legislation, which was passing through Congress in 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.4] US troop numbers were about 184,000 at the end of 1965, about 385,000 at the end of 1966 and about 485,000 at the end of 1967. They were over 500,000 in 1968 and peaked at about 540,000 in 1969 before falling.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.b] Westmoreland's methods turned the war into a contest of firepower and numbers. They killed many people but did not break communist will, and they carried heavy costs for Vietnamese civilians and Americans alike.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.0] Westmoreland's strategy was attrition (wearing the enemy down by killing more than they could replace) through search and destroy. Large units hunted communist forces in the countryside, using helicopters, artillery and air power, and success was measured by the body count.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.1] Attrition failed because the communists could choose when to fight and replaced losses from a large population and from supplies carried down the Ho Chi Minh Trail. So the killing did not break their will.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.2] Heavy firepower, including napalm (a sticky burning fuel) and the defoliant Agent Orange (a chemical that strips leaves so the enemy cannot hide), killed civilians and drove peasants into government-controlled camps or towards the Viet Cong.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.3] Pacification (winning over the countryside) and the building of a stable South Vietnamese government lagged behind. The Saigon regime was led by generals, and Nguyen Van Thieu became president in September 1967 after elections criticised as flawed.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.4] In November 1967 Westmoreland told Washington that the end was in view. This set up the shock of the Tet Offensive.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.5] On 16 March 1968 American soldiers killed several hundred unarmed villagers at My Lai. The massacre was exposed publicly in November 1969.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.6] More than 30,000 Americans were dead by the end of 1968. The draft (compulsory military service) gave deferments to most college students, so it fell on those without them and spread the war's costs through American families.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.b] As the war grew, Johnson's public statements were increasingly doubted. From 1965 his critics in Congress, universities, churches and the press challenged the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.0] Journalists used the term credibility gap from the mid-1960s for the gap between official statements on the war and what reporters found in Vietnam. Johnson played the war down in public to protect the Great Society, so the later gap between his optimism and events hurt him.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.1] The first teach-in (a meeting of lectures and debate against the war) was held at the University of Michigan on 24 to 25 March 1965. Student and church opposition grew steadily through 1966 and 1967.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.2] Students for a Democratic Society (SDS) led a march of about 20,000 in Washington on 17 April 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.3] Senator J. William Fulbright, chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, held televised hearings in February 1966 that questioned the war's basis. He had helped steer the 1964 resolution through, so his change of mind carried weight.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.4] His book The Arrogance of Power (1966) argued that the United States was acting as a great power imposing its will on a small nation.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.5] On 4 April 1967 Martin Luther King spoke against the war in his Beyond Vietnam speech at Riverside Church, New York, linking the war to poverty and racism.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.6] On 28 April 1967 Muhammad Ali refused induction into the army in Houston and was stripped of his heavyweight title, which made the draft a public issue.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.7] On 21 October 1967 about 50,000 people marched on the Pentagon, showing that opposition had spread well beyond students.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.b] By 1967 Americans were arguing about why the country was in Vietnam at all. Three ideas shaped the argument: that the war was imperialism, that opinion was turning, and that defence interests drove it.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.0] Imperialism was the critics' charge that a great power was imposing its will on a small nation. Fulbright used this argument, and it challenged the claim that the United States was only defending a free people.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.1] Critics argued that the war was costly and immoral and was draining money and attention from the Great Society.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.2] Supporters argued that withdrawal would damage America's credibility abroad and that the domino theory meant that other countries would fall if South Vietnam did.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.3] The military-industrial complex was the interest shared by defence contractors, the armed forces and government in high defence spending. Eisenhower had warned of it in his farewell address on 17 January 1961, and critics linked it to the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.4] Public opinion means the views of ordinary Americans, measured by polls and shaped by television and the press. It had turned against the war and Johnson from 1967, and by late 1967 Johnson's approval was about 40 per cent or below.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.b] Johnson tried to fund both the war and his domestic programme, and the cost of the war weakened his reform agenda.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.0] The Great Society's biggest laws came first: the Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964), Medicare and Medicaid (July 1965) and the Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965). They were passed before the war's costs became acute, and they are Johnson's most secure achievements.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.1] Johnson tried to pay for the war and the Great Society together, a policy critics called guns and butter. He delayed asking Congress for a tax increase until January 1967.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.2] Federal deficits grew and inflation rose after 1965, passing 4 per cent in 1968. Congress passed a 10 per cent income tax surcharge in June 1968 to restrain spending.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.3] The War on Poverty (declared January 1964) was cut or diverted as military spending grew, so many programmes were underfunded as they expanded.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.4] Urban riots, such as Watts in 1965 and Detroit in 1967, added to the loss of support. By 1967 the war's rising cost and the riots had turned public opinion against new domestic spending, so the Great Society lost momentum.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.b] The Tet Offensive of January 1968 did not defeat the Americans militarily, but it shattered their optimism and ended Johnson's chance of re-election.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.0] Tet began on 30 January 1968 (the night of 30 to 31 January), during the Tet holiday. Viet Cong and North Vietnamese forces attacked more than a hundred towns and cities across South Vietnam, including Saigon and Hue, which was retaken after about a month of fighting.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.1] On 31 January a Viet Cong squad entered the US embassy compound in Saigon and held out for about six hours. Television pictures shocked American opinion, even though the communists failed to hold it.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.2] Westmoreland had said in November 1967 that the end was in view and the enemy could no longer mount major offensives. Tet therefore looked like proof that official optimism was false, an American political defeat though a heavy communist military one.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.3] On 27 February 1968 the television newsman Walter Cronkite broadcast a report calling the war a stalemate. It was widely reported as a sign of changing opinion.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.4] In February 1968 Westmoreland and the Joint Chiefs asked for about 206,000 more troops. Clark Clifford, Secretary of Defense from 1 March 1968, advised against it. The Wise Men (senior outside advisers) told Johnson in late March that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.5] In the New Hampshire primary on 12 March 1968 the anti-war Democrat Eugene McCarthy ran Johnson close. Robert Kennedy entered the race in mid-March, so Johnson was weakened within his own party.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.6] On 31 March 1968 Johnson announced a partial halt to the bombing north of the 20th parallel, offered talks and said he would not seek re-election. Peace talks began in Paris in May, but the war continued.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.7] Martin Luther King was assassinated on 4 April 1968. Robert Kennedy was shot on 5 June and died on 6 June. Rioting between police and protesters marked the Democratic convention in Chicago in August.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.8] Hubert Humphrey lost narrowly to the Republican Richard Nixon in November, who won the presidency after promising an honourable end to the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.b] A huge American effort failed to defeat a determined enemy. Historians and contemporaries give several linked reasons, and the best answers back each with evidence.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.0] The war was a limited one. The US avoided invading North Vietnam, mining Haiphong harbour (the North's main port) or using nuclear weapons, partly from fear of bringing in China or the Soviet Union, and this limited the pressure on Hanoi.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.1] North Vietnam and the Viet Cong used bases in Laos and Cambodia and the Ho Chi Minh Trail, which the US was reluctant to attack. So they could withdraw and rebuild, and their determination did not break.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.2] The South Vietnamese government was weak and corrupt, and the Army of the Republic of Vietnam could not hold the territory cleared by American forces.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.3] The strategy of attrition and the body count measure were criticised within the US army and in the press as misleading about the progress of the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.4] Johnson's gradual escalation and his refusal to consider a clear withdrawal kept options open while costs and divisions at home grew. He never explained clear aims to the public, so home support drained away.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.5] Historians differ on the cause. Some say Johnson was trapped by the assumptions of the Cold War, while others argue that he made avoidable choices on escalation in 1964 and 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.b] Johnson was the president who made Vietnam an American war and also the president of the Great Society. He was a decision-maker at the centre of events, not a witness.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.0] His career: House of Representatives 1937, Senate 1949, Majority Leader 1955 to 1961, vice president 1961 to 1963, and president from 22 November 1963 to January 1969.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.1] His domestic record was one of the greatest legislative achievements of any president: the Civil Rights Act (1964), the Voting Rights Act (1965), Medicare, Medicaid and federal aid to education.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.2] In Vietnam he took the decisions of 1964 and 1965 to bomb North Vietnam and send combat troops, always in steps. He hoped to avoid a wider war with China or the Soviet Union, as in Korea in 1950.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.3] He shaped events by making those decisions and was shaped by them. The Tet shock and falling support forced him out in 1968, and protesters outside the White House chanted at him about how many children he had killed that day.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.4] Contemporaries saw him as a forceful, domineering leader who was crude, devious and driven. The credibility gap made many doubt his word, though supporters pointed to his achievements for the poor and for black Americans.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.5] Historians in the 1970s, such as the journalist David Halberstam in The Best and the Brightest (1972), blamed the arrogance of the advisers he inherited.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.6] Later historians, such as Robert Dallek in Flawed Giant (1998) and Fredrik Logevall in Choosing War (1999), stress that Johnson had alternatives in 1964 and 1965 and chose escalation, partly to protect his domestic reputation.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.b] Examiners reward a clear judgement, stated at the start, that weighs Johnson's domestic achievements against his failure in Vietnam and sets contemporary views against later ones.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.0] Success at home: the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act, Medicare and Medicaid are judged to be major achievements of twentieth-century social reform, passed in 1964 and 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.1] Failure abroad: the United States did not win in Vietnam, and about 58,000 Americans died in the whole war. The war damaged the Great Society and the public's trust in government.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.2] Contemporaries were divided. In 1964 and 1965 most Americans admired him as a strong, effective leader, but by late 1967 his approval had fallen to about 40 per cent or below. Protesters chanted about how many children he had killed that day.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.3] Later historians disagree. Halberstam blamed the arrogant advisers, while Dallek and Logevall argue that Johnson made avoidable choices. The debate is whether he was trapped by Cold War assumptions or chose escalation.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.4] Overall he was a great domestic president whose war undermined his presidency. The credibility gap was his lasting legacy, a problem of trust between president and public that continued to shape politics after Vietnam.
