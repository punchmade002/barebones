[hist-eur6-s9.0.b] To understand the civil rights movement you need to know how black Americans lost the rights they had briefly won after the Civil War, and why the Constitution let southern states get away with it. Pressure for change began to build only when millions of black Americans moved and fought in the Second World War.
[hist-eur6-s9.0.0] Three Reconstruction amendments (changes to the Constitution made after the Civil War) promised equality: the 13th (1865) ended slavery, the 14th (1868) gave citizenship and equal protection of the law, and the 15th (1870) gave black men the vote.
[hist-eur6-s9.0.1] These gains collapsed. By the 1890s southern states had taken back control of their own governments and stripped black men of the vote, so the amendments existed on paper but were not enforced.
[hist-eur6-s9.0.2] Federalism is the division of power between the federal government in Washington and the state governments. Southern states claimed the right to run their own race laws as states' rights.
[hist-eur6-s9.0.3] The federal government and the Supreme Court could override the states, which is why the courts and Congress became the targets of the movement.
[hist-eur6-s9.0.4] Discrimination means unequal treatment because of race. In the South it was written into law; in the North, and in housing and jobs everywhere, it was enforced by custom, so a black family could be refused a house or a job with no law requiring it.
[hist-eur6-s9.0.5] Jim Crow is the name for the system of laws that enforced segregation (the legal separation of the races) in the South.
[hist-eur6-s9.0.6] The Great Migration was the movement of black Americans from the rural South to cities in the North and West. It gave black communities more votes and political weight outside the South, and it brought the northern ghettos that later exploded in riots.
[hist-eur6-s9.0.7] The Second World War started postwar pressure for change, because black Americans who had fought for democracy abroad were no longer willing to accept segregation at home. In July 1948 Truman's Executive Order 9981 desegregated the armed forces.
[hist-eur6-s9.1.b] Segregation was enforced by state law across the South and by custom in much of the North, and the courts were the first route to change. The NAACP chose to attack it case by case through the law.
[hist-eur6-s9.1.0] Jim Crow in the South meant separate schools, buses, waiting rooms and water fountains. Black voters were kept off the rolls by poll taxes (a fee to vote that poor black families could not pay) and literacy tests (reading tests applied unfairly by local officials).
[hist-eur6-s9.1.1] Intimidation by the Ku Klux Klan and local officials completed the system, because anyone who tried to register risked violence or loss of a job.
[hist-eur6-s9.1.2] In the North discrimination in housing and jobs was enforced by custom and by restrictive covenants, which were clauses in property deeds forbidding owners to sell to black buyers.
[hist-eur6-s9.1.3] Plessy v Ferguson (1896) upheld Louisiana's separate railway carriages under the doctrine of separate but equal facilities. In practice this meant inferior schools, transport and public services for black Americans, because the facilities were separate but never equal.
[hist-eur6-s9.1.4] The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP, founded in 1909 and run by lawyers) led the legal campaign against segregation through cases on education and voting in the 1930s and 1940s and on into the 1950s, with Thurgood Marshall leading the cases.
[hist-eur6-s9.1.5] Two NAACP successes show the strategy. Smith v. Allwright (1944) ended the all-white Democratic primary (the party election that decided who won in the South), and Sweatt v. Painter (1950) concerned the University of Texas law school.
[hist-eur6-s9.1.6] Brown v Board of Education of Topeka, decided unanimously on 17 May 1954 under Chief Justice Earl Warren, overturned separate but equal in public schools and ruled that segregated public schools were unconstitutional.
[hist-eur6-s9.1.7] Brown II in 1955 ordered desegregation "with all deliberate speed". That vague phrase let southern states delay, so progress was very slow, and by 1964 only a small fraction of southern black children attended mixed schools.
[hist-eur6-s9.2.b] The Brown ruling did not end segregation, because white southerners resisted it and the federal government hesitated. The murder of Emmett Till and the crisis at Little Rock made the issue national.
[hist-eur6-s9.2.0] Emmett Till, aged 14 and from Chicago, was brutally murdered in Mississippi in August 1955. Two white men were acquitted by an all-white jury in September 1955, which shocked black Americans.
[hist-eur6-s9.2.1] His mother held an open-casket funeral so that the world could see what had been done to him, and he became a symbol of the cause.
[hist-eur6-s9.2.2] The Southern Manifesto of March 1956 was signed by 101 members of Congress, all from the South. It pledged to resist desegregation by all lawful means, so it showed that southern political leaders would fight Brown.
[hist-eur6-s9.2.3] At Little Rock in early September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus used the Arkansas National Guard to bar nine black students from Central High School, defying a federal court order.
[hist-eur6-s9.2.4] President Eisenhower answered by sending federal troops (the 101st Airborne) and federalising the Guard, which took it out of Faubus's control. The nine entered under guard in late September 1957, so the federal government enforced the law by force.
[hist-eur6-s9.2.5] The Civil Rights Act of 1957, signed in September, was the first federal civil rights law since Reconstruction. It created a Civil Rights Commission and a Civil Rights Division in the Justice Department.
[hist-eur6-s9.2.6] It did little for voting in practice, because juries in the South often refused to convict anyone charged with keeping black citizens from voting.
[hist-eur6-s9.3.b] From 1960 young activists turned from courtrooms to non-violent direct action, which meant peaceful protest that broke segregation rules in public and accepted the consequences. It exposed segregation to national and international audiences.
[hist-eur6-s9.3.0] On 1 February 1960 four students from North Carolina Agricultural and Technical College sat at a Woolworth's lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, where black customers were not served. Sit-ins spread across the South within weeks, reaching dozens of cities.
[hist-eur6-s9.3.1] The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC, pronounced snick) was formed in April 1960 at Shaw University, Raleigh, North Carolina, with the help of Ella Baker, to coordinate student protest across the South.
[hist-eur6-s9.3.2] The Freedom Rides began in May 1961 and were organised by the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE, led by James Farmer). Their aim was to test a Supreme Court ruling against segregation in interstate bus terminals.
[hist-eur6-s9.3.3] The riders met violence. A bus was burned by a mob near Anniston, Alabama, and riders were beaten in Anniston and Birmingham.
[hist-eur6-s9.3.4] The Kennedy administration sent marshals to protect the riders, and the Interstate Commerce Commission ordered desegregated terminals in late 1961. The Rides therefore won a federal response by provoking violence.
[hist-eur6-s9.3.5] The Albany Movement in Georgia in 1961 to 1962 was King's failure. Police Chief Laurie Pritchett arrested demonstrators in large numbers without violence and used neighbouring jails, so there were no shocking images, and King left with little achieved.
[hist-eur6-s9.3.6] Albany showed that violence by the police helped the movement, because it won sympathy. In Birmingham the following year Bull Connor supplied that violence.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.b] In 1963 the movement chose a city where the police were likely to use violence, and television carried the result into every home. The events of this one year turned the federal government towards a civil rights law.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.0] The Birmingham campaign in Alabama in April and May 1963 was led by King and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), with Fred Shuttlesworth. Commissioner of Public Safety Eugene "Bull" Connor used police dogs and fire hoses against demonstrators.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.1] In early May hundreds of schoolchildren marched and were arrested, the so-called Children's Crusade. Photographs and television footage were shown across the world, which shocked opinion and built support for federal civil rights legislation.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.2] An agreement on 10 May desegregated lunch counters and fitting rooms in downtown shops. Bombings and rioting followed on 11 May, so the violence came after the agreement, not before it.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.3] In April 1963 King wrote the Letter from Birmingham Jail to eight white clergymen who had called the protests unwise and untimely. It argued for civil disobedience against unjust laws.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.4] On 11 June 1963 Governor George Wallace made his "stand in the schoolhouse door" at the University of Alabama to block black students. The same evening Kennedy's television address called civil rights a moral issue.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.5] Medgar Evers, the NAACP field secretary in Mississippi, was shot dead outside his home on 12 June. Kennedy sent a civil rights bill to Congress in June 1963.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.6] Kennedy was first reluctant about the March on Washington, fearing violence, and then backed it. On 28 August 1963 about 250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial.
[hist-eur6-s9.4.7] The speakers included John Lewis of SNCC and King, whose "I Have a Dream" speech became the defining statement of the movement. Organisers included A. Philip Randolph and Bayard Rustin.
[hist-eur6-s9.5.b] Kennedy's bill of June 1963 became law only after his death and a long Senate struggle. The Act of 2 July 1964 was the first law to use federal power to end segregation in everyday life.
[hist-eur6-s9.5.0] After Kennedy was assassinated on 22 November 1963, Johnson used the public's memory of him and his own skill as a former Senate leader to push the bill through, which is why it passed in Johnson's name.
[hist-eur6-s9.5.1] Southern senators filibustered, which means they kept talking to delay a vote. It was the longest filibuster to that date, and the Senate ended it by cloture (a vote to close debate) on 10 June 1964, the first time cloture was used on a civil rights bill.
[hist-eur6-s9.5.2] The Civil Rights Act was signed on 2 July 1964. Title II banned segregation in public accommodations such as hotels, restaurants and theatres.
[hist-eur6-s9.5.3] Title VI allowed the government to withhold federal money from segregated programmes, and it enabled the Justice Department to sue segregated schools, so federal power could now end school segregation.
[hist-eur6-s9.5.4] Title VII banned employment discrimination by race, colour, religion, sex or national origin, and the Act set up the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission to enforce it.
[hist-eur6-s9.5.5] The 24th Amendment, ratified in 1964, banned poll taxes in federal elections, removing one barrier to black voting.
[hist-eur6-s9.5.6] Freedom Summer in Mississippi in 1964 was run by SNCC and others with northern student volunteers who registered voters. Three workers, James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, were murdered in June 1964 and their bodies found in August.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.b] The 1964 Act left the vote largely untouched. King's campaign at Selma was designed to force Washington to act, and it produced the second great law.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.0] Selma, Alabama, was the scene of an SCLC voter-registration campaign in early 1965, led by King to dramatise how black citizens were denied the vote in Alabama.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.1] On Bloody Sunday, 7 March 1965, state troopers and a sheriff's posse attacked marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge. Television pictures of the attack outraged the nation.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.2] On 15 March 1965 Johnson addressed Congress to call for the Voting Rights Act and used the movement's words "we shall overcome". The march from Selma to Montgomery was completed in late March 1965.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.3] The Voting Rights Act, signed on 6 August 1965, suspended literacy tests and similar devices in areas with a history of discrimination. It sent federal examiners to register voters and required federal approval of voting-law changes in those areas.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.4] The results were quick. In Mississippi black voter registration rose from about 7 per cent in 1964 to about 60 per cent by 1967, and black elected officials began to take office in the South.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.5] The Fair Housing Act, signed on 11 April 1968, a week after King's death, banned discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing. Enforcement was weak at first, so it did not quickly end residential segregation.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.6] Liberalism is the belief in an active federal government that uses its power for civil rights and social welfare, and it was the basis of Johnson's laws.
[hist-eur6-s9.6.7] They passed in 1964 to 1965 because of Kennedy's death, Democratic majorities in Congress after Johnson's landslide in the 1964 election, and public reaction to violence seen on television.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.b] King was the best-known leader of the civil rights movement, both a participant who led marches and a symbol who shaped how the nation saw the cause. His role in the Montgomery bus boycott belongs to its own case study, so only a reference to it appears here.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.0] King was born in Atlanta, Georgia, on 15 January 1929, the son of a Baptist minister, and was ordained as a Baptist minister himself. He later married Coretta Scott King.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.1] He earned a PhD in theology at Boston University in 1955. His commitment to non-violent direct action came from Gandhi's methods and from Christian teaching on love.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.2] His philosophy of non-violence combined these two, and he argued that the movement should win over white opinion by accepting suffering rather than returning violence. Civil disobedience (deliberately breaking an unjust law and accepting the punishment) was part of this.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.3] He used the media deliberately. Peaceful marchers attacked by police made a powerful contrast on television, which is why he chose to protest where violence was likely.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.4] He was a founder and first president of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, formed in 1957 to coordinate church-based civil rights protest across the South.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.5] His main successes were Montgomery (1955 to 1956), Birmingham (1963) and Selma (1965). The Albany Movement (1961 to 1962) was a setback.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.6] He wrote the Letter from Birmingham Jail in April 1963, spoke at the March on Washington in 1963 and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1964, aged 35, the youngest recipient up to that time.
[hist-eur6-s9.7.7] He shaped events and was shaped by them. He learned from Albany to choose Birmingham, where Bull Connor's violence helped him, and in later years he was pushed to the left by the riots and by Black Power.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.b] After 1965 King turned from southern segregation to northern housing, poverty and war, and this brought him failure and new enemies. His death in 1968 ended the career, and opinion of him has changed since.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.0] King campaigned in Chicago in 1966 against housing discrimination, against mayor Richard Daley. White crowds attacked marchers, and an agreement achieved little, so this was his main setback in the North.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.1] In 1967 he linked the civil rights struggle with poverty and the Vietnam War, and he spoke against the war in his Riverside Church speech on 4 April 1967, exactly a year before his death. This lost him the support of Johnson and of some civil rights leaders.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.2] His Poor People's Campaign was planned to bring poor Americans of all races to Washington in 1968. In Memphis, Tennessee, in March and April 1968 he was supporting a strike by sanitation workers when he was killed.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.3] He was shot at the Lorraine Motel in Memphis on 4 April 1968. James Earl Ray pleaded guilty to the murder in March 1969, and riots followed in many cities.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.4] Contemporaries were divided. Supporters admired him, but many southern whites opposed him, and Malcolm X criticised the March on Washington and non-violence. Later Black Power leaders judged him too moderate.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.5] The FBI under J. Edgar Hoover wiretapped King and tried to discredit him through its COINTELPRO operations, which were exposed in the mid-1970s. Johnson distanced himself from King over Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.6] Historians now regard him as the most important leader of the movement, and the King holiday was signed into law in 1983 and first observed in January 1986.
[hist-eur6-s9.8.7] Historians increasingly add that he was one leader among many, stressing the role of local activists, women and SNCC organisers such as Ella Baker and Fannie Lou Hamer, so the debate is whether leaders or the wider movement drove change.
[hist-eur6-s9.9.b] By 1965 many black Americans, especially in northern cities, felt that slow change and the limits of the new laws had not touched their lives. Some turned to more radical politics.
[hist-eur6-s9.9.0] Frustration grew for several reasons. Change was slow, the northern ghettos had poor housing and unemployment, police were hostile, and the 1964 and 1965 laws did little for northern cities, where segregation was not written into law.
[hist-eur6-s9.9.1] Malcolm X (born Malcolm Little, 1925) joined the Nation of Islam, led by Elijah Muhammad. He criticised integration and non-violence in the early 1960s, so his message was separation and self-defence.
[hist-eur6-s9.9.2] He left the Nation of Islam in March 1964 and made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1964, after which he adopted a more inclusive position.
[hist-eur6-s9.9.3] He was shot dead at the Audubon Ballroom in Harlem on 21 February 1965. The Autobiography of Malcolm X, written with Alex Haley and published in 1965, made him a hero to many young black people.
[hist-eur6-s9.9.4] Stokely Carmichael of SNCC used the slogan Black Power in June 1966 in Mississippi. It expressed a demand for black self-determination and community control, and it split SNCC and CORE from King's approach of integration and non-violence.
[hist-eur6-s9.9.5] The Black Panther Party for Self-Defense was founded in Oakland, California, in October 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale. It combined armed self-defence with community programmes such as a free breakfast programme for children.
[hist-eur6-s9.9.6] The Panthers clashed with police and were infiltrated by the FBI, so they faced hostility from the authorities.
[hist-eur6-s9.10.b] In the mid-1960s the northern ghettos exploded in violence. The riots showed that the laws passed in the South had not touched the problems of poor black neighbourhoods in the cities.
[hist-eur6-s9.10.0] The Watts riot in Los Angeles began on 11 August 1965 after a police traffic stop. The National Guard was called in, large areas burned and about 34 people died.
[hist-eur6-s9.10.1] Newark in July 1967 had 26 deaths. Detroit in July 1967 had 43 deaths and needed federal paratroops, sent in by Johnson.
[hist-eur6-s9.10.2] The causes were northern ghettos, police behaviour, unemployment, and laws that did not reach the North.
[hist-eur6-s9.10.3] The riots also produced a white backlash, as many white Americans reacted to the violence by turning against further reform, which fed the politics of law and order.
[hist-eur6-s9.10.4] The Kerner Commission, chaired by Governor Otto Kerner of Illinois, was appointed in July 1967 and published its report on 29 February 1968. It warned that "our nation is moving toward two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal".
[hist-eur6-s9.10.5] It blamed white racism, poverty and police behaviour for the riots, and recommended large spending on jobs, housing and schools.
[hist-eur6-s9.10.6] Johnson did not adopt its recommendations, so the report changed little.
[hist-eur6-s9.11.b] Poverty in the inner cities became a national concern in the 1960s, and Johnson's War on Poverty was the federal response. Its limits help to explain why the ghettos stayed poor.
[hist-eur6-s9.11.0] Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) estimated that tens of millions of Americans lived in poverty and were largely invisible to the affluent majority.
[hist-eur6-s9.11.1] Johnson announced "an unconditional war on poverty" in his State of the Union address in January 1964, and the Economic Opportunity Act of August 1964 created the Office of Economic Opportunity, the Job Corps and Community Action Programs.
[hist-eur6-s9.11.2] The Community Action Programs required the maximum feasible participation of the poor. They brought conflict with city governments, because mayors objected to community action bodies that challenged city hall. Head Start began in 1965.
[hist-eur6-s9.11.3] The programmes were underfunded relative to the war in Vietnam, because spending was squeezed by it, and many were cut back as the 1960s ended.
[hist-eur6-s9.11.4] Daniel Patrick Moynihan's report The Negro Family: The Case for National Action (1965) argued that family breakdown held back black progress. Critics said it blamed the victims.
[hist-eur6-s9.11.5] The official poverty rate fell from about 22 per cent in 1959 to about 12 per cent in 1969, helped by economic growth and by Medicare, Medicaid, food stamps and Social Security.
[hist-eur6-s9.11.6] The black poverty rate stayed about two to three times the white rate, which shows that the fall in poverty did not close the racial gap.
[hist-eur6-s9.12.b] Black poverty did not disappear when segregation laws ended, because its causes were economic and geographical as well as legal.
[hist-eur6-s9.12.0] Unemployment was high in the inner cities, and poor schooling left young people without the skills to find work.
[hist-eur6-s9.12.1] Housing segregation kept black families in the ghettos, because discrimination in sales and rentals barred them from the white suburbs where many jobs were moving.
[hist-eur6-s9.12.2] Many industrial jobs in the cities were lost after 1968, and this weakened inner-city economies further.
[hist-eur6-s9.12.3] Poverty rates among black Americans therefore remained far higher than among whites through the 1960s and 1970s.
[hist-eur6-s9.13.b] Crime and drugs became political issues in the 1960s, and presidents began to answer them with new federal powers. The effects fell most heavily on poor black communities.
[hist-eur6-s9.13.0] Heroin addiction was concentrated in inner cities such as Harlem in the 1960s. In 1971 public concern about heroin use by American soldiers in Vietnam added to the alarm.
[hist-eur6-s9.13.1] Crime and urban disorder were used by Nixon in 1968 and by later Republican candidates as a political issue under the slogan of law and order, which appealed to white voters alarmed by the riots.
[hist-eur6-s9.13.2] The Controlled Substances Act was passed in 1970 and the Drug Enforcement Administration was created in 1973.
[hist-eur6-s9.13.3] On 17 June 1971 Nixon called drug abuse "public enemy number one" and launched a war on drugs. His programme included treatment as well as enforcement, so it was not only a policing campaign.
[hist-eur6-s9.13.4] Organised crime was also a concern. Senator Estes Kefauver's televised hearings in 1950 to 1951 exposed the Mafia to a national audience.
[hist-eur6-s9.13.5] The Organized Crime Control Act of 1970 included the RICO provisions, which let prosecutors target criminal organisations.
[hist-eur6-s9.14.b] In the 1980s the war on drugs was escalated and became enforcement-led. Its heaviest effects were on black communities.
[hist-eur6-s9.14.0] Crack cocaine, a cheap smokable form of cocaine, spread in poor urban areas from about 1985. Violence was linked to the trade, which fell on poor black neighbourhoods.
[hist-eur6-s9.14.1] Reagan announced a renewed war on drugs in 1982. Nancy Reagan's Just Say No campaign, also from 1982, promoted drug prevention. Ronald Reagan's own measures were enforcement and the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986.
[hist-eur6-s9.14.2] The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 set mandatory minimum sentences (fixed minimum prison terms that judges could not reduce), with crack sentenced as severely as one hundred times the weight of powder cocaine.
[hist-eur6-s9.14.3] This disparity fell heavily on black defendants, because crack was concentrated in poor black neighbourhoods and powder cocaine was used more by wealthier users.
[hist-eur6-s9.14.4] The prison population rose steeply through the 1980s, and black men were imprisoned at several times the rate of white men.
[hist-eur6-s9.15.b] After 1968 the great laws were in place, but arguments shifted to schools and affirmative action, which means giving preference in admissions or jobs to groups that have suffered discrimination. Black political power grew even as other problems remained.
[hist-eur6-s9.15.0] In Boston in 1974 court-ordered busing, which carried children between neighbourhoods to integrate schools, met violent white protest, showing that northern whites resisted school integration.
[hist-eur6-s9.15.1] In Regents of the University of California v. Bakke in 1978 the Supreme Court struck down fixed racial quotas in university admissions but allowed race as one factor, so affirmative action was limited but not ended.
[hist-eur6-s9.15.2] Black political power rose. Carl Stokes became mayor of Cleveland in 1967 (the first black mayor of a major US city), Thurgood Marshall joined the Supreme Court in 1967, and Harold Washington became mayor of Chicago in 1983.
[hist-eur6-s9.15.3] Jesse Jackson ran for the Democratic presidential nomination in 1984 and 1988. Black elected officials rose from about 1,500 in 1970 to about 7,000 by 1989.
[hist-eur6-s9.15.4] Housing and school segregation persisted, and white flight, the move of white families from cities to the suburbs, kept neighbourhoods and schools divided.
[hist-eur6-s9.16.b] Examiners reward a judgement, stated early and sustained, that weighs the legal achievements of the movement against the persistence of inequality. The main distinction is between legal and economic equality, and between the periods before and after 1968.
[hist-eur6-s9.16.0] Achievements: the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act and the Fair Housing Act ended legal segregation and gave black Americans the vote on equal terms in practice. Black officeholders and a black middle class appeared.
[hist-eur6-s9.16.1] Limits: economic inequality, poverty, residential and school segregation, policing, imprisonment and drugs meant that legal equality did not bring equal opportunity.
[hist-eur6-s9.16.2] Different periods: 1945 to 1968 was transformed in law, while 1968 to 1989 saw slow, contested change.
[hist-eur6-s9.16.3] Contemporary white opinion was divided, with many northern whites supporting civil rights laws but resisting busing and open housing in their own neighbourhoods. Black militants judged progress too slow.
[hist-eur6-s9.16.4] Later historians differ: some see the period as a great success that was later undermined, while others argue that its gains were narrower and less lasting than they appeared.

[hist-eur6-s1.0.b] The US Constitution (drafted 1787) divides power between the federal government in Washington and the fifty state governments. That division, called federalism, shaped the civil rights and social policy fights of the period because southern states defended segregation as their own business.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.0] The Bill of Rights (1791) added the Tenth Amendment, which reserves powers not given to the federal government to the states, so states controlled schools, policing, voting rules and marriage law.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.1] Before the 1960s most southern states enforced segregation (the separation of black and white people, often called Jim Crow) by state law, and federal action against it was met with the argument that such matters belonged to the states. This argument is known as states' rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.2] In 1948 southern Democrats angry at Truman's civil rights programme left the party and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party (the Dixiecrats) with Strom Thurmond as candidate. It carried four Deep South states: Alabama, Louisiana, Mississippi and South Carolina.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.3] The Civil Rights Act 1957 was the first federal civil rights law since Reconstruction (the years after the Civil War when the North tried to protect black rights in the South). It set up a Civil Rights Commission and let the Justice Department act over voting rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.4] In September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus of Arkansas used the National Guard to stop nine black students entering Little Rock Central High School. Eisenhower replied by sending federal troops, including the 101st Airborne Division, to enforce integration, so the federal government overruled a state governor.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.5] The Civil Rights Act, signed on 2 July 1964, rested on the federal power to regulate interstate commerce (trade between states), which allowed Congress to ban segregation in hotels, restaurants and other public places even though they were privately owned.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.6] The Voting Rights Act, signed on 6 August 1965, suspended literacy tests used to keep black people off the voting lists and sent federal examiners to register voters. It put federal law above state practice, and it is a separate law from the 1957 and 1964 Acts.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.b] The Constitution gives law-making, executing and judging to three different bodies, so that no one can rule alone. Each can also limit the others, a system called checks and balances, and the period from 1945 to 1989 shows each check being used.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.0] Congress (the Senate and House of Representatives) makes laws, raises taxes and declares war; the President executes the laws, commands the armed forces as commander in chief and conducts foreign policy; the Supreme Court interprets the Constitution.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.1] The President can veto (refuse to sign) bills, but Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds majority in both houses. The Senate must also confirm senior appointments and ratify treaties by a two-thirds vote, which stops a President acting alone.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.2] Republicans won both houses of Congress in the November 1946 midterm elections (held halfway through a presidential term), using the slogan "Had enough?". It was the first Republican control since the 1928 elections, so Truman faced a hostile Congress until January 1949.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.3] That Congress passed the Taft-Hartley Act in 1947, restricting trade union activity such as the closed shop (a workplace where only union members can be hired), and overrode Truman's veto in June 1947. Truman did not simply give in: he campaigned against that Congress and won in 1948.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.4] Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer: Truman seized the steel mills in April 1952 during a strike without congressional authority. In June 1952 the Supreme Court ruled that he had exceeded his powers, a check on the President by the courts.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.5] The Twenty-Second Amendment, passed by Congress in March 1947 and ratified in 1951, limits a president to two elected terms. It was a direct response to Roosevelt winning four elections, so it is a check on the length of a President's power, not on a single act.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.b] From the 1950s the Supreme Court and Congress used their powers more boldly, first to extend rights and then to limit presidents after Nixon and during Reagan's presidency.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.0] The Warren Court (led by Chief Justice Earl Warren, appointed by Eisenhower in 1953) used judicial review, the power to strike down acts that break the Constitution, to extend civil rights. Brown v. Board of Education (1954) rested on the Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.1] Miranda v. Arizona (1966) on the rights of arrested suspects was another Warren Court ruling, so the Court was shaping social policy as well as checking the President.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.2] United States v Nixon (July 1974): the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Nixon must hand over the White House tapes, and his resignation followed on 9 August 1974.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.3] Earlier, in November 1973, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto to limit the President's power to commit troops without congressional approval, so Congress took back a power the President had been using.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.4] The Boland Amendments (1982 to 1984) restricted US aid to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua. Officials in the Reagan administration kept the Contras supplied in breach of them, which is the core of the Iran-Contra affair.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.b] From the 1930s the President's staff and agencies grew steadily, a development called the presidential bureaucracy. It meant that more policy was shaped inside the White House and its agencies rather than in Congress, which is why people began to worry about presidential power.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.0] The Executive Office of the President was created in 1939 under Roosevelt, giving the President a personal staff separate from the government departments, so the President no longer depended only on cabinet departments.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.1] The National Security Act of July 1947, signed by Truman, created the National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and the National Military Establishment. It was renamed the Department of Defense in 1949.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.2] The National Security Council gave the President a forum of advisers on foreign and defence policy, so decisions could be made without going through the State Department and Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.3] The Council of Economic Advisers was created by the Employment Act of 1946, which committed the federal government to promote maximum employment, production and purchasing power. This made the President responsible for the health of the economy.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.4] Public opinion, meaning what ordinary voters think, increasingly shaped presidents as polls and media grew. Kennedy held live televised press conferences from January 1961, using them as a direct channel to the public that bypassed newspapers and Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.5] Arthur Schlesinger Jr. described the expanded executive in The Imperial Presidency (1973). The phrase became the standard label for concern over presidential power after Watergate,, which also led Congress to reassert its oversight of presidents.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.b] Roosevelt led the US through the Depression and most of the Second World War, and his coalition and the wartime economy shaped the politics that Truman inherited in 1945. Roosevelt's New Deal (from 1933) had expanded federal power and welfare, and 'liberalism' in the US sense came to mean an active federal government promoting welfare and civil rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.0] Roosevelt won four presidential elections (1932, 1936, 1940 and 1944) and served from March 1933 until his death, so Americans had known no other president for years.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.1] The New Deal coalition combined urban workers, trade unions, Catholics and Jews, farmers, southern whites and black voters who moved to the Democrats from the 1930s. It gave the Democrats a majority, but it held together very different groups.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.2] Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945, and Harry Truman, vice president since January 1945, became president while the Second World War and the start of the Cold War were still in progress.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.3] Truman inherited a wave of strikes in 1945 and 1946 as the wartime economy was converted to peacetime production (reconversion), when workers wanted higher pay while prices were rising.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.4] Truman's party was divided between northern liberals and conservative southern Democrats. The southerners often voted with Republicans in Congress (the conservative coalition) to block reform, so a Democratic president could not rely on a Democratic Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.b] Truman was a participant in the domestic politics of his presidency, and he took over a divided party and a hostile Congress, so much of his programme was blocked. His popularity swung from an upset victory in 1948 to deep unpopularity by 1952.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.0] Born in Lamar, Missouri, in 1884, he worked as a farmer, a haberdasher (a men's clothing seller) and a county court official. He served as an artillery captain in France in the First World War.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.1] He owed his early career to Tom Pendergast's Kansas City Democratic machine, so he reached the Senate in 1934, taking his seat in 1935.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.2] The Truman Committee (1941 to 1944), a Senate committee he chaired, investigated waste and fraud in wartime production and gave him a reputation for careful oversight.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.3] As vice president from January 1945 he was not told of the atomic bomb project until he became president on 12 April 1945, so he relied heavily on advisers at first.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.4] In September 1945 he sent Congress a 21-point programme of reforms that continued the New Deal, but reconversion, inflation and strikes blocked most of it. In 1947 Congress overrode his veto of the Taft-Hartley Act.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.5] His civil rights programme began with the report To Secure These Rights (October 1947) from the President's Committee on Civil Rights. Executive Order 9981 (26 July 1948) then ordered the desegregation of the armed forces, which a president could do without Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.6] His loyalty programme (Executive Order 9835, March 1947) required checks on federal employees for disloyalty. It fed the wider Red Scare, the fear of communists in America, and is judged damaging to civil liberties.
[hist-eur6-s1.5.7] In January 1949 he outlined the Fair Deal: national health insurance, a wider Social Security system, a higher minimum wage and public housing. Congress blocked national health insurance and most other proposals; the Housing Act of 1949 and a rise in the minimum wage were among the main successes.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.b] Truman's own combative style won the 1948 election against the odds, but the Korean War, prices and scandal wore down his popularity. Contemporaries and historians have therefore judged him very differently.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.0] In 1948 he beat Thomas Dewey, 303 electoral votes to 189, even though the Democrats split three ways: Thurmond's Dixiecrats on the right and Henry Wallace's Progressives on the left.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.1] He ran a combative cross-country whistle-stop train campaign (speaking from the back of a train at small towns) attacking the Republican Congress as a do-nothing Congress, so the hostile Congress became his best argument.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.2] The Chicago Daily Tribune printed the headline "Dewey Defeats Truman" before the result was known, which is why the upset became famous.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.3] Contemporaries were often dismissive: the 1946 joke "To err is Truman" mocked his handling of strikes and prices, and his Gallup approval rating fell to about 22 per cent in early 1952.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.4] By 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply because of the Korean War, rising prices and corruption scandals in several federal agencies, and he announced in March 1952 that he would not seek re-election.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.5] Historians since the 1970s, and David McCullough's biography Truman (1992), have generally ranked him among the stronger presidents for his decisiveness and his civil rights initiatives.
[hist-eur6-s1.6.6] They also judge his domestic record more limited than he hoped and his loyalty programme damaging to civil liberties.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.b] After Truman, the Republican Eisenhower accepted the New Deal's main structures rather than undoing them, and Kennedy then promised a more active government. Both presidents faced the federal-state conflict over civil rights, and Kennedy's death left his bills to Johnson.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.0] Eisenhower, a Republican and former Allied commander in the Second World War, called his approach Modern Republicanism. He accepted most New Deal programmes rather than dismantling them, though he was not a New Dealer himself.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.1] He extended Social Security to about 10 million more workers in 1954 and created the Department of Health, Education and Welfare in 1953, so the New Deal's welfare structures grew under a Republican.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.2] The Federal-Aid Highway Act of June 1956 funded an Interstate Highway System of about 41,000 miles, the largest federal public works programme of the period, which shows a Republican using federal money for a national project.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.3] In his Farewell Address (January 1961) Eisenhower warned against the "military-industrial complex", the close link between the armed forces and the arms industry, which he feared could gain too much influence.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.4] Kennedy won the 1960 election narrowly over Richard Nixon, and his New Frontier, presented in his nomination speech of July 1960, promised a more active federal role at home and in space.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.5] Kennedy's tax cut was proposed in January 1963 and passed as the Revenue Act of February 1964 under Johnson, cutting the top income tax rate from 91 to 70 per cent. His civil rights bill was sent to Congress in June 1963 but only passed after his assassination.
[hist-eur6-s1.7.6] Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas on 22 November 1963; Lyndon Johnson was sworn in as president on Air Force One that afternoon, so the same day saw both the end of one presidency and the start of the next.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.b] Johnson's domestic programme was the largest federal social effort since the New Deal. He used Kennedy's death and his own legislative skill to pass a flood of laws in 1964 and 1965, until the Vietnam War began to drain money and attention from it.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.0] Johnson, Senate Majority Leader from 1955 to 1961, used his legislative skills (the persuasive "Johnson treatment") and the national mood after Kennedy's death to pass Kennedy's stalled bills.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.1] The Civil Rights Act of 2 July 1964 banned segregation in public places and discrimination in employment on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.2] In a speech at the University of Michigan on 22 May 1964, Johnson set out the Great Society, a programme to end poverty and racial injustice.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.3] The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 launched the War on Poverty, which included Job Corps (job training for young people) and community action programmes.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.4] In the 1964 election Johnson won about 61 per cent of the popular vote against Barry Goldwater, who carried only six states, giving him a huge majority in Congress to pass further laws.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.5] The Voting Rights Act of 6 August 1965 suspended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters in areas where discrimination was proven. Medicare for the elderly and Medicaid for the poor were signed into law on 30 July 1965.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.6] The Elementary and Secondary Education Act (April 1965) and the Higher Education Act (1965) provided federal aid to schools and colleges, and Head Start for poor pre-school children began in 1965. The Immigration and Nationality Act (1965) ended national-origin quotas.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.7] The Department of Housing and Urban Development was created in 1965, giving the federal government a cabinet department for cities and housing.
[hist-eur6-s1.8.8] The cost of the Vietnam War from 1965 drained money and attention from the Great Society, and Republican gains in the 1966 midterms slowed new legislation.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.b] Between 1969 and 1981 three presidents faced scandal, recession and inflation, and the public's trust in the presidency fell. Watergate was the turning point, because it forced a resignation and showed Congress and the Court could check a President.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.0] Richard Nixon won the 1968 election narrowly and was re-elected in 1972 by carrying 49 states, so his fall in 1974 came soon after a landslide.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.1] Nixon's domestic record included the Environmental Protection Agency (1970), wage and price controls (August 1971) to fight inflation, and the expansion of affirmative action (favouring minorities) in federal contracts.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.2] Five men were arrested for burgling the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate complex on 17 June 1972, and the Washington Post's reporting kept the scandal in the news. Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974, after the Supreme Court ordered him to hand over his tapes.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.3] Gerald Ford, who had become vice president in December 1973 after Spiro Agnew resigned, became president and pardoned Nixon on 8 September 1974. The pardon damaged his standing, and with inflation and recession in 1974 to 1975 he lost narrowly to Carter in 1976.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.4] Jimmy Carter, a Democrat from Georgia, won the 1976 election and negotiated the Panama Canal treaties of 1977, under which the canal passed to Panama at the end of 1999.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.5] The Camp David Accords of September 1978, brokered by Carter, led to a peace treaty between Egypt and Israel signed in March 1979. Carter also created the Departments of Energy (1977) and Education (1979).
[hist-eur6-s1.9.6] High inflation and the 1979 oil shock undermined Carter. His televised speech of July 1979 on a "crisis of confidence", nicknamed the malaise speech, was seen by critics as blaming the public.
[hist-eur6-s1.9.7] The Iran hostage crisis held 52 American diplomats and citizens in Iran from 4 November 1979 to 20 January 1981, 444 days. An attempted rescue in April 1980 failed, and the crisis was a major cause of Carter's defeat.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.b] Ronald Reagan won in 1980 promising smaller government and tax cuts, reversing the liberal direction of the New Deal and Great Society. His first term set the conservative agenda, and his second was clouded by Iran-Contra.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.0] Ronald Reagan defeated Carter in 1980 and was re-elected in 1984, carrying 49 states against Walter Mondale, which shows how popular he stayed.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.1] In his first inaugural address (20 January 1981) Reagan said that "government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem", which summed up his case against federal power.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.2] The Economic Recovery Tax Act of August 1981 cut income tax rates by about 25 per cent over three years and lowered the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.3] Reagan's first term combined cuts to many domestic social programmes with a large rise in defence spending, a pattern known as Reaganomics.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.4] When air traffic controllers in the PATCO union struck in August 1981, Reagan dismissed the strikers, signalling a hard line on public sector unions, because the strikers worked for the federal government.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.5] Iran-Contra was revealed in November 1986: the administration had sold arms to Iran and diverted the money to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua despite congressional restrictions, the Boland Amendments.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.6] The Tower Commission (February 1987) criticised Reagan's detached management style. National Security Adviser John Poindexter and his aide Oliver North were among those prosecuted.
[hist-eur6-s1.10.7] Reagan's popularity survived the scandal and he left office in January 1989 with high approval ratings, earning the nickname "the Teflon president" because scandals did not stick to him.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.b] Judging presidents means weighing what each achieved against where each failed, and contemporary verdicts often differ from later ones. A good answer gives a stated judgement with dated evidence on each side.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.0] Truman: his civil rights initiative, including desegregating the armed forces, and his leadership in the early Cold War (the Marshall Plan and NATO) are usually seen as successes.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.1] Truman's critics point to the failure to pass national health insurance, because a hostile Congress blocked it, and to the unpopularity of the Korean War.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.2] Johnson: the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts and Medicare are counted as major achievements, while his Vietnam policy is widely judged a failure that drained the Great Society.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.3] Nixon and Ford: Nixon's opening to China and his arms control agreements are credited as achievements, but Watergate and the secret bombing of Cambodia are judged serious failings, and Ford's pardon of Nixon cost him public trust.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.4] Yet the Court's ruling and the War Powers Resolution show that Congress and the Court acted as effective checks on the President.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.5] Carter: the Camp David Accords are judged a success, while the Iran hostage crisis, inflation and his 1980 defeat are the main charges against him.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.6] Reagan: supporters credit his tax cuts and conservative revival, and they claim he helped to end the Cold War, which is their view and not settled fact. Critics point to the rising deficit, the 1981 to 1982 recession and Iran-Contra.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.7] Public opinion polls made presidential popularity visible from the 1930s onwards, and the Watergate and Iran-Contra scandals led Congress to reassert its oversight of presidents.
[hist-eur6-s1.11.8] Contemporary verdicts often differed from later ones: Truman left office in 1953 deeply unpopular, yet his reputation rose sharply in later historians' rankings, as did Eisenhower's, while Johnson's soared in 1964 and collapsed by 1968.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s4: 4. Domestic factors in US foreign policy: McCarthyism, the anti-war movement and race relations
[hist-eur6-s4.0.b] US foreign policy was not made in a vacuum: presidents had to carry voters, Congress and the press with them. After 1945, fear of communism abroad turned into fear of subversion (secret undermining of the government from within) at home, and later protest and racial injustice put pressure on policy too.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.0] Fear of subversion grew out of Cold War events: the Truman Doctrine of March 1947 (a pledge to resist communist expansion, called containment) and the Soviet atomic test of August 1949.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.1] The communist victory in China (People's Republic proclaimed 1 October 1949) and the Korean War (from 25 June 1950) added to the fear, so Americans began to suspect spies and traitors at home.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.2] Congressional committees could summon witnesses and demand answers. The Fifth Amendment of the Constitution allowed a witness to refuse to answer questions that might incriminate them. The Senate and the House of Representatives are separate chambers, each running its own investigations.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.3] Segregation in the South, known as Jim Crow, was a system of laws keeping Black and white Americans apart in schools, transport and public places. Black Americans were fighting it in the Black freedom struggle, and the Vietnam War escalated under President Johnson from 1965.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.4] Public opinion means what ordinary Americans thought, as measured by polls and the press. It set limits on what a president could do abroad, because a policy that voters rejected cost votes and support in Congress.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.5] Mass media means newspapers, radio and above all television. Television carried McCarthy, the Vietnam War and the civil rights struggle into homes across the country, so that millions saw events at the same moment.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.6] Liberalism, in US usage, means support for civil liberties (such as free speech), civil rights and an active federal government. McCarthyism attacked it, while civil rights and anti-war campaigners defended it.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.7] McCarthyism means reckless accusation of communist subversion, made without proof. The Red Scare is the wider wave of fear of communists that began before McCarthy and outlasted him.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.b] The panic about communists did not start with Joseph McCarthy. By 1947 the government and Congress had already set up machinery to hunt for disloyal people, and Hollywood was an early target.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.0] Truman's loyalty programme (Executive Order 9835, March 1947) set up loyalty review boards to investigate federal employees, and the Attorney General kept a list of 'subversive' organisations. Many federal employees feared losing their jobs over any past political association.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.1] The House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) held hearings on communist influence in Hollywood in October 1947. The Hollywood Ten, writers and directors including Dalton Trumbo, refused to say whether they were communists, and Congress cited them for contempt in November 1947.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.2] The Ten were convicted of contempt of Congress in 1948. Their appeals failed, so they served prison terms of up to a year from 1950. This mattered because it showed that refusing to answer a congressional committee could end in prison.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.3] The studio heads, meeting at the Waldorf-Astoria hotel in New York in November 1947, issued the Waldorf Statement. They promised not to employ the Ten or anyone suspected of communism, and this created the Hollywood blacklist (a secret ban on employing named people).
[hist-eur6-s4.1.4] The blacklist ran for about a decade, and it began to break in 1960 when Trumbo was credited for a film. This shows how long a fear of association could shut people out of their careers.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.5] HUAC (House, from 1938, Hollywood hearings in 1947) must not be confused with McCarthy's later Senate subcommittee. McCarthy was never a member of HUAC, and his own investigations did not concern Hollywood.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.b] Real and alleged spy cases between 1948 and 1953 convinced many Americans that traitors were at work in government. Laws and prosecutions followed, and the fear spread into everyday working life.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.0] Whittaker Chambers, an ex-communist who had been a courier and later a Time editor, accused Alger Hiss, a former State Department official, before HUAC in August 1948. Chambers said Hiss was a communist who had passed documents to him; Hiss denied it.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.1] A time-bar (a legal limit on how long after an act charges may be brought) prevented a spy charge, so Hiss was tried for perjury. The first trial in 1949 ended in a hung jury; he was convicted in January 1950 and served about three and a half years.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.2] Richard Nixon, a California congressman on HUAC, pressed the Hiss case, and it made his national name. Historians still debate Hiss's guilt, though most think he was guilty. The case was highly publicised.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.3] Klaus Fuchs, a German-born British physicist who had worked on the Manhattan Project (the wartime atomic bomb programme), confessed in January 1950 and was arrested in February 1950. His confession led to the Rosenbergs' ring and so strengthened fears of atomic spies.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.4] Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were arrested in 1950 and convicted in 1951 of conspiracy to commit espionage, for passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union. They were executed at Sing Sing on 19 June 1953.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.5] Eleven Communist Party leaders were tried in 1949 under the Smith Act (1940), and the Supreme Court upheld their convictions in Dennis v. United States (1951). The McCarran Internal Security Act of September 1950 forced Communist organisations to register, and Congress overrode Truman's veto of it.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.6] The Red Scare reached teachers, trade unionists and entertainers. Loyalty oaths were widely required of public employees, including teachers, and many people lost jobs for refusing to name former associates, so dissent itself became dangerous.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.b] McCarthy was a little-known senator until early 1950. He then seized on a moment when Americans were frightened, and he made accusation his political weapon.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.0] On 9 February 1950, in a Lincoln Day speech to a Republican women's club at Wheeling, West Virginia, McCarthy claimed to hold a list of communists working in the State Department. The numbers he gave later varied, and the speech text is disputed.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.1] The speech became a national sensation because of the timing: the Hiss conviction in January 1950, the arrest of Fuchs, the Soviet bomb and the fall of China had left Americans ready to believe in traitors.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.2] The Tydings Committee, a Senate subcommittee chaired by Millard Tydings, reported in July 1950 and called the charges a fraud and a hoax. This was the committee's verdict. Tydings then lost his Maryland seat in November 1950 after a campaign in which McCarthy's allies attacked him.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.3] McCarthy's methods were guilt by association (damning people for the company they had kept), accusations without evidence, unnamed lists, shifting numbers, bullying of witnesses and personal smears. The cartoonist Herblock (Herbert Block of the Washington Post) coined the word McCarthyism in March 1950.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.4] In June 1950 Margaret Chase Smith, a Republican senator from Maine, delivered her Declaration of Conscience. She criticised smear tactics without naming McCarthy, showing that some in his own party were uneasy. This mattered because criticism from inside his party was harder to dismiss.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.5] In 1951 McCarthy attacked General George Marshall, a respected wartime commander. Eisenhower, campaigning in 1952, did not defend Marshall in McCarthy's presence in Wisconsin, a sign of how much Republicans feared to cross him. This is an example of how far fear of McCarthy reached.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.b] The Republican victory of 1952 gave McCarthy an official platform, but the same publicity that built him also destroyed him once television and Senate colleagues turned on him.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.0] Republicans won the presidency and Congress in 1952. From January 1953 McCarthy chaired the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, which gave him the power to summon officials and hold hearings.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.1] In 1953 he investigated the Voice of America (the government's overseas radio service) and the State Department's overseas libraries. His aides Roy Cohn, chief counsel, and G. David Schine toured State Department libraries in Europe checking them.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.2] On 9 March 1954 Edward R. Murrow's CBS programme See It Now used McCarthy's own speeches and film to criticise him. It was effective because viewers saw his methods in his own words. This is why it hurt him more than press criticism.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.3] McCarthy then turned his inquiries on the army, partly over the treatment of Schine, who had been drafted. The resulting Army-McCarthy hearings, televised from 22 April to 17 June 1954, showed him to a national audience as a bully.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.4] On 9 June 1954 Joseph Welch, the army's counsel, rebuked McCarthy for attacking a young lawyer in Welch's firm, asking 'Have you no sense of decency, sir?' This became a turning point in public opinion against him.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.5] On 2 December 1954 the Senate voted 67 to 22 to censure him, for conduct that tended to bring the Senate into dishonour, or loosely conduct unbecoming a senator. A committee, the Watkins Committee, had recommended the censure, and after the vote his influence vanished.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.b] McCarthy was a direct participant who used the Red Scare to build a national following, and he was also shaped by the events around him. Contemporaries and historians have judged him very differently.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.0] Joseph McCarthy was born on 14 November 1908 in Grand Chute, Wisconsin, studied law at Marquette University and became a circuit judge in 1939. He served in the Marines from 1942 to 1945, later calling himself 'Tail-Gunner Joe', a claim that was exaggerated.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.1] He won the Republican primary against the incumbent Robert La Follette Jr and was elected to the Senate in 1946 (he took his seat in January 1947). He was re-elected in 1952. This is why he could claim a mandate from Wisconsin voters.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.2] As a participant he made the Wheeling speech of February 1950 and the investigations of 1953 to 1954, chairing the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations. The Red Scare existed before him, and he rode it, exploiting the Hiss conviction, the fall of China, the Soviet bomb, Fuchs and Korea.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.3] His fall was also shaped by events. Television exposure, the Army-McCarthy hearings, the turning of Senate colleagues and Eisenhower's refusal to help him brought him down, and the term McCarthyism outlived him as a word for smear and witch-hunt.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.4] McCarthy died on 2 May 1957, aged 48, of acute hepatitis linked to heavy drinking. His early death at 48 ended his career as well as his influence.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.5] Contemporaries split. Many Republicans and conservatives, and much of the Catholic and Midwest public, backed him, while liberals, most of the press and many Senate colleagues denounced his methods as reckless smears.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.6] Historians since Richard Rovere (Senator Joe McCarthy, 1959) have judged him a demagogue (a leader who stirs up fear for personal gain).
[hist-eur6-s4.5.7] Ellen Schrecker (Many Are the Crimes, 1998) argues that McCarthyism was a wider system, including the FBI under J. Edgar Hoover, HUAC and both parties, which he exploited rather than created, so removing him did not end the Red Scare.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.8] M. Stanton Evans (Blacklisted by History, 2007) offered a minority defence of him. The Venona decrypts (Soviet cables made public from 1995) show a number of Soviet agents in the US government, but they proved real Soviet spying without vindicating McCarthy's methods or lists.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.b] When the Republicans took office in 1953, the loyalty rules of Truman were replaced, not abandoned. The new president kept the anti-communist climate in place and avoided a public fight with McCarthy.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.0] Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10450 in April 1953. It replaced Truman's loyalty standard with 'security risk' criteria for federal employment, so many dismissals followed.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.1] Under the new criteria, past membership in suspect organisations could count against a person as well as proven disloyalty, which made it easy to dismiss employees on suspicion. For example, an old membership could cost someone a federal post.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.2] Eisenhower avoided naming McCarthy in public for most of 1953 and 1954, and kept his criticism private or indirect. This public caution meant the charges spread until the Army hearings of 1954.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.3] Richard Nixon, Eisenhower's vice president, had built his national reputation on the Hiss investigation. This made anti-communism a bipartisan position (shared by both parties) in 1950s politics.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.b] The climate of suspicion changed what officials said and did about China, the Soviet Union and Asia, because anyone who looked soft on communism risked a political attack.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.0] Republicans charged from late 1949 that Truman and the State Department had lost China, after the communist victory. McCarthy's attacks on State Department officials amplified the charge.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.1] Owen Lattimore, a Johns Hopkins China scholar, was accused by McCarthy in March 1950 of being a leading Soviet agent. He was never charged with espionage; he was indicted for perjury in 1952, and the charges were dropped in 1955.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.2] State Department China specialists John Paton Davies and John S. Service were removed from their posts: Service was dismissed in 1951 and Davies in 1954. This weakened the government's expert knowledge of Asia.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.3] The campaign made it politically risky for Democrats to show any willingness to negotiate with communist states, which constrained diplomacy in the Korean and Chinese crises.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.4] John Foster Dulles was secretary of state from 1953 to 1959, and his department, with security chief Scott McLeod, applied the new security standards. The climate pressed the administration to avoid any open conflict with McCarthy.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.5] Soviet propaganda and allied critics used McCarthyism, and images of racial violence and discrimination such as the Little Rock crisis of 1957, to argue that American democracy was a sham, which damaged America's image abroad.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.b] Opposition to the Vietnam War started on university campuses in 1965 and grew into a mass movement. Students had a personal stake because the draft could send them to fight.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.0] The first major teach-in (an all-night meeting of lectures and debate) was held at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, on 24 and 25 March 1965. Several thousand attended, and teach-ins spread to many campuses in the following weeks.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.1] Students for a Democratic Society (SDS) emerged in 1960 and was famous for the Port Huron Statement of 1962, largely written by Tom Hayden. Its Washington march of April 1965 drew about 15,000 to 25,000 people.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.2] By 1969 SDS had split, with the Weathermen emerging from it. This fragmentation shows that the movement was divided as well as large.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.3] The Selective Service System (the draft) called up men aged 18 to 26. Student deferments favoured the better-off, so poorer and Black men were more likely to be sent to Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.4] Many young men resisted by burning draft cards or going to Canada. The first draft lottery, which selected men for call-up by birthdate, was held on 1 December 1969 and made young men more aware of the war's impact on their lives.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.5] Muhammad Ali, formerly Cassius Clay, refused induction in Houston on 28 April 1967, citing his religious beliefs as a Nation of Islam minister. He was stripped of the world title and boxing licence.
[hist-eur6-s4.8.6] He was convicted of draft evasion in June 1967 (five years and a fine; he stayed free on appeal), and the Supreme Court overturned the conviction in June 1971. As a famous champion, his refusal made the draft a matter of conscience for a mass audience.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.b] From 1967 protest moved from the campuses into churches, the streets and the national conventions. Television brought each event to a mass audience, which made it hard for the government to ignore.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.0] On 4 April 1967, at Riverside Church in New York, Martin Luther King gave his 'Beyond Vietnam' speech. He called the US government 'the greatest purveyor of violence in the world today', and argued that the war drained resources from the war on poverty.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.1] King was criticised by some allies and by the press for linking civil rights to the war, but his speech linked civil rights to peace.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.2] The March on the Pentagon on 21 October 1967 drew tens of thousands and many arrests. Norman Mailer took part and wrote The Armies of the Night (1968), which won the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.3] The Democratic National Convention met in Chicago from 26 to 29 August 1968. Police and the National Guard clashed with protesters in Grant Park and on Michigan Avenue, under Mayor Richard Daley, in front of television cameras.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.4] The protesters chanted that the whole world was watching, a chant that was televised. Hubert Humphrey was nominated.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.5] The Vietnam Moratorium of 15 October 1969 brought demonstrations across the country. A separate large march followed in Washington on 15 November 1969, and it must not be called a Moratorium.
[hist-eur6-s4.9.6] Nixon replied with a televised speech on 3 November 1969, appealing to the silent majority of Americans who did not protest. This shows that many Americans disapproved of the protesters.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.b] In 1970 and 1971 anger over the widening war led to deaths on campuses and a leak that damaged the government's case. Together they pressed Nixon to bring troops home.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.0] Nixon announced the Cambodian incursion on 30 April 1970, and campus protests and a student strike followed. This is why the war's widening, not only its length, sparked protest.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.1] On 4 May 1970 Ohio National Guardsmen fired on students at Kent State University, killing four and wounding nine. At Jackson State in Mississippi, on 15 May 1970, police killed two. Kent State was in Ohio and Jackson State in Mississippi.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.2] The Pentagon Papers were a secret Defense Department history of US involvement in Vietnam, leaked by Daniel Ellsberg. The New York Times began publishing on 13 June 1971, the government obtained a halt, and the Supreme Court ruled for the press on 30 June 1971.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.3] Congress repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, the August 1964 vote that had given the president wide war powers, in January 1971, and in 1973 it cut off funds for US combat in Indochina, so Congress itself began to act against the war.
[hist-eur6-s4.10.4] Protest and public opinion put pressure on Nixon to withdraw troops and to Vietnamise the war (hand more fighting to South Vietnamese forces). Johnson's decision of March 1968 not to seek re-election was also a response to public opinion.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.b] Racial discrimination at home was a serious problem for US foreign policy, because it weakened America's claim to lead the free world. The audience that mattered was the newly independent states of Asia and Africa.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.0] President Truman's Executive Order 9981, of 26 July 1948, ordered equality of treatment in the armed services. It was partly motivated by the need to answer communist criticism of US racism; Truman also faced the 1948 election and the Black vote. Full integration took years, until the Korean War.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.1] In Brown v. Board of Education, on 17 May 1954, the Supreme Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional. The ruling was reported abroad as evidence of progress, though desegregation was slow in the South.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.2] The Justice Department's brief in Brown urged the Court to remember the effect abroad, since segregation was a Cold War embarrassment, a point Mary Dudziak later stressed.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.3] At Little Rock Central High School in September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus resisted the entry of nine Black students. Eisenhower sent federal troops (the 101st Airborne) to enforce it, and Soviet media used the images around the world.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.4] The Freedom Rides of May 1961 (buses attacked in Alabama) and the Birmingham campaign of spring 1963 (police dogs and fire hoses used on children, widely televised) were international news. They undermined the US claim to be a model of democracy.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.5] Soviet and Chinese propaganda broadcast stories of segregation and racial violence to the Third World, which is why US diplomats argued that events like Little Rock damaged the country's standing in newly independent Asian and African states.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.6] Black leaders linked race to foreign policy. King tied the cost of the war in Vietnam to the funds needed for the poor. Malcolm X toured Africa in 1964 and argued that the American struggle was an international human rights matter.
[hist-eur6-s4.11.7] The Civil Rights Act of July 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of August 1965 were landmark reforms. Historians such as Mary Dudziak (Cold War Civil Rights, 2000) argue that foreign opinion was one factor, though not the main one.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.b] Judgements that weigh the pressure of domestic fears, protest and race against the Cold War and strategic aims earn the most credit. Each force had real effects and clear limits.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.0] McCarthyism was the strongest constraint in the early 1950s: it damaged civil liberties, weakened expert advice on China and Asia, and made negotiation with communists risky. But the Red Scare predated McCarthy and outlasted him, and some Soviet spying was real, as the Venona decrypts show.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.1] The anti-war movement shaped public opinion, Johnson's decision of 1968 and the pace of Nixon's withdrawals. But it was divided, often unpopular with the silent majority, and the war continued for years after Johnson's decision.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.2] Historians disagree about whether protests changed the outcome of the war or simply sharpened a decision the government had already reached.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.3] Race relations were an embarrassment that pushed leaders towards reform, because Soviet propaganda used Little Rock, and the Birmingham campaign was also international news. But reform was driven mainly by the Black freedom movement itself and by domestic politics.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.4] Overall, domestic opinion limited and shaped policy, but strategic and Cold War considerations stayed the main drivers of US foreign policy.
[hist-eur6-s4.12.5] Contemporaries often saw McCarthy and the protesters through party and class lines. Later historians such as Rovere and Schrecker judge McCarthyism harshly, while Evans defends it using Venona, so the verdict depends on the view taken.
