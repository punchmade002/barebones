TARGET hist-eur4-s11: 11. Case study: the Second Vatican Council
[hist-eur4-s11.0.b] Pope John XXIII called the Council to renew the Catholic Church and bring it into closer contact with the modern world.
[hist-eur4-s11.0.0] Angelo Roncalli, aged 76, was elected Pope John XXIII on 28 October 1958, and he was regarded as a caretaker pope when he was chosen.
[hist-eur4-s11.0.1] He announced the Council on 25 January 1959, and he used the term aggiornamento, meaning updating, to describe its aim of renewal.
[hist-eur4-s11.0.2] The Church faced declining practice in Western Europe, the spread of secularisation and the division between Catholics and other Christians.
[hist-eur4-s11.0.3] The Council was intended to deal with the relationship between the Church and the modern world, the liturgy, and Christian unity.
[hist-eur4-s11.0.4] Pius XII (pope 1939–58) had centralised authority in Rome, and by the 1950s the Church faced pressure from secularisation, the loss of the working class in industrial Europe and the demands of theologians for renewal.
[hist-eur4-s11.0.5] John XXIII wanted a pastoral Council, to renew the Church's teaching and practice rather than to condemn errors.
[hist-eur4-s11.0.6] He also hoped the Council would help the Church speak to ordinary people in a world of nuclear weapons, television and rapid social change, and bring non-Catholic Christians closer to Rome.
[hist-eur4-s11.0.7] Preparations from 1960 were carried out by commissions dominated by Roman Curia officials, including Cardinal Ottaviani, who expected a short Council to approve prepared drafts.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.b] The Council met in four sessions in Rome, and its work was carried on after John XXIII's death in June 1963 by Pope Paul VI.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.0] The Council opened on 11 October 1962 in St Peter's Basilica, with about 2,500 bishops attending, along with observers from other Christian churches.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.1] John XXIII died on 3 June 1963, and Cardinal Giovanni Battista Montini was elected Pope Paul VI on 21 June 1963.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.2] Paul VI kept the Council open and oversaw its completion, and it closed on 8 December 1965.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.3] Joseph Ratzinger, later Pope Benedict XVI, attended as a theological adviser, and the Council drew on the expertise of many theologians.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.4] At the first session (October to December 1962) the bishops rejected the prepared drafts and elected their own commission members, led by progressive cardinals such as Suenens of Belgium, Frings of Germany and Léger of Canada; this gave the Council its own direction.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.5] The second session (September to December 1963) debated the nature of the Church and collegiality, the idea that the bishops share authority with the pope.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.6] The third session (autumn 1964) approved Lumen Gentium and the decree on ecumenism, though a conservative minority and last-minute papal changes caused tension in a week known as Black Week.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.7] The fourth session (autumn 1965) approved the declaration on religious liberty, Nostra Aetate and Gaudium et Spes; Paul VI also set up the Synod of Bishops in 1965 to continue consultation.
[hist-eur4-s11.1.8] Observers from Orthodox, Anglican and Protestant churches attended the sessions, and the proceedings were widely covered by the press, radio and television.
[hist-eur4-s11.2.b] The Council issued four constitutions and several declarations and decrees, and the constitutions set out its main teaching.
[hist-eur4-s11.2.0] Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963) reformed the liturgy and allowed the use of local languages in the Mass.
[hist-eur4-s11.2.1] Lumen Gentium (1964) described the Church as the people of God, and it gave a larger role to the laity and to bishops collectively.
[hist-eur4-s11.2.2] Dei Verbum (1965) addressed revelation and Scripture, and it encouraged the study of the Bible by Catholics.
[hist-eur4-s11.2.3] Gaudium et Spes (1965) addressed the Church in the modern world, including economic life, marriage, peace and social justice.
[hist-eur4-s11.2.4] Unitatis Redintegratio (1964) committed the Church to ecumenism, the search for unity with other Christian churches.
[hist-eur4-s11.2.5] Nostra Aetate (1965) addressed non-Christian religions and rejected the charge of collective Jewish guilt for the death of Christ.
[hist-eur4-s11.2.6] Dignitatis Humanae (1965) affirmed religious liberty, the right of people to follow their conscience in religion.
[hist-eur4-s11.3.b] The Council changed Catholic worship and relations with other faiths, and these changes were the most visible results.
[hist-eur4-s11.3.0] Changes to the Mass began in 1964, when local languages were allowed in parts of it, and the new Roman Missal of 1969–70 completed the reform, so that Mass was said in the vernacular and the priest faced the congregation.
[hist-eur4-s11.3.1] Lay Catholics were encouraged to take a more active part in worship, reading the Scriptures and serving as ministers.
[hist-eur4-s11.3.2] The Church began formal dialogue with other Christian churches, and observers from other churches attended the sessions.
[hist-eur4-s11.3.3] Relations with Judaism changed, and Nostra Aetate became the basis for improved Catholic–Jewish dialogue.
[hist-eur4-s11.3.4] Pope John Paul II visited the Great Synagogue of Rome on 13 April 1986, the first pope in modern times to enter a synagogue, and he called Jews the elder brothers of Catholics.
[hist-eur4-s11.3.5] Priests and religious orders were asked to return to their founders' ideals and to adapt their dress and rules, and many nuns changed their habits.
[hist-eur4-s11.3.6] In December 1965 Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras of Constantinople lifted the mutual excommunications of 1054 between the Catholic and Orthodox churches (see the notes on the ecumenical movement).
[hist-eur4-s11.3.7] The Council also accepted the principle of religious liberty, that the state should not coerce people in matters of faith, which reversed the earlier teaching and ended the ideal of the Catholic confessional state.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.b] The Council divided Catholics, and its reforms produced a traditionalist reaction as well as progressive demands for further change.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.0] Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre, a French bishop who had led opposition at the Council, founded the Society of St Pius X in 1970, which rejected the Council's teaching on religious liberty and the new liturgy; he was excommunicated in 1988 after consecrating four bishops without papal approval.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.1] Progressive Catholics argued that the Council had not gone far enough on the role of women, on collegiality and on sexual ethics.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.2] Paul VI's encyclical Humanae Vitae of 25 July 1968 reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception, and it was widely rejected by Catholics in the West.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.3] Many Catholics in Western Europe continued to attend Mass less frequently after the 1960s, a trend that historians link to the wider secularisation of society.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.4] A papal commission on birth control reported in 1966 with a majority favouring a relaxation of the ban after the introduction of the pill, so the encyclical's confirmation of the ban in 1968 came as a shock to many Catholics.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.5] Many theologians and bishops' conferences in the Netherlands, Belgium, West Germany and elsewhere publicly voiced dissent, and in practice many Catholic couples used contraception.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.6] Humanae Vitae is often seen as a turning point because it opened a gap between the Church's teaching and the practice of lay Catholics, and weakened the authority of the hierarchy.
[hist-eur4-s11.4.7] The Dutch Church, which held a pastoral council in 1968–70, was seen as the most progressive in Europe, and Rome moved to restore control under Paul VI and John Paul II.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.b] Karol Wojtyła, a Polish bishop who had taken part in the Council, became pope in 1978 and led the Church for almost three decades.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.0] Born in Wadowice in 1920, Wojtyła was made a bishop in Kraków in 1958 and archbishop in 1964, and he took part in the Second Vatican Council, where he contributed to the drafting of Gaudium et Spes.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.1] He was elected pope on 16 October 1978, and he was the first non-Italian pope since 1523.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.2] He travelled widely as pope, and his visits to Poland in 1979 and later were occasions when the Church and the national identity of Poland were linked.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.3] He was shot on 13 May 1981 in St Peter's Square by Mehmet Ali Ağca and visited his attacker in prison in December 1983.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.4] He reaffirmed traditional Catholic teaching on contraception, abortion and the priesthood, which contemporary critics and supporters saw as a conservative turn after the Council.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.5] Historians disagree about his legacy: supporters see him as a defender of human dignity, while critics argue that he resisted reform and handled abuse scandals badly.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.6] Contemporaries in the communist states saw him as a challenge to their authority, and his support for Solidarity made him a political figure.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.7] He travelled more than any previous pope, making over a hundred foreign journeys, and drew huge crowds in Poland, Latin America, Africa and Europe.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.8] He visited Ireland from 29 September to 1 October 1979, the first visit by a pope, saying Mass before about a million people in the Phoenix Park, Dublin, and visiting Drogheda, Galway, Knock and Limerick; at Drogheda he appealed to paramilitaries to end violence in Northern Ireland.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.9] He opposed the ordination of women, liberation theology, and a change to the discipline of clerical celibacy, and under his authority the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, led by Cardinal Ratzinger from 1981, disciplined theologians.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.10] He launched World Youth Days in the mid-1980s and promoted new movements such as Opus Dei and the Neocatechumenal Way, and he apologised for historical wrongs of Christians.
[hist-eur4-s11.5.11] He met Gorbachev at the Vatican on 1 December 1989, a sign that the Church had outlasted the communist regimes; he died in April 2005.
[hist-eur4-s11.6.b] The effects of the Council on practice are debated because it coincided with wider social change.
[hist-eur4-s11.6.0] Influence on religious practice: Mass attendance fell steeply in the 1960s and 1970s in Western Europe, though the same decline occurred among Protestants who had no Council, which suggests social causes.
[hist-eur4-s11.6.1] In Ireland practice stayed high into the 1980s, but the number of vocations and Mass attendance began to fall, and the authority of the Church was shaken later.
[hist-eur4-s11.6.2] Vocations to the priesthood and religious life fell in the 1970s, as many priests and nuns left their orders in the years after the Council and fewer young people entered.
[hist-eur4-s11.6.3] Weekly Mass attendance in Western Europe's Catholic countries fell: in France and the Netherlands it dropped by half or more by the 1980s, while Poland and Ireland remained exceptions.
[hist-eur4-s11.6.4] The Council did lead to increased lay involvement, in parish councils, readers and Eucharistic ministers, and to a stronger Catholic interest in social justice and in peace.
[hist-eur4-s11.7.b] Historians differ on whether Vatican II was a successful renewal or a cause of decline, and the judgement depends on which measure is used.
[hist-eur4-s11.7.0] Achievements: the vernacular liturgy, the new relations with other faiths, and a more open style of Church leadership.
[hist-eur4-s11.7.1] Limits: falling practice, declining vocations to the priesthood and the conflict between traditionalists and progressives that continued after 1965.
[hist-eur4-s11.7.2] Contemporaries in the Church were divided, with some seeing the Council as a new beginning and others as a betrayal of tradition.
[hist-eur4-s11.7.3] Later historians stress that the changes in religious practice were part of wider social trends, and that the Council's effects differed between countries.
[hist-eur4-s11.7.4] Traditionalists blame the Council for decline; progressives argue that the Church failed to implement its reforms, especially on the role of women and collegiality.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s4: 4. Reform movements in Eastern Europe, the collapse of the Soviet Union, and fragmentation and realignment, 1953–1992
[hist-eur4-s4.0.b] Gorbachev was the Soviet leader whose reforms opened the way to the end of communist rule in eastern Europe and to the break-up of the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.0] Born in 1931 in Privolnoye in the Stavropol region, he studied law at Moscow State University, rose through the Communist Party in Stavropol, and became a full member of the Politburo in 1980.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.1] He became General Secretary of the Communist Party on 11 March 1985, and in 1988 he became chairman of the Supreme Soviet, and in 1990 the first executive president of the USSR.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.2] He pursued glasnost, which meant greater openness and criticism of the past, and perestroika, a restructuring of the economy that tried to combine market elements with planning.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.3] He was a participant who chose not to use force in eastern Europe, and he withdrew Soviet troops from Afghanistan by February 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.4] Contemporaries in the West welcomed him: Margaret Thatcher, before he came to power, judged that he was a man she could do business with, and Reagan negotiated arms agreements with him.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.5] Conservatives at home regarded him as having destroyed the Soviet system, while many Russians later blamed him for economic collapse.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.6] Historians differ on whether he intended to end communism or to save it, and most agree that his reforms had consequences he did not foresee.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.7] Gorbachev rose under the patronage of Yuri Andropov, and as General Secretary he drew on a generation of younger reformers, including Alexander Yakovlev and Eduard Shevardnadze (foreign minister from 1985).
[hist-eur4-s4.0.8] His early slogan was uskorenie, acceleration of economic growth; when it failed he moved to perestroika and, at the 27th Party Congress in February 1986, to openness.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.9] Democratisation: in 1989 the first partly free elections to the Congress of People's Deputies were held in March, and in 1990 the party gave up its constitutional monopoly of power.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.10] He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1990 for his role in ending the Cold War.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.b] Karol Wojtyła, elected pope in 1978, supported the Polish opposition to communism and became a political figure as well as a religious leader.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.0] Born in Wadowice in 1920, he was Archbishop of Kraków and became a cardinal in 1967 before being elected pope on 16 October 1978, the first non-Italian pope since 1523.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.1] His visit to Poland from 2 to 10 June 1979 drew very large crowds, and it encouraged Poles to organise independently of the state.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.2] He met Lech Wałęsa during his 1983 visit to Poland and gave moral support to Solidarity, which the Polish authorities tried to suppress.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.3] He was shot in St Peter's Square on 13 May 1981 by Mehmet Ali Ağca, and he attributed his survival to divine intervention.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.4] Contemporaries in the Polish and Soviet governments saw him as a threat, and the Polish state security services monitored the Church closely.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.5] Historians disagree about how far he caused the 1989 revolutions; many see him as a moral leader who strengthened Polish civil society, while others stress the economic and political factors that made communism unsustainable.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.6] During the 1979 visit he spoke in Warsaw's Victory Square in a Mass attended by hundreds of thousands, and many Poles took his call for the renewal of their land as encouragement to demand change.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.7] Gdańsk strikers in August 1980 displayed the pope's portrait at the shipyard gate, and many Poles saw his election and visit as having given them the confidence to organise.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.8] After martial law he visited Poland in 1983 and 1987 and used sermons to defend workers' rights without calling for revolt; in 1989 he received Gorbachev in the Vatican on 1 December.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.b] Reform in eastern Europe repeatedly appeared and was suppressed, which showed the limits of Soviet tolerance.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.0] East German workers rose on 17 June 1953 against increased work norms and shortages, and Soviet tanks suppressed the uprising in East Berlin and other towns; it spread to hundreds of towns before it was crushed.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.1] Poznań workers protested in June 1956, and Władysław Gomułka returned to power in October 1956 after a confrontation with Moscow that was resolved without Soviet intervention.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.2] Poland's October 1956 settlement allowed limited autonomy for the Church and for intellectuals, but the Polish party maintained one-party rule.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.3] Czechoslovakia's Alexander Dubček became First Secretary of the Communist Party in January 1968, and he launched the Prague Spring reforms.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.4] The Soviet response in each case combined concession and threat: Poland in 1956 won a new leadership, Hungary in 1956 was crushed, and Czechoslovakia in 1968 was invaded.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.5] Poland's Gomułka ended forced collectivisation in 1956 and released Cardinal Wyszyński from internment, but by the 1960s he had reimposed tight controls.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.b] The Prague Spring of 1968 tried to create socialism with a human face, and Soviet and Warsaw Pact forces ended it by force.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.0] The Action Programme of April 1968 proposed freedom of expression, a looser press, the rehabilitation of victims of the 1950s and limits on the secret police.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.1] Dubček's government also planned economic reforms that allowed more market mechanisms, and it promised a federal settlement between Czechs and Slovaks.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.2] On 20–21 August 1968 Warsaw Pact forces from the Soviet Union, Poland, Hungary and Bulgaria invaded Czechoslovakia (East German units were mobilised but did not cross), and Dubček was arrested and taken to Moscow; Romania refused to take part.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.3] Albania withdrew from the Warsaw Pact in protest against the invasion.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.4] The Brezhnev Doctrine, set out in a Pravda article in September 1968 and in Brezhnev's speech to the Polish party congress that November, held that socialist states had a duty to intervene when socialism was threatened in another socialist state.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.5] Gustáv Husák replaced Dubček and presided over a period of normalisation, in which many reformers were removed from office and purged from the party.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.6] Charter 77, a document of Czechoslovak dissidents issued in January 1977 and led by Václav Havel, demanded that the state observe the human rights it had accepted.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.b] Solidarity was the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, and its rise forced the communist state to recognise an opposition.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.0] Strikes at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk in August 1980 led to the Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980, which recognised the right to form independent trade unions.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.1] Solidarity was led by Lech Wałęsa, an electrician at the shipyard, and at its peak in 1981 it had about ten million members.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.2] Its demands included free trade unions, the right to strike, better food supplies and access to the media.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.3] On 13 December 1981 General Wojciech Jaruzelski declared martial law, arrested Solidarity leaders and banned the union.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.4] The Polish authorities argued that martial law prevented a Soviet invasion; historians accept that Moscow had pressed for firm action but that Jaruzelski also acted to keep communist control, and Soviet archives show the Soviet leaders did not plan to send troops in.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.5] Solidarity was legalised again in April 1989 after the Round Table talks between the government and the opposition from February to April 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.b] In 1989 communist rule collapsed across eastern Europe in a series of peaceful transfers and one violent revolution, largely without Soviet intervention.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.0] In Poland, Solidarity won almost all of the seats it contested in the June 1989 elections, and in August 1989 Tadeusz Mazowiecki became prime minister of the first non-communist government in the Soviet bloc since 1948.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.1] In Hungary the government began dismantling the border fence with Austria in May 1989, allowed East Germans to cross to Austria from September 1989, and Imre Nagy was reburied as a national hero in June 1989, while in the autumn the party accepted multi-party elections.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.2] East German citizens fled through Hungary and Czechoslovakia, and mass demonstrations in Leipzig and East Berlin forced Erich Honecker's resignation on 18 October 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.3] The Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November 1989 after an announcement that was misread as permitting unrestricted crossings.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.4] In Czechoslovakia the Velvet Revolution began with a student demonstration on 17 November 1989, and Václav Havel was elected president on 29 December 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.5] In Bulgaria Todor Zhivkov was removed on 10 November 1989, and in Romania Nicolae Ceaușescu was executed on 25 December 1989 after a violent uprising.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.b] Gorbachev's reforms at home and his non-intervention abroad removed the props that had kept communist states in power.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.0] Chernobyl exploded on 26 April 1986 in Soviet Ukraine, and the official response was criticised for concealment, which strengthened the case for glasnost.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.1] The INF Treaty of 8 December 1987 eliminated US and Soviet intermediate-range missiles, a landmark in arms control.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.2] Gennady Gerasimov's remark in October 1989 that the Brezhnev Doctrine had been replaced by the Sinatra Doctrine meant that Eastern European states could choose their own path.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.3] Lithuania declared independence on 11 March 1990, and other Soviet republics followed in demanding sovereignty.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.4] The Warsaw Pact was dissolved on 1 July 1991 and Comecon was dissolved in June 1991.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.5] A conservative coup against Gorbachev on 19–21 August 1991 failed after Boris Yeltsin rallied opposition in Moscow, and it accelerated the break-up of the union.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.6] The Soviet Union was dissolved on 26 December 1991, a day after Gorbachev resigned as its president.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.7] Gorbachev and Reagan met at Geneva (November 1985) and Reykjavik (October 1986), where they discussed deep cuts in nuclear weapons, and signed the INF Treaty at Washington in December 1987.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.8] Gorbachev told the United Nations in December 1988 that the Soviet Union would cut its armed forces by 500,000 men and withdraw divisions from eastern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.9] Boris Yeltsin was elected president of the Russian republic in June 1991 and became the main rival to Gorbachev's authority.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.10] The eight leaders who staged the coup in August 1991 included the vice-president, the head of the KGB and the defence minister; it left Gorbachev weakened and the republics moved to leave the union.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.b] The end of communism led to the reunification of Germany, the break-up of Yugoslavia and the new alignments of central Europe.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.0] The German Democratic Republic and the Federal Republic were unified on 3 October 1990, under the Two Plus Four settlement signed in September 1990.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.1] The Visegrád group of Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary was formed in February 1991 to coordinate their approach to integration with the West; its members later joined NATO (1999 for Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic) and the EU (2004).

[hist-eur4-s4.8.3] Others stress the agency of opposition movements such as Solidarity and Charter 77, which kept reform alive through repression.
