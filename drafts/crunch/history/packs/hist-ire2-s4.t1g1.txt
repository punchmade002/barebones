[hist-ire2-s4.0.b] By 1886 Parnell had built a disciplined Irish Party and an alliance with Gladstone's Liberals, and the First Home Rule Bill had just been defeated in the Commons in June 1886. Over the next four years he kept his distance from agrarian violence, and his enemies tried to destroy him with forged evidence and failed.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.0] Background: Parnell had been chairman of the Irish Parliamentary Party since 1880, and in the 1885 election the party won 85 of the 103 Irish seats. This strength is why Liberals and Conservatives alike had to take Home Rule seriously.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.1] The Plan of Campaign (October 1886) was launched by William O’Brien, John Dillon and Timothy Harrington. Tenants offered what they thought a fair rent and, if the landlord refused it, paid it into a fund instead.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.2] Parnell did not endorse the Plan, because he feared that evictions and violence would alarm British Liberals. His main concern was the Liberal alliance, so he spent much of 1887–89 away from Ireland and the daily running of the party.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.3] In March 1887 The Times began a series called Parnellism and Crime. On 18 April 1887 it printed a letter, apparently signed by Parnell, that excused the Phoenix Park murders (the killing of Lord Frederick Cavendish and T.H. Burke in May 1882), so it aimed to link him to assassination.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.4] The letter was one of several forged by Richard Pigott, a discredited Dublin journalist, who sold them to The Times.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.5] The government set up a Special Commission of three judges in 1888, by Act of Parliament, to examine the charges against Parnell and about 65 other Irish MPs. It sat from 1888 into 1889.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.6] Under cross-examination by Sir Charles Russell, Parnell’s counsel (lawyer), in February 1889 Pigott collapsed. He had misspelled the word hesitancy in the same way as in the forged letter, which exposed the forgery, and he fled to Madrid, where he shot himself on 1 March 1889.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.7] Parnell was cheered by MPs in the Commons and visited Gladstone at Hawarden in December 1889. In 1890 The Times paid him £5,000 in an out-of-court settlement.
[hist-ire2-s4.0.8] The Commission’s report (February 1890) rejected the forged letters, though it criticised the methods of the Land League (the tenant organisation of the Land War). Parnell now stood at the height of his reputation.
[hist-ire2-s4.1.b] Parnell’s private life, hidden for years, now decided his career. The divorce case of November 1890 turned the Liberal alliance and the Catholic hierarchy (the bishops) against him and split the party.
[hist-ire2-s4.1.0] Parnell had lived with Katharine O’Shea for years. Her husband Captain William O’Shea, a former Irish Party MP, filed for divorce in December 1889 and named Parnell as co-respondent (the man accused of adultery with the wife).
[hist-ire2-s4.1.1] The case was heard on 15–17 November 1890. Parnell did not contest it, so a decree nisi (the first order ending the marriage) was granted.
[hist-ire2-s4.1.2] On 25 November the Irish Party re-elected Parnell as chairman, before Gladstone’s letter to John Morley was published. The letter made Gladstone’s position plain: his leadership of the Liberals would be made almost useless if Parnell stayed.
[hist-ire2-s4.1.3] Non-conformist Liberals threatened to desert Gladstone, and the pressure on him to break with Parnell grew.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.b] Parnell refused to step aside, so the pressure from the Liberals turned into an open fight inside his own party and in the country.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.0] On 29 November Parnell issued a manifesto, To the People of Ireland. It attacked the Liberals and disclosed details of private talks with Gladstone, and it deepened the bitterness.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.1] On 3 December 1890 the Catholic bishops, with Archbishop Walsh prominent, condemned Parnell as unfit to lead. The manifesto came first, on 29 November; the bishops’ condemnation followed on 3 December.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.2] The debate in Committee Room 15 at Westminster lasted from 1 to 6 December 1890. On 6 December about 45 MPs led by Justin McCarthy walked out, leaving a minority of about 26–28 with Parnell.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.3] The anti-Parnellites (the majority) included Dillon, William O’Brien, McCarthy and the fierce critic Tim Healy, and were later organised as the Irish National Federation (1891). The Parnellites kept control of the Irish National League.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.4] Parnell’s candidates lost by-elections in North Kilkenny (December 1890), North Sligo (April 1891) and Carlow (July 1891), in vicious campaigns, which showed that the country as well as the party had turned against him.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.5] He married Katharine on 25 June 1891 and died at Brighton on 6 October 1891, aged 45. He was buried at Glasnevin, and a very large crowd attended.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.b] This part covers the years of Parnell’s peak influence, his fall and the overall judgement. The strengths and weaknesses below are the usual basis for essays on him.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.0] What he did: between 1886 and 1890 he combined an alliance with Gladstone with firm control over a disciplined party of about 85 MPs. He was born in 1846 and was 45 when he died, so his career ended while he was still in his prime.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.1] He was a participant in the Pigott and O’Shea affairs. He lost control of events after the divorce, when his refusal to step aside split the movement.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.2] Contemporary nationalists were divided: the Parnellites saw him as a leader betrayed by Liberals and bishops, while the anti-Parnellites saw a leader who put himself above the cause.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.3] Contemporary unionists (opponents of Home Rule) saw him as a dangerous agitator, until the Pigott collapse made The Times and his accusers look foolish.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.4] Strengths: party discipline, tactical skill, ability to unite Fenians (physical-force nationalists), land reformers and constitutionalists, and a clear command of Commons procedure.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.5] Weaknesses: aloofness and an autocratic style, dependence on the Liberals, ambiguity about violence in the Land War, and a private life that he did not manage, which handed his enemies the means to destroy him.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.6] Historians differ: F.S.L. Lyons in Charles Stewart Parnell (1977) stresses the tragic collision of ambition and scandal. Paul Bew in C.S. Parnell (1980) presents a conservative landlord politician whose strategy had limits.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.7] His legacy was the idea of an independent Irish party. He also left the Parnell legend of a betrayed leader, a martyr, which later nationalists and writers, including Yeats and Joyce, drew on.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.b] Gladstone’s last attempt at Home Rule was killed by the House of Lords, which could reject a bill passed by the Commons. The Irish Party then spent the 1890s in feud.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.0] The Liberals returned to office after the 1892 election with a small majority that depended on the Irish MPs. Gladstone, aged 83, introduced the Second Home Rule Bill on 13 February 1893.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.1] The Bill proposed an Irish legislature of two houses, and it kept a reduced number of Irish MPs at Westminster, to meet the objection that Ireland would otherwise be excluded from the parliament that still ruled the empire.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.2] The Commons passed the Bill in September 1893 by 301 votes to 267, after eighty-two sittings, so the elected house backed it and only the Lords stood in the way.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.3] The House of Lords threw it out on 8 September 1893 by 419 votes to 41, so a huge Conservative and Unionist majority in the Lords outweighed the Commons. Gladstone retired in March 1894.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.4] Unionists had mobilised against the Bill, so Ulster resistance was already organised, notably at the Ulster Convention in Belfast in June 1892.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.5] The Conservatives under Salisbury won the 1895 election, and Unionist governments held office for most of the next ten years. They tried to kill Home Rule with kindness, through land purchase, local government and the Congested Districts Board.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.6] In the 1890s the Irish National Federation (Dillon and McCarthy), Healy’s followers and Redmond’s small Parnellite group fought each other, and voters became disillusioned.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.7] The United Irish League was founded by William O’Brien at Westport, Co. Mayo, in January 1898 to revive the land agitation in the west. Do not confuse it with the Irish National League or the Irish National Federation.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.8] The parties reunited in January 1900, and Redmond was elected chairman in February 1900.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.b] Redmond rebuilt the party, won land reform and then used the Liberals’ dependence on his votes to put Home Rule on the statute book. The last step came at a price in Ulster.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.0] Redmond and William O’Brien took part in the Land Conference of December 1902–January 1903, with landlord representatives, which led to the Wyndham Act (1903).
[hist-ire2-s4.5.1] In May 1907 a national convention of the party rejected the Irish Council Bill, a limited devolution scheme introduced by Chief Secretary Augustine Birrell. It offered less than Home Rule, so the party refused it.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.2] Two general elections in 1910 (January and December) left the Liberals short of a majority without Irish nationalist votes. The nationalist MPs numbered in the low 80s and held the balance.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.3] The Lords rejected the People’s Budget (1909), so the Liberals, backed by Redmond’s party, passed the Parliament Act (1911). It removed the Lords’ veto, leaving only a delay of two years; it did not itself grant Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.4] Asquith introduced the Third Home Rule Bill in April 1912. It became law on 18 September 1914, and a Suspensory Act held it back until the end of the war, which Redmond accepted.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.5] Under pressure from the unionist threat, in March 1914 Asquith proposed that Ulster counties could opt out for six years. Redmond, who had resisted, accepted it, a concession he had previously refused.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.6] In June 1914 Redmond insisted that 25 of his nominees join the Irish Volunteers’ provisional committee, and the committee accepted, so he gained control of the new force.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.b] Redmond led the reunited party for eighteen years and believed that Home Rule could be won by constitutional methods and kept by loyalty to the empire. The First World War wrecked that belief.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.0] John Redmond (1856–1918) was born at Ballytrent, Co. Wexford, into a Catholic landed family. He was educated at Clongowes, became a barrister and was an MP from 1881.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.1] He stayed loyal to Parnell in 1890, which is why he led the small Parnellite group after Parnell died in 1891. He sat for Waterford City from 1891 until his death in March 1918.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.2] As a participant he reunited the party in 1900 and negotiated the Land Conference, the 1911 Parliament Act and the 1912 Bill, relying on his control of the party’s votes.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.3] Contemporary nationalists generally admired him as the leader who delivered Home Rule. Critics such as Griffith and the IRB attacked his reliance on Liberal goodwill.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.4] In August 1914 he offered the Volunteers for the defence of Ireland. His Woodenbridge speech, in Co. Wicklow on 20 September 1914, urged them to fight in the war, and this split the Irish Volunteers.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.5] The great majority followed him as the National Volunteers, and about 10,000–12,000 stayed with Eoin MacNeill’s Irish Volunteers (estimates vary).
[hist-ire2-s4.6.6] Strengths: party management, which kept the reunited party together, parliamentary skill, a genuine achievement in land and the Home Rule Act, and personal integrity.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.7] Weaknesses: dependence on the Liberals, failure to resolve the Ulster problem, acceptance of partition in principle in 1914 and poor judgement of the new separatism.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.8] Historians differ: Denis Gwynn’s The Life of John Redmond (1932) is sympathetic, and Dermot Meleady’s John Redmond: The National Leader (2013) presents a statesman wrecked by war. Others see a leader out of touch with the new generation.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.b] Separatism means full independence from Britain, whereas Home Rule meant a parliament in Dublin within the United Kingdom. Arthur Griffith proposed a separatist policy of abstention and economic self-reliance as an alternative to Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.0] Arthur Griffith, a Dublin printer and journalist, founded the newspaper The United Irishman in 1899 with William Rooney.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.1] Cumann na nGaedheal was founded in 1900 to unite groups promoting Irish language, industry and the national cause, so it gathered scattered separatist and cultural groups in one body.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.2] In The Resurrection of Hungary (1904) Griffith argued that Ireland should follow Hungary, which had won its own parliament within a dual monarchy (Austria-Hungary, in 1867) after refusing to sit in the Vienna parliament. This is abstentionism: refusing to attend the imperial parliament.

[hist-ire2-s4.7.4] The weekly Sinn Féin followed in 1906. The Dungannon Clubs of Bulmer Hobson merged with Cumann na nGaedheal in 1907 as the Sinn Féin League, and the party was fully constituted as Sinn Féin in 1908.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.5] In the North Leitrim by-election of February 1908 the Sinn Féin candidate C.J. Dolan, who had left the Irish Party, was beaten by roughly three to one.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.6] Sinn Féin lost support after 1908 and the party remained small, but its ideas about abstention and self-reliance were revived in 1917.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.b] The IRB (Irish Republican Brotherhood) was the secret Fenian organisation committed to physical force. A younger generation revived it, and the Ulster Volunteers (the unionist armed force formed in 1913) became the model for militarism, meaning armed volunteer forces used as a political instrument.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.0] Tom Clarke, a Fenian who had served about fifteen years in prison, returned from the USA in December 1907 and opened a tobacconist’s shop in Dublin.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.1] With Bulmer Hobson and Seán Mac Diarmada, Clarke revived the IRB and founded the paper Irish Freedom (1910).
[hist-ire2-s4.8.2] Eoin MacNeill’s article The North Began, published in An Claidheamh Soluis on 1 November 1913, argued that the Ulster Volunteers had shown that Irishmen could arm and organise to defend their rights.

CANDIDATES hist-ire2-s3: 3. Case study: the elections of 1885 and 1886 — issues and outcomes
[hist-ire2-s3.0.b] The two elections of 1885 and 1886 were fought over one question, Home Rule. To follow them you need to know what Home Rule meant, who Parnell was by 1885 and how British government worked.
[hist-ire2-s3.0.0] Home Rule was the demand for an Irish parliament in Dublin to handle domestic affairs, while Ireland stayed inside the Union of 1800 (the Acts of Union joining Ireland to Great Britain). It is not separatism, which means full independence.
[hist-ire2-s3.0.1] Butt's Home Rule League (1873) had made Home Rule a parliamentary movement, pursued by votes at Westminster rather than by force.
[hist-ire2-s3.0.2] By 1885 Charles Stewart Parnell had risen fast: Land League president (1879), chairman of the Irish Parliamentary Party (1880), imprisoned in Kilmainham Gaol (October 1881 to May 1882) and founder of the Irish National League (1882), which replaced the banned Land League.
[hist-ire2-s3.0.3] Gladstone's Liberal government (1880-85) answered the Land War (the campaign by tenant farmers against landlords) with coercion, meaning emergency laws such as the Coercion Act of 1881 and the Crimes Act of 1882. Nationalists never forgave this.
[hist-ire2-s3.0.4] The Phoenix Park murders of 6 May 1882 (Lord Frederick Cavendish and Thomas Burke) left Liberals and nationalists with a bitter legacy, which is why co-operation between them in 1885 was surprising.
[hist-ire2-s3.0.5] In Britain the Liberals (led by W.E. Gladstone) and the Conservatives (led by Lord Salisbury) alternated in power. A government needs a majority of seats in the Commons, and the House of Lords could still reject a bill.
[hist-ire2-s3.0.6] Lord Hartington led the Whig wing of the Liberals, the cautious, landowning side, so he was likely to resist radical change such as Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s3.0.7] Ulster had a Protestant majority in the north-east, above all Belfast and east Ulster, and a Catholic nationalist majority in the south and west of the province. Unionists wanted to keep the Union; nationalists wanted Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s3.1.b] Two Acts rewrote the electoral map just before the 1885 election, and Parnell's organised party was the main beneficiary. This was a step towards democracy, which means government through elected representatives, though here for men only.
[hist-ire2-s3.1.0] The Representation of the People Act 1884 (the Third Reform Act) gave the vote to male householders and lodgers in the counties on the same terms as the boroughs (towns), ending the old gap between town and country. It applied to Ireland, and women were still excluded.
[hist-ire2-s3.1.1] In Ireland the electorate rose from about 220,000 to about 740,000, more than tripling, because the new voters were mostly small farmers and agricultural labourers (estimates vary a little).
[hist-ire2-s3.1.2] The Redistribution of Seats Act 1885 abolished many small boroughs and made most constituencies single-member, though a few, such as Cork City and Dublin University, stayed two-member. Ireland kept its 103 seats.
[hist-ire2-s3.1.3] Single-member rural seats favoured a party organised in every parish, and the Irish National League had branches across the country, run by local committees and often led by priests.
[hist-ire2-s3.1.4] Candidates were chosen at constituency conventions, where the clergy were influential. From 1884 each candidate had to take the pledge, an undertaking to sit, act and vote with the Irish Party, so the party could vote as a bloc.
[hist-ire2-s3.1.5] Voting stayed secret under the Ballot Act of 1872, so tenants could vote against their landlords without fear of eviction.
[hist-ire2-s3.1.6] The Catholic hierarchy, led by William Walsh, Archbishop of Dublin, and Thomas Croke, Archbishop of Cashel, backed the party in return for its support on Catholic education.
[hist-ire2-s3.2.b] Parnell's aim was to hold the balance of power, meaning a smaller party whose votes decide which larger party governs. He would bargain with both British parties and give support to whichever offered more on Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s3.2.0] At Cork on 21 January 1885 Parnell declared that no man had the right to fix the boundary to the march of a nation. This was a demand for Home Rule as a national claim, not a limited local parliament.
[hist-ire2-s3.2.1] In 1884 Conservatives such as Lord Randolph Churchill joined the Irish MPs in attacking the Liberal Lord Lieutenant, Earl Spencer (Lord Lieutenant 1882-85), over the Maamtrasna murders of August 1882.
[hist-ire2-s3.2.2] At Maamtrasna, Myles Joyce was hanged in December 1882 despite doubts about his guilt, so nationalists used the case as a symbol of the injustice of Liberal coercion.
[hist-ire2-s3.2.3] That common cause is called the Maamtrasna alliance, and it opened the way to co-operation between Parnell and the Conservatives, though the two sides wanted different things.
[hist-ire2-s3.2.4] On 9 June 1885 the Irish Party voted with the Conservatives on an amendment to Gladstone's budget and brought down his government. Lord Salisbury formed a minority Conservative ministry, one that could survive only while others did not combine against it.
[hist-ire2-s3.3.b] With Salisbury in office, Parnell tested what each side would give. Home Rule emerged as the only demand that satisfied him.
[hist-ire2-s3.3.0] Lord Carnarvon, Salisbury's Lord Lieutenant of Ireland (a Conservative), met Parnell secretly in London in August 1885. The Crimes Act was allowed to lapse, which removed coercion, a major grievance.
[hist-ire2-s3.3.1] The Ashbourne Act (August 1885) provided state loans for tenant purchase, so that tenants could buy their land from landlords. It was part of the Conservatives' offer.
[hist-ire2-s3.3.2] Joseph Chamberlain, a Liberal radical, proposed an elected Central Board for Ireland, discussed through Captain William O'Shea. Parnell and Gladstone's Cabinet both rejected it in 1885, which left Home Rule as the demand.
[hist-ire2-s3.3.3] At Arklow in October 1885 Parnell set out a demand for a legislature with control of Irish domestic affairs, and he stressed that Ireland would not be satisfied with less.
[hist-ire2-s3.3.4] Parnell's manifesto of 21 November 1885 told Irish voters in Britain to vote against Liberal candidates, because of Liberal coercion in 1881-82. This was reversed after the Hawarden Kite.
[hist-ire2-s3.4.b] The election produced a hung Parliament, one in which no party has an overall majority, with the Irish Party holding the balance. It also split Ireland along religious and regional lines.
[hist-ire2-s3.4.0] Polling ran from late November to mid-December 1885. The Irish Party won 85 of the 103 Irish seats, plus T.P. O'Connor at Liverpool Scotland, giving 86 MPs in all.
[hist-ire2-s3.4.1] Outside Ulster the only seats the party did not win were the two for Dublin University. The party swept the south and west (85 = 17 in Ulster + 68 elsewhere).
[hist-ire2-s3.4.2] In Ulster the party won 17 of 33 seats, in the Catholic-majority districts of the south and west of the province. Unionists won the rest, strongest in the east of Ulster.
[hist-ire2-s3.4.3] In Great Britain the Liberals won about 335 seats, the Conservatives about 249 and the Irish Party 86, in a House of 670 (counts vary slightly).
[hist-ire2-s3.4.4] Neither big party could govern without the Irish: the Irish Party plus the Conservatives could equal the Liberals, and Irish votes could decide who governed. Parnell held the balance of power.
[hist-ire2-s3.4.5] The Irish vote in British cities, especially Liverpool, may have helped Conservative candidates in some constituencies after the manifesto, though its effect is hard to measure.
[hist-ire2-s3.4.6] Nationalists and Liberals saw the result as a national mandate, a clear verdict that Ireland, outside Protestant Ulster, wanted Home Rule. Unionists and Conservatives replied that two Irelands had voted.
[hist-ire2-s3.5.b] Gladstone moved to Home Rule within weeks of the election and took office again in February 1886. His move cost him part of his own party.
[hist-ire2-s3.5.0] On 17 December 1885 newspapers published the Hawarden Kite, a leak by Gladstone's son Herbert reporting that Gladstone favoured Home Rule. Hawarden, in Flintshire, was Gladstone's home, and a kite is a trial balloon to test opinion.
[hist-ire2-s3.5.1] Salisbury's ministry was defeated on 26 January 1886 on an amendment about allotments for labourers (Jesse Collings), with the Irish voting against it. Gladstone formed his third ministry in February 1886, with John Morley as Chief Secretary for Ireland.
[hist-ire2-s3.5.2] Joseph Chamberlain joined the Cabinet but resigned in March 1886, along with Sir George Trevelyan, because they would not accept Home Rule, which showed the Liberals splitting. Lord Hartington declined to serve.
[hist-ire2-s3.5.3] Historians disagree why Gladstone took up Home Rule: genuine conviction after the 1885 result, or tactical manoeuvre to keep the Liberals together, as argued by A.B. Cooke and John Vincent.
[hist-ire2-s3.6.b] The Government of Ireland Bill was Gladstone's attempt to give Ireland self-government without breaking up the empire. It reserved to Westminster the matters that made the empire one state.
[hist-ire2-s3.6.0] Gladstone introduced the Government of Ireland Bill on 8 April 1886. It would have set up an Irish legislature in Dublin with an executive (government) responsible to it.
[hist-ire2-s3.6.1] The Crown, defence, foreign policy, trade and the customs remained with Westminster, so Ireland would not have been independent.
[hist-ire2-s3.6.2] Ireland was to pay a fixed share, one fifteenth, of imperial expenditure. The Irish members would no longer sit at Westminster, which meant Ireland would have no voice in imperial decisions.
[hist-ire2-s3.6.3] The Irish legislature was to have two orders, designed to reassure Protestants and property owners, and it was forbidden to establish or endow any religion.
[hist-ire2-s3.6.4] A companion Land Purchase Bill would have offered loans to buy out the landlords. It was unpopular in Britain and was not proceeded with, which weakened the main bill.
[hist-ire2-s3.6.5] Parnell accepted the bill as a basis for a settlement, because it granted an Irish legislature, though he objected to its financial terms.
[hist-ire2-s3.7.b] The Liberal split, not the Conservatives alone, defeated the First Home Rule Bill. Both sides had set out their arguments during weeks of debate.
[hist-ire2-s3.7.0] The bill was debated for several weeks in April-June 1886, and the second reading was defeated in the early hours of 8 June 1886 by 341 votes to 311, a majority of 30.
[hist-ire2-s3.7.1] About 93 Liberals, led by Hartington and Chamberlain, voted with the Conservatives against the bill. They became the Liberal Unionists, a Liberal group that opposed Home Rule and kept the Union.
[hist-ire2-s3.7.2] John Bright, the veteran radical, also opposed the bill. Chamberlain argued that it would break up the empire and treat Ulster Protestants unfairly.
[hist-ire2-s3.7.3] Conservatives such as Lord Randolph Churchill argued that Home Rule meant separation and the loss of the Protestant minority's rights.
[hist-ire2-s3.7.4] Gladstone's supporters argued that coercion had failed, that Ireland's verdict was clear and that Home Rule would give Ireland a settled government and reconcile it to the Crown.
[hist-ire2-s3.7.5] In his closing speech on 7 June 1886 Gladstone said Ireland "stands at your bar, expectant, hopeful, almost suppliant", an appeal to conscience made before the vote.
[hist-ire2-s3.7.6] After the defeat Gladstone dissolved Parliament in late June 1886 and called a general election.
[hist-ire2-s3.8.b] The crisis of 1886 created organised unionism in Ulster and in the south. Unionists feared an Irish parliament would be a Catholic parliament.
[hist-ire2-s3.8.0] In a letter of February 1886 Lord Randolph Churchill wrote that the Orange card was the one to play against Home Rule, meaning an appeal to Ulster Protestants (the Orange Order is their loyalist society). He spoke at the Ulster Hall, Belfast, on 22 February 1886.
[hist-ire2-s3.8.1] The phrase Ulster will fight and Ulster will be right is associated with Churchill in 1886, and unionists used it as a slogan for decades.
[hist-ire2-s3.8.2] The Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union, formed in 1885 by southern unionists, mainly landlords and professionals, who had property to lose, organised opposition to Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s3.8.3] Ulster Protestants established a Loyalist Anti-Repeal organisation in 1886 (repeal meaning undoing the Union), and the Orange Order grew sharply.
[hist-ire2-s3.8.4] The Belfast riots began in early June 1886, with the bill's defeat in prospect, and lasted into September. About 30 or more people were killed and many injured (estimates vary).
[hist-ire2-s3.8.5] The violence deepened sectarian division, and both sides used it as evidence. Nationalists blamed unionist mobs and the Orange Order; unionists blamed Home Rule agitation.
[hist-ire2-s3.9.b] The voters rejected Gladstone, and the Conservatives governed for most of the next twenty years. The elections left a changed political landscape.
[hist-ire2-s3.9.0] In July 1886 the Conservatives and Liberal Unionists won a combined majority of over 100 over the Gladstonian Liberals and the Irish Party.
[hist-ire2-s3.9.1] Salisbury formed his second government in July 1886, which survived because it was supported by the Liberal Unionists, who entered a coalition with the Conservatives in 1895.
[hist-ire2-s3.9.2] The Irish Party again won about 85 seats, so the nationalist case was undiminished.
[hist-ire2-s3.9.3] Home Rule was now the policy of the Liberal Party. The Liberal-Nationalist alliance became the central fact of Irish parliamentary politics until 1914, because Home Rule now depended on Liberal votes.
[hist-ire2-s3.9.4] Home Rule could pass only when the Liberals had a majority able to overcome the Lords, which was not achieved until the Parliament Act of 1911, because the Liberal split had weakened them.
[hist-ire2-s3.9.5] Unionism became a lasting organised force in Ireland and Britain, and this was the origin of the Ulster question, the problem of Protestant Ulster's opposition to Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s3.9.6] Parnell was at the height of his power, with a disciplined party and a Liberal ally. The Pigott forgeries and the O'Shea divorce came next.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.b] Parnell was the central figure in the case study. He made the Irish Party decisive and tied Home Rule to the Liberals.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.0] Parnell was born in 1846 at Avondale, Co. Wicklow, a Protestant landowner and an MP from 1875, and leader of the Irish Party.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.1] He built the party machine, using the National League, the pledge and conventions, so that the party could deliver its votes as a bloc.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.2] He bargained with Carnarvon in 1885 and issued the manifesto against the Liberals, then supported Gladstone, showing tactical skill in keeping both British parties guessing.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.3] In the Galway by-election of February 1886 he imposed Captain O'Shea on the party against Tim Healy's objections, which showed his control but sowed resentment.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.4] He was a participant who led the campaigns and the bargaining, but events also shaped him: he depended on Gladstone surviving, and the Liberal split and unionist backlash limited him.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.5] Nationalist contemporaries saw the 85 seats as a personal triumph and a national mandate. Unionists and many Conservatives saw an agitator linked to violence, a reputation The Times later attacked with forged letters.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.6] Examiners ask about his strengths, such as discipline and the pledge, tactics with both parties, balance of power, and appeal to Fenians and constitutionalists, and skill in the Commons. His weaknesses were reliance on a British ally, secrecy about his private life, imposing O'Shea, and aloofness.
[hist-ire2-s3.10.7] F.S.L. Lyons (1977) sees 1885-86 as Parnell's zenith; others note that his strategy depended on Gladstone surviving the Liberal split.
[hist-ire2-s3.11.b] Documents on this case study are speeches, newspaper reports and cartoons. Each can be placed by who produced it, when and why.
[hist-ire2-s3.11.0] Nationalists (Freeman's Journal, United Ireland) presented the 1885 result as a national verdict and the First Home Rule Bill as a just but limited settlement.
[hist-ire2-s3.11.1] Unionists (Belfast News-Letter, the Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union) presented Home Rule as a threat to the empire, to property and to Protestants.
[hist-ire2-s3.11.2] Gladstone's speeches argued that Home Rule was a moral duty after the failure of coercion; his critics called it a surrender to agitators.
[hist-ire2-s3.11.3] Liberal Unionist speeches, by Chamberlain and Hartington, argued that Irish self-government would endanger the union of the two islands.
[hist-ire2-s3.11.4] Punch, drawn by John Tenniel and others, usually depicted Irish agitators as violent or ape-like, as in The Irish Frankenstein of 1882, and took an anti-Home Rule line. Some Irish and radical papers published pro-Home Rule cartoons.
[hist-ire2-s3.11.5] A cartoon can be placed by its date and symbols: Gladstone as the old man with an axe, Parnell as a tempter or bully, Hibernia (Ireland personified) as a woman, John Bull (Britain personified) as a stout man.
[hist-ire2-s3.11.6] Historians differ on why Gladstone took up Home Rule: conviction after the 1885 result, or tactics to keep the Liberals together, as argued by A.B. Cooke and John Vincent in The Governing Passion (1974).
[hist-ire2-s3.12.b] The elections were a triumph for the Irish Party and a defeat for Home Rule. Examiners reward paired judgements.
[hist-ire2-s3.12.0] Achievement: the Irish Party won a national mandate, forced Home Rule onto the British agenda and won over a major British party.
[hist-ire2-s3.12.1] Limit: the party could not pass a bill without a British majority, and Gladstone's conversion split the Liberals and put the Conservatives in power.
[hist-ire2-s3.12.2] Achievement: the bill's defeat was narrow, and the Liberal alliance gave nationalists a realistic route to Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s3.12.3] Limit: the elections showed that Ulster Protestants were opposed, a problem that Parnell's party never answered.
[hist-ire2-s3.12.4] Contemporary nationalists blamed the Liberal Unionists and the House of Commons; contemporary unionists said the vote showed the Union was safe.
[hist-ire2-s3.12.5] Later historians see 1886 as the origin of the Ulster question as well as of the Liberal-Nationalist alliance, which makes it a turning point whatever its immediate failure.

CANDIDATES hist-ire2-s2: 2. Land agitation and land reform, 1870–1914
[hist-ire2-s2.0.b] Most Irish farmers were tenants at will or on short leases, and the law gave them little protection against eviction or rent increases. Gladstone's first big Irish reform tried to help them, but it left the central problem untouched, and that failure is why a mass land movement followed.
[hist-ire2-s2.0.0] Land was owned by a small landlord class, mostly Protestant and often absentee (living elsewhere); most farmers rented their holdings, usually without a lease, and paid rent to the landlord or his agent.
[hist-ire2-s2.0.1] Memory of the Great Famine (1845-50) and of the evictions that followed it made insecurity feel especially threatening, because a tenant who lost his holding had nowhere else to live.
[hist-ire2-s2.0.2] The tenants' grievances were insecurity, rack-renting (very high rents), eviction and no payment for improvements they had made. Their three demands became known as the Three Fs: fair rent, fixity of tenure (the right to stay while paying rent) and free sale of the tenant's interest in the holding.
[hist-ire2-s2.0.3] Ulster had the Ulster Custom, a tradition of tenant right that let an outgoing tenant sell his interest in his holding. Gladstone's Land Act of 1870 gave it legal force, though protection for tenants elsewhere was weak.
[hist-ire2-s2.0.4] The Landlord and Tenant (Ireland) Act of 1870 offered compensation for improvements and for disturbance (eviction other than for non-payment of rent), but it did not control rents.
[hist-ire2-s2.0.5] Landlords could therefore still raise rents beyond what tenants could pay and then evict for non-payment, which the Act did not forbid. This left tenants without real security of tenure, the main reason for the later land agitation.
[hist-ire2-s2.0.6] The purchase clauses (the Bright clauses) offered loans to help tenants buy their holdings, but they were little used, so little land changed hands under them.
[hist-ire2-s2.1.b] A farming crisis turned long-standing grievances into a mass movement. Michael Davitt supplied the organisation, and Charles Stewart Parnell the national leadership.
[hist-ire2-s2.1.0] From 1877 poor harvests, falling prices and cheap American grain imports hit Irish farmers hard, and the wet summer of 1879 brought a partial potato failure, which reminded people of the Famine.
[hist-ire2-s2.1.1] Rent arrears (unpaid rent) mounted, so evictions rose sharply: about 460 families were evicted in 1877 and more than 2,000 in 1880.
[hist-ire2-s2.1.2] The New Departure of 1878 linked Fenians (revolutionaries in the IRB), land agitation and constitutional politics. Davitt met John Devoy of Clan na Gael, the Irish-American Fenian organisation, in the USA.
[hist-ire2-s2.1.3] Davitt organised a meeting at Irishtown, Co. Mayo, on 20 April 1879 against a landlord's high rents, which showed that tenants could be drawn into open protest.
[hist-ire2-s2.1.4] At Westport in June 1879 Parnell told tenants to keep a firm grip of their homesteads (the farms and houses they lived in). This was his first major public identification with the agitation.
[hist-ire2-s2.1.5] The Land League of Mayo was founded in August 1879. The Irish National Land League followed in Dublin in October 1879, with Parnell as president and Davitt as a leading organiser.
[hist-ire2-s2.1.6] The League's programme was to reduce rents first and then to make tenants owners of their land, known as peasant proprietorship. Davitt personally favoured land nationalisation (state ownership of the land).
[hist-ire2-s2.1.7] Funds came from Irish America: Parnell and John Dillon toured the United States in 1880 raising money, and Land League branches multiplied across the south and west in 1880.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.b] The League fought evictions with collective action rather than force, and the government answered with coercion (emergency laws that restrict normal rights). This struggle is known as the Land War.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.0] The League's methods included rent strikes, in which tenants refused to pay unless rents were cut, and the shunning of anyone who profited from an eviction.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.1] At Ennis in September 1880 Parnell urged that anyone who took a farm from which a tenant had been evicted should be shunned by the whole community, which is the policy that gave us the word boycott.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.2] The first victim was Captain Charles Boycott, agent for Lord Erne at Lough Mask, Co. Mayo, whose workers and tradesmen left him in 1880. The word boycott entered the language.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.3] In November 1880 about fifty Orange labourers from the north were brought in to save Boycott's harvest, guarded by about 1,000 soldiers and police. The huge protection for so few workers made the operation a national joke.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.4] Moonlighting, the night-time maiming of cattle and attacks on property, accompanied the campaign. Parnell's opponents, unionists and many Liberals, blamed the League for this agrarian crime.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.5] The Ladies' Land League, a separate organisation led in Dublin by Anna Parnell (Parnell's sister) from early 1881, kept the campaign going when the men were jailed and helped evicted families with huts and relief. It was dissolved in 1882.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.6] Gladstone's Chief Secretary, W.E. Forster, secured the Protection of Person and Property Act in March 1881, allowing detention without trial, so hundreds of local leaders were jailed.
[hist-ire2-s2.2.7] Parnell's obstruction of the coercion bill in the Commons in early 1881 contributed to the introduction of closure (a rule allowing a debate to be ended by vote).
[hist-ire2-s2.3.b] The 1881 Act met most of the League's demands on paper. It gave tenants what the 1870 Act had withheld, though some tenants were left out at first.
[hist-ire2-s2.3.0] The Land Law (Ireland) Act (August 1881) granted the Three Fs: fair rents set by land courts, fixity of tenure while the tenant paid the rent, and free sale of the tenant's interest. A body called the Land Commission oversaw the system.
[hist-ire2-s2.3.1] The courts fixed fair (judicial) rents for fifteen years, and the first judicial rents cut rents by about one fifth on average, a real gain for tenants.
[hist-ire2-s2.3.2] The landlord kept ownership but shared control of the holding with the tenant, a system known as dual ownership. This is why the Act did not end landlordism, only weakened it.
[hist-ire2-s2.3.3] Leaseholders and tenants in arrears were excluded at first, so these groups did not benefit until the Arrears Act of 1882 dealt with arrears.
[hist-ire2-s2.4.b] Parnell did not want the League to accept the Act without testing it, which led to a clash with Gladstone. The crisis ended with the League suppressed and a deal that helped tenants in arrears.
[hist-ire2-s2.4.0] Parnell advised testing the Act by sending selected test cases to the land courts, in effect telling tenants to wait rather than rush in.
[hist-ire2-s2.4.1] Gladstone denounced Parnell at Leeds on 7 October 1881, and Parnell was arrested on 13 October 1881 and held in Kilmainham.
[hist-ire2-s2.4.2] The League replied with the No Rent Manifesto on 18 October 1881, telling tenants to pay no rent. The manifesto failed to win broad support, and the Catholic hierarchy opposed it.
[hist-ire2-s2.4.3] The Land League was suppressed on 20 October 1881, two days after the manifesto, which ended it as an open organisation.
[hist-ire2-s2.4.4] The Kilmainham settlement of spring 1882 was an understanding between Parnell and the government, and Parnell was released in May 1882. Do not confuse it with his imprisonment in Kilmainham in 1881-82.
[hist-ire2-s2.4.5] The Arrears Act (August 1882) cleared arrears of rent for tenants with small valuations (rent under £30), with the state contributing. It helped those left out in 1881.
[hist-ire2-s2.4.6] The Irish National League replaced the suppressed Land League in October 1882.
[hist-ire2-s2.5.b] When prices fell again in 1885-86, a new campaign sought rent reductions estate by estate rather than nationally. It was a local, organised version of the earlier rent campaign.
[hist-ire2-s2.5.0] The Plan of Campaign was launched in October 1886 in United Ireland by William O'Brien, John Dillon and Timothy Harrington, not by Parnell or Davitt.
[hist-ire2-s2.5.1] Tenants on an estate offered a reduced rent. If the landlord refused, they paid it into a fund held by trustees, which supported evicted tenants.
[hist-ire2-s2.5.2] It was applied on estates such as those of Lord Clanricarde in Co. Galway, the Vandeleur estate at Kilrush, Co. Clare, and Lord Massereene in Co. Louth.
[hist-ire2-s2.5.3] Arthur Balfour, Chief Secretary from 1887, answered with the Criminal Law and Procedure (Ireland) Act of 1887, called the Crimes Act, allowing trial by magistrates without a jury for listed offences.
[hist-ire2-s2.5.4] On 9 September 1887 police fired on a crowd at Mitchelstown, Co. Cork, killing three. Nationalists called it a massacre, and Balfour was nicknamed Bloody Balfour.
[hist-ire2-s2.5.5] A papal rescript from the Holy Office in Rome condemned the Plan of Campaign and boycotting in April 1888, an unwelcome intervention for nationalists.
[hist-ire2-s2.5.6] Parnell was never close to the Plan because the Liberal alliance mattered more to him. The movement was weakened by the Parnell split of 1890-91.
[hist-ire2-s2.6.b] Conservative governments tried to defuse the land question by funding tenants' purchase of their farms. The policy is called constructive unionism, or killing Home Rule with kindness, because it hoped reform would reduce support for nationalism.
[hist-ire2-s2.6.0] The Ashbourne Act (1885), passed by Salisbury's Conservative government (Lord Ashbourne), advanced loans of the full purchase price to tenants who bought from willing landlords, repayable by annuities (fixed yearly payments).
[hist-ire2-s2.6.1] Arthur Balfour's Land Purchase Act (1891) extended the funds for purchase, so that more tenants could buy.
[hist-ire2-s2.6.2] The Congested Districts Board (1891) targeted the poor, overcrowded districts of the west. It bought and divided estates and aided fisheries, crafts and farming.
[hist-ire2-s2.6.3] Gerald Balfour's Land Act (1896), by Arthur's brother, made minor changes to the purchase terms, and little changed in practice.
[hist-ire2-s2.6.4] Defenders of constructive unionism say it removed a major grievance by turning tenants into owners.
[hist-ire2-s2.6.5] Critics argue that it made landlords richer, while the problem of poor western farms remained.
[hist-ire2-s2.7.b] A negotiated settlement between landlords and tenant leaders brought mass tenant purchase. This was the peaceful end of the long conflict, and the state paid for it.
[hist-ire2-s2.7.0] The Land Conference met in Dublin in December 1902, with landlord and tenant representatives: Lord Dunraven (chair), Captain John Shawe-Taylor, William O'Brien and John Redmond, among others. It proposed a purchase scheme with a bonus to landlords.
[hist-ire2-s2.7.1] The Wyndham Act (1903), introduced by the Chief Secretary George Wyndham, offered tenants loans of the full price repayable over about 68 years at low interest.
[hist-ire2-s2.7.2] Landlords received a bonus of 12 per cent of the price as an inducement to sell.
[hist-ire2-s2.7.3] More than 200,000 tenants bought or agreed to buy within about six years. The bonus proved costly to the Treasury, so the terms were revised.
[hist-ire2-s2.7.4] The Labourers (Ireland) Act (1906), a Liberal measure, provided for cottages for farm labourers, with tens of thousands built by 1914. The Evicted Tenants Act (1907) enabled reinstatement of tenants evicted in the Land War.
[hist-ire2-s2.7.5] The Birrell Land Act (1909), named for the Chief Secretary Augustine Birrell, allowed compulsory purchase where landlords refused to sell and gave stronger powers to the Estates Commissioners.
[hist-ire2-s2.7.6] By 1914 most tenants had bought, or were buying, their holdings, and landlordism was finished as a political and social force. Tenant proprietorship (tenants owning their farms) had replaced it.
[hist-ire2-s2.8.b] Davitt was the son of an evicted Mayo tenant who became a Fenian, then the organiser of the Land League and later a voice for labour. He was both a leader of the land movement and its historian.
[hist-ire2-s2.8.0] Born in 1846 at Straide, Co. Mayo, he was evicted with his family in 1850 and grew up in Haslingden, Lancashire. He lost his right arm in a cotton-mill accident aged 11, and educated himself while working.
[hist-ire2-s2.8.1] He joined the IRB (the Fenian movement), was arrested for arms trafficking in 1870 and sentenced to fifteen years. He was held at Dartmoor and released on licence in December 1877, after about seven years.
[hist-ire2-s2.8.2] Through the New Departure he linked Fenians with the land movement, organised the Irishtown meeting in April 1879 and founded the Land League of Mayo in 1879. He was the main organiser beside Parnell's national leadership.
[hist-ire2-s2.8.3] He was re-arrested in February 1881 and held at Portland until May 1882. His prison experience produced Leaves from a Prison Diary (1885).
[hist-ire2-s2.8.4] He argued for land nationalisation and was dismayed that the League accepted peasant proprietorship. He opposed violence against individuals, and his social radicalism set him apart from Parnell.
[hist-ire2-s2.8.5] In 1890 he opposed Parnell's continued leadership after the O'Shea divorce, and he founded the newspaper Labour World in the same year.
[hist-ire2-s2.8.6] He resigned his seat in 1899 in protest at the Boer War, published The Fall of Feudalism in Ireland in 1904, and died in 1906. As both participant and writer, he left the best insider account of the movement.
[hist-ire2-s2.8.7] Contemporaries saw him as a dangerous agitator (unionists) or a hero of the tenants (nationalists). Historians now stress his social radicalism and how he joined Fenians and constitutionalists.
[hist-ire2-s2.9.b] The land campaign changed who owned Irish land, but it was less successful in solving rural poverty and in delivering Home Rule. Judgement questions reward paired points: what was won and what was left undone.
[hist-ire2-s2.9.0] Achievement: agitation forced the state to concede the Three Fs in 1881 and then to finance the transfer of land from landlords to tenants by 1914.
[hist-ire2-s2.9.1] Limit: the settlement came through state funding and compromise, not land nationalisation, so labourers and small western farmers gained least.
[hist-ire2-s2.9.2] Achievement: the Land League mobilised a mass base, which made Parnell's party a national force.
[hist-ire2-s2.9.3] Limit: the agitation depended on Parnell's control, and violence, divisions over the Plan of Campaign and the papal condemnation weakened it.
[hist-ire2-s2.9.4] Contemporary unionists claimed concessions were extracted by intimidation, while nationalists said that only pressure had moved Westminster.
[hist-ire2-s2.9.5] Historians differ on whether constructive unionism worked: it removed a major grievance, but did not stop the growth of nationalism.
[hist-ire2-s2.9.6] Davitt's social vision (land nationalisation, labour rights) was more radical than Parnell's, who favoured peasant proprietorship.
