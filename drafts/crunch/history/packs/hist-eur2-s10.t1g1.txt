[hist-eur2-s10.1.6] Wartime censorship limited what could be published at the front, so the most critical works came out in 1918 or after.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s1: 1. The Second Reich, Bismarck's foreign policy and the changing balance of power, 1871–1890
[hist-eur2-s1.0.b] The German Empire was proclaimed in 1871 after Prussia's wars against Denmark, Austria and France. It combined a strong monarchy with an elected parliament, and it upset the old balance of power in Europe.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.0] The German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, while Paris was still under siege in the Franco-Prussian War; King Wilhelm I of Prussia became German Emperor (Kaiser).
[hist-eur2-s1.0.1] The Empire was a federation of 25 states: four kingdoms (Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg), smaller duchies and principalities, and three free cities. Prussia held about two thirds of the territory and population.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.2] The Kaiser was hereditary King of Prussia, commanded the army, appointed and dismissed the Chancellor and could declare war with Bundesrat consent.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.3] The Chancellor was responsible to the Kaiser, not to parliament. Bismarck held the office from 1871 to 1890 and was also Prussian Minister-President for almost all of that time.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.4] The Bundesrat (federal council) represented the state governments. Prussia held 17 of its 58 votes in 1871, enough to block any proposed constitutional change.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.5] The Reichstag was elected by universal male suffrage (men aged 25 and over) by secret ballot. It could pass or reject laws and the budget, but it could not appoint or dismiss the Chancellor or ministers.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.6] The Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871) made France pay an indemnity of 5 billion francs and cede Alsace and part of Lorraine, including Strasbourg and Metz; German troops stayed until it was paid, which France did by 1873.
[hist-eur2-s1.0.7] A new Great Power in the centre of Europe, with a growing population and industry, changed the balance of power: France was weakened and embittered, while Britain, Russia and Austria-Hungary had to adjust to a united Germany.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.b] Bismarck governed by managing parliament and identifying 'enemies of the Reich' in turn. The Kulturkampf against the Catholic Church is covered in the notes on church-state tensions.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.0] Until 1878 Bismarck relied on the National Liberals in the Reichstag, who supported free trade and the Reich's unity.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.1] After two attempts on the life of Wilhelm I in May and June 1878 (by Hödel and Nobiling), Bismarck used the fear of revolution to pass the Anti-Socialist Law in October 1878. It banned socialist organisations, meetings and newspapers but still let socialists stand for the Reichstag.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.2] The law did not stop the SPD: the party's vote kept rising, and by the 1890 election it won about 1.4 million votes and 35 seats.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.3] The tariff of 1879 ended free trade by protecting German iron and steel and agricultural products. It split the National Liberals, and Bismarck now relied on the Conservatives and the Catholic Centre Party.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.4] To win workers away from socialism, Bismarck introduced state insurance: sickness insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old age and invalidity insurance (1889). Germany was the first major state to create such a scheme.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.5] Bismarck's mix of repression and welfare is often described as the 'carrot and stick'. The insurance schemes were limited in coverage and benefits, and they failed to reduce support for the SPD.
[hist-eur2-s1.1.6] Bismarck won army funding by repeated confrontations with the Reichstag; in the 1887 election, held after the Reichstag rejected the army bill (the Septennate), the pro-government Kartell of Conservatives and National Liberals won a majority.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.b] After 1871 Bismarck declared Germany a 'satiated' power that wanted no more territory in Europe. His aim was to keep the new Reich safe from a hostile coalition, above all one that included France.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.0] French feeling after the loss of Alsace-Lorraine produced revanche, the desire for revenge and the recovery of the lost provinces; Bismarck assumed that France would never become a reliable friend.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.1] His strategy was to keep France without allies by keeping the other powers friendly with Germany or at odds with each other. Contemporaries spoke of his fear of coalitions against Germany.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.2] Bismarck wanted the Great Powers to feel that they needed good relations with Berlin more than with each other; he also steered rivalries towards the periphery of Europe, such as the Balkans and Africa.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.3] In the War-in-Sight crisis of spring 1875 a German newspaper article suggested that Germany might attack France to stop its rearmament (France had passed a new army law). Britain and Russia warned Germany to hold back; historians disagree on whether Bismarck was really planning war or merely bluffing.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.4] The crisis showed Bismarck that Britain and Russia would not allow France to be crushed a second time, and it fed his fear of a hostile coalition.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.5] Franco-German tension flared again in 1886–87 under General Boulanger, a popular war minister who stirred up revanchist feeling, and in the Schnaebelé affair (April 1887), when a French frontier official was arrested by the Germans and later released.
[hist-eur2-s1.2.6] Bismarck backed French colonial expansion, such as the occupation of Tunisia in 1881, and cooperated briefly with France against Britain over Egypt and Africa in 1884–85, to turn French attention away from Alsace-Lorraine.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.0] The Three Emperors' League (October 1873) linked Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia as a conservative understanding to resist revolution and settle disputes among the three monarchies.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.1] The League was only an understanding, and it came under strain in the Balkan crisis of 1875–78, when Christian peoples rose against Ottoman rule and Russia fought Turkey in 1877–78.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.2] The Treaty of San Stefano (March 1878) created a large Bulgaria and gave Russia great influence in the Balkans. Austria-Hungary and Britain refused to accept it.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.3] At the Congress of Berlin (13 June to 13 July 1878), chaired by Bismarck, who called himself an 'honest broker', the powers cut Bulgaria down and divided it into three parts.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.4] The Congress recognised the full independence of Serbia, Montenegro and Romania. Austria-Hungary was allowed to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina, and Britain took Cyprus; Russia kept Bessarabia and gains in the Caucasus.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.5] Russians felt that Bismarck had sided against them at Berlin and had cheated them of the fruits of their victory over Turkey; Russian newspapers attacked Germany, and the League lapsed.
[hist-eur2-s1.3.6] The League was renewed secretly in June 1881 for three years (and again in 1884), with promises of neutrality if one member went to war with a fourth power and agreed Balkan spheres. It broke down in 1887 over the crisis in Bulgaria.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.0] The Dual Alliance of 7 October 1879 was a secret defensive treaty between Germany and Austria-Hungary: each promised to help the other if attacked by Russia, and to stay neutral if attacked by another power, unless Russia joined that power.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.1] Bismarck signed it to guard against a Russian attack and to bind Austria-Hungary to Germany; Wilhelm I accepted it reluctantly because it seemed to threaten his friendship with Tsar Alexander II.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.2] The Dual Alliance lasted until 1918 and was the permanent core of Germany's alliance system.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.3] The Triple Alliance (20 May 1882) added Italy, which was angered by France's occupation of Tunisia in 1881 and wanted support against France and recognition as a Great Power.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.4] Under the Triple Alliance, Germany and Austria-Hungary promised help if Italy was attacked by France; Italy would help Germany if attacked by France, and all three would act together if any faced two or more Great Powers.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.5] Italy declared that it would not fight Britain, and its relations with Austria-Hungary were poor because of the Italian-speaking territories (Trentino and Trieste) in Habsburg hands.
[hist-eur2-s1.4.6] The treaty was renewed regularly until 1914; Italy stayed out of the war in August 1914 and joined the Allies in May 1915.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.0] The crisis in Bulgaria (1885–87), where Russia and Austria-Hungary competed for influence after Prince Alexander of Battenberg was forced to abdicate in 1886, ended the Three Emperors' League in 1887.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.1] Bismarck replaced it with the Reinsurance Treaty of 18 June 1887, a secret agreement between Germany and Russia for three years. Each promised neutrality if the other was at war with a third power, except if Germany attacked France or Russia attacked Austria-Hungary.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.2] Germany also recognised Russia's interests in Bulgaria and promised diplomatic support over the Straits.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.3] Bismarck did not tell Austria-Hungary or Italy of the treaty, and it can be argued that it conflicted in spirit with the Dual Alliance.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.4] In November 1887 Bismarck banned the Reichsbank from accepting Russian bonds as security for loans (the Lombardverbot). Russia turned to France for loans, which helped to bring the two powers together.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.5] Britain, Italy and Austria-Hungary signed the Mediterranean Agreements in 1887 to hold the status quo in the Mediterranean and Near East, with Bismarck's encouragement. This tied Britain loosely to the Triple Alliance powers.
[hist-eur2-s1.5.6] The Reinsurance Treaty was not renewed in 1890 after Bismarck's fall, and Russia moved towards France (see the notes on the structures of European diplomacy).
[hist-eur2-s1.6.b] Bismarck was not an enthusiast for colonies. He changed his mind in 1884, partly because of pressure from merchants and the colonial movement and partly for diplomatic reasons.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.0] In 1884–85 Germany claimed South West Africa (Lüderitz), Togoland, the Cameroons, German East Africa and territory in the Pacific including north-east New Guinea.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.1] Bismarck preferred protectorates run by chartered companies at their own cost rather than colonies governed by the state.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.2] The colonial claims brought friction with Britain, especially over South West Africa and East Africa, and the Gladstone government protested; Bismarck used the disputes to build pressure on Britain and to cooperate with France.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.3] Bismarck summoned the Berlin Conference (November 1884 to February 1885) on West Africa; it set rules for the partition of the continent (see the notes on the New Imperialism).
[hist-eur2-s1.6.4] Relations with Britain improved after Lord Salisbury took office in 1885. Bismarck tried to keep Britain friendly through the Mediterranean Agreements and hoped that colonial quarrels would not damage the relationship.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.5] Anglo-German tension was also dynastic: the future Frederick III's wife, Victoria, was Queen Victoria's daughter, and Bismarck distrusted the liberal influence of the English-born crown princess.
[hist-eur2-s1.6.6] Bismarck is said to have told an explorer in 1888 that his map of Africa lay in Europe, meaning that colonies mattered to him mainly for their effect on European diplomacy.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.0] Wilhelm I died on 9 March 1888 aged 90. His son Frederick III, a liberal who was fatally ill with throat cancer, reigned for 99 days and died on 15 June 1888.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.1] The new Kaiser, Wilhelm II, aged 29, wanted to rule personally and disagreed with Bismarck about handling the workers; he wanted to ease the anti-socialist laws, which Bismarck wanted to make permanent.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.2] The Anti-Socialist Law was due to expire in September 1890. After the Reichstag election of February 1890, in which the Kartell parties lost seats and the SPD vote grew, Bismarck mooted a coup d'etat to change the constitution.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.3] Wilhelm II also objected to Bismarck's attempt to limit the ministers' access to the Kaiser through the Cabinet Order of 1852, and to the Chancellor's talks with the Catholic Centre leader Windthorst.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.4] Bismarck offered his resignation on 18 March 1890, after the Kaiser made it clear that he would be dismissed otherwise. It was accepted two days later, and General Leo von Caprivi became Chancellor.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.5] The British magazine Punch published the cartoon 'Dropping the Pilot' by John Tenniel on 29 March 1890, showing the Kaiser watching the old pilot leave the ship.
[hist-eur2-s1.7.6] In the following months the Reinsurance Treaty was not renewed and Germany signed the Heligoland-Zanzibar treaty with Britain (July 1890).
[hist-eur2-s1.8.b] Bismarck made the unification of Germany possible in the 1860s, and then governed it as Chancellor until 1890. This block covers the Chancellorship; his earlier career belongs to the notes on unification.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.0] Bismarck (1815–98) came from a Junker landowning family at Schönhausen in Prussia, and became Minister-President of Prussia in 1862.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.1] He was made Prince in 1871 and served as Chancellor under Wilhelm I, Frederick III and Wilhelm II. He controlled foreign policy personally, and most of the major policy decisions of the Empire were his.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.2] He saw himself as a conservative protecting the monarchy and Prussian power; he used nationalism, tariffs and social insurance to keep the new Reich stable.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.3] Contemporaries admired the 'Iron Chancellor' who had made Germany great: nationalists and conservatives adored him, while Catholics, Liberals and socialists suffered under his measures and called them persecution.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.4] Foreign observers respected him as a master diplomat. Punch's cartoon of 1890 and the many Bismarck monuments erected after his death show how large he loomed.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.5] After his dismissal he used the press to criticise Wilhelm II's governments, and published his memoirs, Thoughts and Recollections. He died on 30 July 1898 at Friedrichsruh.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.6] Historians are divided. A.J.P. Taylor and others praised his skill as a diplomat who kept the peace; Lothar Gall called him a 'white revolutionary' who changed Europe from above.
[hist-eur2-s1.8.7] Hans-Ulrich Wehler argued that Bismarck's rule was a kind of Bonapartist dictatorship that kept the old elites in power by 'negative integration', uniting the Reich against domestic 'enemies' such as Catholics and socialists; critics say it left Germany with a weak parliamentary tradition.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.0] Foreign success: Germany fought no war after 1871 and avoided a two-front war while Bismarck was in office.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.1] Foreign limit: the system was complicated and depended on Bismarck personally; the Reinsurance Treaty and the Dual Alliance pulled in opposite directions, and a successor could not keep all the balls in the air.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.2] Foreign limit: the Dual Alliance committed Germany to Austria-Hungary in a Balkan region where Habsburg and Russian interests clashed, and the treatment of Russia in 1878 and 1887 helped to push it towards France.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.3] Foreign success: the Congress of Berlin avoided a European war in 1878, and Bismarck was able to turn colonial rivalry to Germany's advantage.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.4] Domestic success: he created a unified state, a national market, a common currency (the mark, 1873), a central bank (the Reichsbank) and the first national state insurance system in Europe.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.5] Domestic failure: the Kulturkampf and the Anti-Socialist Law did not break the Catholic Church or the SPD; both emerged stronger, and the Reichstag remained weak.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.6] Contemporary view: Germans in 1890 saw a statesman who had made Germany one of the strongest states in Europe.
[hist-eur2-s1.9.7] Later view: historians stress the unfinished nature of his domestic settlement, which left his successors with an unreformed constitution and a divided society.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s5: 5. The conduct of war, 1914–1918
[hist-eur2-s5.0.b] All the Great Powers expected a short war. Germany's plan was to defeat France in about six weeks and then turn on Russia, but the plan failed and the Western Front became a stalemate by the end of the year.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.0] Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August 1914 and took Liège, then swept through Belgium and northern France under the Schlieffen Plan, modified by the younger Moltke, who weakened the right wing.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.1] The British Expeditionary Force, a small professional army, fought its first major action at Mons on 23 August 1914 and retreated with the French.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.2] The First Battle of the Marne (5-12 September 1914) halted the German advance about 40 kilometres from Paris. The Germans withdrew to the River Aisne and dug in.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.3] Both sides then tried to outflank each other in the 'race to the sea', and the line of trenches reached the North Sea and the Swiss frontier by late 1914.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.4] The First Battle of Ypres (October-November 1914) was a hard-fought defensive success for the British and French; the BEF lost much of its regular army and Britain had to rely on volunteers.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.5] At Christmas 1914 troops along parts of the line took part in unofficial truces, which the high commands then forbade.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.6] In the east, Russia invaded East Prussia, but General Hindenburg and his chief of staff Ludendorff destroyed the Russian Second Army at Tannenberg (26-30 August 1914), capturing about 90,000 men, and defeated the First Army at the Masurian Lakes in September.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.b] Defensive weapons were stronger than the means of attack, so battles on the Western Front produced very high casualties for small gains.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.0] Trench systems had a front line, support and reserve trenches linked by communication trenches, with barbed wire and the shell-torn ground of no man's land between the opposing lines.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.1] Soldiers lived with mud, rats, lice, cold and the constant danger of shelling and snipers. Trench foot and shell shock (what we now call combat stress) were common.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.2] The machine gun and quick-firing artillery made frontal attacks costly; most battle casualties were caused by shellfire.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.3] Attackers relied on long preliminary bombardments, which warned the enemy and churned up the ground. From 1916 the 'creeping barrage' moved ahead of infantry as they advanced.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.4] Poison gas was first used on a large scale by Germany at the Second Battle of Ypres (22 April 1915), when chlorine was released against French colonial and Canadian troops. Phosgene and mustard gas followed, and gas masks reduced its effect.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.5] Tanks were first used by Britain at Flers-Courcelette on 15 September 1916. They were slow and unreliable, but at Cambrai (November 1917) hundreds were used together with artillery and infantry.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.6] Aircraft were used for reconnaissance, artillery spotting and, later, fighter combat and bombing. Germany used Zeppelins and Gotha bombers against British cities.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.7] By 1918 both sides used combined arms: Germany developed stormtrooper infiltration tactics, and the Allies combined tanks, aircraft, artillery and infantry.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.b] In a war of attrition each side tried to wear down the other's manpower and resources. Generals on both sides believed a breakthrough was possible, and they continued costly offensives.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.0] In 1915 the Allies launched offensives at Neuve Chapelle, Artois and Loos, and the Germans used gas at Ypres. None broke the line, and shell shortages in Britain caused a political crisis in May 1915.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.1] Verdun (21 February to 18 December 1916): the German chief of staff Falkenhayn attacked the symbolically important French fortress, intending, according to his later account, to bleed the French army white. Both sides lost hundreds of thousands of men.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.2] The French commander at Verdun, General Pétain, rotated divisions through the battle, and the road known as the Sacred Way supplied the front. Verdun became a symbol of French endurance.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.3] The Somme (1 July to 18 November 1916) was a joint British-French offensive planned partly to relieve Verdun. On the first day the British Army lost about 57,000 casualties, of whom about 19,000 were killed, the worst single day in its history.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.4] The Somme advanced the Allied line only a few kilometres and cost over a million casualties in total across all armies. It was the first big battle for Kitchener's volunteer 'Pals' battalions, and wiped out the male population of some local communities.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.5] Nivelle's offensive in April 1917 along the Chemin des Dames failed with heavy losses, causing mutinies in the French army; Pétain replaced Nivelle and restored morale by improving conditions.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.6] Passchendaele (the Third Battle of Ypres, July to November 1917) was fought in the Ypres salient, partly to clear German submarine bases in Belgium. Heavy rain and shell damage turned the ground to mud, and the final gain was only about 8 kilometres.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.7] At Cambrai (20 November 1917) Britain used hundreds of tanks in a surprise attack; the gains were mostly lost to a German counter-attack.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.0] The Central Powers broke the Russian line at Gorlice-Tarnow in May 1915, forcing a great Russian retreat from Poland and Galicia.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.1] The Brusilov Offensive (June 1916), led by General Brusilov against Austria-Hungary, was the most successful Allied offensive of the year. It took many prisoners and forced Germany to move troops east, but it cost Russia about a million casualties and could not be sustained.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.2] Romania joined the Allies in August 1916 and was largely overrun by Germany and its allies by the end of the year.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.3] Defeats, losses, shortages and the failure of the Tsar's government led to the February Revolution of 1917 (see the notes on Tsarist Russia). The Provisional Government continued the war, and the Kerensky Offensive of July 1917 collapsed.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.4] After the Bolsheviks seized power in October 1917, they signed an armistice in December 1917 and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918, giving up large parts of Poland, the Baltic lands, Ukraine and Finland.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.5] The treaty let Germany move over a million men west for the offensives of 1918, but it also tied down troops in the occupied east.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.0] Gallipoli: after Ottoman entry into the war in October-November 1914, Britain and France tried to force the Dardanelles in March 1915. The land campaign began on 25 April 1915 with landings of British, French, Australian and New Zealand (ANZAC) troops. The campaign was stalemated and the troops were evacuated by 9 January 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.1] Gallipoli damaged the reputation of Churchill, who had promoted it, but helped to build the sense of national identity of Australia and New Zealand.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.2] Italy joined the Allies in May 1915 under the Treaty of London, promised territory from Austria-Hungary. Eleven battles on the Isonzo brought little gain, and Italy suffered a disastrous defeat at Caporetto in October 1917 before recovering at Vittorio Veneto (October-November 1918).
[hist-eur2-s5.4.3] In the Middle East, the British fought the Ottomans in Mesopotamia (surrender at Kut in April 1916) and in Palestine, where General Allenby captured Jerusalem in December 1917 and won at Megiddo in September 1918. The Arab Revolt was supported by T.E. Lawrence.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.4] In the Balkans, Serbia was overrun by the Central Powers in 1915; the Allies maintained a front at Salonika. Bulgaria asked for an armistice on 29 September 1918, and the Ottomans and Austria-Hungary followed in October and November.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.5] Germany's colonies were captured by the Allies, though German East Africa held out until the end of the war.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.0] Britain used its fleet to blockade Germany, stopping imports of food and raw materials. Germany suffered shortages, and the winter of 1916-17, known as the turnip winter, was especially severe.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.1] The German High Seas Fleet was cautious. The only major fleet engagement, Jutland (31 May to 1 June 1916), cost Britain 14 ships and about 6,000 men, and Germany 11 ships and about 2,500 men. The British fleet kept control of the North Sea and the German fleet remained mostly in port.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.2] Germany turned to submarines. The sinking of the liner Lusitania on 7 May 1915, with almost 1,200 dead including 128 Americans, outraged American opinion, and Germany limited its campaign.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.3] On 1 February 1917 Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, hoping to starve Britain into surrender within months. British shipping losses were very high in spring 1917, and Britain had food stocks for only weeks.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.4] The adoption of the convoy system in 1917 and improved anti-submarine methods reduced losses, and the campaign failed to knock Britain out of the war while bringing the USA in.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.b] Mass war needed mass armies. Continental states already had conscription, but Britain had relied on volunteers and had to change.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.0] Germany and France called up all fit men under the pre-war system of conscription. Russia did the same.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.1] In Britain, Kitchener's recruiting campaign raised over two million volunteers by the end of 1915. As losses grew and numbers fell, the Military Service Act of January 1916 introduced conscription for unmarried men aged 18 to 41; it was extended to married men in May 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.2] Conscientious objectors could appear before tribunals, and about 16,000 men claimed that status. Some were jailed or sent to non-combatant units.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.3] The Military Service Act of 1916 did not apply to Ireland. A proposal in April 1918 to extend conscription to Ireland provoked the Conscription Crisis, a nationwide campaign of opposition, and was never put into effect.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.4] The USA introduced conscription through the Selective Service Act of May 1917. Germany passed the Auxiliary Service Law in December 1916, which required all men aged 17 to 60 to work for the war effort.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.5] Conscription and casualties raised the question of 'reserved occupations': governments had to keep skilled workers in munitions, mines and railways, and sometimes replaced them with women (see the notes on women in the workforce).
[hist-eur2-s5.7.0] The Zimmermann Telegram, a secret German offer to Mexico of an alliance against the USA, was intercepted by British intelligence and published in the American press in March 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.1] The USA declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917, after the submarine campaign sank American ships and the Zimmermann Telegram, as an 'associated power' rather than a formal ally. Its troops under General Pershing arrived in strength during 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.2] With Russia out, Ludendorff planned the Spring Offensive (Kaiserschlacht), launched on 21 March 1918 with 'Operation Michael'. It made large gains and came close to Amiens, but did not break the Allied line.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.3] In response, the Allies set up a unified command under Marshal Foch in April 1918. German troops were exhausted and short of supplies, and a counter-attack at the Second Battle of the Marne (July 1918) turned the tide.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.4] The Hundred Days began with the Battle of Amiens on 8 August 1918, which Ludendorff called the 'black day of the German army'. Allied tanks, aircraft and artillery pushed the Germans back to the Hindenburg Line, which was broken in late September.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.5] On 29 September 1918 Ludendorff told the Kaiser that Germany needed an armistice at once. Germany asked for terms using Wilson's Fourteen Points, and the armistice was signed in a railway carriage at Compiègne at 5 a.m. on 11 November, taking effect at 11 a.m.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.6] Military deaths in the war are usually put at about nine to ten million, with many more wounded, and civilian deaths in the millions.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.b] Douglas Haig commanded the British Expeditionary Force in France from December 1915 until the end of the war. He is the most controversial British commander of the war.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.0] Born in Edinburgh in 1861 into a whisky-distilling family, Haig attended Sandhurst, joined the cavalry and served in the Sudan and the Boer War before becoming a corps commander in 1914.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.1] He succeeded Sir John French as commander-in-chief of the BEF in December 1915 and was promoted field marshal in January 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.2] He commanded the Somme offensive of 1916, the Arras offensive and Passchendaele in 1917, and the defensive battles of 1918. On 11 April 1918 he issued his 'backs to the wall' order.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.3] He led the Allied advance in the Hundred Days of 1918, in which British forces played the leading part and took many prisoners.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.4] After the war he was made an earl and given a grant of money. He founded the British Legion in 1921 and supported the sale of poppies. He died in 1928, and his funeral was a national occasion.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.5] Contemporaries: King George V and the army trusted him; Lloyd George distrusted him, thought his battles wasteful, and withheld reinforcements in early 1918. Many soldiers, and the public after the war, mourned him.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.6] From the 1920s memoirs, notably Lloyd George's, criticised him. In the 1960s Alan Clark's The Donkeys popularised the phrase 'lions led by donkeys', which Clark misattributed to German generals.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.7] Historians such as Denis Winter and Gerard De Groot depicted Haig as a callous and unimaginative butcher; John Terraine, Gary Sheffield and others argue that he adapted, learned from experience and led the army to victory in 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.8] Many historians now accept that his attritional strategy was shaped by circumstances: the strength of defences, the need to support France, and the poor communications that limited control over battles.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.0] Allied success: the blockade, attrition and the arrival of American troops in 1918 wore down Germany's capacity to fight, and 1918's coordinated tank, artillery and air attacks broke its army.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.1] Limit: the Western Front battles of 1915-17 produced casualties out of proportion to gains, and Passchendaele and the Somme became symbols of waste.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.2] German success: Germany held the Western Front for four years, knocked Russia out of the war and nearly won in spring 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.3] German failure: the Schlieffen Plan failed in 1914, unrestricted submarine warfare brought in the USA, and the army was exhausted by 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.4] Contemporary view: the troops themselves, and many officers, criticised the high command; later views range from Clark's 'donkeys' to Sheffield's 'Forgotten Victory'.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s8: 8. Industrialisation, economic crisis and revolution in Tsarist Russia, 1871–1920
[hist-eur2-s8.0.b] Russia was the largest state in Europe, but it was poor, overwhelmingly rural and ruled by an autocratic tsar with no parliament.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.0] Serfdom was abolished by Alexander II in 1861. Peasants had to pay redemption payments to the state over 49 years for the land they received, and these payments left many in debt.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.1] Most peasants held land in the mir (village commune), which periodically redistributed strips among households. This discouraged investment, and farming methods were backward.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.2] The census of 1897 showed a population of about 126 million, about 80 per cent of them peasants. Russia was an empire of many peoples, and Russians were less than half the population.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.3] In 1871 Russia had little heavy industry, relatively few railways and a weak banking system; it exported grain and relied on foreign capital and technology.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.4] Alexander II's reforms (zemstva, courts, army reform) were limited, and he was assassinated by the revolutionary group People's Will on 13 March 1881.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.5] His son Alexander III (1881-94) and grandson Nicholas II (1894-1917) upheld autocracy, backed by the Orthodox Church, the nobility, the army and the secret police (the Okhrana), and pursued Russification of national minorities.
[hist-eur2-s8.0.6] Nicholas II declared in 1895 that hopes for a constitution were 'senseless dreams'; revolutionary movements, including Social Revolutionaries (SRs) based on the peasantry and Marxist Social Democrats, developed underground.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.b] Sergei Witte, Finance Minister from 1892 to 1903, led a state-directed 'great spurt' of industrial growth, paid for by foreign loans, taxation and grain exports.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.0] Witte believed that Russia must industrialise to remain a Great Power, and the state built railways, ordered goods and protected industry with tariffs.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.1] Railways were the key: the Trans-Siberian Railway began in 1891 and the main line to Vladivostok was open by about 1904 (completed in full in 1916). Rail mileage roughly doubled in the 1890s.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.2] He put the rouble on the gold standard in 1897 to attract foreign investors. French and Belgian loans and investment paid for mines, ironworks and railways, helped by the Franco-Russian Alliance.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.3] Coal, iron and steel grew in the Donbas in Ukraine, oil in Baku, and engineering and textiles in St Petersburg and Moscow. Output of pig iron and coal rose several times between 1890 and 1900, at a rate often put at about 8 per cent a year.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.4] Industry was concentrated in huge factories such as the Putilov works in St Petersburg, which employed tens of thousands of workers, making labour organisation and protest easier.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.5] The cost fell on the peasantry through high indirect taxes, and grain was exported even when people were hungry. Witte himself recognised the risk and warned about peasant discontent.
[hist-eur2-s8.1.6] The industrial depression of 1900-03 caused unemployment and strikes. By 1914 Russia was the world's fifth industrial power by output, but its output per head was low and it relied on foreign capital.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.0] The peasant economy suffered from small plots, outdated methods, rapid population growth and redemption payments. Land hunger grew as families increased.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.1] A harvest failure in 1891 caused a famine across the Volga region and the black earth provinces. A cholera epidemic in 1892 killed hundreds of thousands.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.2] Government relief was slow, and private and zemstvo volunteers, including Tolstoy and Chekhov, played a major part. The famine weakened trust in the regime.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.3] The famine encouraged educated Russians to form opposition groups: the Union of Liberation (1903), liberals who wanted a constitution, and the SRs.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.4] Peasant unrest grew in the early 1900s with attacks on landlords' estates in 1902 in Kharkov and Poltava provinces, and violence rose again in 1905-06.
[hist-eur2-s8.2.5] Workers were mostly first-generation peasants who kept links with their villages, which spread discontent between city and countryside.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.0] Russia and Japan were rivals in Manchuria and Korea. Russia had leased Port Arthur from China in 1898, and the Trans-Siberian Railway gave it a route to the Far East.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.1] Interior minister Plehve is reported to have wanted 'a short victorious war' to stop revolution, but it did the opposite.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.2] Japan attacked Port Arthur without warning on 8 February 1904. The Russian army suffered defeats at Mukden (February-March 1905), and Port Arthur surrendered in January 1905.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.3] Russia's Baltic Fleet sailed around the world and was destroyed by the Japanese at Tsushima on 27-28 May 1905.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.4] The Treaty of Portsmouth (5 September 1905), mediated by US President Theodore Roosevelt, gave Japan southern Sakhalin, Port Arthur and a free hand in Korea.
[hist-eur2-s8.3.5] The defeat humiliated the autocracy, revealed the weakness of its army, transport and administration, and was the immediate trigger for the 1905 revolution.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.0] On Sunday 22 January 1905 (9 January in the old Russian calendar) a peaceful procession led by the priest Georgy Gapon went to the Winter Palace in St Petersburg to present a petition for reforms to the tsar. Troops opened fire, killing and wounding hundreds, in 'Bloody Sunday'.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.1] The massacre destroyed the people's trust in the tsar as their protector. Strikes, peasant risings, mutinies (the battleship Potemkin in June 1905) and national movements in Poland and the Caucasus followed.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.2] In October 1905 a general strike paralysed the railways and cities. The Petersburg Soviet (council) of workers' deputies was formed, with Trotsky as a leading member.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.3] Witte advised the Tsar to grant concessions. The October Manifesto (17 October 1905) promised civil liberties, an elected Duma with legislative powers, and a wider franchise.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.4] The Manifesto split the opposition: liberals (Octobrists) accepted it, while socialists did not. The army stayed loyal, the Soviet leaders were arrested in December, and an armed rising in Moscow was crushed.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.5] A large French loan in April 1906 helped the government to survive without relying on the Duma.
[hist-eur2-s8.4.6] The Fundamental Laws (April 1906) kept the Tsar's title of autocrat, his control of the army, foreign policy and ministers, and his right to veto laws and to dissolve the Duma and rule by decree between sessions.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.b] Peter Stolypin, Prime Minister from 1906 to 1911, combined repression with agrarian reform in a bid to create a conservative, prosperous peasant class.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.0] The First Duma (May-July 1906) demanded land reform and ministers answerable to it, and was dissolved after 73 days. The Second Duma (1907) was also dissolved.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.1] In June 1907 the Tsar and Stolypin changed the electoral law, so that landowners and the rich dominated the Third Duma (1907-12) and the Fourth Duma (1912-17).
[hist-eur2-s8.5.2] Stolypin crushed disorder with field courts-martial from 1906, which executed over a thousand people. The hangman's noose was called 'Stolypin's necktie'.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.3] The land reform of November 1906 let peasants leave the commune, claim their land as private property and consolidate their strips. The Peasant Land Bank helped them buy land, and resettlement in Siberia was encouraged.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.4] By 1914 about two million households (about a quarter) had left the communes, but most peasants stayed in the mir. Stolypin said he needed twenty years of peace.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.5] He was shot by a revolutionary who was also a police informer, Dmitri Bogrov, at the Kiev opera in September 1911, and died days later.
[hist-eur2-s8.5.6] After 1912 workers' protest revived, with strikes following the shooting of miners at the Lena goldfields in April 1912, and strikes in St Petersburg in summer 1914.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.0] Russia mobilised the largest army, but it was poorly led and short of rifles, shells and boots. Defeats at Tannenberg and the Masurian Lakes in 1914 and the great retreat of 1915 cost millions of casualties and prisoners. The Brusilov Offensive of 1916 was a success but cost heavily.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.1] The war strained the economy: railways were overloaded, inflation rose as the government printed money, and food and fuel were short in the cities by winter 1916-17. Over fifteen million men were mobilised, reducing farm output.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.2] Nicholas II took personal command of the army at the front in September 1915, leaving his wife Alexandra and Rasputin influential in government. Ministers changed rapidly. The Duma's Progressive Bloc demanded a government that had public confidence.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.3] Grigori Rasputin, a peasant mystic whom the Tsarina trusted to ease the haemophilia of the heir, Alexei, was murdered by aristocrats in December 1916, a sign of the regime's isolation.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.4] On 8 March 1917 (23 February in the Russian calendar), International Women's Day, women textile workers in Petrograd demanded bread. Strikes grew into a general strike, and the soldiers of the garrison joined the crowds instead of firing on them.
[hist-eur2-s8.6.5] The Duma set up a Provisional Committee, and the Petrograd Soviet was revived. Army commanders told Nicholas to go, and he abdicated on 15 March 1917 (2 March in the Russian calendar), ending 300 years of Romanov rule. His brother Michael declined the crown.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.0] The Provisional Government, headed first by Prince Lvov and from July by Alexander Kerensky, shared power with the Soviet, an arrangement known as 'dual power'. Order No. 1 of the Soviet told soldiers to obey the Soviet's orders.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.1] The Provisional Government introduced civil liberties but continued the war and delayed land reform until a Constituent Assembly was elected. The Kerensky offensive in July 1917 failed, and desertions rose.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.2] Lenin returned from exile in Switzerland in April 1917 and issued the April Theses, demanding 'all power to the Soviets', peace, land to the peasants and bread for workers. The Bolsheviks grew in strength in the Soviets.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.3] A Bolshevik rising in the July Days failed, but in August-September General Kornilov's attempted coup against Kerensky was defeated with the help of the Bolsheviks, who gained arms and prestige.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.4] The Bolsheviks won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow Soviets by September. Trotsky chaired the Petrograd Soviet and organised the Military Revolutionary Committee.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.5] On the night of 6-7 November 1917 (24-25 October in the old calendar) Bolshevik forces took bridges, stations and the telegraph, and the Winter Palace fell. Lenin announced the formation of a Soviet government under the Council of People's Commissars.
[hist-eur2-s8.7.6] The new government issued decrees on peace and land and the right of nations to self-determination. In the Constituent Assembly election, the SRs won the most votes (about 40 per cent) and the Bolsheviks about a quarter; the Assembly met once in January 1918 and was dissolved by force.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.0] Lenin signed an armistice in December 1917 and, after Trotsky's attempt to stall, accepted the harsh Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918. Russia gave up Poland, the Baltic provinces, Ukraine and Finland, a huge loss of land, population, coal and industry.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.1] The Bolsheviks moved the capital to Moscow in March 1918 and created the Cheka (December 1917) to crush opposition. The Romanov family were shot at Ekaterinburg in July 1918.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.2] The civil war (1918-20) was fought between the Reds (Bolsheviks), organised by Trotsky as War Commissar into the Red Army, and the Whites, a loose mix of generals, monarchists and liberals such as Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich and Wrangel.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.3] Britain, France, Japan and the USA sent troops to support the Whites and to guard supplies after 1918, but their effort was limited.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.4] The Reds held central Russia with its railways, industry and capital and could move troops; the Whites were scattered and divided, and many peasants feared a return of the landlords.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.5] War Communism (1918-21) meant seizing grain from peasants, nationalising industry and rationing, and the Red Terror targeted opponents.
[hist-eur2-s8.8.6] The Red Army defeated Kolchak in Siberia (1919), Denikin and Yudenich (1919-20), and Wrangel in the Crimea in November 1920. In 1920 Poland defeated a Soviet advance on Warsaw. Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Poland became independent states.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.0] Achievement: Witte's industrialisation built railways and heavy industry, and growth after 1906 was strong; Stolypin's reforms began to create a class of independent farmers.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.1] Limit: industrial growth depended on foreign capital and on a poor peasantry, and it produced a discontented urban working class in huge factories.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.2] Limit: political reform stopped short. The Dumas were weak, the autocrat could dismiss ministers, and Nicholas II mistrusted reformers and failed to use the chance of 1905-06.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.3] Why the regime survived 1905 but not 1917: in 1905 the army stayed loyal and the opposition split; in 1917 the army was worn down by three years of defeat, the cities starved and the soldiers joined the strikers.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.4] Historians' views: the optimists (such as Leopold Haimson and Hans Rogger in some respects) argue that Russia was modernising and that the war, not structural failure, caused collapse; pessimists argue that the autocracy was too weak to survive modernisation, and Orlando Figes stresses its failure to reform.
[hist-eur2-s8.9.5] Contemporary views: liberals blamed the tsar's obstinacy; the Bolsheviks portrayed 1917 as a workers' revolution, though historians stress that it was also a coup by a disciplined party exploiting the failure of the Provisional Government.
