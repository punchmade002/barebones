[hist-ire4-s1.2.3] Between 1.5 and 2 million people emigrated between 1845 and 1855 (estimates vary), the great majority to the United States, with several hundred thousand to British North America and to Britain.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.4] The census showed Ireland's population falling from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.5 million in 1851.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.5] Famine emigrants were mostly Catholic, poor, unskilled and Irish-speaking in many cases, and came heavily from the west and south-west; many travelled as whole families rather than as single young adults.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.6] Emigration was panic flight rather than planned movement: in 1847 about 300,000 destitute Irish people landed at Liverpool alone, and many stayed there.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.7] By 1851 the number of Irish-born in Britain had risen to about 730,000, and about a fifth of Liverpool's population was Irish-born.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.0] Public works were the main relief in 1846, but the pay was low and late and heavy labour weakened starving workers.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.1] The Temporary Relief Act of 1847 replaced the public works with soup kitchens, which fed about 3 million people a day at their peak in the summer of 1847 before closing that autumn.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.2] The Poor Law Extension Act of June 1847 made the Poor Law unions pay for relief, and its Gregory clause (quarter-acre clause) denied relief to anyone holding more than a quarter acre unless they gave up the land.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.3] Rising poor rates gave landlords a reason to clear smallholders, and evictions rose sharply between 1847 and 1852.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.4] Assisted emigration: some landlords paid passages because shipping tenants abroad was cheaper than paying rates to support them in the workhouse.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.5] Major Denis Mahon of Strokestown, Co. Roscommon, sent 1,490 tenants via Liverpool to Quebec in 1847; hundreds died on the crossing or soon after at Grosse Isle, and Mahon was shot dead on 2 November 1847.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.6] Lord Palmerston sent about 2,000 tenants from his Sligo estate to Canada in 1847, and many arrived destitute; Lord Lansdowne paid passages for several thousand tenants from Kenmare, Co. Kerry, in the early 1850s.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.7] The Earl Grey scheme (1848–1850) shipped about 4,000 orphan girls from Irish workhouses to Australia as domestic servants.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.8] The Encumbered Estates Act of 1849 allowed bankrupt estates to be sold, and many new owners cleared tenants to consolidate farms.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.0] Pre-paid passages: relatives already abroad bought tickets from shipping agents in America and posted them home, so people with no money could still leave.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.1] Much Famine emigration was funded this way rather than by landlords or the state; assisted emigration paid for only a small minority of those who left.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.2] Chain-emigration: the first member of a family to settle abroad sent money and tickets for the next, who in turn paid for others, so whole families and parishes moved in stages.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.3] Emigrants from the same district tended to settle in the same streets and parishes abroad, where kin found them lodgings and work.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.4] Letters home described wages, prices and fares and were read aloud in villages, spreading information about where to go.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.0] Most transatlantic emigrants first crossed to Liverpool, which had the most frequent sailings; others sailed direct from Cork harbour, Dublin, Limerick, Sligo, Derry, Galway and smaller ports.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.1] Fares to Quebec were cheaper than to New York, so the poorest emigrants went to British North America.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.2] The US Passenger Acts of 1847 limited the number of passengers per ship and raised fares on the US route, diverting more of the poorest and sickest emigrants to Canada.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.3] Steerage passengers were crammed into the lower decks of sailing ships for a voyage of six weeks or more, with poor ventilation, little water and food often below what the law required.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.4] Typhus (ship fever), spread by lice, and dysentery killed thousands at sea; ships with high death rates became known as coffin ships.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.5] Of about 100,000 people who sailed for British North America in 1847, an estimated 17,000 or more died at sea, in quarantine or soon after arrival.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.6] Ports of arrival included New York, Boston, Philadelphia, Quebec and Saint John, where fever sheds and quarantine stations were set up.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.b] Grosse Isle (Grosse Île) was the quarantine station for the port of Quebec and the first North American landfall for tens of thousands of Famine emigrants.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.0] Grosse Isle is an island in the St Lawrence River about 48 km downstream from Quebec City.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.1] The quarantine station was opened in 1832 to stop cholera reaching Quebec from emigrant ships.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.2] Every ship bound for Quebec had to stop there; a medical officer inspected passengers, and the sick were landed at the hospital while the healthy were held in sheds or on board.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.3] The medical superintendent was Dr George Mellis Douglas, who had been its medical superintendent since 1836.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.4] The station was built for the normal flow of a season, and its hospital sheds held only a few hundred patients when the 1847 emigration began.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.0] The first ship of the season, the Syria, arrived in mid-May 1847 with fever on board, and the hospital was full within days.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.1] By 31 May 1847 about 40 ships carrying some 12,500 passengers lay at anchor off the island, in a queue stretching down the river.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.2] Ships waited days or weeks for inspection, and the sick and dead lay in the holds alongside the healthy, so fever spread among people who had been well.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.3] Tents and new sheds were put up, but sick and dying people lay on the ground and on the beaches; healthy passengers were crowded into sheds where many caught typhus.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.4] Dr Douglas warned the authorities in advance and asked for more help, and his reports are a major source on the season; several doctors on the island caught typhus and some died.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.5] Catholic priests from the diocese of Quebec, many of them French-Canadian, and Anglican clergy took turns on the island; several caught typhus and some died.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.6] More than 5,000 people were buried on the island in 1847, most of them Irish; some estimates of the deaths on the island are higher.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.7] Passengers passed as healthy carried typhus up the river: about 6,000 Irish emigrants died in the fever sheds at Montreal in 1847, and Montreal's mayor John Mills died of typhus while helping the sick.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.8] Hundreds of children orphaned at Grosse Isle and in the fever sheds were adopted by French-Canadian families, many of whom let them keep their Irish surnames.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.0] Partridge Island, the quarantine station at Saint John, New Brunswick, faced a similar fever crisis in 1847 with ships from Ireland and Liverpool.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.1] New York received far more Famine emigrants than Quebec, but the poorest and sickest were more often diverted to Canada by the dearer US fares; New York's emigration commissioners opened the Castle Garden landing depot in 1855 to protect arriving emigrants.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.2] The medical staff erected a monument on the island in 1853 to the dead of 1847.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.3] The Ancient Order of Hibernians erected a large Celtic cross on the island's highest point, unveiled on 15 August 1909, with inscriptions in Irish, English and French; the Irish text blames the Famine on British rule more bluntly than the English text.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.4] The quarantine station closed in 1937, and the island became a Canadian national historic site; after Irish-Canadian campaigning it became, in the 1990s, Grosse Île and the Irish Memorial National Historic Site.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.5] Nationalists from John Mitchel onwards used Grosse Isle as evidence that British policy, not only the blight, killed the Irish poor; others stressed landlord clearances and the limits of colonial resources.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.6] Grosse Isle shows the human cost of unregulated mass emigration, the failure of governments on both sides of the Atlantic to prepare, and the generosity of French-Canadian Quebec.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.0] Relief versus loss: emigration saved hundreds of thousands from starvation, but it emptied the poorest districts and began a century of population decline.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.1] Choice versus force: pre-paid tickets show emigrants making their own family decisions, while clearances and assisted schemes such as Mahon's show tenants being pushed out.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.2] Contemporary view: officials, landlords and many economists welcomed emigration as a cure for overpopulation; John Mitchel and the Young Irelanders saw it as extermination.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.3] Later view: Kerby Miller (Emigrants and Exiles, 1985) argued that emigrants saw themselves as exiles banished by England, a view that shaped Irish-American nationalism.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.4] Revisionist historians of the 1950s to 1970s played down British blame, while Cormac Ó Gráda, Christine Kinealy and others since the 1990s have stressed failures of relief policy alongside the natural disaster.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.5] Grosse Isle has become the leading memorial to the Famine dead outside Ireland, though the great majority of Famine emigrants went to the United States and Britain.
