[hist-ire4-s3.9.b] Judgements on Irish political life in America and Britain turn on whether the Irish gained real influence despite discrimination, or only built machines and patronage. Assimilation, adopting the language, customs and habits of the new country, is the thread: political participation was one route to it.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.0] Achievement: Irish politicians built lasting power in American cities and in some British seats, so they gave immigrants a route into public life.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.1] Limit: machine politics relied on patronage and corruption, and reform movements challenged it from the 1880s onwards.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.2] Success against discrimination: by the early 20th century Irish Americans were prominent in the police and city government, and Al Smith and Joseph Kennedy had reached national prominence, a sign that discrimination was being overcome.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.3] Contemporary view: nativists and reformers condemned Irish politicians as corrupt, while Irish communities defended them as champions of immigrants.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.4] Later view: historians disagree on whether Irish political success advanced assimilation or delayed it, because machine politics kept communities separate.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.5] Britain versus the USA: the Irish in Britain had votes sooner, but their political voice was channelled into the Irish Parliamentary Party and Labour rather than into city machines.

CANDIDATES hist-ire4-s1: 1. Emigration before and during the Famine, 1840–1855, and case study: Grosse Isle
[hist-ire4-s1.0.b] Emigration was already an established part of Irish life before the potato blight struck in 1845. What changed over time was who left and where they went, and that pattern is the background to the Famine exodus.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.0] Ulster Presbyterians (Scots-Irish) were the first large group of Irish emigrants to North America, sailing to the American colonies through the 18th century.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.1] After 1815 the flow widened, and about one million people left Ireland between 1815 and 1845. Estimates vary, so say 'about one million'.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.2] Pre-Famine emigrants were often Protestant small farmers, weavers and tradesmen from Ulster and the midlands. They could pay their own fares and had some capital to start again abroad.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.3] From the 1830s Catholic emigrants from the south and west made up a growing share. They were poorer and less skilled than the earlier emigrants, so the typical emigrant was changing.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.4] Canada (British North America) was the cheapest crossing of the Atlantic, so many emigrants landed there and then moved on into the United States.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.5] Britain received the poorest emigrants, because the crossing to Liverpool or Glasgow cost only a few shillings.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.6] The 1841 census counted about 420,000 Irish-born people in Britain, concentrated in Liverpool, Glasgow, Manchester and London.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.b] Not all emigration was permanent. Many Irish people worked abroad for a season or settled into the poorest jobs and districts of British and North American towns, driven out by hardship at home and drawn by work abroad.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.0] Seasonal migration meant going abroad for part of the year and then returning. Tens of thousands of labourers from Mayo, Donegal, Galway and other western counties crossed each summer to English and Scottish harvests and brought home cash for the rent.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.1] In Britain Irish labourers worked as navvies (labourers digging canals and railways), as dockers, as building labourers and in textile mills.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.2] Irish districts grew in the poorest parts of British cities, such as Little Ireland in Manchester and St Giles in London.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.3] In North America Irish men dug canals such as the Erie Canal in New York State (opened 1825) and built roads and early railways, while Irish women went into domestic service.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.4] Push factors (conditions that drove people out) were the subdivision of holdings into tiny plots, a population that had risen to about 8.2 million by the 1841 census, dependence on the potato, the decline of domestic linen and wool spinning, and evictions for rent arrears.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.5] Pull factors (attractions abroad) were higher wages and demand for labour in industrial Britain and North America, cheap land in Canada, and letters from earlier emigrants describing work abroad.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.6] Timber ships carried Canadian timber to Britain and Ireland and returned westwards nearly empty, so they took emigrants cheaply as ballast (weight to steady the empty ship).
[hist-ire4-s1.2.b] Repeated failures of the potato, the food of the poorest, turned a steady flow of emigrants into a mass exodus. Ireland was then part of the United Kingdom, and most of its rural poor held tiny plots from landlords.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.0] Potato blight, caused by the water mould Phytophthora infestans, destroyed about a third of the crop in 1845 and almost all of it in 1846.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.1] The 1847 crop was largely free of blight but very small because little seed had been planted, and blight returned in 1848. The year 1847 is remembered as Black 47 for its deaths and fever.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.2] About one million people died of starvation and famine-related disease between 1845 and 1851 (estimates vary). Note that the death toll is dated 1845–1851, while the emigration decade runs to 1855.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.3] Between 1.5 and 2 million people emigrated between 1845 and 1855 (estimates vary), the great majority to the United States, with several hundred thousand to British North America and Britain.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.4] Deaths and emigration together cut Ireland's population from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.5 million in 1851.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.5] Famine emigrants were mostly Catholic, poor and unskilled, and many were Irish-speaking. They came heavily from the west and south-west, and many travelled as whole families rather than as single young adults.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.6] Emigration was panic flight rather than planned movement. In 1847 about 300,000 destitute Irish people landed at Liverpool alone, and many stayed there.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.7] By 1851 about 730,000 Irish-born people lived in Britain, and about a fifth of Liverpool's population was Irish-born.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.b] Government relief did little to keep people on the land, and some of it pushed them off. These policies are part of the explanation for why so many chose, or were forced, to leave.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.0] Public works were the main relief in 1846. The pay was low and late, and heavy labour weakened the starving people it was meant to help.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.1] The Temporary Relief Act of 1847 replaced the public works with soup kitchens, which fed about 3 million people a day at their peak in the summer of 1847 before closing that autumn.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.2] The Poor Law Extension Act of June 1847 made the Poor Law unions (local districts running the workhouses) pay for relief. Its Gregory clause (quarter-acre clause) denied relief to anyone holding more than a quarter acre unless they gave up the land.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.3] The Gregory clause meant a starving smallholder had to surrender the family holding to get food.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.4] Rising poor rates (local taxes that paid for relief) gave landlords a reason to clear smallholders, and evictions rose sharply between 1847 and 1852.
[hist-ire4-s1.3.5] The Encumbered Estates Act of 1849 allowed bankrupt estates to be sold, and many new owners cleared tenants to consolidate farms into larger units.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.b] Some landlords and the state paid for people to leave. Assisted emigration means passages paid by landlords, unions or governments, and it was the clearest case of tenants being pushed out.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.0] Landlords paid passages because shipping tenants abroad was cheaper than paying rates to support them in the workhouse.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.1] Major Denis Mahon of Strokestown, Co. Roscommon, sent about 1,490 tenants (the usual figure) via Liverpool to Quebec in 1847. Many died on the voyage or soon after, and Mahon was shot dead on 2 November 1847.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.2] Lord Palmerston assisted tenants from his Sligo estate to Canada in 1847, and there were reports of their poor condition on arrival.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.3] Lord Lansdowne assisted tenants from Kenmare, Co. Kerry, in the early 1850s. The number was several thousand.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.4] The Earl Grey scheme (1848–1850) sent about 4,000 orphan girls from Irish workhouses to Australia as domestic servants. Do not confuse it with landlord-assisted emigration to Canada.
[hist-ire4-s1.4.5] Assisted emigration paid for only a small minority of those who left, so most Famine emigrants paid their own way or had it paid by relatives.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.b] Most Famine emigrants were not helped by landlords. They were helped by their own families abroad, through two linked practices that the syllabus names as key concepts.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.0] Pre-paid passages were tickets bought from shipping agents by relatives already abroad and posted home, so people with no money could still leave.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.1] Much Famine emigration was funded this way rather than by landlords or the state, because assisted emigration paid for only a small minority of those who left.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.2] Chain-emigration meant the first member of a family to settle abroad sent money and tickets for the next, who in turn paid for others. In this way whole families and parishes moved in stages.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.3] Pre-paid passages were the tickets themselves, while chain-emigration was the stage-by-stage pattern they produced along family networks. Sending one ticket once is not chain-emigration.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.4] Emigrants from the same district tended to settle in the same streets and parishes abroad, where kin found them lodgings and work.
[hist-ire4-s1.5.5] Letters home described wages, prices and fares and were read aloud in villages, which spread information about where to go and how to get there.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.b] Where a poor emigrant went depended on the cost of the fare, and what the voyage was like depended on what that fare could buy.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.0] Most transatlantic emigrants first crossed to Liverpool, which had the most frequent sailings. Others sailed direct from Cork harbour, Dublin, Limerick, Sligo, Derry, Galway and smaller ports.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.1] Fares to Quebec were cheaper than to New York, so the poorest emigrants went to British North America.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.2] The US Passenger Act of 1847 limited the number of passengers per ship and so raised fares on the US route, which diverted more of the poorest emigrants to Canada.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.3] Steerage passengers (those in the cheapest accommodation) were crammed into the lower decks of sailing ships for a voyage of six weeks or more, with poor ventilation, little water and poor food.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.4] Typhus (ship fever), spread by lice, and dysentery killed thousands at sea. Ships with high death rates became known as coffin ships.
[hist-ire4-s1.6.5] Ports of arrival included New York, Boston, Philadelphia, Quebec and Saint John (New Brunswick), where fever sheds and quarantine stations were set up.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.b] Grosse Isle (Grosse Île) was the quarantine station for the port of Quebec and the first North American landfall for tens of thousands of Famine emigrants. A quarantine station holds arriving ships and passengers apart so that disease cannot spread inland.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.0] Grosse Isle is an island in the St Lawrence River, the great river that carries ships to Quebec, about 48 km downstream from Quebec City.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.1] The quarantine station was opened in 1832 to stop cholera reaching Quebec from emigrant ships.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.2] Every ship bound for Quebec had to stop there. A medical officer inspected the passengers, and the sick were landed at the hospital while the healthy were held in sheds or on board.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.3] Dr George Mellis Douglas had been medical superintendent of the station since 1836.
[hist-ire4-s1.7.4] The station was built for the normal flow of a season, so it was unprepared for the numbers and the fever of 1847.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.b] In 1847 the station was overwhelmed by fever ships. The events show what happened when mass emigration met an unprepared system.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.0] The first ship of the season, the Syria, arrived in mid-May 1847 with fever on board, and the hospital was full within days.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.1] By 31 May 1847 about 40 ships carrying about 12,500 passengers lay at anchor off the island (some sources say about 13,000), in a queue stretching down the river.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.2] Ships waited days or weeks for inspection, and the sick and dead lay in the holds alongside the healthy, so fever spread among people who had been well.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.3] Tents and new sheds were put up, but sick and dying people lay on the ground and on the beaches. Healthy passengers were crowded into sheds, where many caught typhus.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.4] Dr Douglas warned the authorities in advance and asked for more help, and his reports are a major source on the season. Several doctors on the island caught typhus and some died.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.5] Catholic priests, many of them French-Canadian, and Anglican clergy took turns on the island. Several caught typhus and some died.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.6] More than 5,000 people were buried on the island in 1847, most of them Irish. Some estimates of the deaths there are higher.
[hist-ire4-s1.8.7] Passengers passed as healthy carried typhus up the river, and about 6,000 Irish died in the fever sheds at Montreal in 1847. Montreal's mayor John Mills died of typhus while helping the sick.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.b] The suffering at Grosse Isle was matched by generosity, and the island's story gains meaning when set beside other ports. This is the material that lifts an account of 1847 into an argument about significance.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.0] Hundreds of children orphaned at Grosse Isle and in the fever sheds were adopted by French-Canadian families, many of whom let them keep their Irish surnames. This shows the generosity of Quebec.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.1] Partridge Island, the quarantine station at Saint John, New Brunswick, had a similar fever crisis in 1847.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.2] New York received far more Famine emigrants than Quebec, but the poorest and sickest were more often diverted to Canada by the dearer US fares. The Castle Garden emigrant landing depot opened there in 1855.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.3] Grosse Isle shows the human cost of unregulated mass emigration, and the failure of governments on both sides of the Atlantic to prepare.
[hist-ire4-s1.9.4] Most Famine emigrants went to the United States and Britain, not Quebec, so Grosse Isle shows one part of the story rather than the typical journey.
[hist-ire4-s1.10.b] The dead of 1847 were remembered on the island itself, and what was said there became part of a wider argument over who was to blame for the Famine.
[hist-ire4-s1.10.0] The Ancient Order of Hibernians erected a large Celtic cross on the island's highest point, unveiled on 15 August 1909, with inscriptions in Irish, English and French.
[hist-ire4-s1.10.1] The Irish text of the cross blames British rule for the Famine more bluntly than the English text does, so the memorial speaks differently to different readers.
[hist-ire4-s1.10.2] The quarantine station closed in 1937 and the island became a Canadian national historic site. Since the 1990s it has been Grosse Île and the Irish Memorial National Historic Site.
[hist-ire4-s1.10.3] John Mitchel and later nationalists used Grosse Isle as evidence that British policy, not only the blight, killed the Irish poor.
[hist-ire4-s1.10.4] Others stressed landlord clearances and the limits of colonial resources, which means the island is used to support more than one interpretation.
[hist-ire4-s1.11.b] Judging Famine emigration means weighing what it achieved against what it cost, and how people at the time saw it against how historians see it now.
[hist-ire4-s1.11.0] Relief versus loss: emigration saved many from starvation, but it emptied poor districts and began long-term population decline.
[hist-ire4-s1.11.1] Choice versus force: pre-paid tickets show families making their own decisions, while clearances and assisted schemes such as Mahon's show tenants being pushed out.
[hist-ire4-s1.11.2] Contemporary view: officials, landlords and many economists welcomed emigration as a cure for overpopulation, while John Mitchel and the Young Irelanders saw it as expulsion.
[hist-ire4-s1.11.3] Later view: Kerby Miller (Emigrants and Exiles, 1985) argued that emigrants saw themselves as exiles banished by England.
[hist-ire4-s1.11.4] Revisionist historians of the 1950s to 1970s played down British blame, while Cormac Ó Gráda and Christine Kinealy, since the 1990s, stress failures of relief policy as well as the blight.
[hist-ire4-s1.11.5] Grosse Isle has become a leading memorial to the Famine, but most Famine emigrants went to the United States and Britain, so it must be set against that wider pattern.
