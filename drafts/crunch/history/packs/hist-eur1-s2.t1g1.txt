[hist-eur1-s7.0.b] From the eighteenth century, British farming was transformed by consolidation of land, new crops and machinery, which raised output and drove labourers off the land.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.0] Enclosure replaced open fields and common land with consolidated farms; it was carried out largely through thousands of Acts of Parliament, most of them passed between 1750 and 1850.

[hist-eur1-s7.0.2] Jethro Tull's seed drill, introduced in the early 1700s, sowed seed in rows at a regular depth and reduced the waste of broadcast sowing.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.3] Selective breeding by Robert Bakewell in Leicestershire improved livestock, producing sheep and cattle with more meat and wool.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.4] The General Enclosure Acts of 1801 and 1845 simplified the procedure, and the Corn Laws of 1815 protected British grain growers by keeping out cheap imports.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.5] Mechanisation, including the threshing machine, reduced the need for hand labour at harvest, and anger over it fed the Swing Riots of 1830 in southern England.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.6] Many agricultural labourers lost their common rights and their access to land, and they moved to towns or emigrated.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.7] New fertilisers, including guano imported from Peru and the superphosphate produced by John Bennet Lawes from 1842, raised yields from the 1840s.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.0] In France the Revolution of 1789 abolished feudal dues and divided many estates, so the peasantry owned much of the land by 1815, and farming was typically small-scale.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.1] In Prussia the Stein–Hardenberg reforms from 1807 abolished serfdom in stages and allowed peasants to become owners of their land, though many had to give up part of it to the noble estates.

[hist-eur1-s7.1.4] The agricultural crisis of 1846–47 helped to create the economic conditions that fed the revolutions of 1848.

[hist-eur1-s7.2.4] The coke-smelting process of Abraham Darby at Coalbrookdale produced iron on a larger scale, and iron was used for rails, bridges and machinery.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.5] The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830 and was the first steam-hauled passenger railway with a timetable, and the rail network expanded rapidly in the 1840s.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.6] George Stephenson's locomotive Rocket won the Rainhill Trials in 1829, showing that steam locomotives could haul trains effectively.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.7] Henry Bessemer's converter (1856) made steel cheaply, and the Great Exhibition of 1851 presented Britain as the workshop of the world.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.0] Belgium was the first country on the European continent to industrialise, with coal mines in Hainaut and Liège and early iron and textile works.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.1] France industrialised more slowly, with the textile industry in Alsace and Normandy, and a railway network that was built largely from the 1840s.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.2] The first French line, from Saint-Étienne to Andrézieux, carried coal in the late 1820s, and the Railway Law of 1842 set out a national network under state and company partnership.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.3] In the German lands the Ruhr coalfield and the Silesian coalfield grew rapidly, and the first German railway, between Nuremberg and Fürth, opened in 1835.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.4] The Zollverein's removal of internal tariffs encouraged trade and investment in the German lands, supporting industrial growth in Prussia and the Rhineland.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.5] Krupp's steel works at Essen expanded from the 1850s, and the steel industry of the Ruhr was built on coal and iron from the region.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.6] Industrial growth on the continent was often financed by banks and investment, while in Britain it was financed mainly from profits and private capital.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.0] Factories concentrated labour under supervision, with workers working long hours at fixed times, often 12 to 14 hours a day in British cotton mills.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.1] Children and women made up a large share of the textile workforce, and children could start work at an early age.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.2] The Factory Act of 1833 barred children under nine from most textile factories and limited older children to shorter hours, with inspectors to enforce it.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.3] The Mines Act of 1842 banned women and children under ten from working underground in coal mines.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.4] The Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited the working day of women and young persons in British textile mills to ten hours.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.5] Accidents, machinery injuries, poor ventilation and long shifts were common, and little compensation was paid to injured workers.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.6] The Luddite attacks on machinery in 1811–16 showed the hostility of hand workers to the new machines, and the Sadler Committee of 1832 gathered evidence of the abuse of child workers.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.b] Laissez-faire was the belief that markets should be left free from government control. It shaped British policy in the period, though the state increasingly intervened in working conditions and public health.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.0] Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) argued that free trade and competition produce prosperity, and laissez-faire ideas were widely accepted by British economists.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.1] The Corn Laws, which kept British grain prices high by restricting imports, were repealed in 1846 under Peel, a change that marked a victory for free trade.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.2] The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 required the poor to enter workhouses to receive relief, reflecting the belief that the state should not support the able-bodied.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.3] The state intervened in working hours and child labour through the Factory Acts, but it refused to regulate wages or the general conditions of industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.4] Continental governments, notably Prussia and France, were more willing to use the state to promote industry through tariffs, railways and state-backed banks.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.5] Thomas Malthus and David Ricardo gave laissez-faire a theoretical support, while Friedrich List argued in 1841 that German industry needed protective tariffs.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.0] Manchester, the centre of cotton manufacturing, grew from about 75,000 people in 1801 to about 300,000 in 1851.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.1] Industrial cities such as Liverpool, Leeds and Birmingham also grew quickly, and new towns grew up around coal and iron works.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.2] Migration from the countryside, including Irish migrants fleeing the Famine, brought large numbers of workers into the cities in the 1840s.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.3] In the German lands the Ruhr towns of Essen, Bochum and Dortmund expanded quickly during the 1850s and 1860s, drawing workers from rural Prussia, with migrants from the Polish-speaking east arriving in large numbers later.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.4] Paris grew through migration from the provinces, and its population roughly doubled to about a million in the first half of the nineteenth century.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.5] The 1851 census showed that for the first time more than half of the people of Britain lived in towns.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.0] Overcrowded housing, poor sanitation and open sewers in the industrial towns caused high death rates and frequent epidemics.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.1] Cholera reached Britain in 1831–32 and again in 1848–49, and it struck Paris in 1832 and 1849, killing thousands in each city.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.2] Edwin Chadwick's Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population (1842) showed the links between poor living conditions and disease, and it led to public health reform.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.3] The Public Health Act of 1848 created a General Board of Health in Britain, with powers over drainage, water supply and the removal of waste.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.4] In 1854 the London physician John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in Soho to a contaminated water pump on Broad Street, supporting the link between water and disease.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.5] Friedrich Engels described the conditions of the working class in Manchester in The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845), a witness account that influenced the debate on reform.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.6] Industrial towns had back-to-back houses and cellar dwellings, such as Little Ireland in Manchester, with no drains and shared privies.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.7] The Peabody Trust, founded in 1862, built model housing for London's poor, and the Public Health Act of 1875 made sanitary provision compulsory for local authorities.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.0] The Guizot Law of 1833 required each commune in France to maintain a boys' primary school and encouraged teacher training, and it became the basis of the French primary system.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.1] Prussia had required children to attend school since the eighteenth century (a decree of 1763), and the Prussian state supervised village schools and their teachers.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.2] The British government made its first grant for education in 1833, and it gave grants to the voluntary religious schools run by the churches.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.3] The Elementary Education Act of 1870 in Britain, introduced by W. E. Forster, set up school boards to provide schools where voluntary provision was inadequate.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.4] Rising literacy spread the reach of newspapers and political ideas, which helped the spread of nationalism and political mobilisation among the middle and working classes.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.0] Gains: industrial growth raised output and national wealth, and agricultural improvements increased food production in the long run.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.1] Costs: the enclosure of common land and industrial work drove many people into poverty and urban slums in the short term.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.2] Gains: factory legislation, public health reform and primary education improved the lives of many people by the 1870s.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.3] Costs: child labour, unsafe factories and disease remained serious problems even after reform began.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.4] Overall: the economic changes of the period brought real gains in wealth and education, but they were achieved at a heavy social cost, and governments responded slowly and unevenly to the problems they created.
