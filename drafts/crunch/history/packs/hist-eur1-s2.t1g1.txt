TARGET hist-eur1-s2: 2. Case study: the 1848 Revolution in Germany
[hist-eur1-s2.0.b] In 1848 revolutions broke out in one capital after another within a few weeks. Germany was one part of a Europe-wide wave, so it helps to know the pattern before looking at the German case.
[hist-eur1-s2.0.0] The first rising came in Palermo in January 1848. Then Paris rose on 22-24 February, when King Louis-Philippe fell and the Second Republic was declared; this news was the spark for the rest.
[hist-eur1-s2.0.1] The risings then came fast: Vienna on 13 March, Budapest on 15 March, Venice on 17 March, and Berlin and Milan on 18 March (Milan's Five Days of street fighting ran 18-22 March). Prague's rising came later, in June.
[hist-eur1-s2.0.2] The common causes were the crop failures and economic depression of 1846-47, which made ordinary people desperate, and middle-class liberal demands for constitutions (written limits on a ruler's power).
[hist-eur1-s2.0.3] Nationalism (the wish of a people with a shared language and culture to form its own state) drove Germans, Italians, Hungarians and Czechs alike, so each rising had a national as well as a liberal side.
[hist-eur1-s2.0.4] The example of Paris spread so quickly because newspapers, railways and the telegraph carried the news within days, which encouraged crowds elsewhere to press their own rulers.
[hist-eur1-s2.0.5] Britain and Russia escaped revolution in 1848. By 1849 nearly every rising had been defeated by the old monarchies and their armies, a pattern the German case follows.
[hist-eur1-s2.1.b] To understand 1848 in Germany you need to know what the revolutionaries wanted to change. Germany was not one country but a loose league of states.
[hist-eur1-s2.1.0] The German Confederation, set up in 1815, was a loose league of German states (39 at its foundation) with a Diet, its assembly of state delegates, at Frankfurt under Austrian presidency. Austria and Prussia were the two great powers inside it.
[hist-eur1-s2.1.1] Metternich's system of repression, meaning censorship, police spies and the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819, had kept liberal and national movements down. His fall in March 1848 removed the system's chief defender.
[hist-eur1-s2.1.2] The Zollverein (customs union) of 1834 had already linked most German states economically under Prussia, with Austria outside it, so many Germans were used to thinking of a Germany without Austria.
[hist-eur1-s2.2.b] The German revolution grew out of economic distress, liberal frustration with the system of 1815 and the wish for a united German nation. Each cause affected a different group, and news from Paris in February 1848 acted as the trigger.
[hist-eur1-s2.2.0] Potato blight from 1845 and poor grain harvests in 1846 and 1847 sharply raised food prices, so the poor went hungry and hunger riots broke out in many areas.
[hist-eur1-s2.2.1] A commercial and industrial depression in 1847 brought bankruptcies and unemployment. It hit artisans (skilled craftsmen) already threatened by factory competition; the Silesian weavers' rising of 1844 had shown the desperation of hand workers.
[hist-eur1-s2.2.2] Liberals, mostly educated middle-class men, wanted constitutions, freedom of the press and assembly, trial by jury and a German national parliament.
[hist-eur1-s2.2.3] Nationalists wanted a German nation state, but disagreed whether it should include the German lands of the Habsburg Empire, the territories ruled by Austria's emperor.
[hist-eur1-s2.2.4] Peasants in the south and west wanted an end to the remaining feudal dues and services owed to landlords, which were a burden left over from the old order.
[hist-eur1-s2.2.5] Liberal opposition had grown in the southern state parliaments and in Prussia's United Diet of 1847, which refused to vote a loan for a railway without constitutional concessions from the king.
[hist-eur1-s2.2.6] The trigger was the news of the February revolution in Paris, which showed that a monarch could be overthrown and encouraged all these groups to act at once.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.b] Within weeks of the Paris revolution, rulers across the German states gave way to crowds rather than risk being overthrown. The pattern was protest, then concession.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.0] A mass meeting at Mannheim in Baden on 27 February 1848 demanded a free press, trial by jury, citizens' militias and a German parliament, and these March demands spread across the states.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.1] Rulers appointed liberal March ministries, promised constitutions and lifted censorship in Baden, Württemberg, Hesse, Saxony and Hanover, because they feared a revolution like the one in Paris.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.2] In Bavaria, Ludwig I, already discredited by his affair with the dancer Lola Montez, abdicated in March 1848.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.3] In Berlin, troops fired on a crowd on 18 March, and a night of barricade fighting left about 200 to 300 civilians dead (sources differ).
[hist-eur1-s2.3.4] On 19 March Frederick William IV withdrew the troops from Berlin and was forced to bare his head before the bodies of the dead in the palace courtyard, a public sign of surrender.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.5] On 21 March he rode through Berlin wearing the black, red and gold colours of German nationalism and declared that Prussia would merge into Germany.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.6] He appointed a liberal ministry under the Rhineland businessmen Camphausen and Hansemann and called a Prussian National Assembly, elected in May 1848. Do not confuse it with the all-German assembly at Frankfurt.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.7] Peasant risings in the south-west in March forced governments to begin abolishing feudal dues.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.b] The liberals who now held influence needed a way to create a German parliament. They began with an informal meeting, which also showed the split between moderates and radicals.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.0] The Vorparlament (pre-parliament), a self-appointed gathering of liberal politicians, met at Frankfurt from 31 March to 3 April 1848. It was not the Frankfurt National Assembly, which came later.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.1] It rejected the radicals' call for a republic and arranged elections to a national assembly by broad manhood suffrage (the vote for adult men), although each state set its own rules and some excluded servants and men on poor relief.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.2] Disappointed radicals in Baden, led by Friedrich Hecker and Gustav Struve, launched a republican rising in April 1848, which federal troops defeated at Kandern on 20 April.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.b] The elected assembly met in May 1848 and was meant to write a constitution for all Germany. Its membership explains both its quality and its weakness.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.0] The Frankfurt National Assembly opened on 18 May 1848 in the Paulskirche (St Paul's Church) in Frankfurt.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.1] About 800 men sat at some point, including substitutes, because membership changed over time. Most were lawyers, judges, civil servants, teachers and professors, so it was nicknamed the professors' parliament.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.2] Craftsmen were few, and there were almost no peasants or workers among the deputies, which means the poor were barely represented and had little reason to defend the Assembly.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.3] The Assembly elected Heinrich von Gagern, a moderate liberal from Hesse-Darmstadt, as its president, which shows that moderates led it.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.4] Deputies formed clubs named after their meeting places, the forerunners of German political parties, broadly a right, a liberal centre and a democratic left.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.5] On 29 June 1848 it chose Archduke John of Austria as Reichsverweser (imperial regent) at the head of a Provisional Central Power. This government had no army, civil service or tax revenue of its own, so it relied on the state governments.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.b] The Assembly had to decide what rights Germans should have and which territory the new Germany should cover. The first was settled, but the second divided it.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.0] The Basic Rights of the German People, passed in December 1848, guaranteed equality before the law, freedom of the press, speech, religion, assembly and movement, and trial by jury.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.1] They abolished the nobility as a privileged estate, landlords' private courts and feudal dues, and abolished the death penalty except under martial law.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.2] Grossdeutsch (Greater German) meant a Germany including the German-speaking Habsburg lands. Catholics, Austrians and many southerners favoured it.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.3] Kleindeutsch (Lesser German) meant a Germany united under Prussia without Austria. Gagern and most Protestant northern liberals favoured it.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.4] The Grossdeutsch plan failed because Prince Felix Schwarzenberg, Austria's chief minister from November 1848, insisted, finally in March 1849, that the whole multinational Habsburg Empire must enter any German state or none of it would.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.5] The Assembly also voted in 1848 to include part of the Prussian province of Posen, where Poles were the majority, and Czech leaders refused to send deputies to Frankfurt. Liberal nationalism could deny other nations' rights.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.b] A quarrel over two border duchies tested the Assembly's power and exposed that it could not enforce its will.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.0] The duchies of Schleswig and Holstein were ruled by the King of Denmark. Holstein, wholly German, belonged to the German Confederation, while Schleswig, with a mixed Danish and German population, did not.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.1] When the new Danish king, Frederick VII, moved to incorporate Schleswig into Denmark in March 1848, the German population rose and set up a provisional government at Kiel.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.2] Prussian troops under General Wrangel invaded on the Confederation's behalf, which the Assembly hailed as a national war.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.3] Under pressure from Britain and Russia, Prussia signed the Malmö armistice in August 1848 without consulting Frankfurt.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.4] The Assembly first rejected the armistice and then, having no army of its own, accepted it on 16 September 1848, which showed its dependence on Prussia.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.5] Radicals rioted in Frankfurt on 18 September and murdered two conservative deputies, Prince Felix Lichnowsky and General Hans von Auerswald. The Assembly had to be saved by Prussian and Austrian troops.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.6] Fighting resumed in 1849, but Prussia made peace with Denmark in 1850, the duchies' army was defeated at Idstedt in July 1850, and the London Protocol of 1852 confirmed the Danish king's rule.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.b] By late 1848 the old governments had regained control of their capitals through force, while the liberals at Frankfurt were still debating.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.0] In June 1848 the Austrian general Windischgrätz bombarded Prague and crushed the Czech rising, and in July Radetzky defeated Piedmont at Custoza.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.1] A radical rising in Vienna in October 1848 was crushed when Windischgrätz and the Croatian commander Jelačić retook the city on 31 October.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.2] Robert Blum, a radical Frankfurt deputy from Saxony, was shot in Vienna on 9 November 1848, despite his immunity as a member of the Assembly, which showed how little the Assembly's protection was worth.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.3] Schwarzenberg became Austrian chief minister in November, and on 2 December 1848 the 18-year-old Franz Joseph replaced Emperor Ferdinand.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.4] In Berlin, Frederick William IV appointed a conservative ministry under Count Brandenburg, and General Wrangel's troops entered the city on 10 November 1848 without resistance.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.5] On 5 December 1848 the king dissolved the Prussian National Assembly and imposed a constitution of his own, which kept royal control of the army and ministers.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.6] In May 1849 Prussia brought in the three-class franchise, which divided voters into three classes by the taxes they paid. Each class chose a third of the electors, so a small wealthy minority had a vastly greater say than the poor majority.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.b] With Austria and Prussia recovering, the Assembly finished its constitution and offered the crown to Prussia's king. His answer decided the revolution's fate.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.0] The Frankfurt constitution, completed on 27-28 March 1849, created a federal German Empire with a hereditary emperor, a lower house elected by manhood suffrage and an upper house representing the states.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.1] On 28 March 1849 the Assembly elected Frederick William IV Emperor of the Germans by 290 votes, with 248 deputies abstaining.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.2] Frederick William gave an evasive answer to the Assembly's delegation on 3 April and formally refused the crown on 28 April 1849. He would not accept a crown from an elected assembly rather than from his fellow princes, and he feared Austria's reaction.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.3] In private he described it as a crown from the gutter, tainted by revolution.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.4] Austria rejected the constitution and recalled its deputies in April 1849, because a German empire without Austria threatened its position.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.5] Twenty-eight smaller states accepted the constitution by mid-April, but Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony and Hanover rejected it, so it never came into force.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.b] After the crown was refused, radicals tried to force the princes to accept the constitution, and Prussian troops put them down.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.0] In May 1849 radicals rose in Saxony, the Palatinate and Baden to force the princes to accept the constitution. Richard Wagner and Mikhail Bakunin took part in the Dresden rising.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.1] Prussian troops crushed the Dresden rising in May and the Baden and Palatinate risings by July 1849, when the fortress of Rastatt surrendered. The Baden campaign was commanded by Prince William of Prussia, the later William I.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.2] Some rebels were executed and many imprisoned, and thousands fled, so the radical movement lost many of its leaders.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.3] The moderates left Frankfurt, and about 100 radical deputies moved to Stuttgart as a rump parliament (a small remnant of the Assembly) until Württemberg troops dispersed them on 18 June 1849.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.4] Thousands of revolutionaries emigrated, many to the United States, where these Forty-Eighters included Friedrich Hecker and Carl Schurz.
[hist-eur1-s2.11.b] Prussia then tried to gain the unity the Assembly had failed to win, but Austria was strong enough to stop it.
[hist-eur1-s2.11.0] The Erfurt Union plan, drawn up by Joseph von Radowitz in 1849-50, aimed at a Kleindeutsch union under Prussia by agreement of the princes, and an Erfurt Parliament met in March-April 1850. It is not the Zollverein of 1834.
[hist-eur1-s2.11.1] Austria, backed by Russia, threatened war, and by the Punctation of Olmütz (29 November 1850) Prussia abandoned the union. Prussians remembered it as the humiliation of Olmütz, a defeat for Prussia.
[hist-eur1-s2.11.2] The old Confederation and its Diet were restored in 1851 under Austrian presidency, and the Diet repealed the Basic Rights that year.
[hist-eur1-s2.11.3] Otto von Bismarck was Prussia's delegate at the Diet in Frankfurt from 1851, so he saw Austrian power at close quarters.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.b] The revolution failed for several linked reasons. The strongest answers weigh them: the Assembly lacked force while the rulers kept theirs, and the other causes follow from that.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.0] The Frankfurt Assembly had no army, money or administration, so it depended on the very governments it was trying to limit. The Malmö armistice and the September riots showed this.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.1] The rulers kept control of their armies and bureaucracies, which stayed loyal and were used to retake Prague, Vienna and Berlin once the shock of March had passed.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.2] Liberals were divided from radicals and workers: middle-class deputies feared a social revolution and a republic more than they feared the princes.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.3] Peasants lost interest once governments abolished most feudal dues in 1848, as Austria did in September 1848, so they stopped supporting the revolution.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.4] Months of debate on the Basic Rights and the Grossdeutsch-Kleindeutsch question gave Austria and Prussia time to recover.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.5] National rivalries with Danes, Poles and Czechs split the revolutionaries and gave the Habsburg army willing allies, such as Jelačić's Croats.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.6] Austria recovered and Russia backed the old order; Russia also crushed Hungary in 1849.
[hist-eur1-s2.12.7] The refusal of the crown by Frederick William IV left the constitution without the one state strong enough to enforce it.
[hist-eur1-s2.13.b] Judging 1848 means weighing what it achieved against what it failed to do, and comparing how people saw it then and later.
[hist-eur1-s2.13.0] Failure: no united Germany was created, the Basic Rights were repealed by the restored Diet in 1851, and the old Confederation returned.
[hist-eur1-s2.13.1] Achievement: feudal dues and remaining serfdom were abolished, so peasants gained a lasting benefit, and Prussia kept a constitution and parliament after 1850, however limited.
[hist-eur1-s2.13.2] Failure: liberals were discredited as impractical, and many concluded that unity would come only through Prussian power, not speeches and majority votes, a lesson Bismarck confirmed in his 'blood and iron' speech of 1862.
[hist-eur1-s2.13.3] Achievement: the Assembly was Germany's first national parliament, created political parties and provided a model for the Weimar constitution of 1919 and the Basic Law of 1949.
[hist-eur1-s2.13.4] Contemporary views: conservatives celebrated the restoration of order, while radicals and exiles, including Marx and Engels, blamed the cowardice of the liberal middle class. Marx edited the Neue Rheinische Zeitung in Cologne in 1848-49.
[hist-eur1-s2.13.5] Later views: A. J. P. Taylor wrote in 1945 that 'German history reached its turning-point and failed to turn', linked to the Sonderweg idea of a German special path away from liberal democracy.
[hist-eur1-s2.13.6] More recent historians stress the revolution's lasting gains in political culture and participation.

CANDIDATES hist-eur1-s1: 1. The Vienna settlement and the system of Metternich under attack, 1815–1848
[hist-eur1-s1.0.b] To understand the settlement of 1815 you need to know what it was reacting to. Napoleon had spread revolutionary ideas across Europe, and the victors wanted to undo his work and make sure it could not happen again.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.0] The French Revolution and Napoleon spread liberal ideas (written constitutions, elected parliaments) and national ideas (that a people sharing a language and history should form a state), which unsettled the rulers who had fought him.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.1] Napoleon dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in 1806 and set up the Confederation of the Rhine the same year, so Germany was reorganised before 1815 and the old order could not simply be switched back on.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.2] Napoleon was beaten at the Battle of Leipzig (the Battle of the Nations) in October 1813. The first Treaty of Paris (30 May 1814) left France roughly its 1792 frontiers.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.3] Napoleon escaped from Elba in March 1815 (the Hundred Days) and was finally beaten at Waterloo on 18 June 1815. The second Treaty of Paris (20 November 1815) then reduced France to roughly its 1790 frontiers, imposed an indemnity of 700 million francs and left an allied army of occupation.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.4] The great powers of 1815 were Austria, Britain, Prussia and Russia, with defeated France soon readmitted to their company.
[hist-eur1-s1.0.5] The Habsburg (Austrian) Empire was a dynastic, multinational state ruled by one family. Its peoples included Germans, Hungarians (Magyars), Czechs, Slovaks, Poles, Croats, Serbs, Slovenes, Romanians and Italians, so nationalism threatened to break it apart.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.b] Four ideas run through the whole period. Metternich's system makes sense only as conservatism and autocracy defending themselves against liberalism and nationalism.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.0] Liberalism means constitutions, elected parliaments, civil liberties such as a free press and free speech, and limits on rulers. It was supported mainly by the educated middle class and students, which is why universities became centres of trouble.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.1] Nationalism means that a people sharing a language, culture and history should have its own state. For Germans and Italians it meant unity; for Greeks, Belgians and Poles it meant independence from foreign rule.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.2] Autocracy means rule by one person with unlimited power and no constitution or elected body. In practice Austria, Russia and Prussia were autocracies, so liberal demands for constitutions challenged them directly.
[hist-eur1-s1.1.3] Conservatism was Metternich's own belief that monarchy, aristocracy, the Church, tradition and hierarchy are the basis of order. He therefore saw liberals and nationalists as threats to society itself, not just to his government.
[hist-eur1-s1.2.b] The powers that defeated Napoleon met at Vienna to redraw the map of Europe. They wanted to contain France, restore the old order and prevent another bid for European domination.
[hist-eur1-s1.2.0] The Congress met from autumn 1814, as the delegations gathered between September and November 1814, and its Final Act was signed on 9 June 1815, nine days before Waterloo (18 June 1815).
[hist-eur1-s1.2.1] The leading delegates were Metternich for Austria, Castlereagh for Britain (Wellington replaced him in early 1815), Tsar Alexander I for Russia, Hardenberg for Prussia and Talleyrand for defeated France. The first four are the Big Four, with Talleyrand as the fifth delegate.
[hist-eur1-s1.2.2] Metternich was Austrian foreign minister (from 1809) at the Congress and hosted it at Vienna, so he dominated the talks. He became state chancellor only in 1821.
[hist-eur1-s1.2.3] The aims were to contain France, restore legitimate rulers, create a balance of power, reward the victors and prevent revolution. The last aim explains why the powers ignored the demands of liberals and nationalists.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.b] Two principles guided the settlement. Legitimacy meant putting back the rulers Napoleon had removed, and the balance of power meant that no single state should be strong enough to dominate Europe again.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.0] Legitimacy: rulers deposed by Napoleon were restored. Louis XVIII returned in France (1814), Ferdinand VII in Spain (1814) and Ferdinand IV (later Ferdinand I of the Two Sicilies) in Naples (1815). The Pope regained the Papal States.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.1] Balance of power: a ring of buffer states was built around France so that it could not expand again. This ring included the Kingdom of the Netherlands, enlarged with Belgium (the former Austrian Netherlands), and Piedmont-Sardinia, strengthened by gaining Genoa.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.2] Prussia was placed on the Rhine so it could guard against France, and Switzerland's neutrality was guaranteed in 1815, completing the barrier against France.
[hist-eur1-s1.3.3] Britain, represented by Castlereagh, favoured a moderate peace that did not humiliate France, because a resentful France would be a lasting danger to the balance.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.b] The victors were rewarded with territory, mostly in the places that would block France or balance each other. The gains of Austria, Prussia and Russia shaped central Europe for decades.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.0] Austria gave up the Austrian Netherlands but gained Lombardy-Venetia in northern Italy, and also the Tyrol, Salzburg and the Illyrian provinces and Dalmatia on the Adriatic coast.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.1] Habsburg relatives ruled Tuscany and Modena, and Parma went to Marie-Louise (Napoleon's Habsburg wife), so Austria dominated the Italian peninsula either directly or through relatives.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.2] Prussia gained the Rhineland, Westphalia, about two-fifths of Saxony and Swedish Pomerania. Holding the Rhineland made Prussia the guardian of the Rhine frontier against France, the 'watch on the Rhine'.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.3] Russia gained most of the Duchy of Warsaw as the Kingdom of Poland (Congress Poland), with the Tsar as its king.
[hist-eur1-s1.4.4] Britain kept colonies and bases taken during the wars: Malta, the Cape Colony, Heligoland and a protectorate over the Ionian Islands. These gave its navy bases and secured sea routes.
[hist-eur1-s1.5.b] Italy shows the settlement's priorities clearly. Old rulers and Austrian power mattered more to the diplomats than the wishes of the peoples being governed.
[hist-eur1-s1.5.0] Italy after 1815 was divided into separate states, not a nation. Austria dominated it through Lombardy-Venetia and its Habsburg relatives, the Papal States were restored to the Pope, and Piedmont was strengthened by Genoa as a barrier against France.
[hist-eur1-s1.5.1] Nationalist and liberal hopes awakened by the French Revolution and Napoleon were ignored. Germans, Italians, Poles and Belgians were placed under rulers chosen by the great powers.
[hist-eur1-s1.5.2] Judgement of the settlement: it kept the peace among the great powers, but it ignored liberal and national feeling, so it stored up trouble that later broke out in 1820, 1830 and 1848.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.b] Napoleon had destroyed the old German structure, and the Congress had to put something in its place. It chose a loose league of rulers, not a nation-state, which suited Austria.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.0] The Federal Act (June 1815, signed on 8 June) replaced the Holy Roman Empire, dissolved in 1806, with a German Confederation (Bund) of 39 sovereign states. Counts vary slightly with later changes, but 39 is the figure to use.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.1] Its only institution was a Diet (Bundestag) at Frankfurt, made up of delegates sent by the member governments, with Austria presiding. There was no elected national parliament, so ordinary Germans had no voice in it.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.2] Important decisions needed large majorities or unanimity, and the member states kept their own rulers and armies. This meant the Bund could act only when Austria and Prussia agreed (Austro-Prussian dualism).
[hist-eur1-s1.6.3] Its main purpose was the security of its members against France and against revolution, not German unity, which is why it gave Germans no national institutions.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.4] Article 13 of the Federal Act promised each state a constitution with assemblies of estates. Saxe-Weimar (1816), Bavaria (1818), Baden (1818) and Württemberg (1819) granted constitutions.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.5] Austria granted none, and Prussia's king, who had promised a constitution in 1815, did not grant one. This angered liberals, who felt the promise of Article 13 had been broken.
[hist-eur1-s1.6.6] Austria and Prussia were the two dominant members, and their rivalry shaped German politics until 1866.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.b] After the settlement the powers wanted a way to defend it. Two alliances were signed in 1815, and they are easily confused because they had very different purposes.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.0] The Holy Alliance (26 September 1815) was proposed by Alexander I and signed by Russia, Austria and Prussia. It pledged rulers to govern by Christian principles, and Britain refused to join.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.1] Castlereagh called the Holy Alliance 'a piece of sublime mysticism and nonsense' and Metternich called it 'a loud-sounding nothing'. Yet Metternich used it to bind the eastern monarchies together, so it came to stand for the eastern autocracies.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.2] The Quadruple Alliance (20 November 1815, the same day as the second Treaty of Paris) was the working treaty. Britain, Austria, Prussia and Russia bound themselves to uphold the settlement, keep France in check and meet periodically.
[hist-eur1-s1.7.3] Those periodic meetings gave birth to the Congress System, the series of congresses of 1818–1822 at which the great powers managed crises together.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.b] The congresses began as cooperation among the victors, but they turned into a tool for crushing revolutions. Britain would not follow that path, and the split ended the system.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.0] Aix-la-Chapelle (1818) ended the allied occupation of France and admitted France to the Concert of Europe, making the Quadruple Alliance a Quintuple Alliance.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.1] Troppau (1820): Austria, Prussia and Russia signed the Troppau Protocol, claiming the right to intervene against revolutions that threatened other states. Britain refused, and France did not sign. Castlereagh's State Paper of May 1820 rejected general intervention.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.2] Laibach (1821) applied the Troppau principle: Austria was authorised to restore Ferdinand in Naples, and Austrian troops crushed the Neapolitan revolution in 1821, and also the Piedmontese rising of the same year.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.3] Verona (1822): the eastern powers approved French intervention in Spain. France invaded in 1823 and restored Ferdinand VII as an absolute ruler. Wellington represented Britain at Verona and withdrew.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.4] Castlereagh died (suicide) in August 1822, and George Canning became British foreign secretary. Canning broke with the system and recognised the Latin American republics in the 1820s.
[hist-eur1-s1.8.5] After Britain's withdrawal, what remained was an alliance of the three eastern autocracies (Austria, Prussia and Russia).
[hist-eur1-s1.9.b] Metternich's aim was to preserve the 1815 settlement and the multinational Habsburg Empire, which liberalism and nationalism threatened to break apart. At home that meant autocracy backed by watchfulness.
[hist-eur1-s1.9.0] His aims were to preserve the 1815 settlement and the Habsburg Empire, to resist liberalism and nationalism, and to keep Austria's leadership in Germany and Italy.
[hist-eur1-s1.9.1] Austria was an autocracy under Emperor Francis I (ruled to 1835) and then Ferdinand I (1835–1848), who was mentally unfit. There was no constitution or parliament, and power rested with the emperor, his ministers, the army and the bureaucracy.
[hist-eur1-s1.9.2] Under Ferdinand I government was carried on by a State Conference in which Metternich's rival, Count Kolowrat, was powerful, so Metternich was not in sole control at home.
[hist-eur1-s1.9.3] Inside Austria, police, informers, the opening of letters and strict censorship of books, newspapers and the theatre watched and silenced critics, so liberal ideas could spread only in secret.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.b] Metternich needed a reason to extend his methods from Austria to the whole of Germany. The murder of a conservative writer in 1819 gave him one.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.0] In March 1819 the conservative dramatist August von Kotzebue was murdered by Karl Sand, a Burschenschaft (student fraternity) member. Metternich used the murder to argue that radical students were a danger to every German government.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.1] Ministers met at Carlsbad in August 1819 and the Diet adopted the Decrees in September 1819. Friedrich von Gentz helped Metternich draft them.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.2] The Decrees banned the Burschenschaften, put each university under a government curator (supervisor), and allowed liberal professors and students to be dismissed or expelled.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.3] They also imposed censorship on newspapers and shorter publications (under 20 printed sheets), so that new liberal ideas could not easily be printed.
[hist-eur1-s1.10.4] A Central Investigating Commission at Mainz was set up under the Decrees to hunt down revolutionary conspiracies across the German states.
[hist-eur1-s1.11.b] The Hambach Festival of May 1832 alarmed the princes. The Diet responded by tightening the controls of 1819 even further.
[hist-eur1-s1.11.0] The Six Articles (28 June 1832), passed by the Diet after the Hambach Festival, reasserted the princes' authority: state parliaments could not withhold taxes to force concessions, and the Diet could intervene in states where order broke down.
[hist-eur1-s1.11.1] Further measures in July 1832 (the Ten Articles) banned political associations, public meetings and the display of political colours, and tightened press controls. Banning colours was meant to stop displays such as the black, red and gold flags shown at Hambach.
[hist-eur1-s1.12.b] Metternich could not hold Austria's position by Austrian strength alone. He relied on the congresses to justify intervention, and on two other powers to make it work.
[hist-eur1-s1.12.0] Abroad, Metternich used the congresses and the alliance with Russia and Prussia to justify intervention, and Austrian troops crushed risings in Naples and Piedmont (1821) and in Modena, Parma and the Papal Legations (1831).
[hist-eur1-s1.12.1] The system relied on Prussia's cooperation inside the Bund and on Russia's support in Europe, so it was weakened when either went its own way.
[hist-eur1-s1.12.2] Seen together, so that the two sides explain each other, the system had an international side (the Congress System and intervention) and an internal side (censorship, police, the Carlsbad Decrees and the Bund).
[hist-eur1-s1.13.b] The first challenges to the settlement came from liberal and nationalist students and from army-led revolts in the south of Europe. Metternich could crush all of them, but they showed that the old order was not secure.
[hist-eur1-s1.13.0] The first Burschenschaft was founded at Jena in June 1815. These student fraternities spread liberal nationalism, and adopted black, red and gold, linked to the Lützow Free Corps of the war of liberation and later the German national colours.
[hist-eur1-s1.13.1] The Wartburg Festival (18 October 1817) gathered about 500 students to mark 300 years since Luther's Reformation (1517) and the fourth anniversary of Leipzig. The students called for German unity, and conservative books were burned.
[hist-eur1-s1.13.2] The revolutions of 1820–21 broke out in Spain (January 1820, an army rising that restored the 1812 constitution), Portugal (1820), Naples (July 1820) and Piedmont (March 1821). Kings were forced to grant constitutions.
[hist-eur1-s1.13.3] These revolutions were suppressed. Austrian troops restored absolutism in Naples and Piedmont in 1821, and French troops did so in Spain in 1823.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.b] The Greek revolt against the Ottoman Empire was different from the revolutions in southern Europe, because the great powers themselves ended up helping the rebels. This went directly against Metternich's wishes.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.0] The Greek rising began in 1821. Lord Byron died at Missolonghi in April 1824, and his death boosted philhellenism (enthusiasm for the Greek cause) across Europe.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.1] Britain, France and Russia intervened on the rebels' side, for example when their fleets destroyed the Turkish-Egyptian fleet at the Battle of Navarino on 20 October 1827.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.2] The London Protocol (February 1830) recognised Greek independence, and the Convention of London (1832) made Greece a monarchy under Otto of Bavaria.
[hist-eur1-s1.14.3] Metternich had opposed this support for rebels against a legitimate ruler. The case showed that the powers could no longer be held to a common line against revolution.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.b] The year 1830 was a larger blow than anything in the 1820s, because revolution succeeded in the west and rocked the settlement's borders. Only the eastern risings were crushed.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.0] The July Revolution in France (July 1830, the 'Three Glorious Days') overthrew Charles X after his July Ordinances. Louis-Philippe, Duke of Orléans, became 'King of the French'. Charles X was the last king of the senior Bourbon line.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.1] Revolt broke out in Brussels in August 1830, separating Belgium from the Netherlands and breaching the 1815 settlement, which had joined them. The London Conference (1830–31) recognised Belgian independence, and Leopold of Saxe-Coburg became Leopold I in July 1831.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.2] The Treaty of London (1839) guaranteed Belgian neutrality, so the great powers accepted the change.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.3] The November Uprising (November 1830) in Russian Poland was crushed in 1831, and Nicholas I replaced the Polish constitution with the Organic Statute in 1832.
[hist-eur1-s1.15.4] Risings in Modena, Parma and the Papal Legations in February 1831 were put down by Austrian troops, so Metternich's armies were again the means of repression in Italy.
[hist-eur1-s1.16.b] The unrest of 1830 reached Germany too. Some princes gave way, and in 1832 a mass festival made the demand for unity and liberty public.
[hist-eur1-s1.16.0] Several states gained constitutions after the 1830 unrest: Saxony and Hesse-Kassel (1831), Brunswick (1832) and Hanover (1833).
[hist-eur1-s1.16.1] In 1837 the new king of Hanover cancelled the constitution, and seven Göttingen professors who protested, among them the Grimm brothers, were dismissed (the Göttingen Seven). This showed the limits of liberal gains.
[hist-eur1-s1.16.2] The Hambach Festival (27 May 1832) took place at Hambach Castle in the Bavarian Palatinate and brought about 20,000–30,000 people together under black, red and gold flags.
[hist-eur1-s1.16.3] The crowds demanded German unity, a free press and popular sovereignty (the idea that power comes from the people, not the ruler). This was what prompted the Six Articles and the July 1832 measures.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.b] While Metternich policed ideas, Prussia was quietly uniting Germany's economy. This gave it an advantage that Austria could not match.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.0] Prussia's Customs Law of May 1818 abolished internal duties across its scattered territories and set a low external tariff.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.1] Neighbouring states joined Prussia's system. The treaties were agreed in 1833, and the Zollverein (German Customs Union) came into force on 1 January 1834 with 18 states. Baden joined in 1835.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.2] Members traded freely with each other behind a common external tariff and shared the customs revenue in proportion to population.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.3] Austria stayed outside, because its protected industries and separate tariff system could not fit a low-tariff union. Austria was never a member, and Metternich feared the union was building Prussian influence.
[hist-eur1-s1.17.4] Leadership of the Zollverein gave Prussia economic leadership of Germany and accustomed the smaller states to looking to Berlin rather than Vienna.
[hist-eur1-s1.18.b] The system survived the shocks of 1830, but the revolution of 1848 brought it down within days. Economic distress in the 1840s had left many people ready to revolt.
[hist-eur1-s1.18.0] News of the February 1848 revolution in Paris, together with the economic distress of the 1840s, sparked demonstrations in German and Austrian cities.
[hist-eur1-s1.18.1] On 13 March 1848 students and workers demonstrated in Vienna and troops fired on the crowd. The court made Metternich resign that evening, and he fled, reaching England.
[hist-eur1-s1.18.2] His fall removed the main defender of the 1815 settlement, and revolution spread across the Habsburg lands and the German states.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.b] Prince Clemens von Metternich, a Rhineland aristocrat, guided Habsburg foreign policy from 1809 to 1848 and gave his name to the conservative order of 1815–1848.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.0] Born in Koblenz on 15 May 1773, he studied at Strasbourg and Mainz and married Eleonore von Kaunitz (1795), granddaughter of the former chancellor Kaunitz. French revolutionary armies took his family's Rhineland estates, which shaped his lifelong hatred of revolution.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.1] He was ambassador to Saxony (Dresden, 1801), Prussia (Berlin, 1803) and France (Paris, 1806), and arranged the marriage of Marie-Louise to Napoleon (1810). He was Austrian foreign minister from October 1809, was made a prince in 1813 and became state chancellor in 1821.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.2] He was a direct participant: he hosted and dominated the Congress of Vienna, led the congresses, drafted the Carlsbad Decrees and the 1832 measures, and sent Austrian troops into Italy.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.3] He believed that liberalism and nationalism would destroy the multinational Habsburg Empire, so he defended monarchy, legitimacy and the balance of power. He denied that there was a Metternich system, seeing his policy as the defence of the natural order.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.4] His policy was shaped by events he could not control: Greek independence, the 1830 revolutions and the Zollverein all showed the limits of his power, and 1848 ended his career.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.5] In 1847 he dismissed Italy as 'a geographical expression', meaning it was not a nation.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.6] After exile in England (1848) and Brussels he returned to Vienna in 1851 and died there on 11 June 1859. His memoirs (papers edited by his son Richard) were published from 1880.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.7] Contemporaries: conservative rulers, including the Tsar, praised him as the guardian of peace, while liberals and nationalists saw him as the symbol of reaction, censorship and police rule.
[hist-eur1-s1.19.8] Historians: Heinrich von Srbik (1925) and Henry Kissinger (A World Restored, 1957) praised him for the stable peace he built. Paul Schroeder (1962) was critical, calling his policy mere repression,. German nationalist historians such as Treitschke saw him as the enemy of German unity, and Alan Sked (2008) defended him.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.b] A strong answer states a clear judgement at the start and then weighs success against limits with dates. A useful split is short term against long term, and international peace against domestic repression.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.0] Success: the great powers fought no general war among themselves between 1815 and the Crimean War (1853–56).
[hist-eur1-s1.20.1] Limit: the peace owed much to exhaustion after the long wars and to Britain's naval power, not only to Metternich.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.2] Success: revolutions in Italy (1821, 1831) and liberal movements in Germany (1819, 1832) were crushed, the Habsburg Empire survived intact until 1848, and Austria kept the presidency of the Bund.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.3] Limit: the Congress System broke up in the 1820s because Britain withdrew, and Greek and Belgian independence changed the 1815 map.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.4] Limit: Prussia's Zollverein built a German economic unit without Austria, laying a foundation for Prussian leadership.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.5] Limit: it was repression without reform, with no Austrian constitution and the nationalities unsatisfied, so the causes of unrest remained.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.6] Overall: the system delayed liberal and national change for over thirty years but did not remove its causes, and it collapsed within days of the Vienna rising of March 1848.
[hist-eur1-s1.20.7] Contemporary against later views: conservatives saw a guardian of peace and liberals a symbol of reaction, while later historians divide between Srbik, Kissinger and Sked in his defence and Schroeder against him.

CANDIDATES hist-eur1-s7: 7. The changing economy and society: agriculture, industrialisation, urban growth and education
[hist-eur1-s7.0.b] Between 1815 and 1871 Europe was changing from a world of farmers and craft workers to one of factories and towns. Britain led the way, so it helps to know what the change was and why it began there.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.0] Industrialisation, often called the 'Industrial Revolution', was the shift from hand production at home or in small workshops (the domestic system, where families worked for merchants in their own houses) to machine production in factories powered first by water and then by steam.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.1] The British industrial revolution began in the later eighteenth century in cotton textiles, coal and iron, which is why Britain is described as the first industrial nation.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.2] Britain industrialised first because it had coal and iron ore close together, capital (money to invest) from trade and farming, and overseas and colonial markets to sell to.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.3] It also had a large merchant fleet and navy, rivers, canals and ports for moving goods, and political stability, so investors could trust that their factories would not be seized or wrecked by war.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.4] Britain's population roughly doubled between 1801 and 1851 (the first census was in 1801). This gave industry both workers to hire and customers to buy its goods.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.5] A growing population was fed because farming had improved, which is why changes in agriculture and industry belong together in this topic.
[hist-eur1-s7.0.6] Before 1815 most Europeans lived on the land and worked in farming. Western Europe was ahead of central and eastern Europe in both farming and industry, so change was uneven across the continent.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.b] Britain's farming was transformed from the eighteenth century onwards. The first big change was in how land was held, because farmers could not improve land that was shared and scattered.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.0] Enclosure replaced open fields and common land with compact, hedged farms. In the open-field system villagers farmed scattered strips and shared common land, which made it hard for anyone to try new methods.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.1] Most enclosure was done by private Acts of Parliament. There were thousands of them, and most were passed between 1750 and 1850.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.2] The General Enclosure Acts of 1801 and 1845 simplified the procedure, so landowners no longer needed a separate Act for every village.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.3] The result was larger, more efficient farms and higher output, because owners could drain land, choose crops and breed animals without interference from neighbours.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.4] The cost fell on the poor. Cottagers and labourers (people with a cottage and a few rights on the common but little or no land) lost their common rights and their access to land.
[hist-eur1-s7.1.5] Without land of their own, many became wage labourers, moved to the towns or emigrated, which fed the growth of the industrial towns.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.b] Enclosed farms could be run more intelligently. New crop rotations and better breeding allowed farmers to feed more people and more animals from the same land.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.0] The Norfolk four-course rotation grew wheat, turnips, barley and clover in turn. It ended the fallow year (a year when land was left unplanted to recover), so every field produced something every year.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.1] Turnips and clover fed livestock through the winter, so farmers could keep more animals and did not have to slaughter many in autumn. More animals also meant more manure to fertilise the fields.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.2] The rotation was popularised in the eighteenth century, and Charles 'Turnip' Townshend is associated with it.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.3] As background from before 1815, Jethro Tull's seed drill (early 1700s) sowed seed in rows at a regular depth, reducing the waste of scattering seed by hand.
[hist-eur1-s7.2.4] Also as background, Robert Bakewell in Leicestershire used selective breeding (choosing the best animals as parents) to improve sheep and cattle, breeding them for meat.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.b] From the 1840s science and machines began to raise yields and cut the labour needed on farms. This helped output, but it also left farm workers more insecure.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.0] Guano (dried seabird droppings, rich in nutrients) was imported from Peru as fertiliser from the 1840s, so farmers could restore soil without leaving it fallow.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.1] John Bennet Lawes patented superphosphate fertiliser in 1842 and founded the Rothamsted experiments in Hertfordshire, which tested crops and fertilisers scientifically.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.2] Justus von Liebig's book on chemistry applied to agriculture (1840) showed that plants take nutrients from the soil, and field drainage of wet land also raised yields from the 1840s.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.3] The threshing machine, which separates grain from straw, reduced the need for hand labour at harvest. Reapers followed, such as the American McCormick reaper shown at the Great Exhibition in 1851.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.4] Steam power came onto farms later in the period, so machines could do work once done by hand.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.5] Anger over threshing machines, which took away winter work, fed the Swing Riots of 1830 in southern and eastern England, when farm labourers broke the machines.
[hist-eur1-s7.3.6] Life for agricultural workers was harsh: low wages, insecure seasonal work, the loss of common rights and poor cottages. Exams ask about both farm and factory workers.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.b] Grain was Britain's most important crop, and landowners wanted its price kept high. The Corn Laws were their protection.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.0] The Corn Law of 1815 banned imports of foreign wheat until the home price was high. This protected British grain growers by keeping out cheap foreign grain.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.1] The same law kept bread dear for townspeople and factory workers, which is why it became a target of free-trade critics.
[hist-eur1-s7.4.2] The Corn Laws were repealed in 1846.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.b] Farming on the continent changed more slowly than in Britain, and the pattern of landholding depended on what each state had done to the old system of lords and peasants.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.0] In France the Revolution of 1789 abolished feudal dues (payments peasants owed to their lords). By 1815 peasants owned much of the land in small farms.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.1] French farms stayed small and methods were slow to change. Many peasants stayed on the land, which slowed migration into industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.2] In Prussia the Stein–Hardenberg reforms began with the October Edict of 1807 (Stein), which abolished hereditary serfdom (peasants bound to the land and owing service to a lord), in effect from 1810.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.3] Hardenberg's Regulation Edict of 1811 let peasants become owners of their land, but only by surrendering part of it, often a third to a half, to the lord. This is why the nobility kept large estates.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.4] The Junker estates (the big estates of the Prussian landowning nobility) of the east grew large and were modernised. They produced grain for export and for the growing cities.
[hist-eur1-s7.5.5] New crops spread on the continent: the potato became the staple (main everyday food) of the poor, and sugar beet was grown in France and the German lands.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.b] The poor depended on a few crops, so when those failed the result was hunger across much of Europe. The crisis also pushed people to leave.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.0] The potato blight (a disease that rots the crop) struck from 1845, and grain harvests were poor in 1846 and 1847. Food prices rose and there was hunger and food riots across western and central Europe.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.1] In Britain the 1840s are often called the 'Hungry Forties'.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.2] The hunger came together with a commercial and industrial depression in 1847, which helped create the conditions for the revolutions of 1848.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.3] Emigration from the German lands to the United States grew strongly in the late 1840s and 1850s, as rural people and artisans (skilled craft workers) sought land and work abroad.
[hist-eur1-s7.6.4] Ireland also lost many people. In the Famine of 1845–49 about a million died and about a million emigrated.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.b] Three industries led the first industrial revolution: coal, iron and cotton textiles, with the factory and steam power at the centre. Steam and cotton show how invention changed where and how people worked.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.0] James Watt improved the steam engine, patenting a separate condenser in 1769 and adding rotary motion (turning a wheel) from the 1780s. His partner was Matthew Boulton of Birmingham.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.1] Steam was used to pump water out of coal mines and to drive machinery in factories. Because engines did not need a river, factories no longer had to be built beside fast water.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.2] Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769) and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779) mechanised spinning, the making of thread from fibre.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.3] Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785) spread weaving into factories as well. Hand-loom weavers, who worked at home, declined as a result.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.4] Raw cotton for the Lancashire mills was imported in large quantities, mostly from the southern United States.
[hist-eur1-s7.7.5] Britain became the leading exporter of cotton goods, which is why Lancashire was the centre of the industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.b] Machines, rails and bridges all needed iron, and later steel. Britain's lead in metals showed in 1851, when it put its industry on show.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.0] Abraham Darby's coke smelting at Coalbrookdale in Shropshire (1709) used coke (coal heated to remove impurities) to smelt iron. It was background to the growth of the iron industry, which produced iron on a larger scale.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.1] Iron was used for rails, bridges and machines, which is why iron and coal grew together with railways and factories.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.2] Henry Bessemer's converter (1856) made steel cheaply. Steel is stronger than iron, and cheap steel was the basis of later heavy industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.8.3] The Great Exhibition of 1851 was held in the Crystal Palace in Hyde Park, London. It showed Britain as the 'workshop of the world', a nickname for the leading industrial nation.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.b] Railways combined iron, coal and steam. They changed how goods and people moved and created demand for more industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.0] The Stockton and Darlington Railway opened in 1825, so steam locomotives were already in use before the Liverpool line.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.1] The Stephensons' Rocket (built by George and Robert Stephenson) won the Rainhill Trials in 1829, showing that steam locomotives could haul trains effectively.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.2] The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened on 15 September 1830. It was the first inter-city railway worked by steam locomotives, with timetabled passenger trains.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.3] A railway boom, known as 'railway mania', followed in Britain in the mid-1840s, and the rail network expanded rapidly.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.4] Railways made transport of goods and people cheaper and faster, and they created national markets instead of local ones.
[hist-eur1-s7.9.5] They created demand for coal, iron and engineering, and they gave work in construction, so railways pulled other industries along.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.b] Britain's lead did not mean the rest of Europe followed at once. Obstacles delayed continental industry, but Belgium overcame them first.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.0] The continent was slower because of the French wars, poorer transport, internal tariffs (taxes on goods crossing borders) and many separate states in the German lands, less accessible coal and smaller markets.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.1] A peasantry that stayed on the land also meant fewer workers moved into factories.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.2] Continental states copied British machines and hired British engineers to catch up.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.3] Belgium was the first country on the continent to industrialise, with coal in Hainaut and Liège. It had the advantage of coal, which powered engines and furnaces.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.4] John Cockerill, who was English-born, set up ironworks and engineering works at Seraing, near Liège, in 1817.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.5] The Brussels–Mechelen railway, opened on 5 May 1835, was Belgium's first railway, steam-worked and the first stage of a state-built network.
[hist-eur1-s7.10.6] Continental industry relied more on investment banks and on state help, through railways and tariffs, than British industry did. In Britain firms grew largely from their own profits and private capital.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.b] France industrialised more slowly than Britain or Belgium. Its growth was mixed, with some regions advanced and a large rural population behind.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.0] Textile industry developed in Alsace (Mulhouse) and Normandy (Rouen), such as cotton spinning and weaving.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.1] The Schneider brothers took over the ironworks at Le Creusot in 1836, for example, building up iron and engineering in central France.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.2] The Saint-Étienne–Andrézieux line of 1827 carried coal and was horse-drawn at first.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.3] The Railway Law of 11 June 1842 set out a national network in which the state provided the land and track bed while private companies laid the track and ran the lines.
[hist-eur1-s7.11.4] Growth was faster in the 1850s and 1860s under the Second Empire.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.b] The German lands industrialised late but fast. Removing barriers between states and building on rich coalfields let them catch up.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.0] The Zollverein (customs union) of 1834 removed internal tariffs between member states, so trade and investment grew across the German lands.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.1] The first German railway ran from Nuremberg to Fürth in Bavaria and opened on 7 December 1835.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.2] The Ruhr and Silesian coalfields grew rapidly, which gave cheap fuel for iron and steel.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.3] Alfred Krupp's works at Essen expanded from the 1850s, and the steel industry of the Ruhr was built on coal and iron from the region.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.4] Friedrich List argued in The National System of Political Economy (1841) for railways and protective tariffs for young German industry.
[hist-eur1-s7.12.5] By 1871 Germany was becoming the leading industrial power on the continent.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.b] The factory changed how people lived their working lives. Instead of working at home at their own pace, workers had to obey the machine and the clock.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.0] The factory system concentrated workers under supervision, working fixed hours to the pace of the machines for wages, with fines and discipline for lateness or mistakes.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.1] Hand workers such as handloom weavers declined because machines produced cloth more cheaply.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.2] Hours were long, often 12 to 14 a day in cotton mills, so workers had little time or energy outside work.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.3] Children and women made up a large share of the textile workforce, and children could start work at an early age because they were cheap to employ.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.4] Unfenced machinery caused accidents and injuries, and little or no compensation was paid to injured workers.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.5] Poor ventilation, dust and heat made mills unhealthy, so lung disease and exhaustion were common.
[hist-eur1-s7.13.6] In the mines, children and women worked underground in dangerous conditions, such as hauling coal in the dark.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.b] Workers resisted the machines, reformers gathered evidence, and Parliament passed laws. Each law covered a particular group and place, so you need to know which is which.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.0] The Luddites (1811–16, in the Midlands and the North of England) broke machines, which showed the hostility of hand workers who feared losing their living.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.1] The Sadler Committee of 1832 (led by Michael Sadler MP) gathered evidence of the abuse of child workers. Lord Ashley, later 7th Earl of Shaftesbury, campaigned for factory reform.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.2] The Factory Act of 1833 barred children under nine from textile mills (silk mills excepted), and limited children of 9–13 to nine hours and young persons of 13–18 to twelve hours a day.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.3] The 1833 Act also required two hours' schooling a day for child workers, and four inspectors were appointed to enforce it.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.4] The Mines Act of 1842 banned women and girls, and boys under ten, from working underground.
[hist-eur1-s7.14.5] The Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited women and young persons in textile mills to ten hours a day. A Factory Act of 1844 came in between.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.b] Many British thinkers and governments believed the state should leave the economy alone. This belief explains both why change was so fast and why the response to its harms was slow.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.0] Laissez-faire is the belief that the state should leave the economy, prices, wages and trade to the free market.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.1] Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) argued that free trade and competition produce prosperity. Thomas Malthus (Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798) and David Ricardo (Principles of Political Economy, 1817) were later economists in this tradition.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.2] In practice laissez-faire meant free trade, shown by the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.3] The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 gave relief to the able-bodied poor normally only inside a workhouse, where conditions were made worse than those of the poorest independent labourer. This was the principle of 'less eligibility'.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.4] The limits: the state did intervene in child and female labour (the Factory Acts and Mines Act) and in public health, but not in men's wages or hours.
[hist-eur1-s7.15.5] The continent was more interventionist: Prussia and France promoted railways, tariffs and banks. List argued that young German industry needed protection.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.b] Industry and farming change drew people into towns. Cities grew faster than anyone could house them, which is the root of the slum and disease problems of the industrial towns.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.0] Manchester, nicknamed 'Cottonopolis', grew from about 75,000 people in 1801 to about 300,000 in 1851.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.1] Liverpool, Leeds, Birmingham and Glasgow also grew quickly, because they were centres of trade and industry that offered work.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.2] Migration from the countryside had push causes (enclosure, mechanisation, rural poverty and the 1840s crisis) and pull causes (factory work and wages).
[hist-eur1-s7.16.3] Irish migrants came to British cities in large numbers, especially in the 1840s, because the Famine drove them from home.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.4] Paris grew mainly by migration from the provinces, from about 550,000 in 1801 to about a million by the mid-1840s, with overcrowding in the old centre.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.5] In the Ruhr, Essen, Bochum and Dortmund grew fast in the 1850s and 1860s, drawing workers from rural Prussia. Mass Polish-speaking migration came later, mainly after 1871, so it falls outside this period.
[hist-eur1-s7.16.6] The 1851 census showed that, for the first time, more than half the population of England and Wales lived in towns.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.b] Towns had grown far faster than their housing and water. The result was disease, as the poor lived in places with no drains.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.0] Slums had back-to-back houses (built in rows sharing walls, with no back door or through air), cellar dwellings, overcrowding, no drains, shared privies, polluted water and smoke. Death rates were high, especially among infants.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.1] Little Ireland in Manchester was an Irish slum by the River Medlock, described by Engels.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.2] Friedrich Engels, in The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845, published in German), described Manchester, a witness account that influenced the debate on reform.
[hist-eur1-s7.17.3] Cholera, a disease spread by dirty water, struck Britain in 1831–32, 1848–49 and 1853–54, and Paris in 1832 and 1849. Thousands died in each epidemic. Typhus and tuberculosis also killed.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.b] Inquiry came first and law followed, but reform was slow because many believed the state should not interfere.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.0] Edwin Chadwick's Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain (1842) linked dirt and poor drainage to disease and early death.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.1] The Public Health Act of 1848 created a General Board of Health (Chadwick a member) and local boards of health.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.2] It was largely permissive, meaning towns were not forced to act. Local boards were compulsory only where the death rate was very high.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.3] In 1854 the London physician John Snow traced the Broad Street (Soho) cholera outbreak to a contaminated pump, showing that cholera spread through water.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.4] After the 'Great Stink' of summer 1858, Joseph Bazalgette built London's main drainage system (sewers) from 1859.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.5] The Peabody Trust (1862), founded by the American banker George Peabody, built model dwellings for London's poor.
[hist-eur1-s7.18.6] The Public Health Act of 1875 made sanitary provision compulsory for local authorities, after the period.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.b] As economies and states changed, governments wanted schooled populations. Prussia led, and France followed with a law of its own.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.0] States expanded schooling to produce disciplined, literate workers and soldiers, and loyal citizens and subjects. Churches also had religious and moral aims.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.1] Fear of disorder pushed states too, because schools taught obedience, and later came the need for voters who could read.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.2] Prussia had compulsory schooling from the eighteenth century, confirmed by Frederick the Great's General School Regulations of 1763. The state supervised village schools and teachers, and teacher-training seminaries followed in the nineteenth century.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.3] Prussia was seen as Europe's model because it had among the highest literacy rates.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.4] The Guizot Law of 28 June 1833 (François Guizot, Minister of Public Instruction) required each commune (the smallest local government unit) to maintain a primary school, in practice for boys.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.5] It also required each department to have a teacher-training college (école normale). Schooling was not free or compulsory for all, with free places for the poor only.
[hist-eur1-s7.19.6] Victor Duruy's law of 1867 required communes of 500 or more inhabitants to provide a girls' school.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.b] Britain left schooling to churches for a long time, and the state slowly came in with money, inspection and, in 1870, its own schools.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.0] Schools were run by voluntary religious societies: the Anglican National Society (1811) and the Nonconformist British and Foreign School Society (1814).
[hist-eur1-s7.20.1] They used the monitorial system of Andrew Bell and Joseph Lancaster, in which older pupils (monitors) taught younger ones, so one teacher could manage a large class cheaply.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.2] The first government grant for education came in 1833: £20,000, given to the two societies.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.3] The Committee of the Privy Council on Education (1839) was set up to supervise a larger grant, raised to £30,000, with James Kay (later Kay-Shuttleworth) as secretary. School inspectors began in 1839.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.4] The Newcastle Commission, appointed in 1858 and reporting in 1861, found many children were poorly taught.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.5] Robert Lowe's Revised Code of 1862 introduced 'payment by results', so grants depended on pupils' results in reading, writing and arithmetic.
[hist-eur1-s7.20.6] The Elementary Education Act of 1870, introduced by W. E. Forster, set up elected school boards to build board schools where voluntary schools were insufficient. Fees were still charged and boards could make attendance compulsory locally; national compulsion came in 1880.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.b] Schooling raised literacy, the ability to read and write, and literacy changed politics.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.0] Historians measure literacy by the ability to sign the marriage register. It was highest in Prussia, Scandinavia and Scotland, lower in England and France, and much lower in southern and eastern Europe.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.1] Literacy was higher in Protestant northern Europe and lower in the south and east, so the gap was wide across the continent.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.2] More people could read newspapers, books and pamphlets, and a cheap popular press grew.
[hist-eur1-s7.21.3] Political ideas, nationalism and mass politics spread, because ideas could now reach the middle and working classes in print.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.b] Examiners reward judgement. Weigh what the period gained against what it cost, and note that change was uneven.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.0] Gains: rising output and national wealth, and more food from improved farming.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.1] Costs: loss of land and common rights, child labour and dangerous work, slums and epidemics, and the 1840s hunger.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.2] Gains: railways and cheaper goods, and by the end of the period reform of factories, public health and education.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.3] Costs: government response was slow and uneven because of laissez-faire, so harm continued even after reform began.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.4] Uneven change: Britain went first and furthest and Belgium early, France was slower with its peasant farms, and the German lands grew rapidly after 1850. Eastern and southern Europe were largely agricultural in 1871.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.5] Contemporary views: critics at the time such as Engels, Chadwick and the factory reformers saw misery. Supporters saw progress, as at the Great Exhibition.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.6] Later views: historians still debate whether workers' living standards rose or fell in the early decades (the 'standard of living debate'), with broad agreement that they rose after about 1850.
[hist-eur1-s7.22.7] Verdict: real gains in wealth and education came at a heavy social cost.
