TARGET hist-eur3-s2: 2. Weimar Germany and the rise of Hitler, 1920-1933
[hist-eur3-s2.0.b] The Weimar Republic was founded in 1919 after the German Revolution and the end of the Kaiser's rule, and its constitution contained features that could be used to undermine it.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.0] The National Assembly met at Weimar, away from the unrest in Berlin, and adopted a constitution on 31 July 1919 creating a federal republic with a President elected by the people for seven years and a Chancellor responsible to the Reichstag.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.1] Proportional representation for the Reichstag produced many small parties and made stable majority government very difficult to form.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.2] Article 48 allowed the President to issue emergency decrees and to rule without the Reichstag, a power that became central after 1930.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.3] Many judges, civil servants, army officers and university teachers from the imperial era stayed in post and remained hostile to the republic, and the 'stab in the back' myth blamed the November 1918 politicians, not the army, for defeat.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.4] Friedrich Ebert was the first President, from 1919 until his death in 1925; Paul von Hindenburg, a former field marshal, succeeded him in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.5] The Treaty of Versailles of June 1919 imposed territorial losses, military limits and reparations, and Article 231 placed war guilt on Germany.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.6] The London Schedule of 1921 set Germany's reparations at 132 billion gold marks, a sum that many Germans regarded as impossible and humiliating.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.b] The republic faced armed challenges from left and right in its first years, and its judges and army officers were often sympathetic to the right.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.0] The Spartacist rising of January 1919 and the murders of Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg were put down with the help of Freikorps units.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.1] The Kapp Putsch of March 1920, led by Wolfgang Kapp with the Ehrhardt Freikorps brigade, seized Berlin while the army refused to defend the government; it collapsed after four days when trade unions called a general strike.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.2] Political murders by the far right, including the killing of Matthias Erzberger in 1921 and of Walther Rathenau in June 1922, showed the weakness of the state's protection of its leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.3] The judiciary favoured the right: the statistician Emil Gumbel counted 354 political murders by the right and 22 by the left between 1919 and 1922, with far heavier sentences for left-wing killers.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.4] Germany fell behind on reparations deliveries of timber and coal, and in January 1923 France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr, the main industrial region, to take coal and goods.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.5] The German government under Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno called for passive resistance in the Ruhr, which the government funded by printing money.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.b] The printing of money to pay for passive resistance caused hyperinflation in 1923, which wiped out savings and deepened distrust of the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.0] By November 1923 one US dollar was worth about 4.2 trillion marks, prices rose by the hour, workers were paid daily or twice daily and rushed to spend their wages, and barter replaced money in many places.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.1] Middle-class savings, pensions and war loans were largely wiped out, leaving many people who had supported the old order resentful of the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.2] Debtors, speculators and large industrialists such as Hugo Stinnes, who borrowed to buy assets, gained from the inflation, while people on fixed incomes and savers lost most.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.3] Gustav Stresemann became Chancellor in August 1923 and called off passive resistance in the Ruhr in September.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.4] The Rentenmark, introduced on 15 November 1923 and backed by a mortgage on land and industry, ended the hyperinflation; Hjalmar Schacht, as currency commissioner and then Reichsbank president, kept its supply tightly limited, and it was replaced by the Reichsmark in 1924.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.5] The Dawes Plan of 1924, drawn up by a committee chaired by the American Charles Dawes, set reparations payments that rose with Germany's capacity to pay, provided a loan of 800 million gold marks, and led the French to leave the Ruhr by 1925.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.6] The Dawes Plan's loans made the German recovery of 1924 to 1929 dependent on American money, which was withdrawn when the Wall Street Crash began in October 1929.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.b] Hitler's attempt to seize power in Bavaria in 1923 failed, but it gave him national publicity and a platform for his ideas.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.0] Hitler joined the small German Workers' Party in Munich in September 1919; it was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) in February 1920 with a 25-point programme, and Hitler became its leader with dictatorial powers in July 1921.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.1] The Nazi party's early support came from Bavarian ex-soldiers, anti-communist groups and the Sturmabteilung (SA), a paramilitary force founded in 1921.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.2] On 8 to 9 November 1923 Hitler and Erich Ludendorff tried to seize power in Munich, starting in the Bürgerbräukeller beer hall, as a first step to a march on Berlin; Bavarian police fired on the marchers, killing sixteen Nazis, and four police officers died.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.3] Hitler was tried for high treason in early 1924 and used the trial as a national platform; the sympathetic court gave him the minimum sentence of five years, and he served only about nine months at Landsberg prison.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.4] He wrote Mein Kampf in prison, setting out his views on race, Lebensraum and the need to destroy Marxism and the Jews; the first volume was published in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.5] The party had been banned after the putsch; Hitler refounded it in February 1925 and adopted a strategy of winning power legally through elections, but in the prosperous year of 1928 it won only 12 seats and 2.6 per cent of the vote.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.6] Hitler's prominence in the 1929 campaign against the Young Plan, alongside Alfred Hugenberg's nationalist DNVP and its newspapers, gave the Nazis national publicity and respectability on the right.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.b] Stresemann's foreign and economic policies brought Germany a period of relative prosperity and international acceptance, which many historians see as the republic's best years.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.0] As Foreign Minister from 1923 to 1929, Stresemann pursued the revision of Versailles through negotiation rather than force.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.1] The Locarno Treaties of October 1925 confirmed the western borders of Germany and led to Germany's entry into the League of Nations in 1926.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.2] Stresemann shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1926 with the French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.3] The Young Plan of 1929, chaired by the American Owen D. Young, reduced the total of reparations and spread payments to 1988.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.4] Weimar governments built a welfare state: the eight-hour day (1918), large state-funded housing programmes in the later 1920s, and the 1927 Act creating national unemployment insurance, which was funded for far fewer claimants than the Depression produced and broke down after 1929.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.5] Stresemann died in October 1929, and his successors could not secure the political support needed to continue his policies.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.b] The Wall Street Crash and the Depression ended the economic recovery and brought the end of coalition government.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.0] Unemployment in Germany rose from an annual average of about 1.9 million in 1929 to about 6 million registered unemployed by early 1932 as American loans were called in and exports fell.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.1] The coalition of Social Democrats, Centre and liberal parties broke down in March 1930 over unemployment insurance contributions and the budget.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.2] Heinrich Brüning, the new Chancellor from March 1930, governed through Article 48 emergency decrees, cutting wages, pensions and welfare to balance the budget.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.3] Deflationary policies raised unemployment and gave the impression that the government was making the crisis worse, weakening support for the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.4] The banking crisis of July 1931, after the collapse of the Austrian Creditanstalt in May and of the Danat Bank in Germany, deepened the slump.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.5] Brüning hoped deflation would prove that Germany could not pay reparations; Hoover's moratorium suspended them in 1931 and the Lausanne Conference of 1932, under his successor, effectively ended them.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.6] Middle-class and farming groups, badly hit by falling prices, turned to parties that promised radical solutions.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.b] The Nazi party's rapid electoral growth came from its appeal to unemployed people, farmers, small business owners and Protestant voters, especially in rural areas.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.0] In the September 1930 Reichstag election the NSDAP won 107 seats and 18.3 per cent of the vote, up from 12 seats in 1928, becoming the second largest party after the SPD; the Communists also gained, winning 77 seats.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.1] The Nazi party's propaganda combined attacks on the Versailles settlement, the Weimar parties and communism with promises of jobs, bread and national revival.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.2] Goebbels, Gauleiter of Berlin from 1926, ran the party's propaganda with mass rallies, posters, newspapers such as Der Angriff and film, and in 1932 Hitler toured the country by aeroplane, speaking at several rallies a day.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.3] The SA's street violence against communists and Social Democrats gave the party a reputation for strength and drew unemployed young men into its ranks.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.4] In the July 1932 election the NSDAP won 230 seats and about 37 per cent of the vote, making it the largest party in the Reichstag, but Hitler refused any post below Chancellor and Hindenburg refused to appoint him in August.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.5] Nazi voters were disproportionately Protestant, rural and small-town, middle-class (shopkeepers, farmers, white-collar workers, civil servants and pensioners) and young; Catholic areas loyal to the Centre Party and industrial workers loyal to the SPD and KPD were less receptive, though the Nazis did win many working-class votes.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.6] Hitler became a German citizen in February 1932 so that he could stand for President; in March he won about 30 per cent to Hindenburg's 49.6 per cent, and in the run-off on 10 April Hindenburg won about 53 per cent to Hitler's 37 per cent.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.7] Brüning banned the SA and SS in April 1932, but Hindenburg dismissed him in May, and Franz von Papen's government lifted the ban in June, leading to street battles such as the Altona 'Bloody Sunday' of July 1932.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.8] Papen's government removed the Social Democrat government of Prussia in July 1932, in what became known as the Prussian coup.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.9] In the November 1932 election the NSDAP lost about two million votes and 34 seats, falling to 196 seats and 33 per cent, although it remained the largest party; the party was also short of money.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.10] Kurt von Schleicher became Chancellor on 3 December 1932 and tried to split the Nazis by offering the Vice-Chancellorship to Gregor Strasser and to win trade union support with job-creation schemes, but failed to build a majority and resigned on 28 January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.11] Papen, after meeting Hitler at the banker Kurt von Schröder's house in Cologne on 4 January 1933, persuaded Hindenburg to appoint Hitler Chancellor on 30 January 1933 in a cabinet with only three Nazis (Hitler, Frick and Göring), believing that conservatives such as Papen as Vice-Chancellor and Hugenberg could control him.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.b] This entry covers Hitler's route to the Chancellorship. His rule from 1933 is in the notes on the Nazi state in peace.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.0] Born in 1889 at Braunau am Inn on the Austrian side of the border, he served in the German army in the First World War and was awarded the Iron Cross.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.1] He joined the German Workers' Party in September 1919 while working as a political informant for the army in Munich, and became its most effective speaker.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.2] As leader of the NSDAP from 1921, he used rallies, a private army and personal charisma to make the party the main vehicle of the far right.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.3] He was a participant who learned from failure: after the Munich Putsch he chose the legal road to power through elections, while using the SA's street violence to show strength.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.4] Contemporaries were divided: millions of voters saw him as a national saviour, the left saw him as a tool of big business, and conservatives such as Papen saw a demagogue whom they believed they had hired and could control.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.5] Historians agree that he was appointed legally, but differ on why: Alan Bullock, in Hitler: A Study in Tyranny, stressed his opportunism and will to power, while Henry Ashby Turner, in Hitler's Thirty Days to Power (1996), showed that his appointment depended on intrigues among a few men around Hindenburg in January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.b] Weimar governments faced war debts, housing shortages, inflation and then mass unemployment, and their responses moved from welfare spending to deflation to emergency job creation.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.0] After 1918 the republic recognised trade unions and collective bargaining: the Stinnes-Legien agreement of November 1918 gave the eight-hour day and union recognition in return for employers' co-operation.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.1] The Hauszinssteuer, a tax on house owners who had gained from inflation, funded large municipal housing estates in Berlin and Frankfurt in the later 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.2] Stresemann's government ended hyperinflation in 1923 by ending passive resistance, balancing the budget and issuing the Rentenmark, but the middle classes who lost their savings were never compensated in full.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.3] The 1927 Unemployment Insurance Act gave benefit to workers who paid contributions, but it was designed for about 800,000 claimants and was overwhelmed when unemployment passed three million.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.4] Brüning's emergency decrees of 1930 and 1931 cut civil service pay, unemployment benefit and wages and raised taxes in order to balance the budget, a deflationary policy that deepened the slump.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.5] The Voluntary Labour Service (Freiwilliger Arbeitsdienst) of 1931 gave unemployed young men work on public projects for pocket money, and it was widened under Papen and Schleicher.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.6] Papen's September 1932 programme offered tax certificates to employers who hired extra workers, and Schleicher's emergency job-creation programme of December 1932 tried to win union support, but neither had time to take effect before January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.7] Social strain was severe by 1932: millions of families lived on reduced benefit or on poor relief, and thousands of young unemployed men joined the SA, which provided uniforms, meals and shelter.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.b] Judgements of the republic divide between blaming its structure and the Treaty of Versailles, and emphasising the impact of the Depression and the choices of its leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.0] Structural view: proportional representation, Article 48 and the lack of a stable parliamentary majority left the republic dependent on emergency government.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.1] Versailles view: contemporaries on the right blamed the treaty for national humiliation, and this shaped the appeal of parties promising revision.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.2] Achievement: Stresemann's policies produced a recovery between 1924 and 1929, with inflation defeated and Germany admitted to the League of Nations.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.3] Limit: that recovery depended heavily on American loans, so the Wall Street Crash exposed its fragility and left no reserves to cushion the unemployment that followed.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.4] Later historians such as Henry Ashby Turner stress that Nazi electoral support was falling by late 1932 and that Hitler's appointment was not inevitable but resulted from decisions by a small group of conservative politicians.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.5] Contemporary left-wing and liberal critics blamed the conservative elite for undermining the republic, while the Nazi party presented itself as the only force able to restore order.
