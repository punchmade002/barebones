TARGET hist-eur3-s2: 2. Weimar Germany and the rise of Hitler, 1920-1933
[hist-eur3-s2.0.b] The Weimar Republic was born out of defeat, and that shaped how many Germans judged it from the start. To follow the rest of the story you need the basic machinery of the new state and the parties that competed inside it.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.0] Germany lost the First World War: the Kaiser (Wilhelm II) abdicated on 9 November 1918 and a republic was proclaimed the same day, and the armistice of 11 November 1918 ended the fighting.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.1] Democracy means government by elected representatives, with free elections and the rule of law. Weimar was Germany's first democracy, and it was fragile because it was created in defeat and many powerful people never accepted it.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.2] The system had an elected President as head of state and a Chancellor as head of government, who needed the support of a majority in the Reichstag (parliament). Because no party won a majority alone, the Chancellor usually led a coalition of several parties.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.3] The main parties, from left to right, were the Communists (KPD), the Social Democrats (SPD), the Catholic Centre Party, the liberal parties (including Stresemann's DVP), the nationalist DNVP and the Nazis (NSDAP). The SPD, Centre and liberals were the parties that supported the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.4] Reparations are payments that a defeated state makes to the victors for war damage; they became a lasting source of bitterness in Germany.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.5] A putsch is an attempt to seize power by force. Several were tried against the republic, from both left and right.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.6] The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 began the worldwide Depression, which later ended Germany's recovery.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.b] The National Assembly drew up a democratic constitution in 1919, but some of its features and some of the people left in charge of the state made it easy to undermine.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.0] The National Assembly met at Weimar, away from the unrest in Berlin, and adopted a constitution on 31 July 1919 creating a federal republic. All men and women over 20 could vote.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.1] The President was elected by the people for seven years and the Chancellor was responsible to the Reichstag.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.2] Proportional representation for the Reichstag (seats shared out in line with each party's share of votes) produced many small parties, so stable majority government was very difficult to form.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.3] Article 48 allowed the President to issue emergency decrees and to rule without the Reichstag. This power was meant for emergencies, but it became central after 1930, when governments no longer had Reichstag majorities.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.4] Many judges, civil servants, army officers and university teachers from the imperial era stayed in post and remained hostile to the republic, so the state's own servants could not be relied on to defend it.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.5] The 'stab in the back' myth claimed that Germany had not really been beaten and that the politicians who made peace in November 1918, labelled the 'November criminals', had betrayed the army. It blamed these politicians, not the army, for defeat.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.b] The republic's first leaders and its peace settlement set the scene for its early troubles. Many Germans blamed the republic for a treaty that it had been forced to sign.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.0] Friedrich Ebert was the first President, from 1919 until his death in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.1] Paul von Hindenburg, a former field marshal, succeeded Ebert in 1925 and was re-elected in 1932. He was President, never Chancellor,.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.2] The Treaty of Versailles of June 1919 returned Alsace-Lorraine to France, took away Germany's colonies, limited the army to 100,000 men with no air force, demilitarised the Rhineland and imposed reparations.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.3] Article 231, the war-guilt clause, placed the blame for the war on Germany, and was felt as a national humiliation.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.4] The London Schedule of 1921 set Germany's reparations at 132 billion gold marks, a sum that many Germans regarded as impossible and humiliating, so parties promising to overturn Versailles gained appeal.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.b] In its first years the republic faced armed challenges from left and right. Its judges and army officers were often sympathetic to the right, so the threats from the right were treated far more lightly.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.0] The Spartacist rising of January 1919 (a communist uprising in Berlin) and the murders of Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg by Freikorps soldiers (volunteer units of ex-soldiers) were put down with the help of Freikorps units.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.1] The Kapp Putsch of March 1920 was led by Wolfgang Kapp with General Walther von Lüttwitz and the Ehrhardt Brigade, a Freikorps unit. They seized Berlin, and the army refused to defend the government, which left Berlin.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.2] The putsch collapsed after four days because the trade unions called a general strike, so the government was saved by workers rather than by its own army.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.3] Political murders by the far right, including the killing of Matthias Erzberger in 1921 and of Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau in June 1922, showed the weakness of the state's protection of its leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.4] The judiciary favoured the right: the statistician Emil Gumbel counted 354 political murders by the right and 22 by the left between 1919 and 1922, with far heavier sentences for left-wing killers.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.b] In 1923 a dispute over reparations led to a crisis that destroyed the value of German money. This is hyperinflation, the extreme form of inflation, in which prices rise so fast that money loses nearly all its value.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.0] Germany fell behind on reparations deliveries of timber and coal, so in January 1923 France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr, the main industrial region, to take coal and goods.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.1] The German government under Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno called for passive resistance in the Ruhr (workers refusing to co-operate with the occupiers), which the government funded by printing money.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.2] Printing so much money made it worth less and less, which is the cause of the hyperinflation of 1923. Before the war there were about 4.2 marks to the US dollar; by November 1923 there were about 4.2 trillion.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.3] Prices rose by the hour, so workers were paid daily or twice daily and rushed to spend their wages before they lost value, and barter replaced money in many places.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.4] Middle-class savings, pensions and war loans were largely wiped out, because they were fixed sums in a currency that no longer bought anything. This left many people who had supported the old order resentful of the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.5] Debtors, speculators and large industrialists such as Hugo Stinnes, who borrowed to buy assets, gained from the inflation, because they repaid their debts in worthless money, while people on fixed incomes and savers lost most.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.b] Once passive resistance ended and a new currency was issued, the republic recovered. The Dawes Plan then gave Germany a workable way to pay reparations, but it tied recovery to foreign money.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.0] Gustav Stresemann became Chancellor in August 1923 (he held the post only until November) and called off passive resistance in the Ruhr in September.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.1] The Rentenmark, introduced on 15 November 1923, was backed by a mortgage on land and industry, which gave people reason to trust it, and it ended the hyperinflation.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.2] Hjalmar Schacht, as currency commissioner and then Reichsbank president, kept the supply of Rentenmarks tightly limited. It was a temporary currency, replaced by the permanent Reichsmark in 1924.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.3] The Dawes Plan of 1924, drawn up by a committee chaired by the American Charles Dawes, set reparations payments that rose with Germany's capacity to pay and provided a loan of 800 million gold marks. It did not reduce the total.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.4] Under the Dawes Plan the French left the Ruhr by 1925, which ended the occupation.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.5] The German recovery of 1924 to 1929 depended on American money lent under the Dawes Plan, so it collapsed when the loans were withdrawn after the Wall Street Crash began in October 1929.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.b] Hitler built a small Bavarian party into a movement with its own private army, and in 1923 tried to use it to seize power by force.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.0] Hitler joined the small German Workers' Party in Munich in September 1919. It was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) in February 1920 with a 25-point programme, and Hitler became its leader with dictatorial powers in July 1921.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.1] The party's early support came from Bavarian ex-soldiers and anti-communist groups, who were angry at defeat and afraid of revolution.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.2] The Sturmabteilung (SA), a paramilitary force founded in 1921, whose street violence gave the Nazis a reputation for strength.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.3] On 8 to 9 November 1923 Hitler and Erich Ludendorff tried to seize power in Munich, starting in the Bürgerbräukeller beer hall, as a first step to a march on Berlin.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.4] The Munich (Beer Hall) Putsch failed: Bavarian police fired on the marchers, killing sixteen Nazis, and four police officers died.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.b] The failed putsch turned out to help Hitler, because the trial made him famous and convinced him to change tactics. His party stayed small in the prosperous years, until a campaign on reparations gave it respectability.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.0] Hitler was tried for high treason in early 1924 and used the trial as a national platform. The sympathetic court gave him five years, the minimum sentence, and he served only about nine months in Landsberg prison.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.1] He wrote Mein Kampf in prison, setting out his views on race, Lebensraum (living space) and the need to destroy Marxism and the Jews. The first volume was published in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.2] The party had been banned after the putsch. Hitler refounded it in February 1925 and adopted a strategy of winning power legally through elections.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.3] The strategy had little effect while Germany was prosperous: in the 1928 Reichstag election the Nazis won only 12 seats and 2.6 per cent of the vote.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.4] Hitler's prominence in the 1929 campaign against the Young Plan, alongside Alfred Hugenberg's nationalist DNVP and its newspapers (Hugenberg was the owner), gave the Nazis national publicity and respectability on the right.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.b] Gustav Stresemann was Chancellor only from August to November 1923, but as Foreign Minister he won back Germany's place among the powers. Most of his achievements belong to this later role.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.0] As Foreign Minister from 1923 until his death in October 1929, Stresemann pursued the revision of Versailles by negotiation rather than force, a policy of fulfilment.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.1] The Locarno Treaties of October 1925 confirmed Germany's western borders (not its eastern ones), which reassured France, and led to Germany's entry into the League of Nations in 1926.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.2] Stresemann shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1926 with the French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand, in recognition of this improved relationship.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.3] The Young Plan of 1929, chaired by the American Owen D. Young, reduced the total of reparations and spread payments to 1988, so it lowered the annual burden on Germany.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.b] Between 1924 and 1929 Germany enjoyed relative prosperity, and many historians see these as the republic's best years. Look closely, though, and the recovery was less secure than it seemed.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.0] Weimar governments built a welfare state: the eight-hour day (1918) and large state-funded housing programmes in the later 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.1] The 1927 Act created national unemployment insurance, but it was funded for far fewer claimants than the Depression produced, so it broke down after 1929.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.2] The recovery depended on American loans, which meant that Germany had no reserves of its own when the loans were called in.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.3] Agriculture and the middle classes did not share fully in the prosperity, so discontent remained beneath the surface.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.4] Stresemann died in October 1929, and his successors could not secure the political support needed to continue his policies.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.b] The Depression is the slump after 1929, with falling output, bank failures and mass unemployment. In Germany it ended the recovery and brought down the coalition governments.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.0] Unemployment rose from an annual average of about 1.9 million in 1929 to about 6 million registered unemployed by early 1932, because American loans were called in and exports fell. It was above three million by 1930.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.1] The grand coalition of Social Democrats, Centre and liberal parties broke down in March 1930 over unemployment insurance contributions and the budget.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.2] Heinrich Brüning of the Centre Party, the new Chancellor from March 1930, governed through Article 48 emergency decrees, cutting wages, pensions and welfare to balance the budget.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.3] These deflationary policies (cutting spending and prices) raised unemployment and gave the impression that the government was making the crisis worse, so support for the republic weakened.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.4] The banking crisis of 1931 deepened the slump: the Creditanstalt in Vienna collapsed in May and the Danat Bank in Germany in July.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.5] Brüning hoped deflation would prove that Germany could not pay reparations. Hoover's moratorium (a pause in payments) suspended them in 1931, and the Lausanne Conference of 1932, under his successor, effectively ended them.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.6] Middle-class and farming groups, badly hit by falling prices, turned to parties that promised radical solutions.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.7] Brüning, Papen and Schleicher in turn governed through the President, with emergency decrees, rather than through a Reichstag majority. These were the presidential cabinets.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.b] The Nazi party's rapid electoral growth came from its appeal to unemployed people, farmers, small business owners and Protestant voters, especially in rural areas, and from skilful propaganda backed by the SA.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.0] In the September 1930 Reichstag election the NSDAP won 107 seats and 18.3 per cent of the vote, up from 12 seats in 1928, becoming the second largest party after the SPD. The Communists also gained, winning 77 seats.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.1] Nazi propaganda combined attacks on the Versailles settlement, the Weimar parties and communism with promises of jobs, bread and national revival, so it appealed to people who felt let down by the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.2] Goebbels, Gauleiter (regional party leader) of Berlin from 1926, ran propaganda with mass rallies, posters, newspapers such as Der Angriff and film. In 1932 Hitler toured the country by aeroplane, speaking at several rallies a day.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.3] The SA's street violence against communists and Social Democrats gave the party a reputation for strength, and it drew unemployed young men into its ranks, which gave them uniforms, meals and shelter.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.4] Nazi voters were disproportionately Protestant, rural and small-town, middle-class (shopkeepers, farmers, white-collar workers, civil servants and pensioners) and young.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.5] Catholic areas loyal to the Centre Party and industrial workers loyal to the SPD and KPD were less receptive, though the Nazis did win many working-class votes.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.b] Through 1932 the Nazis reached their highest vote in a free election, but they never won a majority, and by the end of the year their support was slipping.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.0] Hitler became a German citizen in February 1932 so that he could stand for President. In the first round in March he won about 30 per cent to Hindenburg's 49.6 per cent.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.1] In the run-off on 10 April Hindenburg won about 53 per cent to Hitler's 37 per cent, so Hindenburg was re-elected.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.2] In the July 1932 election the NSDAP won 230 seats and about 37 per cent of the vote, making it the largest party in the Reichstag.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.3] Hitler refused any post below Chancellor, and Hindenburg refused to appoint him in August 1932.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.4] In the November 1932 election the NSDAP lost about two million votes and 34 seats, falling to 196 seats and about 33 per cent, although it remained the largest party. The party was also short of money.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.b] After Brüning, government depended on the President's favour and on private intrigue, and the SA's violence made politics even less stable.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.0] Brüning banned the SA and SS in April 1932, but Hindenburg dismissed him in May. Franz von Papen, Chancellor from June, lifted the ban, which led to street battles such as the Altona 'Bloody Sunday' of July 1932.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.1] Papen's government removed the Social Democrat government of Prussia in July 1932, in what became known as the Prussian coup, so a major state government lost its elected leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.2] Kurt von Schleicher became Chancellor on 3 December 1932 and tried to split the Nazis by offering the Vice-Chancellorship to Gregor Strasser, and to win trade union support with job-creation schemes.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.3] He failed to build a majority and resigned on 28 January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.14.b] The final step to power came through a deal among conservatives who believed they could use the Nazis' popularity for their own purposes.
[hist-eur3-s2.14.0] Papen met Hitler at the banker Kurt von Schröder's house in Cologne on 4 January 1933, and then persuaded Hindenburg to appoint Hitler Chancellor.
[hist-eur3-s2.14.1] Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933, in a cabinet with only three Nazis (Hitler, Wilhelm Frick and Hermann Göring), because the conservatives believed they could control him.
[hist-eur3-s2.14.2] Papen was Vice-Chancellor and Hugenberg was also in the cabinet, so the conservatives held most posts.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.b] This entry covers Hitler's route to the Chancellorship, from his early life to 30 January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.0] Born in 1889 at Braunau am Inn in Austria, he served in the German army in the First World War and was awarded the Iron Cross.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.1] He joined the German Workers' Party in September 1919 while working as a political informant for the army in Munich, and became its most effective speaker.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.2] As leader of the NSDAP from 1921, he used rallies, a private army (the SA) and personal charisma to make the party the main vehicle of the far right.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.3] He was a participant who learned from failure: after the Munich Putsch he chose the legal road to power through elections, while using the SA's street violence to show strength.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.4] Contemporaries were divided: millions of voters saw him as a national saviour, the left saw him as a tool of big business, and conservatives such as Papen saw a demagogue whom they believed they had hired and could control.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.5] Historians agree that he was appointed legally, but differ on why. Alan Bullock, in Hitler: A Study in Tyranny (1952), stressed his opportunism and will to power.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.6] Henry Ashby Turner, in Hitler's Thirty Days to Power (1996), showed that his appointment depended on intrigues among a few men around Hindenburg in January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.b] Weimar governments faced war debts, housing shortages, inflation and then mass unemployment. Their responses moved from welfare spending to deflation to emergency job creation, with mixed success.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.0] After 1918 the republic recognised trade unions and collective bargaining. The Stinnes-Legien agreement of November 1918 gave the eight-hour day and union recognition in return for employers' co-operation.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.1] The Hauszinssteuer, a tax on house owners who had gained from inflation, funded large municipal housing estates in Berlin and Frankfurt in the later 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.2] Stresemann's government ended hyperinflation in 1923 by ending passive resistance, balancing the budget and issuing the Rentenmark, but the middle classes who lost their savings were never compensated in full.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.3] The 1927 Unemployment Insurance Act gave benefit to workers who paid contributions, but it was designed for about 800,000 claimants and was overwhelmed when unemployment passed three million.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.4] Brüning's emergency decrees of 1930 and 1931 cut civil service pay, unemployment benefit and wages and raised taxes in order to balance the budget, a deflationary policy that deepened the slump.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.5] The Voluntary Labour Service (Freiwilliger Arbeitsdienst) of 1931 gave unemployed young men work on public projects for pocket money, and it was widened under Papen and Schleicher.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.6] Papen's September 1932 programme offered tax certificates to employers who hired extra workers, and Schleicher's emergency job-creation programme of December 1932 tried to win union support, but neither had time to take effect before January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.7] Social strain was severe by 1932: millions of families lived on reduced benefit or on poor relief, and thousands of young unemployed men joined the SA, which provided uniforms, meals and shelter.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.b] Judgements of the republic divide between blaming its structure and the Treaty of Versailles, and emphasising the impact of the Depression and the choices of its leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.0] Structural view: proportional representation, Article 48 and the lack of a stable parliamentary majority left the republic dependent on emergency government. Against this, the constitution worked in the stable years of 1924 to 1929.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.1] Versailles view: contemporaries on the right blamed the treaty for national humiliation, and this shaped the appeal of parties promising revision. Against this, Stresemann succeeded in revising reparations and winning acceptance for Germany by negotiation.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.2] Achievement: Stresemann's policies produced a recovery between 1924 and 1929, with inflation defeated and Germany admitted to the League of Nations.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.3] Limit: that recovery depended heavily on American loans, so the Wall Street Crash exposed its fragility and left no reserves to cushion the unemployment that followed.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.4] The Depression was the decisive trigger: the Nazis won 12 seats in 1928 but became a mass party by 1930. Others stress long-term causes such as hostile elites, Versailles and the weak constitution.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.5] Later historians such as Henry Ashby Turner stress that Nazi electoral support was falling by late 1932 and that Hitler's appointment was not inevitable but resulted from decisions by a small group of conservative politicians.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.6] Contemporary left-wing and liberal critics blamed the conservative elite for undermining the republic, while the Nazi party presented itself as the only force able to restore order.
