[hist-eur6-s6.0.b] The Second World War left the United States with the strongest industrial economy in the world, and wartime production and savings provided the platform for the postwar boom.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.0] US factories produced vast quantities of aircraft, ships, tanks and munitions between 1941 and 1945, and the economy expanded rapidly, with unemployment falling to very low levels.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.1] The United States produced roughly half of the world's manufactured goods in 1945, while its European and Japanese rivals had been devastated by the war.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.2] Wartime wage and price controls, and the savings that Americans built up during the war, left households with large sums to spend once rationing and controls ended.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.3] Wartime shortages of consumer goods gave way to a pent-up demand for cars, houses and household appliances after 1945.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.4] The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, known as the GI Bill, paid tuition and living costs for veterans and offered government-guaranteed loans for homes and businesses.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.5] The GI Bill's home loans helped to create a mass market for housing, which fed demand for consumer goods and for the construction industry.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.6] The wartime government became the largest customer in the economy, and it built factories that private firms bought cheaply after 1945, in industries such as aluminium, synthetic rubber and aircraft.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.b] The United States designed the postwar international monetary order, which made the dollar the central currency and financed recovery abroad.

[hist-eur6-s6.1.3] The dollar's role as the reserve currency, and the shortage of dollars in war-damaged Europe (the dollar gap), meant that Marshall aid and American loans were spent on US goods, and American companies could invest abroad cheaply.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.4] The Korean War and NSC-68 raised the federal defence budget from about 14 billion dollars in 1950 to about 50 billion dollars by 1953, which fed the economy's growth.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.5] The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) of 1947 committed the United States and other states to reduce trade barriers, which suited the world's biggest exporter.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.b] Government spending on infrastructure, defence and science drove growth in several key industries during the 1950s and 1960s.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.0] The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 funded an Interstate Highway System of about 41,000 miles, which opened up suburban development and boosted car production and steel.

[hist-eur6-s6.2.2] The National Science Foundation was created in 1950 and federal research funding grew steadily, with universities acting as partners to government research.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.3] The space programme, created after the launch of Sputnik in 1957 and the founding of NASA in 1958, employed thousands of engineers and contractors in the 1960s.

[hist-eur6-s6.2.6] Defence research funded the development of jet aircraft, radar, nuclear power for submarines and later computers, which spread into civilian industry.
