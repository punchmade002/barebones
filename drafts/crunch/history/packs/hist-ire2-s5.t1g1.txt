[hist-ire2-s5.0.b] Unionism was a coalition of Protestants of all classes in Ulster with landlords and business people elsewhere in Ireland, united by opposition to a Dublin parliament.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.0] Ulster had a slight Protestant majority (about 56 per cent in 1911), concentrated in the east: Antrim, Down, Armagh and Londonderry had Protestant majorities, while Tyrone and Fermanagh were narrowly Catholic.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.1] Protestants of every class were unionist: landlords and gentry, industrialists, shopkeepers, Presbyterian tenant farmers and Protestant workers in Belfast.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.2] Southern unionists were a small minority (Protestants were about a tenth of the population outside Ulster), made up of landlords, professionals, businessmen and Church of Ireland clergy, strongest in Dublin and parts of Munster and Leinster.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.3] Most Ulster Presbyterians had supported the Liberals before 1886, and Gladstone’s conversion to Home Rule pushed many into the unionist camp.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.4] Many Protestants had been alarmed by Gladstone’s disestablishment of the Church of Ireland (1869) and by the Land Acts, and saw Home Rule as the next step.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.5] Colonel Edward Saunderson led the Irish Unionist MPs at Westminster from 1886 until his death in 1906, and was followed by Walter Long, then Carson in 1910.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.b] Unionists opposed Home Rule for three linked reasons: religion, economics and identity.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.0] Religion: Protestants feared that a parliament elected by a Catholic majority would be under Catholic Church influence; the slogan was that Home Rule is Rome Rule.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.1] The Catholic hierarchy’s role in the Irish Party’s candidate selection in 1885, and later the Ne Temere decree of 1908, strengthened the argument.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.2] Economy: Belfast’s linen, shipbuilding, engineering and rope-making depended on British and imperial markets and raw materials, and unionists feared tariffs and higher taxes under a Dublin parliament.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.3] The agricultural south and west had a different economy from the industrial north-east, so unionists argued that an Irish parliament would favour agricultural interests.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.4] Identity and empire: Ulster Protestants saw themselves as British, were proud of the empire, and believed that Home Rule was the first step towards separation.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.5] Protestants also feared for their position as a minority in a self-governing Ireland, and many believed nationalists would dispossess them, as in the Land War.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.6] Liberal unionists such as Chamberlain argued that Home Rule would threaten the union of the kingdoms, not only the Ulster minority.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.7] Protestants held a disproportionate share of Ireland’s land, wealth and professions, and feared losing status in a state run by the Catholic majority.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.8] Many Ulster unionists believed that the Land Acts and the Irish Party’s links with Fenians showed that nationalists would not respect property.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.b] From 1885 unionists built organisations to match the National League.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.0] The Orange Order, founded in 1795, revived in the 1880s and gave Ulster unionism a mass base of lodges across the province.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.1] The Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union was founded in 1885 by southern unionists; the Ulster Loyalist Anti-Repeal Union followed in 1886.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.2] The Belfast riots of 1886 sharpened sectarian division (see the notes on the 1885–86 elections).
[hist-ire2-s5.2.3] The Irish Unionist Alliance was formed in 1891 to unite the Irish unionist bodies.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.4] The Ulster Convention, held in the Botanic Gardens in Belfast in June 1892 with about 12,000 delegates, protested against the Second Home Rule Bill; a Dublin convention followed.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.5] The Ulster Unionist Council was founded in March 1905 as a central body for Ulster, bringing together Orange lodges, constituency associations and unionist MPs.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.6] Opposition to the Irish Reform Association’s devolution proposals in 1904–05 convinced Ulster unionists that they needed their own permanent body.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.7] The Ulster Women’s Unionist Council, formed in January 1911 under Lady Londonderry, mobilised tens of thousands of women for the unionist cause.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.b] Carson, a Dublin-born barrister, led the unionist campaign against the Third Home Rule Bill and made the threat of armed resistance credible.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.0] Edward Carson (1854–1935) was born in Dublin, educated at Trinity College, and became a leading barrister, Solicitor General for Ireland in 1892 and Solicitor General for England in 1900–06.

[hist-ire2-s4.2.b] This part covers his years of peak influence, his fall and the overall judgement; the strengths and weaknesses below are the usual basis for essays.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.0] Between 1886 and 1890 he combined an alliance with Gladstone with firm control over a disciplined party of 85 MPs.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.1] He was a participant in the Pigott and O’Shea affairs and lost control of events after the divorce, when his refusal to step aside split the movement.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.2] Contemporary nationalists were divided: the Parnellites saw him as a leader betrayed by Liberals and bishops, and the anti-Parnellites as a leader who put himself above the cause.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.3] Contemporary unionists saw him as a dangerous agitator, until the Pigott collapse made them look foolish.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.4] Strengths: party discipline, tactical skill, ability to unite Fenians, land reformers and constitutionalists, and a clear command of Commons procedure.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.5] Weaknesses: aloofness and autocratic style, dependence on the Liberals, ambiguity about violence in the Land War, and a private life that he did not manage.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.6] Historians differ: F.S.L. Lyons in his 1977 biography stresses the tragic collision of ambition and scandal; Paul Bew in 1980 stresses Parnell as a conservative landlord politician whose strategy had limits.
[hist-ire2-s4.2.7] His legacy was the idea of an independent Irish party; he also left the mythology of a martyr, which later separatists, including Yeats and Joyce, drew on.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.b] Gladstone’s last attempt at Home Rule was killed by the House of Lords, and the Irish Party spent the 1890s in feud.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.0] The Liberals returned to office in 1892 with a small majority that depended on the Irish MPs; Gladstone, aged 83, introduced the Second Home Rule Bill on 13 February 1893.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.1] The Bill proposed a legislature of two houses, and it kept a reduced number of Irish MPs at Westminster, to meet the objection that they would be excluded.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.2] The Commons passed the bill in September 1893 by 301 votes to 267, after eighty-two sittings.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.3] The House of Lords threw it out on 8 September 1893 by 419 votes to 41, and Gladstone retired in March 1894.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.4] Unionists had mobilised against it, notably at the Ulster Convention in Belfast in June 1892 (see the notes on unionism).
[hist-ire2-s4.3.5] The Conservatives under Salisbury won the 1895 election, and the Unionists governed for most of the next ten years, trying to kill Home Rule with kindness.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.6] In the 1890s the Irish National Federation (Dillon and McCarthy), Healy’s followers and Redmond’s small Parnellite group fought each other, and voters became disillusioned.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.7] The United Irish League was founded by William O’Brien at Westport, Co. Mayo, in January 1898 to revive the land agitation in the west.
[hist-ire2-s4.3.8] The parties reunited in January 1900, and Redmond was elected chairman in February 1900; Healy was excluded.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.b] Redmond rebuilt the party, won land reform and then used the Liberals’ dependence on his votes to put Home Rule on the statute book.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.0] Redmond and William O’Brien took part in the Land Conference of 1902–03, which led to the Wyndham Act (see the notes on land reform).
[hist-ire2-s4.4.1] In May 1907 a national convention of the party rejected the Irish Council Bill, a limited devolution scheme introduced by Chief Secretary Augustine Birrell.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.2] In 1910 two elections left the Liberals dependent on the Irish Party: in January the Irish had 82 seats, and in December 84.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.3] After the Lords rejected the People’s Budget (1909), Redmond’s party backed the Liberals and the Parliament Act (1911) removed the Lords’ veto, leaving only a two-year delay.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.4] Asquith introduced the Third Home Rule Bill in April 1912; it passed the Commons three times and became law on 18 September 1914 (see the notes on the Third Home Rule crisis).
[hist-ire2-s4.4.5] The Act was suspended by a Suspensory Act for the duration of the war, which Redmond accepted.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.6] Under pressure from the unionist threat, in March 1914 Redmond accepted a proposal that Ulster counties could opt out for six years, a concession he had previously refused.
[hist-ire2-s4.4.7] In June 1914 Redmond forced the Irish Volunteers to accept 25 of his nominees on their provisional committee.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.b] Redmond led the reunited party for eighteen years and believed that Home Rule could be won by constitutional methods and kept by loyalty to the empire.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.0] John Redmond (1856–1918) was born at Ballytrent, Co. Wexford, into a Catholic gentry family, educated at Clongowes, and became a barrister and MP in 1881.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.1] He stayed loyal to Parnell in 1890 and led the small Parnellite group after Parnell died in 1891; he sat for Waterford City from 1891 until his death.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.2] He was a participant who negotiated the Land Conference, the 1911 Parliament Act and the 1912 Bill, relying on his control of the party’s votes.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.3] Contemporary nationalists generally admired him as the leader who delivered Home Rule; critics such as Griffith and the IRB attacked his reliance on Liberal goodwill.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.4] His pledge in August 1914 that the Volunteers should defend Ireland, and his Woodenbridge speech of 20 September 1914 urging them to fight in the war, split the Irish Volunteers.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.5] About 170,000 followed him into the National Volunteers and about 11,000 stayed with Eoin MacNeill’s Irish Volunteers (estimates vary).
[hist-ire2-s4.5.6] Strengths: party management, parliamentary skill, a genuine achievement in land and the Home Rule Act, and personal integrity.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.7] Weaknesses: dependence on the Liberals, failure to resolve the Ulster problem, acceptance of partition in principle in 1914 and poor judgement of the new separatism.
[hist-ire2-s4.5.8] Historians differ: older accounts such as Denis Gwynn’s sympathetic 1932 biography and Dermot Meleady’s more recent work present a statesman wrecked by war; others see a leader out of touch with the new generation.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.b] Arthur Griffith proposed a separatist policy of abstention and economic self-reliance as an alternative to Home Rule.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.0] Arthur Griffith, a Dublin printer and journalist, founded the newspaper The United Irishman in 1899 with William Rooney.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.1] Cumann na nGaedheal was founded in 1900 to unite groups promoting Irish language, industry and the national cause.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.2] In The Resurrection of Hungary (1904) Griffith argued that Ireland should follow Hungary, which had won its own parliament within a dual monarchy in 1867 by abstaining from the imperial parliament.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.3] At a convention in November 1905 Griffith’s National Council adopted what became the Sinn Féin policy: abstention from Westminster, a national assembly in Dublin and protective tariffs for Irish industry.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.4] Cumann na nGaedheal merged with the Dungannon Clubs of Bulmer Hobson in 1907 to form the Sinn Féin League; Griffith’s weekly Sinn Féin followed in 1906.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.5] In the North Leitrim by-election of February 1908 the Sinn Féin candidate C.J. Dolan, a former Irish Party MP, was beaten by roughly three to one.
[hist-ire2-s4.6.6] Sinn Féin lost support after 1908 and the party remained small, but its ideas about abstention and self-reliance were revived in 1917.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.b] A younger generation of separatists revived the IRB and used the Ulster Volunteers as a model for armed nationalism.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.0] Tom Clarke, a Fenian who had served fifteen years in prison, returned from the USA in December 1907 and opened a tobacconist’s shop in Dublin.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.1] With Bulmer Hobson and Seán Mac Diarmada, Clarke took control of the IRB and founded the paper Irish Freedom (1910).
[hist-ire2-s4.7.2] Eoin MacNeill’s article The North Began, published on 1 November 1913, argued that the Ulster Volunteers had shown that Irishmen could organise to defend their rights.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.3] The Irish Volunteers were founded on 25 November 1913 at the Rotunda Rink in Dublin; MacNeill became chairman and the IRB had a hidden influence.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.4] Cumann na mBan, a women’s organisation to support the Volunteers, was founded in April 1914.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.5] The Howth gun-running on 26 July 1914 landed about 900 rifles from Erskine Childers’s yacht Asgard; British soldiers then fired on a crowd at Bachelor’s Walk in Dublin, killing four.
[hist-ire2-s4.7.6] After Redmond’s Woodenbridge speech the Volunteers split in September 1914: the majority became the National Volunteers and a minority kept the name Irish Volunteers.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.b] The period shows constitutional nationalism at its strongest and the first serious challenge to it.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.0] Achievements of the Home Rule movement: a united parliamentary party, land reform and, by 1914, a Home Rule Act on the statute book.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.1] Limits: Parnell’s fall wasted a decade, the Lords killed the 1893 Bill, and the Ulster question remained unsolved in 1914.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.2] Sinn Féin and the IRB offered a separatist alternative, but before 1914 they were small and divided; their growth after 1914 owed more to war and the 1916 Rising than to their own efforts.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.3] Contemporary unionists judged Redmond a threat to the Union; separatists judged him too moderate; most nationalists in 1914 supported him.
[hist-ire2-s4.8.4] Historians now often argue that Home Rule was not doomed before 1914 and that its collapse came from the war, the Rising and the conscription crisis.
