[hist-eur4-s6.0.b] Western Europe emerged from the war with damaged cities, disrupted trade and food shortages, which made reconstruction the first priority.

[hist-eur4-s6.0.1] Coal shortages in the harsh winter of 1946–47 slowed factories across Western Europe, and rationing of food and fuel continued in several countries into the late 1940s.

[hist-eur4-s6.0.4] About 12 million German refugees and expellees arrived in West Germany by the 1950s; they added to housing shortages but also supplied labour for industry.
[hist-eur4-s6.0.5] Britain remained dependent on dollars and devalued the pound from 4.03 dollars to 2.80 dollars in September 1949.
[hist-eur4-s6.0.6] The Cold War shaped recovery: the Marshall Plan was designed to prevent economic collapse from strengthening communist parties, notably in France and Italy.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.b] US aid under the Marshall Plan supplied dollars and goods, and it encouraged the Western European states to cooperate on trade and payments.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.0] The European Recovery Programme ran from 1948 to 1952 and provided about 13 billion dollars of aid, mostly in the form of grants rather than loans.

[hist-eur4-s6.1.2] The OEEC later became the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development in 1961, after the USA and Canada joined.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.3] Marshall Aid paid for imports of raw materials and machinery, and some of its counterpart funds were used for investment in industry and housing.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.4] The historian Alan Milward argued in The Reconstruction of Western Europe (1984) that Marshall Aid was a modest share of total investment, and that domestic policy did much of the work of recovery.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.5] Sixteen European states received Marshall Aid; Britain, France and West Germany received the largest sums, and conditions encouraged states to remove trade barriers and to reduce inflation.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.6] The European Payments Union of 1950 allowed Western European currencies to be settled through a clearing system and helped to revive trade between members.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.7] Marshall Aid also gave the USA leverage to shape European politics: it supported the Christian Democratic and social democratic parties against the communists and promoted American-style productivity methods.

[hist-eur4-s6.2.2] The Benelux customs union between Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg was agreed in 1944 and came into effect in 1948.

[hist-eur4-s6.2.4] The fixed exchange rate system gave Western economies stable conditions for trade and investment until the strains of the 1960s undermined it.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.5] The GATT rounds, including the Kennedy Round of 1964–67, reduced tariffs on industrial goods across the Western world.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.6] The EEC removed internal tariffs on industrial goods in July 1968 and introduced the Common Agricultural Policy; EFTA's members similarly removed tariffs on industrial goods but not on farm produce.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.7] By the end of 1958 the main Western European currencies had become convertible, ending the controls on trade payments that had survived from the war.
[hist-eur4-s6.3.b] The economic growth of 1945–1973 was fastest in West Germany, France and Italy, and it was driven by reconstruction, planning and cheap energy.

[hist-eur4-s6.3.5] Skilled labour, the adoption of American production methods and the reintegration of trade all helped sustain productivity growth.
[hist-eur4-s6.3.6] West Germany's economy grew at more than 7 percent a year on average in the 1950s, and its unemployment fell to very low levels by 1960.

[hist-eur4-s6.4.8] The Netherlands received migrants from its former colonies of Indonesia and Surinam, and Italy and Spain exported workers to northern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.b] The welfare state gave citizens protection against sickness, unemployment and old age, and it was an important part of the post-war settlement.

[hist-eur4-s6.5.2] France's social security system, set up by ordinances of October 1945, combined health insurance, family allowances and pensions for workers.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.3] The Scandinavian countries developed universal welfare systems, and Sweden's model combined social security with active labour market policies.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.4] West Germany's welfare system was closely tied to the social market economy, and it provided compulsory social insurance funded by employers and employees.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.5] Welfare spending rose steadily across Western Europe in the 1950s and 1960s, and it became a permanent feature of the political consensus.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.6] Britain's Labour government of 1945–51 under Clement Attlee passed the National Insurance Act (1946), set up the NHS and nationalised coal, the railways and the Bank of England.
