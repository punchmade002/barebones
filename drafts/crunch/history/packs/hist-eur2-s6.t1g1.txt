TARGET hist-eur2-s6: 6. War and revolution in Germany, and the Peace Settlement, 1918–1920
[hist-eur2-s6.0.b] To understand the revolution and the peace, you first need to know who was fighting, why Germany was losing, and how Germany was governed. The old system collapsed because it had been built around a war that was now lost.
[hist-eur2-s6.0.0] The war was fought between the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria) and the Allies (Britain, France, Russia until 1917, Italy from 1915 and the USA from 6 April 1917).
[hist-eur2-s6.0.1] By 1918 Germany was losing. Russia had left the war by the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (3 March 1918), but the German spring offensive from March failed, and Allied counter-attacks began at Amiens on 8 August, which Ludendorff called the 'black day of the German army'.
[hist-eur2-s6.0.2] American troops were arriving in large numbers, so Germany could not replace its losses. At home the British naval blockade had caused food shortages throughout the war, including the 'turnip winter' of 1916-17, so civilians were exhausted and hungry.
[hist-eur2-s6.0.3] In the Kaiserreich (the German Empire) the Chancellor answered to the Kaiser, not to the Reichstag (the elected parliament). From 1916 the army leaders Hindenburg (Chief of the General Staff) and Ludendorff (First Quartermaster General) dominated government, so the army's failure discredited the whole system.
[hist-eur2-s6.0.4] German socialism had split. The Majority SPD (Social Democratic Party; Ebert and Scheidemann) had backed the war; the Independent Social Democrats (USPD) broke away in April 1917; the Spartacists on the far left (Luxemburg and Liebknecht) wanted revolution.
[hist-eur2-s6.0.5] Austria-Hungary was a multinational empire, and by 1918 its Czechs, Slovaks, Poles, South Slavs, Romanians and others increasingly wanted independence, which weakened it as the war went badly.
[hist-eur2-s6.1.b] Germany's allies gave way one by one in autumn 1918, and the German army leaders then admitted they could not go on. This pushed Germany into asking for an armistice (an agreement to stop fighting) and into political change.
[hist-eur2-s6.1.0] Bulgaria signed an armistice at Salonika on 29 September 1918. The Ottoman Empire followed with the armistice of Mudros on 30 October 1918, so Germany lost two allies in a month.
[hist-eur2-s6.1.1] On 29 September 1918, at army headquarters in Spa, Ludendorff told the Kaiser and the government that Germany must seek an armistice at once. Allied attacks had broken into the Hindenburg Line (the main German defensive position in France) and Bulgaria had given up.
[hist-eur2-s6.1.2] To please Wilson, the Kaiser appointed the liberal Prince Max of Baden as Chancellor on 3 October 1918. His government included Social Democrats (Scheidemann) and Centre Party members (Erzberger).
[hist-eur2-s6.1.3] On the night of 3-4 October Germany asked Wilson for an armistice on the basis of the Fourteen Points.
[hist-eur2-s6.1.4] Wilson's replies insisted that Germany must end its military autocracy (rule by the army and Kaiser), which encouraged calls for the Kaiser to go. The constitution was changed in late October (28 October) to make the Chancellor responsible to the Reichstag.
[hist-eur2-s6.1.5] Ludendorff resigned on 26 October 1918, so the man who had demanded the armistice was gone before it was signed.
[hist-eur2-s6.1.6] Austria-Hungary was defeated by Italy at the battle of Vittorio Veneto (late October to early November 1918) and signed the armistice of Villa Giusti on 3 November 1918, which came into force on 4 November.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.b] Defeat turned into revolution within a fortnight. A naval order sparked a mutiny, the mutiny spread, and by 9 November the Kaiser's rule had ended and two rival republics had been proclaimed in Berlin.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.0] At the end of October the naval command ordered the High Seas Fleet to sail for a last battle. Sailors at Wilhelmshaven refused.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.1] A mutiny at Kiel, from 3 November, spread within days to ports and cities across Germany. The SPD sent Gustav Noske to Kiel on 4 November to regain control.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.2] Sailors, soldiers and workers formed councils on the Russian model, local bodies that took over authority from the old officials. Kurt Eisner (USPD) declared a Bavarian republic in Munich on 7 November.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.3] On 9 November, with a general strike and crowds in Berlin, Prince Max announced the Kaiser's abdication without his consent and handed the chancellorship to the Social Democrat Friedrich Ebert.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.4] Two republics were proclaimed that day. Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed a parliamentary German republic from a window of the Reichstag, and Karl Liebknecht later proclaimed a 'free socialist republic' from the Berlin palace.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.5] Wilhelm II crossed into the Netherlands on 10 November and signed a formal abdication on 28 November. He lived there until his death in 1941.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.6] The armistice was signed at about 5 am on 11 November 1918 in a railway carriage in the forest of Compiègne, in force at 11 am. Matthias Erzberger of the Centre Party led the German delegation; Marshal Foch signed for the Allies.
[hist-eur2-s6.2.7] Its terms: evacuate occupied France, Belgium, Luxembourg and Alsace-Lorraine; hand over large amounts of weapons, the submarines and much of the fleet; Allied occupation west of the Rhine with bridgeheads; Brest-Litovsk cancelled; blockade kept on until July 1919.
[hist-eur2-s6.3.b] With the Kaiser gone, someone had to run Germany. Ebert's government had to decide whether to build a new order from the revolution or to keep the old institutions working, and its choices shaped the republic's weaknesses.
[hist-eur2-s6.3.0] The Council of People's Representatives, the provisional government set up on 10 November 1918, had three Majority SPD members (including Ebert and Scheidemann) and three USPD members. It ruled until elections; the USPD members resigned on 29 December 1918.
[hist-eur2-s6.3.1] Do not confuse it with the workers' and soldiers' councils, which were local bodies formed during the revolution.
[hist-eur2-s6.3.2] The Ebert-Groener pact, made by telephone on 10 November: General Groener (Ludendorff's successor) pledged the army's support against Bolshevism, and Ebert's government respected officers' authority.
[hist-eur2-s6.3.3] The pact helped the republic survive, because the army would defend order against the far left. It was also a weakness, because it left the old army, civil service and judiciary in place, still loyal to the old order rather than the republic.
[hist-eur2-s6.3.4] The Stinnes-Legien agreement of 15 November 1918 was made between the industrialist Hugo Stinnes and the trade union leader Carl Legien. Employers recognised the unions and accepted the eight-hour day, which won workers' cooperation and helped to block revolution.
[hist-eur2-s6.3.5] The central choice was between a council republic on the Russian model, which the far left wanted, and an elected parliament, which the SPD leaders wanted.
[hist-eur2-s6.4.b] The split on the left turned violent in January 1919. The government used the army and volunteer units to crush the radicals, and this left a lasting bitterness on the left.
[hist-eur2-s6.4.0] The KPD (Communist Party of Germany) was founded at a congress from 30 December 1918 to 1 January 1919 by the Spartacus League and other left-radical groups. They wanted a council republic like Russia's.
[hist-eur2-s6.4.1] The trigger for the rising was the dismissal on 4 January 1919 of Emil Eichhorn, the USPD police chief of Berlin, whom the left saw as a defender of the revolution.
[hist-eur2-s6.4.2] In the Spartacist or January rising (5-12 January 1919) radicals occupied newspaper buildings in Berlin, including the SPD paper Vorwärts.
[hist-eur2-s6.4.3] It was crushed by government troops and Freikorps (volunteer units of ex-soldiers) under Gustav Noske, the SPD member of the government in charge of military affairs.
[hist-eur2-s6.4.4] Luxemburg and Liebknecht were arrested and murdered by Freikorps officers in Berlin on 15 January 1919, so the KPD lost its leaders at once.
[hist-eur2-s6.4.5] Eisner was assassinated on 21 February 1919. A Bavarian Soviet republic existed in Munich in April 1919 and was crushed by government troops and Freikorps in early May 1919.
[hist-eur2-s6.5.b] Once the left was beaten, the SPD could hold elections and write a constitution. The republic was democratic, but it started with heavy handicaps.
[hist-eur2-s6.5.0] In the National Assembly election of 19 January 1919 women voted nationally for the first time (votes for men and women over 20). The SPD was the largest party with about 38 per cent. The Weimar coalition was the SPD, the Catholic Centre Party and the German Democratic Party (DDP).
[hist-eur2-s6.5.1] The Assembly first met at Weimar on 6 February 1919, away from Berlin's unrest. Ebert was elected Reich President on 11 February, and Scheidemann became head of the first government.
[hist-eur2-s6.5.2] The Weimar constitution was passed by the Assembly on 31 July 1919 and signed by Ebert on 11 August. The president was elected for seven years, there was proportional representation, and Article 48 gave emergency powers.
[hist-eur2-s6.5.3] The 'stab in the back' (Dolchstoss) myth claimed that the army had not been beaten in the field but betrayed by socialists, Jews and revolutionaries at home. It was false, because the army leaders themselves had asked for an armistice. Hindenburg repeated it to a Reichstag committee in November 1919.
[hist-eur2-s6.5.4] Nationalists called the politicians who accepted the armistice and the republic the 'November criminals', so the republic was tied in people's minds to defeat.
[hist-eur2-s6.5.5] The Kapp Putsch of March 1920 showed the right's hostility: Wolfgang Kapp and Freikorps units seized Berlin and the army did not resist them. It was defeated within days by a general strike.
[hist-eur2-s6.6.b] While Germany had a revolution, the Habsburg empire simply fell apart along national lines. It broke up in October and November 1918, before the peace treaties, and the treaties then confirmed what had happened.
[hist-eur2-s6.6.0] Emperor Charles I issued a manifesto on 16 October 1918 offering to turn Austria into a federation of nationalities. It came too late, because the peoples now wanted independence.
[hist-eur2-s6.6.1] Czechoslovakia declared independence in Prague on 28 October 1918, with Tomas Masaryk as its first president. A State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs was declared on 29 October, and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (later Yugoslavia) was proclaimed on 1 December.
[hist-eur2-s6.6.2] Poland declared independence in November 1918 (11 November is its independence day; Jozef Pilsudski was its leader), and Hungary became a separate state.
[hist-eur2-s6.6.3] Charles withdrew from state affairs in Austria on 11 November 1918. German-Austria proclaimed a republic on 12 November, and its assembly declared it part of the German republic.
[hist-eur2-s6.6.4] Hungary was in turmoil. Bela Kun's Hungarian Soviet Republic (March to August 1919) ended after a Romanian invasion, when Romanian troops entered Budapest. Admiral Horthy then took power and became regent in 1920.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.b] The Fourteen Points were Wilson's programme for a just peace. Germany asked for an armistice on their basis, so what they promised mattered when the Germans saw the treaty.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.0] Wilson set out the Fourteen Points in an address to Congress on 8 January 1918, as a statement of American war aims and a basis for peace. It was partly a reply to the Bolsheviks, who had published the Allies' secret treaties and called for peace.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.1] Points 1 to 5 called for open covenants (public agreements) of peace openly arrived at, freedom of the seas, removal of economic barriers (free trade), reduction of armaments, and an impartial adjustment of colonial claims in which the interests of the peoples concerned counted equally.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.2] Points 6 to 8 dealt with the evacuation of Russia and Belgium and the return of Alsace-Lorraine to France.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.3] Points 9 to 13 set Italy's frontiers on lines of nationality, called for autonomous development for the peoples of Austria-Hungary, and covered the Balkans and the Ottoman Empire (Turkish parts sovereign, others autonomous, the Dardanelles open). They also demanded an independent Poland with access to the sea.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.4] Point 10 asked for 'autonomous development', not independence, for the peoples of Austria-Hungary. By 1918 Wilson came to back independence for the Czechoslovaks and South Slavs.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.5] Point 14 called for a general association of nations to guarantee independence and territorial integrity (the right of a state to keep its borders) to great and small states alike. This became the League of Nations.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.6] In the pre-armistice agreement (the Lansing note, 5 November 1918) the Allies accepted the Points as the basis of peace. Britain reserved its position on freedom of the seas, and the Allies demanded compensation for damage done to the civilian population.
[hist-eur2-s6.7.7] Many Germans later believed that the harsher Treaty of Versailles broke this agreement.
[hist-eur2-s6.8.b] The conference opened in Paris on 18 January 1919, the anniversary of the German Empire's proclamation in 1871. About thirty states took part, but the leaders of four of them took the decisions, and each had a different aim.
[hist-eur2-s6.8.0] The defeated powers were excluded and Soviet Russia was not invited. A Council of Ten (two leading delegates each from the USA, Britain, France, Italy and Japan) took decisions at first, then the Council of Four (the Big Four) from March 1919. Commissions drafted the terms.
[hist-eur2-s6.8.1] The Big Four were Woodrow Wilson (USA), David Lloyd George (Britain), Georges Clemenceau (France) and Vittorio Orlando (Italy).
[hist-eur2-s6.8.2] Wilson wanted a just peace based on self-determination, with the League built into the treaty. He arrived in France on 13 December 1918 and was the first US president to travel to Europe while in office.
[hist-eur2-s6.8.3] Clemenceau, French Prime Minister, nicknamed 'the Tiger', wanted security for France, which had been invaded in 1870 and 1914. He wanted a weak Germany, a Rhineland buffer and heavy reparations.
[hist-eur2-s6.8.4] France at first wanted the Rhineland separated from Germany. It accepted occupation and demilitarisation of the Rhineland in return for a promised Anglo-American guarantee of French security.
[hist-eur2-s6.8.5] Lloyd George, British Prime Minister, had won the general election of 14 December 1918 amid popular demands to make Germany pay and to try the Kaiser. He nevertheless wanted a peace that would not provoke revenge.
[hist-eur2-s6.8.6] His Fontainebleau memorandum (25 March 1919) warned against a peace that would drive Germany to revenge or Bolshevism. He also wanted to protect British trade, empire and naval power, and to avoid French dominance in Europe.
[hist-eur2-s6.9.b] Other states came to Paris with claims of their own, and most were disappointed. The Germans were given no chance to negotiate, which shaped how they saw the result.
[hist-eur2-s6.9.0] Orlando wanted what the secret Treaty of London (April 1915) had promised Italy to bring it into the war: South Tyrol (Trentino to the Brenner), Trieste, Istria and much of Dalmatia. Fiume was not among the promises, but Italy claimed it as well.
[hist-eur2-s6.9.1] Orlando left the conference in April 1919 over Fiume and returned in May. Italian nationalists, including Gabriele d'Annunzio, spoke of a 'mutilated victory'.
[hist-eur2-s6.9.2] Japan, an ally, proposed a racial equality clause, which was rejected in April 1919. It received Germany's rights in Shandong (China).
[hist-eur2-s6.9.3] Ireland's Sinn Fein sent Seán T. O'Kelly as Dáil Éireann's envoy to Paris in 1919 to ask for a hearing for Irish self-determination. Wilson and the Allies refused to admit the Irish case.
[hist-eur2-s6.9.4] The terms were presented on 7 May 1919 to the German delegation, led by Foreign Minister Ulrich von Brockdorff-Rantzau. There was no negotiation: German counter-proposals were mostly rejected.
[hist-eur2-s6.9.5] In June 1919 the Allies issued an ultimatum to sign or face renewed war, so Germany had no real choice.
[hist-eur2-s6.10.b] The treaty was signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, five years to the day after the assassination at Sarajevo. The Hall of Mirrors was also where the German Empire had been proclaimed in 1871, so the setting was deliberate.
[hist-eur2-s6.10.0] Article 231, the war guilt clause, said that Germany accepted responsibility for itself and its allies for causing all the loss and damage suffered by the Allies in a war imposed on them by the aggression of Germany and its allies. It was the legal basis for reparations.
[hist-eur2-s6.10.1] The word 'guilt' does not appear in Article 231; Germans called it the war guilt clause because they read it as blaming Germany alone.
[hist-eur2-s6.10.2] In the west and north, Alsace-Lorraine went to France, Eupen and Malmedy to Belgium, and North Schleswig to Denmark after a plebiscite (a vote on a border) in 1920.
[hist-eur2-s6.10.3] In the east, most of West Prussia and Posen went to Poland, forming the Polish Corridor to the Baltic, which separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany. Plebiscites were held in Upper Silesia and in East Prussian districts.
[hist-eur2-s6.10.4] Danzig became a Free City under League of Nations protection, so it was neither German nor Polish, and Memel was placed under Allied control (Lithuania seized it in 1923).
[hist-eur2-s6.10.5] The Saar was governed by the League for fifteen years, its coal mines went to France, and a plebiscite was due in 1935.
[hist-eur2-s6.10.6] Germany lost about 13 per cent of its European territory and about a tenth of its population, which is why many Germans saw the treaty as national humiliation.
[hist-eur2-s6.11.b] The rest of the treaty stripped Germany of its colonies, its military strength and its wealth. It was also imposed, which explains the German reaction.
[hist-eur2-s6.11.0] All Germany's colonies were taken and made League mandates (territories run by a victor state on the League's behalf), held by Britain, France, Japan, Belgium, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand.
[hist-eur2-s6.11.1] Disarmament, so that Germany could not attack again: the army was limited to 100,000 long-service volunteers, conscription was banned, and there were to be no tanks, military aircraft or submarines. The navy was limited to six battleships and small craft, and the General Staff was abolished.
[hist-eur2-s6.11.2] The Rhineland was demilitarised (the left bank and a 50 km zone on the right bank), and the Allies occupied the left bank for up to fifteen years, to give France security.
[hist-eur2-s6.11.3] Union of Germany and Austria (Anschluss) was forbidden without the consent of the League Council (Article 80).
[hist-eur2-s6.11.4] Reparations (payments for war damage): no total was set in the treaty. Germany was to pay 20 billion gold marks by May 1921 as an interim payment. In May 1921 the London Schedule fixed 132 billion gold marks in A, B and C bonds.
[hist-eur2-s6.11.5] The C bonds (about 82 billion) were not expected to be paid, so the real obligation was about 50 billion.
[hist-eur2-s6.11.6] Article 227 called for the trial of the Kaiser, but the Netherlands refused to hand him over. Part I of the treaty was the Covenant of the League of Nations.
[hist-eur2-s6.11.7] Germans called it a 'Diktat' (a dictated peace) because they had not been allowed to negotiate. Scheidemann's government resigned on 20 June 1919 rather than sign; the Assembly voted to sign on 23 June under the threat of invasion. Hermann Müller and Johannes Bell signed.
[hist-eur2-s6.12.b] Versailles dealt only with Germany. Separate treaties dealt with Germany's allies, and together they redrew the map of central and south-eastern Europe and the Middle East.
[hist-eur2-s6.12.0] The Treaty of St Germain with Austria (10 September 1919) recognised the new states, limited Austria's army to 30,000 and forbade union with Germany. Austria lost South Tyrol to Italy, the Sudeten German areas to Czechoslovakia and Galicia to Poland.
[hist-eur2-s6.12.1] The Treaty of Trianon with Hungary (4 June 1920) cost Hungary about two-thirds of its territory: Transylvania to Romania, Slovakia (and Ruthenia) to Czechoslovakia, Croatia to the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, and Burgenland to Austria.
[hist-eur2-s6.12.2] Over three million Hungarians were left outside Hungary, which caused bitter and lasting resentment.
[hist-eur2-s6.12.3] The Treaty of Neuilly with Bulgaria (27 November 1919) gave Western Thrace to Greece, which cut Bulgaria off from the Aegean. It also imposed reparations and limited the army to 20,000.
[hist-eur2-s6.12.4] The Treaty of Sèvres with the Ottoman Empire (10 August 1920) divided up the empire, with Armenia and a Kurdish area promised. Mustafa Kemal's nationalists rejected it and defeated Greece (the Greek-Turkish war, 1919-22), and the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) replaced it.
[hist-eur2-s6.12.5] In the Middle East, mandates were allocated at the San Remo conference (April 1920). Britain received Palestine (including Transjordan) and Iraq; France received Syria and Lebanon.
[hist-eur2-s6.13.b] Self-determination is the principle that peoples should choose the state they belong to. Wilson used it as a guide at Paris, but it was applied in some places and not others, which is why the settlement was criticised.
[hist-eur2-s6.13.0] Applied: the settlement created or restored Poland, Czechoslovakia and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes. Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, which broke away from the Russian Empire in 1917-18, became independent.
[hist-eur2-s6.13.1] Plebiscites in contested areas: Schleswig (1920: the north voted for Denmark, the middle zone for Germany), Allenstein and Marienwerder (July 1920: large majorities for Germany), and Carinthia (October 1920: voted to stay in Austria).
[hist-eur2-s6.13.2] Upper Silesia voted in March 1921 with a majority for Germany, followed by a Polish rising. The League partitioned it in October 1921, giving Poland most of the industrial area.
[hist-eur2-s6.13.3] Denied to Germans: union of Germany and Austria was forbidden although Austria's assembly had voted for it in November 1918. About three million Germans (the Sudeten Germans) were placed in Czechoslovakia, and Germans lived in the Corridor and Danzig.
[hist-eur2-s6.13.4] Denied elsewhere: German-speaking South Tyrol went to Italy to give it a strategic frontier on the Brenner, and Hungarians were left in Romania, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia without a choice.
[hist-eur2-s6.13.5] The new states were multinational. Poland had large Ukrainian, Jewish, Belarusian and German minorities, and Czechoslovakia large German and Hungarian minorities. Minority treaties, starting with Poland on 28 June 1919, protected them under League guarantee.
[hist-eur2-s6.13.6] The principle was not applied to the colonies, which became mandates, nor to Ireland, Egypt or India, a fact nationalists there noted.
[hist-eur2-s6.13.7] It could not be applied fully because populations were mixed, states needed ports, railways and defensible frontiers, the victors had their own interests, and wartime promises had been made.
[hist-eur2-s6.14.b] Wilson's central aim was a League that would keep the peace. It was created, but the country whose president had invented it refused to join, which weakened it from the start.
[hist-eur2-s6.14.0] The League of Nations came into force on 10 January 1920, with its seat in Geneva. Its main organs were the Assembly, the Council and the Secretariat. It also had the Permanent Court of International Justice (The Hague) and the International Labour Organisation (1919).
[hist-eur2-s6.14.1] Its Covenant promised collective security, meaning states acting together against an aggressor. Members were to respect and preserve each other's territorial integrity and political independence (Article 10) and to settle disputes by arbitration.
[hist-eur2-s6.14.2] Under Article 16 members applied economic sanctions, and possibly force, against a member that went to war in breach of the Covenant, so aggression was meant to be deterred by the threat of joint action.
[hist-eur2-s6.14.3] Germany and Soviet Russia were excluded at first. Germany joined in 1926 and the USSR in 1934.
[hist-eur2-s6.14.4] Republicans won control of Congress in the November 1918 elections. The treaty needed a two-thirds majority in the Senate, and the Republican chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, Henry Cabot Lodge, proposed reservations, chiefly to Article 10, which could oblige the USA to defend other states.
[hist-eur2-s6.14.5] Wilson refused to compromise. He toured the USA in September 1919 to rally support, collapsed at Pueblo, Colorado, on 25 September and had a stroke on 2 October 1919. The Senate rejected the treaty on 19 November 1919 and again on 19 March 1920.
[hist-eur2-s6.14.6] The USA never joined the League and made a separate peace with Germany (the Treaty of Berlin, August 1921). The Anglo-American guarantee of France lapsed when the Senate did not ratify it.
[hist-eur2-s6.14.7] Without the USA, the strongest Western power, France and Britain were left to enforce the settlement alone.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.b] Woodrow Wilson was President of the USA from 1913 to 1921. He moved from neutral mediator to war leader to peacemaker, and his Fourteen Points and the League shaped the settlement, but he could not win American support for it at home.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.0] Thomas Woodrow Wilson was born on 28 December 1856 in Staunton, Virginia. A historian and political scientist, he was President of Princeton University (1902-10) and Governor of New Jersey (1911-13), then Democratic President from 1912 (in office 1913-21). He won the 1919 Nobel Peace Prize.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.1] He kept the USA neutral from 1914, even after the Lusitania was sunk on 7 May 1915 (128 Americans died), and in January 1917 called for 'peace without victory'. He was re-elected in November 1916 on the slogan 'He kept us out of war'.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.2] Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on 1 February 1917, and the Zimmermann Telegram (a German offer to help Mexico regain lost US territory) was published on 1 March. So on 2 April Wilson told Congress that 'the world must be made safe for democracy'; war came on 6 April.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.3] He announced the Fourteen Points in January 1918 and in February said that self-determination was 'not a mere phrase'. The USA fought as an 'associated power', not a formal ally. He pressed for the Kaiser's removal in the armistice exchanges, so he was a participant, not a witness.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.4] In Paris he was welcomed by huge crowds in December 1918 and January 1919, chaired the commission that drafted the League Covenant and insisted it be in the treaty. To win the League and mandates he compromised on reparations, Article 231, the Saar, the Rhineland, Shandong and South Tyrol.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.5] At home he lost control: Republicans won Congress in November 1918, and he refused Lodge's reservations. His tour ended in a stroke in October 1919, after which his wife Edith controlled access to him, and the Senate rejected the treaty. He died on 3 February 1924.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.6] Contemporary views: Europeans hailed him as a prophet of a new order, while Clemenceau and others were sceptical of his idealism. Keynes portrayed him as outmanoeuvred, and Germans felt disillusioned. Many Americans felt he had entangled the USA in Europe, and nationalists in Ireland and Egypt were disappointed.
[hist-eur2-s6.15.7] Historians: admirers see the founder of liberal internationalism, whose League foreshadowed the United Nations. Critics find him self-righteous, inconsistent on self-determination and politically inept with the Senate, so he was both architect and casualty of the settlement.
[hist-eur2-s6.16.b] The peace did not end Europe's troubles. The war and the treaties left problems that can be grouped as human, economic, political and territorial, each linked to the war or the settlement.
[hist-eur2-s6.16.0] Human cost: about 9-10 million soldiers were killed (estimates vary), and millions were wounded and disabled, leaving widows and orphans. The 1918-19 influenza pandemic killed tens of millions worldwide.
[hist-eur2-s6.16.1] Refugees and minorities: the new borders and the break-up of empires left many people living in states ruled by another nationality, or moving across frontiers.
[hist-eur2-s6.16.2] Economic dislocation: governments owed war debts, inflation hurt savings, regions of France and Belgium were damaged, and trade links between the successor states (the new states that replaced the old empires) were broken. The reparations dispute made matters worse.
[hist-eur2-s6.16.3] Political instability: fear of Bolshevism was real, because Soviet republics appeared in Bavaria and Hungary in 1919 and there were risings in Germany. The new democracies were fragile, and the Kapp Putsch showed that the right also threatened them.
[hist-eur2-s6.16.4] Disputed borders led to wars: the Polish-Soviet war of 1919-21 (the battle of Warsaw in August 1920 and the Treaty of Riga in 1921), the Russian Civil War of 1918-21, the Greek-Turkish war, and d'Annunzio's seizure of Fiume with volunteers in September 1919.
[hist-eur2-s6.16.5] Revisionist states, which wanted the treaties revised (Germany, Hungary, Bulgaria and Italy, a disappointed victor), faced status quo states that wanted them kept (France and the new states). This division weakened the peace.
[hist-eur2-s6.17.b] Historians and examiners judge both the German revolution and the Peace Settlement by weighing what they achieved against what they failed to do. The key to a top answer is pairing each achievement with a limit and reaching a conclusion.
[hist-eur2-s6.17.0] Revolution, achievement: Germany became a democratic republic with the women's vote and the eight-hour day, and the revolution avoided a Bolshevik-style regime.
[hist-eur2-s6.17.1] Revolution, limit: the old elites in the army, civil service and judiciary were kept, the left was split and embittered by the Freikorps killings, and the republic was tied to defeat and Versailles.
[hist-eur2-s6.17.2] Settlement, achievement: new national states appeared where empires had ruled, and the League provided a forum for settling disputes, such as the Aaland Islands (Finland and Sweden) in 1921. Germany was disarmed but kept as a united state.
[hist-eur2-s6.17.3] Settlement, limit: the new states contained large minorities, and revision was demanded by Germany, Hungary, Bulgaria and Italy, who felt cheated.
[hist-eur2-s6.17.4] The core judgement: the treaty was too harsh to be accepted by Germany but not harsh enough to stop it recovering. Enforcement was left to Britain and France, who disagreed, while the USA and Russia were outside the system.
[hist-eur2-s6.17.5] Contemporary view: Keynes, a Treasury adviser in the British delegation who resigned in June 1919, argued in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (December 1919) that the reparations were unrealistic and would ruin recovery. Foch is credited with saying: 'This is not peace. It is an armistice for twenty years.'
[hist-eur2-s6.17.6] Germans, for their part, resented the Diktat and the war guilt clause, so the treaty gave nationalists a grievance to exploit.
[hist-eur2-s6.17.7] Later view: some historians say Germany's grievance was exaggerated and its terms were milder than Brest-Litovsk. Others, such as Margaret MacMillan in Peacemakers (2001), see a serious attempt at a difficult task and argue that later failures, not the treaty itself, led to the Second World War.

CANDIDATES hist-eur2-s5: 5. The conduct of war, 1914–1918
[hist-eur2-s5.0.b] The war of 1914-1918 set two coalitions against each other and spread across the globe and the oceans, which is why it is called a world war. Knowing who was on which side, and a few army terms, makes the battles that follow easier to read.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.0] The Allies (the Entente) began as France, Russia and Britain. They were joined by Italy in May 1915, Romania in August 1916 and the USA on 6 April 1917, which fought as an associated power, meaning alongside the Allies but without a formal alliance.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.1] The Central Powers were Germany and Austria-Hungary, joined by the Ottoman Empire (which entered in October-November 1914) and Bulgaria (October 1915).
[hist-eur2-s5.0.2] A world war is a war fought on several continents and at sea that draws in empires, whole economies and populations. This war shows it through its many fronts: Western, Eastern, Italian, Balkan, Middle East, the colonies and the sea.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.3] A front is the line where two armies face each other, and an offensive is a large attack. A salient is a bulge in the line surrounded on three sides, so its defenders can be shelled from three directions.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.4] A division is a large army unit of many thousands of men, and a corps is a still larger unit made up of several divisions. BEF stands for British Expeditionary Force, Britain's army in France.
[hist-eur2-s5.0.5] Casualties means every man an army loses: killed, wounded, missing and captured, not just deaths. This is why a figure for casualties is always larger than the figure for those killed.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.b] All the Great Powers expected a short war. Germany's plan was to defeat France quickly by marching through Belgium and then turn on Russia, but the plan failed and by the end of the year the Western Front was a stalemate (a deadlock in which neither side can advance).
[hist-eur2-s5.1.0] Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August 1914 and the Liege forts fell in mid-August. The Germans then swept through Belgium and northern France under the Schlieffen Plan, which the younger Moltke, German chief of staff, modified; the traditional view is that he weakened the right wing.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.1] The British Expeditionary Force, a small professional army under Sir John French, fought its first major action at Mons on 23 August 1914. It then retreated alongside the French.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.2] The First Battle of the Marne (5-12 September 1914), fought under the French commander Joffre, halted the Germans after they had come close to Paris. They fell back to the River Aisne and dug in, so the Schlieffen Plan had failed. Moltke was replaced by Falkenhayn.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.3] Both sides then tried to outflank each other (get round the enemy's open end) in the race to the sea. This is why a line of trenches ran from the Belgian coast on the North Sea to the Swiss frontier by late 1914.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.4] The First Battle of Ypres (October-November 1914) was a hard-fought defensive success for the British and French. The old regular BEF was largely destroyed, so Britain had to depend on volunteers from then on.
[hist-eur2-s5.1.5] At Christmas 1914 troops in parts of the line took part in unofficial truces. The high commands forbade a repeat.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.b] Once the armies dug in, the Western Front became a fixed network of earthworks that lasted for years. Trench warfare is fighting from such lines of trenches, and it shaped how millions of soldiers lived.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.0] A trench system had a front line, support and reserve trenches behind it, and communication trenches linking them, so men and supplies could move without crossing open ground. Dugouts gave shelter underground.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.1] Barbed wire and no man's land, the shell-torn ground between the opposing lines, kept the enemy at a distance, which is why a surprise attack was so hard to make.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.2] Soldiers lived with mud, rats, lice, cold, and the constant danger of shelling and snipers. Wet conditions caused trench foot, a painful rotting of the feet from standing in water.
[hist-eur2-s5.2.3] Shell shock, now called combat stress or post-traumatic stress, was a breakdown caused by prolonged bombardment and fear. It is one of the conditions to learn.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.b] On the Western Front the weapons of defence were stronger than the means of attack. This is why battles produced very high casualties for small gains.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.0] The machine gun and quick-firing artillery, together with barbed wire, made frontal attacks (charges straight at the enemy) very costly. Most battle casualties were caused by artillery.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.1] Railways brought the defenders' reserves to a threatened point faster than attackers could advance across broken ground, so a breakthrough was usually sealed off before it could grow.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.2] Attackers relied on long preliminary bombardments, which warned the enemy where the attack would come, churned up the ground and often failed to cut the wire.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.3] From 1916 the creeping barrage, a curtain of shellfire that moved ahead of the infantry as they advanced, gave attackers more protection.
[hist-eur2-s5.3.4] Poor communications added to the problem. Without radio at the front, commanders could not quickly learn what was happening or change orders, so a battle was hard to control once it began.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.b] Both sides searched for a way to break the deadlock. New weapons helped, but none won the war alone, and by 1918 the answer lay in using them together.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.0] Germany first used poison gas on a large scale at the Second Battle of Ypres on 22 April 1915, releasing chlorine against French and French colonial (Algerian) troops. Canadian troops were gassed in the same battle days later.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.1] Phosgene followed from late 1915 and mustard gas from July 1917. Gas masks reduced its effect, so gas caused terror and suffering more than a breakthrough.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.2] Britain first used tanks at Flers-Courcelette on 15 September 1916, during the Somme. They were slow and unreliable. At Cambrai (20 November 1917) about 400 were used together, a sign of what massed tanks could do.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.3] Aircraft were used for reconnaissance and artillery spotting, then for fighter combat and bombing. Germany attacked Britain with Zeppelin raids from 1915 and Gotha bomber raids on London in 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.4.4] By 1918 both sides used combined arms, meaning different weapons working together. Germany developed stormtrooper infiltration tactics, in which small groups slipped past strong points, and the Allies coordinated tanks, aircraft, artillery and infantry.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.b] After the stalemate set in, generals on both sides still believed a breakthrough was possible. When it did not come, the war turned into one of attrition.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.0] A war of attrition is a struggle to win by wearing down the enemy's men and resources rather than by one decisive battle. Verdun, the Somme and Passchendaele are the main examples, and the blockade at sea was attrition by starvation.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.1] In 1915 the Allies launched offensives at Neuve Chapelle (March), in Artois (French) and at Loos (September), and the Germans used gas at Ypres. None broke the line.
[hist-eur2-s5.5.2] A shortage of shells, the shell scandal of May 1915, caused a political crisis in Britain. It helped bring a coalition government and a Ministry of Munitions under Lloyd George, so the army's needs changed British politics.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.b] In 1916 Germany tried to win by hurting France where it would not retreat. The result was a long, costly battle.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.0] Verdun lasted from 21 February to 18 December 1916. The German chief of staff Falkenhayn attacked the symbolically important French fortress, intending, according to his post-war account, to bleed France white.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.1] Each side lost over 300,000 casualties, about 700,000 in total, and the French held.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.2] General Petain took command in late February 1916. He rotated divisions through the battle so no unit was worn out, and supplies reached the front along the Voie Sacree (Sacred Way), the road from Bar-le-Duc.
[hist-eur2-s5.6.3] Verdun became the symbol of French endurance, and the French held.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.b] The Somme was a joint British-French offensive planned partly to relieve the pressure on Verdun. It became the most famous battle of the British Army in the war.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.0] The Somme lasted from 1 July to 18 November 1916. A bombardment of about a week, from 24 June, was meant to destroy the German defences and wire before the infantry advanced.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.1] On the first day the British suffered about 57,000 casualties, of whom about 19,000 were killed. It was the worst day in the history of the British Army, and the 57,000 are casualties, not deaths.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.2] The Allied line advanced up to about 10 kilometres at the deepest point, and far less on most of the front. Total casualties were over a million across all armies, and German losses were also heavy.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.3] It was the first big battle for Kitchener's volunteer Pals battalions, men who enlisted together from the same town. Heavy losses fell at once on particular towns, such as Accrington, home of the Accrington Pals.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.4] Tanks were used for the first time at Flers-Courcelette on 15 September 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.7.5] Historians judge it as failure (the first-day losses and small gains) or as partial success, because it relieved Verdun, wore down the German army and taught the BEF.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.b] Irish soldiers served in large numbers in the British Army, and two Irish divisions took part in some of the best-known battles of 1916 and 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.0] The 36th (Ulster) Division attacked at Thiepval on 1 July 1916, the first day of the Somme, so Irish units shared in its heaviest day.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.1] The 16th (Irish) Division took Guillemont and Ginchy on 3 and 9 September 1916. Tom Kettle was killed at Ginchy on 9 September.
[hist-eur2-s5.8.2] At Messines in June 1917 the two divisions fought side by side and took Wytschaete. Major Willie Redmond MP was killed there.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.b] In 1917 the Allies tried again to break through. The year began with another costly failure and French collapse of morale, and then moved to more limited British operations.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.0] Nivelle's offensive on the Chemin des Dames began on 16 April 1917. It failed with heavy losses, and mutinies spread through many French divisions in May-June 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.1] Petain replaced Nivelle in May 1917 and restored morale by improving conditions and avoiding big attacks, and the French army recovered.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.2] At Arras, from 9 April 1917, the British attacked. The Canadian Corps captured Vimy Ridge on 9-12 April.
[hist-eur2-s5.9.3] At Messines on 7 June 1917, 19 mines exploded under the ridge. It was a limited success and came before Passchendaele.
[hist-eur2-s5.10.b] The British offensive in Flanders in the summer of 1917 became the most notorious battle of the war, but Cambrai later showed what tanks could achieve.
[hist-eur2-s5.10.0] Passchendaele, also called the Third Battle of Ypres, ran from 31 July to 10 November 1917. Its aims were to break out of the Ypres salient and reach the Belgian coast and the German U-boat bases there.
[hist-eur2-s5.10.1] Heavy rain and shell damage turned the ground to mud. Men and guns sank in it, so the advance slowed to a crawl and the final gain was only about 8 kilometres.
[hist-eur2-s5.10.2] Canadian troops took the village of Passchendaele in November 1917, but the gain was so small for the cost that the battle became a symbol of waste.
[hist-eur2-s5.10.3] At Cambrai on 20 November 1917, about 400 tanks attacked by surprise. The gains were mostly lost to a German counter-attack from 30 November.
[hist-eur2-s5.11.b] On the Eastern Front the armies moved over far greater distances than in the west. Russia was large but poorly supplied, and German commanders exploited this.
[hist-eur2-s5.11.0] Russia's invasion of East Prussia drew German troops east at the time of the Marne. At Tannenberg (26-30 August 1914) Hindenburg and Ludendorff destroyed the Russian Second Army under Samsonov and took about 90,000 prisoners.
[hist-eur2-s5.11.1] At the Masurian Lakes in September 1914 the Germans defeated Rennenkampf's First Army.
[hist-eur2-s5.11.2] The Central Powers broke the Russian line at Gorlice-Tarnow in May 1915, forcing a great Russian retreat from Poland and Galicia. Warsaw fell to Germany in August 1915.
[hist-eur2-s5.11.3] The Brusilov Offensive, begun on 4 June 1916 against Austria-Hungary, was the most successful Allied attack of the year. It took many prisoners and forced Germany to move troops east, but it could not be sustained and Russia suffered very heavy losses.
[hist-eur2-s5.11.4] Romania declared war on 27 August 1916 to join the Allies, but Germany and its allies largely overran it, and Bucharest fell in December 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.12.b] Defeat and strain at home brought Russia down. Its exit from the war freed Germany to turn west.
[hist-eur2-s5.12.0] Defeats, losses, shortages and the failure of the Tsar's government led to the February Revolution of 1917 (March by the Western calendar). The Provisional Government that replaced the Tsar continued the war, but the Kerensky Offensive of July 1917 collapsed.
[hist-eur2-s5.12.1] After the Bolsheviks seized power in October 1917 they signed an armistice with Germany in December 1917. By the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918, Russia gave up Poland, the Baltic lands, Ukraine and Finland.
[hist-eur2-s5.12.2] The treaty freed dozens of divisions for the West and so helped Germany's offensives of 1918, but Germany still kept large forces in the occupied east.
[hist-eur2-s5.13.b] After the Ottoman Empire joined the Central Powers, Britain and France tried to knock it out quickly by attacking the Dardanelles, the narrow strait to Constantinople. The plan failed, and the campaign had lasting political and national effects.
[hist-eur2-s5.13.0] The Ottoman Empire entered the war in October-November 1914. The naval attempt to force the Dardanelles came on 18 March 1915 and failed.
[hist-eur2-s5.13.1] Landings followed on 25 April 1915 by British, French and ANZAC (Australian and New Zealand) troops, at ANZAC Cove and Cape Helles. The Royal Dublin Fusiliers and Royal Munster Fusiliers landed at V Beach, Helles.
[hist-eur2-s5.13.2] The 10th (Irish) Division fought at Suvla Bay in August 1915. The campaign was stalemated, with the Ottoman commander Mustafa Kemal (later Ataturk) making his name there, and the troops were evacuated by 9 January 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.13.3] Gallipoli damaged Churchill, First Lord of the Admiralty, who had promoted it, and he left the Admiralty in May 1915.
[hist-eur2-s5.13.4] It became a founding moment for Australian and New Zealand national identity, remembered each year as Anzac Day on 25 April.
[hist-eur2-s5.14.b] The war reached well beyond France and Russia. These campaigns show why it was a world war.
[hist-eur2-s5.14.0] Italy entered the war on the Allied side in May 1915 under the Treaty of London (April 1915), which promised it territory from Austria-Hungary. Eleven battles on the Isonzo brought little gain.
[hist-eur2-s5.14.1] Italy suffered a disastrous defeat at Caporetto on 24 October 1917, then recovered on the Piave and won at Vittorio Veneto (October-November 1918). Do not confuse the 1915 Treaty of London with the 1839 treaty on Belgium.
[hist-eur2-s5.14.2] In the Middle East the British fought the Ottomans in Mesopotamia, where Kut surrendered on 29 April 1916, and in Palestine. Allenby took Jerusalem in December 1917 and won at Megiddo in September 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.14.3] The Arab Revolt began in June 1916 under Sharif Hussein of Mecca, with T. E. Lawrence as British liaison officer, so Britain gained Arab help against the Ottomans.
[hist-eur2-s5.14.4] Serbia was overrun in October-December 1915 after Bulgaria joined the Central Powers. The Allies kept a front at Salonika, and Bulgaria asked for an armistice on 29 September 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.14.5] Most German colonies were captured by the Allies early. German East Africa under Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck held out until he surrendered on 25 November 1918, after the armistice in Europe.
[hist-eur2-s5.15.b] Britain's navy could not easily win a decisive battle, but it could strangle German trade.
[hist-eur2-s5.15.0] Britain used its fleet to blockade Germany, stopping imports of food and raw materials. Germany suffered shortages, and the winter of 1916-17, the turnip winter, was especially severe. This was attrition at sea.
[hist-eur2-s5.15.1] The German High Seas Fleet, built in the pre-war naval race, was cautious. The only major fleet engagement was Jutland (31 May-1 June 1916), between the British Grand Fleet under Jellicoe and Beatty and the German fleet under Scheer.
[hist-eur2-s5.15.2] Britain lost 14 ships and about 6,000 men, and Germany 11 ships and about 2,500 men. The British fleet kept control of the North Sea and the German fleet remained mostly in port.
[hist-eur2-s5.16.b] Unable to beat the Royal Navy on the surface, Germany turned to submarines (U-boats). The campaign nearly starved Britain but brought the USA into the war.
[hist-eur2-s5.16.0] The liner Lusitania was sunk on 7 May 1915 off the south coast of Ireland, near the Old Head of Kinsale, Co. Cork. About 1,200 died, more than 120 of them American, which outraged American opinion.
[hist-eur2-s5.16.1] As a result Germany restricted its campaign, giving the Sussex pledge in May 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.16.2] On 1 February 1917 Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, sinking ships without warning, hoping to starve Britain into surrender within months. April 1917 was the worst month for Allied shipping losses, and Britain faced a serious food crisis.
[hist-eur2-s5.16.3] Convoys, adopted from mid-1917, grouped merchant ships under naval escort, which made them harder for submarines to find and attack. Together with better anti-submarine methods they cut losses.
[hist-eur2-s5.16.4] The campaign failed to knock Britain out of the war and it brought in the USA.
[hist-eur2-s5.17.b] Mass war needed mass armies. Continental states already had conscription, which is compulsory military service, but Britain had relied on volunteers and had to change.
[hist-eur2-s5.17.0] Germany, France, Russia and Austria-Hungary called up trained reservists under their pre-war systems of conscription.
[hist-eur2-s5.17.1] In Britain, Kitchener, Secretary of State for War, raised over two million volunteers by the end of 1915, but losses grew and numbers fell.
[hist-eur2-s5.17.2] The Military Service Act, passed in January 1916 and in force from 2 March 1916, conscripted unmarried men aged 18 to 41 because volunteers were no longer enough. It was extended to married men in May 1916.
[hist-eur2-s5.17.3] Conscientious objectors, men who refused to fight on grounds of conscience, appeared before tribunals. About 16,000 claimed the status, and some were jailed while others were sent to non-combatant work.
[hist-eur2-s5.17.4] The 1916 Act did not apply to Ireland. An attempt in April 1918 to extend conscription there caused the Conscription Crisis, with an anti-conscription pledge on 21 April and a one-day general strike on 23 April 1918. It was never enforced.
[hist-eur2-s5.17.5] The USA introduced conscription through the Selective Service Act of May 1917. Germany passed the Auxiliary Service Law in December 1916, requiring men aged 17 to 60 to do war work.
[hist-eur2-s5.17.6] Governments had to keep skilled workers in reserved occupations such as munitions, mines and railways, and women replaced many men.
[hist-eur2-s5.18.b] The USA stayed neutral for nearly three years. Two German decisions in 1917 changed that.
[hist-eur2-s5.18.0] Unrestricted submarine warfare sank American ships. The Zimmermann Telegram, sent in January 1917, was a secret German offer of an alliance to Mexico against the USA. British intelligence intercepted it and it was published in the American press on 1 March 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.18.1] Wilson asked Congress for war on 2 April 1917, and the USA declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917. Keep these two dates distinct.
[hist-eur2-s5.18.2] General John J. Pershing commanded the American Expeditionary Forces, which arrived in strength during 1918. Their coming raised Allied morale and changed German calculations, because Germany could not match the new men and resources.
[hist-eur2-s5.19.b] With Russia out, Germany had one chance to win before the Americans arrived. The offensive almost succeeded, but its failure left the German army exhausted.
[hist-eur2-s5.19.0] Ludendorff's Spring Offensive (Kaiserschlacht) began on 21 March 1918 with Operation Michael. It made large gains and came close to Amiens, but did not break the Allied line.
[hist-eur2-s5.19.1] Haig issued his backs to the wall order on 11 April 1918, calling on the British army to stand and fight because the line was under severe pressure.
[hist-eur2-s5.19.2] Foch was given coordinating authority at Doullens on 26 March 1918 and made Supreme Allied Commander in April 1918, so the Allies at last had a unified command.
[hist-eur2-s5.19.3] German troops were exhausted and short of supplies. The Allied counter-attack at the Second Battle of the Marne from 18 July 1918 turned the tide.
[hist-eur2-s5.20.b] From August 1918 the Allies advanced almost continuously. Germany's allies collapsed one by one, and it asked for peace.
[hist-eur2-s5.20.0] The Hundred Days began with the Battle of Amiens on 8 August 1918, which Ludendorff called the black day of the German army. Allied tanks, aircraft and artillery pushed the Germans back, and the Hindenburg Line was broken at the end of September.
[hist-eur2-s5.20.1] On 29 September 1918 Ludendorff told the Kaiser that Germany needed an armistice. In early October Germany sent a note to Wilson asking for terms on the basis of the Fourteen Points.
[hist-eur2-s5.20.2] Germany's allies had already collapsed: Bulgaria signed an armistice on 29 September, the Ottoman Empire on 30 October (Mudros) and Austria-Hungary on 3 November 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.20.3] The armistice was signed in a railway carriage at Compiegne at about 5 a.m. on 11 November 1918 and took effect at 11 a.m. Foch led the Allied delegation.
[hist-eur2-s5.20.4] Military deaths are put at about 9 to 10 million, though some estimates are lower at about 8.5 million. Many more were wounded and civilian deaths ran into the millions.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.b] Douglas Haig commanded the British Expeditionary Force in France from December 1915 until the end of the war. He is the most controversial British commander of the war.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.0] Haig was born in Edinburgh in 1861 into the Haig whisky-distilling family. He studied at Oxford (Brasenose College) and Sandhurst, and became a cavalry officer in the 7th Hussars.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.1] After Staff College he served in the Sudan (Omdurman, 1898) and the Boer War (1899-1902). In 1914 he commanded I Corps in the BEF at Mons, the retreat and First Ypres.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.2] He led the First Army from December 1914, which fought at Neuve Chapelle (March 1915) and Loos (September 1915). He succeeded Sir John French as commander-in-chief in December 1915 and became field marshal on 1 January 1917.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.3] His battles in 1916-17 were the Somme (1916) and Arras and Passchendaele (1917).
[hist-eur2-s5.21.4] In 1918 he directed the defence against the Spring Offensive and issued his backs to the wall order on 11 April. He then led the Hundred Days, in which the BEF played the leading part and took many prisoners.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.5] Haig believed in attrition and in a decisive breakthrough exploited by cavalry. His diary on the eve of the Somme shows confidence, but he also supported new weapons such as tanks.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.6] He was a participant shaped by events. The need to support France (Verdun, the French mutinies), the strength of defences and poor communications, with no radio at the front, limited what any commander could do.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.7] After the war he was made Earl Haig in 1919 and voted 100,000 pounds by Parliament. He was one of the founders and first president of the British Legion in 1921.
[hist-eur2-s5.21.8] He supported the poppy appeal (the Haig Fund) for ex-servicemen and died on 29 January 1928, with huge public mourning at his funeral.
[hist-eur2-s5.22.b] People judged Haig very differently in his own time and afterwards. The argument is the part of the topic examiners most like to set as a judgement.
[hist-eur2-s5.22.0] Contemporaries divided. King George V and much of the army trusted him. Lloyd George distrusted him, thought his offensives wasteful and kept reserves in Britain over the winter of 1917-18. Ex-servicemen and the public mourned him in 1928.
[hist-eur2-s5.22.1] Critics came first in memoirs. Lloyd George's War Memoirs, published in the 1930s, attacked him. Alan Clark's The Donkeys (1961) popularised the phrase "lions led by donkeys", a phrase about British soldiers and generals, not a quotation from Haig.
[hist-eur2-s5.22.2] Clark presented the phrase as an exchange between German generals, but no such source has been found, and similar phrases were used of other armies before 1914.
[hist-eur2-s5.22.3] Oh! What a Lovely War (stage 1963, film 1969) and Blackadder Goes Forth (1989) fixed the popular image of foolish generals.
[hist-eur2-s5.22.4] Denis Winter (Haig's Command, 1991) and Gerard De Groot (a critical biography, 1988) portrayed Haig as a callous, unimaginative butcher.
[hist-eur2-s5.22.5] Defenders such as John Terraine (Douglas Haig: The Educated Soldier, 1963) and Gary Sheffield (Forgotten Victory, 2001, and The Chief, 2011) argue that the BEF learned and improved its tactics, which won in 1918 under Haig.
[hist-eur2-s5.23.b] Examiners reward a clear judgement that weighs strengths against limits. These paired points give the evidence for both sides.
[hist-eur2-s5.23.0] Allied strength: the blockade, attrition, American troops and resources, and the combined-arms victory of 1918 wore down Germany's capacity to fight and broke its army.
[hist-eur2-s5.23.1] Allied limit: the Western Front battles of 1915-17 produced casualties out of proportion to gains, and the Somme and Passchendaele became symbols of waste.
[hist-eur2-s5.23.2] German success: Germany held the Western Front for four years, knocked Russia out of the war and nearly won in spring 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.23.3] German failure: the Schlieffen Plan failed in 1914, the U-boat gamble of unrestricted submarine warfare brought in the USA, and the army was exhausted by 1918.
[hist-eur2-s5.23.4] Contemporary view: the troops and many officers criticised the high command. Later views range from Clark's donkeys to Sheffield's Forgotten Victory.
[hist-eur2-s5.24.b] Judging Haig means weighing what he achieved against what he failed to avoid. A clear verdict, kept up throughout, is what examiners look for.
[hist-eur2-s5.24.0] Achievements: victory in 1918, a record of adapting and learning new tactics, and loyalty to the coalition, which held the Allied alliance together.
[hist-eur2-s5.24.1] Limits: the first day of the Somme, the mud of Passchendaele, over-optimism and slowness to stop failing attacks.
[hist-eur2-s5.24.2] Contemporary view: trusted by George V and the army, distrusted by Lloyd George and mourned in 1928. Later view: the donkeys image against the learning curve argument that the BEF improved and won.
[hist-eur2-s5.24.3] Many historians now accept that his attritional strategy was shaped by circumstances: strong defences, the need to support France and poor communications.
