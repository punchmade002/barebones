TARGET hist-eur4-s2: 2. The main crises of the Cold War, Khrushchev and détente
[hist-eur4-s2.0.b] The crises of 1958–62 and the détente that followed only make sense against a few basic facts: who the two sides were, how the Soviet state was run, and what nuclear weapons could do. The Cold War in Europe lasted from about 1945 to 1991.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.0] The Cold War was a rivalry short of direct war between the USA and its Western allies (capitalist democracies) and the Soviet Union and its Eastern European satellites (communist one-party states). The superpowers never fought each other directly.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.1] Instead the rivalry was fought through alliances, the arms race, propaganda, espionage, the space race and a series of crises, so each crisis was a test of nerve that stopped short of war.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.2] The two sides formed alliances: NATO in 1949 and the Warsaw Pact in 1955. Germany was divided in 1949 into the Federal Republic (West) and the German Democratic Republic (East).
[hist-eur4-s2.0.3] Berlin lay deep inside East Germany and was split into four sectors. The three Western sectors formed West Berlin, a Western island inside communist territory, which is why Berlin kept causing crises.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.4] In the Soviet Union the Communist Party ruled alone. Its leader was the First Secretary of the Central Committee, the Presidium (later called the Politburo) was the inner leadership, and the chairman of the Council of Ministers (the premier) headed the government.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.5] Stalin ruled by terror, purges (waves of arrests and executions), the Gulag labour camps and a cult of personality (public worship of the leader). His death on 5 March 1953 opened a struggle for the succession.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.6] The USA used atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 and the Soviet Union tested its first in 1949. Both later built hydrogen bombs. Medium-range missiles reach targets within a region, while intercontinental missiles (ICBMs) reach the other superpower's homeland.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.7] Fidel Castro took power in Cuba in January 1959 and Cuba then moved into the Soviet camp, which put a Soviet ally close to the USA.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.b] The crises before Khrushchev set the pattern of confrontation without direct war, and they explain why NATO was armed and why Germany stayed divided.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.0] The Berlin Blockade (June 1948 to May 1949) was the first major crisis. It was ended by the Western airlift, and it confirmed the division of Germany. NATO was founded in 1949.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.1] The Korean War began in June 1950 with the North Korean invasion of the South. It persuaded Western governments that communism was expansionist, so they rearmed.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.2] As a result NATO was rearmed and given a unified command under Eisenhower, appointed its first Supreme Allied Commander in Europe in December 1950, and the West pushed to rearm West Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.3] Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 opened a more flexible phase, and an armistice in Korea followed in July 1953.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.b] Khrushchev led the Soviet Union from Stalin's death in 1953 to 1964 and set the terms of the Cold War between de-Stalinisation and nuclear confrontation. He was shaped by Stalin's system, and then attacked it.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.0] He was born in 1894 in Kalinovka, a village in Kursk province, of peasant stock. He worked as a miner and metalworker before rising through the Communist Party.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.1] Under Stalin he became first secretary of the Moscow party organisation in 1935 and of the Ukrainian party in 1938, which shows he was a trusted servant of the Stalinist system.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.2] He took part in Stalin's purges in Moscow and Ukraine in the late 1930s. This later made his attacks on Stalin's terror open to challenge, because he had helped to carry it out.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.3] In the West contemporaries saw him as blustering, unpredictable and dangerous, but also as a man who could be dealt with, as shown by his 1959 visit to the USA and his 'kitchen debate' with Vice-President Nixon.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.4] Mao Zedong's China called him a 'revisionist' who had betrayed Marxism-Leninism. By 1964 his Soviet colleagues saw him as a reckless leader of 'hare-brained schemes'.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.5] Historians such as William Taubman, in Khrushchev: The Man and His Era (2003), describe a reformer who opened Soviet society after Stalin but whose policies were improvised and often failed, and who never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.b] After Stalin's death no one had a clear right to rule, so Khrushchev won power by defeating rivals one at a time. Note that he held the party post first and became head of government only later.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.0] He became First Secretary of the Central Committee in September 1953, the party post that was the real centre of power.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.1] Lavrentiy Beria, the former secret police chief, was arrested in June 1953 and executed in December 1953, which removed the most feared rival.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.2] Georgy Malenkov was forced to resign as premier in 1955, weakening another rival who had been the head of government since Stalin's death.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.3] In June 1957 Khrushchev defeated the 'anti-party group' of Molotov, Malenkov and Kaganovich, with the help of Marshal Zhukov and the Central Committee.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.4] In March 1958 he became chairman of the Council of Ministers (premier) as well as First Secretary, so from then on he held both the leading party post and the top government post.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.b] At the 20th Party Congress in 1956 Khrushchev did two things that changed Soviet politics: he attacked Stalin's record, and he redefined the Soviet struggle with the West as competition rather than war.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.0] On 25 February 1956, in a secret speech to a closed session of the 20th Party Congress, he denounced Stalin's cult of personality and his terror against party members.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.1] The attack was limited, because it condemned Stalin's crimes against party members and the cult of personality, not communism, collectivisation or the one-party state.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.2] The text of the speech was published in the West in 1956, within months, so it soon became known across the world and not just in the Soviet bloc.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.3] Its shock in Eastern Europe fed the Polish October of 1956, in which Władysław Gomułka came to power, and the Hungarian Uprising, because people hoped the bloc's rulers would loosen their control.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.4] At the same congress he argued that war with the capitalist states was not inevitable. This became known as peaceful coexistence: competition would be economic and ideological, not military.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.b] Khrushchev's denunciation of Stalin was followed by real changes, which made Soviet life less frightening, though they stopped well short of democracy.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.0] He released many political prisoners from the Gulag, the system of labour camps, so that people who had been held under Stalin returned to ordinary life.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.1] The secret police were curbed, their role reduced, and they were placed under party control, so they could no longer act as a power above the party.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.2] A cultural 'thaw' allowed more open discussion in literature, but it had limits, because the party still decided how far criticism could go.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.3] At the 22nd Party Congress in October 1961 Stalin's body was removed from the Lenin Mausoleum, a public sign that his reputation had been cast down.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.b] Khrushchev tried to raise food output and make the economy more responsive. His schemes brought early gains but then failures, and the failures fed the resentment that ended his rule.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.0] The Virgin Lands campaign from 1954 ploughed up grassland in Kazakhstan and western Siberia to grow grain, and it produced good harvests in its early years.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.1] Soil erosion, drought and poor management later brought failures, most seriously in 1963, when the harvest was bad and grain had to be imported from the West.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.2] After his 1959 visit to the USA he also launched a maize (corn) campaign, copying the American farms he had seen.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.3] In 1957 industry was decentralised into regional economic councils (sovnarkhozy), so that decisions moved from Moscow ministries to the regions.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.4] In 1962 the party was split into industrial and agricultural branches. Both reforms were resented by officials, because they threatened their jobs, and both were reversed by his successors.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.b] Khrushchev promised Soviet citizens a better life and a race to overtake the USA. Space successes gave him prestige abroad, but the economy did not deliver at home.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.0] He launched a large programme of new flats and promised more consumer goods, and he promised that the Soviet Union would overtake the USA.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.1] The 1961 party programme at the 22nd Congress promised that communism would be built 'in the main' by 1980, a target that raised hopes that could not be met.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.2] In June 1962 a strike at Novocherkassk over higher food prices and wage cuts was crushed when troops fired on the crowd and more than twenty people were killed, a sign of the limits of his reforms.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.3] Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, was launched on 4 October 1957, and Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on 12 April 1961.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.4] These successes alarmed the West, which feared a 'missile gap', meaning that the Soviet Union might be ahead in long-range missiles, and they boosted Soviet prestige.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.b] Peaceful coexistence showed in a series of gestures to the West and to Yugoslavia, but the limits of the policy showed when the Soviet bloc itself was challenged.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.0] In May 1955 the Austrian State Treaty was signed: Soviet and Western troops left Austria and it became neutral, which showed Moscow could agree to a withdrawal.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.1] In 1955 Khrushchev visited Josip Broz Tito in Belgrade to mend the split between the Soviet Union and Yugoslavia that had opened in 1948.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.2] The Geneva summit of July 1955 brought together the USA, the USSR, Britain and France, and it was the first post-war summit of the four powers, so it signalled that talking was possible.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.3] The Soviet crushing of the Hungarian Uprising in November 1956 showed that he would use force to keep the bloc together, whatever he said about coexistence.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.4] In July 1959, at the American National Exhibition in Moscow, he and Vice-President Nixon held the 'kitchen debate' on the merits of communism and capitalism.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.5] In September 1959 he visited the USA and held talks with President Eisenhower at Camp David, which produced the 'spirit of Camp David', a brief thaw that the U-2 incident later ended.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.b] Khrushchev's changes divided the communist world. Mao's China rejected them, and the quarrel meant that Moscow could no longer speak for all communists.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.0] Mao rejected de-Stalinisation and peaceful coexistence, regarding them as a betrayal of Marxism-Leninism. The ideological dispute grew from the late 1950s.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.1] The dispute became open in 1960, when Soviet advisers were withdrawn from China, and open polemics (public attacks in print) followed in the early 1960s.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.2] The split meant the Soviet Union faced two rival communist powers and could not count on Chinese support in Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.3] Albania sided with China, stopped taking part in the Warsaw Pact in the early 1960s and formally withdrew in 1968. The communist world was no longer united behind Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.b] Berlin was the most dangerous flashpoint in Europe, because West Berlin was an enclave inside East Germany and a window to the West. In 1958 Khrushchev tried to force the Western powers out.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.0] West Berlin was a showcase of Western prosperity inside communist East Germany, so every East German could compare the two systems.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.1] It was also the main escape route for East Germans. About 2.7 million people left East Germany for the West between 1949 and 1961 (sources vary slightly), mostly young and skilled.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.2] This drained the GDR's workforce and threatened its survival, which is why Moscow and East Berlin wanted the exit closed.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.3] On 27 November 1958 Khrushchev issued an ultimatum giving the Western powers six months to end their occupation of West Berlin and make it a demilitarised 'free city'. Otherwise he would hand control of access to East Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.10.4] The West refused and the deadline passed without Khrushchev carrying out his threat, so the ultimatum failed.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.b] After the thaw of 1959, two events in 1960 and 1961 ended hopes of a Berlin settlement and made a harder solution likely.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.0] On 1 May 1960 an American U-2 spy plane flown by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union near Sverdlovsk.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.1] Eisenhower would not apologise, so the Paris summit of May 1960 collapsed and the thaw of 1959 ended.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.2] Powers was exchanged for a Soviet spy in February 1962, so the affair ended with a swap rather than a trial verdict standing.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.3] At the Vienna summit in June 1961 Khrushchev pressed the new US president, John F. Kennedy (inaugurated January 1961), to accept a Berlin settlement.
[hist-eur4-s2.11.4] Kennedy refused and strengthened US forces, so the crisis deepened over the summer.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.b] Faced with the refugee flow and a failed ultimatum, East Germany sealed the border. The Wall solved the GDR's problem but became the great symbol of a divided Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.0] East German forces under Walter Ulbricht began building the Berlin Wall overnight on 12–13 August 1961, with Soviet approval. At first it was barbed wire, then concrete, and it closed the border between the two halves of the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.1] In October 1961 US and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, but both sides drew back.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.2] The Western powers protested but did not use force against the Wall. They accepted a divided city as better than war, which Khrushchev had judged correctly.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.3] Between 1961 and 1989 the Wall and the border fortifications were guarded, and people who tried to cross were shot or arrested.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.4] The refugee flow stopped and the GDR stabilised, and Soviet leaders regarded the Wall as a means of doing exactly that.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.5] On 26 June 1963 Kennedy spoke in West Berlin and used the words 'Ich bin ein Berliner' to show Western support.
[hist-eur4-s2.12.6] The Wall stood until 9 November 1989 and was the symbol of the divided Europe until then, because it showed that people had to be kept in by force.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.b] The Cuban Missile Crisis was the closest the Cold War came to nuclear war, and it was resolved by a compromise.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.0] Khrushchev wanted to protect Castro after the Bay of Pigs invasion of April 1961, a failed US-backed invasion by Cuban exiles, not an invasion by American troops.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.1] He also wanted to balance American Jupiter missiles in Turkey and Italy, and to make up for Soviet inferiority in long-range missiles, since medium-range missiles in Cuba could reach much of the USA. Possibly he also wanted leverage over Berlin.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.2] Photographs from a U-2 flight of 14 October were shown to Kennedy on 16 October 1962 and revealed Soviet medium-range missiles being installed in Cuba.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.3] Kennedy's advisers (ExComm, the Executive Committee of the National Security Council) weighed an air strike against a naval blockade and chose the blockade.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.4] Kennedy announced a naval 'quarantine' of Cuba on 22 October 1962 and demanded the removal of the missiles. He avoided the word blockade because a blockade is an act of war.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.5] For about a week the two superpowers were on the brink of war.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.6] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed to withdraw the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur4-s2.13.7] The secret part of the deal was a US promise to remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey, which was done in 1963 and not made public at the time.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.b] For Europeans the crisis was a warning, because their fate seemed to be settled by the two superpowers while they looked on.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.0] The European allies were informed, not consulted. Kennedy sent former secretary of state Dean Acheson to brief President de Gaulle in Paris, and de Gaulle gave firm support.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.1] British Prime Minister Harold Macmillan was in regular contact with Kennedy during the crisis.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.2] Europeans feared that the Soviets would answer a move on Cuba with a move on Berlin, so the crisis showed that Berlin and Cuba were linked.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.3] The crisis made West European leaders more aware that decisions on nuclear war would be taken in Washington and Moscow, which strengthened de Gaulle's case for an independent French deterrent.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.4] Khrushchev's prestige was damaged at home and in China, because the retreat looked like a defeat.
[hist-eur4-s2.14.5] Both sides then sought safeguards: the Moscow–Washington hotline (1963) and the Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed on 5 August 1963.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.b] Each superpower relied on nuclear weapons to deter attack, and the arms race under Khrushchev made that deterrence ever more dangerous.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.0] Nuclear deterrence means preventing attack by holding enough nuclear weapons to destroy an aggressor even after its first strike, a condition later called mutually assured destruction (MAD), a 1960s term.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.1] In the mid-1950s the USA followed 'massive retaliation' (the threat of an all-out nuclear response, set out by John Foster Dulles in 1954). NATO shifted to 'flexible response', a range of possible responses, in 1967.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.2] The Soviet Union tested the Tsar Bomba, about 50 megatons, on 30 October 1961, the largest nuclear explosion ever set off.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.3] Western Europe depended on the US nuclear umbrella under NATO, yet there were doubts about whether the USA would risk its own cities to defend Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.15.4] Britain tested its first atomic bomb in 1952 and France in 1960, as part of an independent deterrent. France left NATO's integrated military command in 1966.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.b] The fear that deterrence could fail led to limited agreements. Each was a step, but none ended the arms race.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.0] The Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed in Moscow on 5 August 1963 by the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union, banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.1] It did not ban underground tests, and France and China did not sign it, so its effect was limited.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.2] The Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968 is a different treaty: non-nuclear states promised not to acquire nuclear weapons, while the nuclear powers agreed to pursue disarmament.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.3] SALT I was signed in Moscow on 26 May 1972 by Nixon and Brezhnev. It limited anti-ballistic missile systems (the ABM Treaty) and placed an interim limit on strategic missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.16.4] SALT II was signed in Vienna in June 1979 but was never ratified by the US Senate, and it was shelved after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.b] Détente means the easing of tension, from about 1969 to the late 1970s, through summits, arms-control treaties and treaties on European borders. It came about because both sides had reasons to want it.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.0] Détente is usually dated from about 1969, with Nixon, Brezhnev and Brandt, and not from 1963. The hotline and Test Ban Treaty were its forerunners.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.1] The shock of Cuba had shown both sides how close nuclear war could come, and the cost of the arms race was heavy.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.2] By about 1970 the two sides had rough nuclear parity, so neither could expect to win an arms race.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.3] The Soviet Union wanted Western trade and technology and recognition of the post-war borders.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.4] The USA was entangled in Vietnam, and the Sino-Soviet split gave Moscow another reason to ease tension in the West.
[hist-eur4-s2.17.5] West Germany wanted to ease the division of Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.b] West Germany led the way in détente with Ostpolitik, a policy of reaching agreements with the East. It was Brandt's policy, not Adenauer's, and it settled many of the questions left by the war.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.0] Willy Brandt, West German Chancellor from 1969 to 1974, pursued Ostpolitik to improve relations with Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.1] The Treaty of Moscow of 12 August 1970 renounced the use of force between the Federal Republic and the Soviet Union, so it removed a fear of war from the German question.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.2] The Treaty of Warsaw of December 1970 recognised the Oder–Neisse line as Poland's western border, and Brandt knelt at the Warsaw Ghetto memorial, a gesture of remorse that won wide attention. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1971.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.3] The Four Power Agreement on Berlin of 3 September 1971 guaranteed access between West Berlin and West Germany and confirmed the Western presence in the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.18.4] The Basic Treaty of December 1972 established relations between the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic, which led to both joining the United Nations in 1973.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.b] The Helsinki conference was the high point of détente. It gave each side something it wanted, and over time the Western gain proved the more important.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.0] The Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe produced the Helsinki Final Act on 1 August 1975, signed by 35 states including the USA, Canada and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.1] It treated the post-war borders in Europe as inviolable, a Soviet gain, because Moscow had long wanted its control of Eastern Europe accepted.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.2] It committed the signatories to respect human rights, a Western gain, and later Eastern dissidents used this commitment to press their governments.
[hist-eur4-s2.19.3] It was not a peace treaty and was not legally binding, so it relied on the signatories' political commitment.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.b] Détente broke down in the late 1970s. Its end opened the second Cold War, a phase of renewed confrontation.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.0] Soviet forces invaded Afghanistan in December 1979, which ended détente because it convinced the USA that Moscow was expansionist again.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.1] The USA responded with a grain embargo in January 1980 and, with some allies, a boycott of the Moscow Olympics in 1980.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.2] SALT II, signed in June 1979, was shelved and never ratified by the US Senate, so the arms limits it would have set never took effect.
[hist-eur4-s2.20.3] Détente had collapsed because each side read it differently and the arms building had continued.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.b] A new arms race in Europe led to the deployment of intermediate-range missiles in the early 1980s, and it provoked mass protest.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.0] The Soviet Union deployed SS-20 intermediate-range missiles aimed at Western Europe from the late 1970s, which threatened Western European cities.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.1] NATO's double-track decision of 12 December 1979 agreed to deploy Pershing II and ground-launched cruise missiles unless talks with the Soviet Union succeeded.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.2] The talks failed. Deployment began in November 1983 and the Soviet delegation walked out of the Geneva arms talks that month.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.3] Large peace demonstrations took place in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands in 1981–83 against the new missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.21.4] One example was the Greenham Common women's peace camp in Berkshire, England, which began in 1981.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.b] Ronald Reagan's election brought a harder line towards Moscow. This raised tension to its highest point since Cuba before talks reversed it.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.0] Reagan was elected in November 1980. In March 1983 he called the Soviet Union an 'evil empire', and he announced the Strategic Defense Initiative ('Star Wars') in the same month.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.1] In November 1983 NATO held the exercise Able Archer 83. Soviet fears of a surprise attack were heightened, though historians still debate how alarmed Soviet leaders really were.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.2] Mikhail Gorbachev became Soviet leader in March 1985, which opened a new phase because he was willing to negotiate real cuts.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.3] At the Reykjavik summit in October 1986 the two leaders discussed deep cuts in weapons.
[hist-eur4-s2.22.4] The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was signed in Washington in December 1987 and removed the Euromissiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.b] Examiners ask which events changed the course of the Cold War, so know the order and what each one changed.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.0] Berlin Blockade, 1948–49: confirmed the division of Germany and led to NATO.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.1] Stalin's death in 1953 began a more flexible phase, and the Korean armistice followed in July 1953.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.2] Hungary, November 1956: the crushing of the uprising showed that the Soviet Union would use force to keep its bloc together.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.3] Berlin Wall (1961) and Cuba (1962) were the high points of confrontation, after which both sides sought safeguards such as the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.4] Prague Spring: Warsaw Pact forces invaded Czechoslovakia in August 1968, confirming the Brezhnev Doctrine, the Soviet claim that it could intervene to keep communism in power in the bloc, but it did not end détente.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.5] Helsinki, 1975: the high point of détente, which gave dissidents in the East a human rights standard.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.6] Afghanistan and the double-track decision, 1979, ended détente and began the second Cold War, and Reagan's election in 1980 hardened it.
[hist-eur4-s2.23.7] Gorbachev from 1985, the Reykjavik summit of October 1986 and the INF Treaty of 1987 reversed the arms race, and 1989 ended Soviet control of eastern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.b] Khrushchev's removal showed that the party, not the leader, held the final power. It was also unusual, because he was not killed or imprisoned.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.0] On 14 October 1964 the Central Committee removed him from the posts of First Secretary and chairman of the Council of Ministers. He had been on holiday on the Black Sea coast and was recalled.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.1] Leonid Brezhnev became First Secretary and Alexei Kosygin became chairman of the Council of Ministers, so the two posts were again held by different men.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.2] His critics cited the Cuban retreat, the failing agriculture, the repeated reorganisations that angered party officials, and his erratic personal style.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.3] He was pensioned off and lived in retirement, largely at a dacha (country house) outside Moscow, until his death in 1971. He was the first Soviet leader removed without being killed or imprisoned.
[hist-eur4-s2.24.4] His fall confirmed that the Soviet leadership would not tolerate a single figure dominating policy, which shaped the more collective style of the Brezhnev years.
[hist-eur4-s2.25.b] Détente is judged by what it achieved in treaties and what it failed to change in the arms race.
[hist-eur4-s2.25.0] Achievements: arms limits in SALT I, the German treaties such as the Moscow Treaty and the Basic Treaty, and Helsinki's human rights standard.
[hist-eur4-s2.25.1] Limits: arms building continued despite the agreements, and each side read détente differently, since the West hoped for restraint and the Soviet Union for recognition of its gains.
[hist-eur4-s2.25.2] Détente ended in 1979 with Afghanistan and the Euromissiles, which suggests it had never settled the basic rivalry.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.b] Khrushchev's record is judged by contrasting his bold moves with the setbacks that ended his rule, and a stated verdict such as 'partly successful' scores better than a narrative.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.0] At home, his achievements were de-Stalinisation, the release of prisoners, the housing programme and the space successes of Sputnik and Gagarin.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.1] At home, his failures were the Virgin Lands crisis, the reversed reorganisations, Novocherkassk and promises that went unkept.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.2] Abroad, his achievements were peaceful coexistence, the Austrian State Treaty, the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.3] Abroad, his failures were Hungary, the failed Berlin ultimatum, the Wall (a propaganda defeat), the Cuban retreat and the split with China.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.4] Compared with Gorbachev: both were reformers from above who lost power. Khrushchev kept the one-party system and the bloc by force, while Gorbachev let both go.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.5] Contemporary Western views often saw a bully and gambler, and his Soviet colleagues attacked a schemer whose 'hare-brained schemes' and boasting were reckless.
[hist-eur4-s2.26.6] Later historians judge him as a reformer who made the system less brutal and opened it, but never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s1: 1. Origins of the Cold War, the division of Germany and the Sovietisation of Eastern Europe, 1945–1949
[hist-eur4-s1.0.b] The Cold War grew out of a wartime partnership that was always uneasy. The USA, Britain and the Soviet Union were allies against Hitler, but they stood for opposite systems and never fully trusted one another.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.0] The Grand Alliance began in 1941, when Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June and the USA entered the war in December. The allies fought together because they shared an enemy, not because they shared aims.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.1] Distrust was old. The West had intervened against the Bolsheviks after 1917, and Stalin had signed the Nazi–Soviet Pact with Hitler in August 1939, so each side suspected the other of putting its own interests first.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.2] Capitalism means private ownership and production for profit in a market economy; it was the system the USA defended. Western democracy added free elections and several parties competing for power.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.3] Communism, as practised in the Soviet bloc (the group of states under Soviet leadership), meant a single party controlling the state, with industry publicly owned and centrally planned. The two systems each claimed to be the future, which made lasting friendship unlikely.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.4] Soviet war dead numbered over 20 million (estimates go up to about 27 million), and Germany had invaded Russia through Poland in 1914 and again in 1941. This is why Stalin demanded security on his western frontier.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.5] The Cold War is the rivalry of the two blocs short of direct war between the superpowers (the USA and the Soviet Union). It was fought by propaganda, aid, alliances, arms and crises, and is dated about 1945 to 1991.
[hist-eur4-s1.0.6] The term was used by the American financier Bernard Baruch in a 1947 speech and popularised by the journalist Walter Lippmann's book The Cold War (1947).
[hist-eur4-s1.1.b] Even while Germany was still undefeated, quarrels over strategy and over Poland showed that the allies wanted different things from the peace. These disputes shaped the bargaining at Yalta.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.0] The second front argument: Stalin pressed the Western allies to open a second front in western Europe to relieve the Red Army, and resented the delay because the Soviet Union was bearing the brunt of the fighting.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.1] Katyn: Polish officers had been murdered by the Soviet NKVD (secret police) in 1940. In 1943 the Polish government-in-exile in London asked for an inquiry, so the Soviet Union broke off relations with it.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.2] The Warsaw Rising of August–October 1944 was a Polish revolt against the Germans. The Red Army did not come to its aid, which Poles and the West read as Stalin letting non-communist resistance be crushed.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.3] In October 1944 in Moscow, Churchill and Stalin informally agreed spheres of influence (areas where one power's wishes would prevail) in the Balkans. This percentages agreement was an informal note, not a formal treaty.
[hist-eur4-s1.1.4] The percentages gave the Soviet Union predominance in Romania (90 per cent) and Bulgaria (75 per cent), and Britain, with the USA, predominance in Greece (90 per cent). Yugoslavia and Hungary were shared 50/50.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.b] Yalta was the last wartime meeting of the three leaders, held while Germany's defeat was certain. It settled how Germany would be handled and made promises about Poland, but it did not divide Europe.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.0] The Yalta Conference met in the Crimea from 4 to 11 February 1945, attended by Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.1] Germany was to be divided into occupation zones, with France to receive one, and Berlin was to be divided into sectors. These arrangements fixed who would hold which part of Germany after surrender.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.2] On Poland, the Soviet-backed Lublin government was to be broadened to include non-communists and free elections were promised. Poland's eastern border was also moved west.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.3] The Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections in liberated states. Stalin's later disregard of it fed Western distrust, because the promise was broken in the very states it covered.
[hist-eur4-s1.2.4] The Soviet Union agreed to enter the war against Japan after Germany's defeat, and a founding conference of the United Nations was agreed (held in San Francisco in April 1945).
[hist-eur4-s1.2.5] Yalta is often blamed for splitting Europe, but it only agreed zones for Germany and promises on Poland. The division followed from the Red Army's presence on the ground and from later events.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.b] Between Yalta and the end of the war the people and the balance of power changed. New Western leaders, a new weapon and the Red Army's position in eastern Europe all hardened attitudes.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.0] Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945 and Harry Truman became president. Truman took a harder line towards Moscow, so cooperation became less likely.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.1] Clement Attlee replaced Churchill as British prime minister in July 1945, during the Potsdam Conference, after a British election. Truman, Stalin and Attlee therefore finished the conference.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.2] By May 1945 the Red Army held Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, eastern Germany and most of Czechoslovakia. Soviet power on the ground shaped what the West could demand.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.3] Stalin, as reported by the Yugoslav communist Milovan Djilas, said that whoever occupies a territory also imposes on it his own social system. This shows what he expected to happen where the Red Army stood.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.4] Stalin wanted a buffer zone (a belt of friendly states acting as a barrier) along his western frontier, and a sphere of influence in eastern Europe. He argued this was needed for security after the invasions of 1914 and 1941 and the losses of the war.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.5] Historians ask how far these aims were defensive. Whatever Stalin's intention, Western leaders saw a buffer zone built by force and rigged politics as expansion.
[hist-eur4-s1.3.6] The USA tested its first atomic bomb on 16 July 1945, during Potsdam, and used atomic bombs on Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August). This gave the USA a monopoly, a weapon the Soviet Union lacked, until the first Soviet atomic test in August 1949.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.b] At Potsdam the victors had to decide what to do with a defeated Germany. They agreed broad aims, but because each power ran its own zone, the aims were never fully put into practice.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.0] The Potsdam Conference met from 17 July to 2 August 1945. Truman and Stalin were joined by Churchill, who was then replaced by Attlee.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.1] Potsdam set four aims, the four Ds: demilitarisation, denazification, democratisation and decentralisation. Germany was also to be run as a single economic unit, so that the zones would not become separate economies.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.2] On reparations (payment taken from a defeated state for war damage), each power took them from its own zone. The Soviet Union was also to get a share of industrial equipment from the western zones.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.3] Land east of the Oder and Neisse rivers was placed under Polish administration until a peace treaty. Millions of Germans were expelled from it and from Czechoslovakia.
[hist-eur4-s1.4.4] No peace treaty followed, so Germany remained under the four occupying powers, who met as the Allied Control Council in Berlin. It could act only if all four agreed, which made disagreement easy to turn into deadlock.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.b] Germany was run by four occupying powers, each controlling one area. Berlin, the old capital, was inside the Soviet zone, and that position later made it the focus of tension.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.0] The Soviet Union held the east, Britain the north-west (including the Ruhr, the industrial heartland), and the USA the south. France held the south-west.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.1] The zones were planned by the Allies in 1944, but France's zone was agreed at Yalta and carved out of the American and British zones. So France came in later and was the junior partner.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.2] Berlin lay deep inside the Soviet zone. It was divided into four sectors: Soviet (east), American, British and French.
[hist-eur4-s1.5.3] The Western sectors could be reached only along agreed routes through Soviet-held territory. This meant the Western powers depended on Soviet cooperation to supply their part of the city, and gave Stalin a way to apply pressure.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.b] The Allies had agreed to treat Germany as one unit, but the occupiers wanted different things. Between 1945 and 1947 the Soviet and Western zones moved steadily apart.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.0] The Soviet Union dismantled factories and took reparations from its zone, which slowed recovery there. The West wanted German industry to revive, so the two policies pulled in opposite directions.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.1] In 1946 General Lucius Clay, the US military governor, halted reparations deliveries from the US zone to the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.2] In September 1946 the US Secretary of State James Byrnes made a speech at Stuttgart saying the USA would stay in Germany and wanted its recovery. It told Germans and the Soviets that the West would not withdraw.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.3] In April 1946 the Socialist Unity Party (SED) was formed in the Soviet zone by the forced merger of the communists and the social democrats. This gave the Soviet zone a ruling party under communist control.
[hist-eur4-s1.6.4] On 1 January 1947 the American and British zones were merged as Bizonia, to make recovery easier. By 1947 the Western powers had concluded that the single economy agreed at Potsdam could not be restored.
[hist-eur4-s1.7.b] In 1946 both sides stopped hiding their suspicions. Speeches and telegrams put into words the idea that the two systems were opponents.
[hist-eur4-s1.7.0] In his election speech of February 1946, Stalin said capitalism made war inevitable. Western leaders took this as a sign that he saw conflict with them as unavoidable.
[hist-eur4-s1.7.1] George Kennan, a US diplomat in Moscow, sent his Long Telegram on 22 February 1946. It argued that Soviet hostility was built into the Soviet system and that Soviet power would yield to firm resistance.
[hist-eur4-s1.7.2] Containment, the American policy of resisting the spread of Soviet power and communism, was named in Kennan's anonymous 'X' article of 1947. The telegram supplied the ideas, not the word.
[hist-eur4-s1.7.3] The Soviet counterpart was the Novikov telegram of September 1946, which described the USA as seeking world supremacy. The two sides were reading each other in the worst light.
[hist-eur4-s1.7.4] Churchill spoke at Westminster College, Fulton, Missouri, on 5 March 1946, with Truman present: 'From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent.' Stettin is now Szczecin in Poland.
[hist-eur4-s1.7.5] The Iron Curtain was Churchill's image of the line dividing Soviet-controlled eastern Europe from the West. Later the term was used for the fortified border itself.
[hist-eur4-s1.7.6] Stalin replied in Pravda in March 1946, comparing Churchill to Hitler and calling the speech a call to war against the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s1.8.b] By 1947 Britain was too weak to keep supporting states on the edge of Soviet influence. The USA stepped in, and in doing so announced a principle that guided its policy for years.
[hist-eur4-s1.8.0] In February 1947 Britain told the USA that it could no longer support Greece and Turkey, because it could not afford to. This created the opening for the USA to take on that role.
[hist-eur4-s1.8.1] The Greek Civil War (1946–49) was fought between the government and communist guerrillas. The guerrillas were defeated in 1949 and Greece stayed in the Western camp; it was the first test of the Truman Doctrine.
[hist-eur4-s1.8.2] Turkey was under Soviet pressure over the Straits (the waterway linking the Black Sea to the Mediterranean), so it also looked to the West.
[hist-eur4-s1.8.3] On 12 March 1947 Truman asked Congress for about 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey. Congress approved it in May 1947.
[hist-eur4-s1.8.4] Truman went wider than these two states: 'It must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures.' This is the Truman Doctrine, a commitment to contain communism, not just to help Greece.
[hist-eur4-s1.9.b] Truman's doctrine offered help to states under direct pressure. The Marshall Plan offered economic help to the whole of Europe, on the reasoning that hunger and ruin would make communism attractive.
[hist-eur4-s1.9.0] George Marshall, the US Secretary of State, announced the plan in a speech at Harvard University on 5 June 1947.
[hist-eur4-s1.9.1] Its aims were economic recovery, stopping communism's appeal (people who are poor and desperate are more likely to turn to communists), and rebuilding European markets for American goods.
[hist-eur4-s1.9.2] The European Recovery Programme ran from 1948 to 1952 and provided about 13 billion dollars, mostly as grants. Ireland took part, receiving its aid mostly as loans.
[hist-eur4-s1.9.3] The Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC) was set up in April 1948, with 16 states sharing the aid and coordinating how it was used.
[hist-eur4-s1.9.4] The offer was open to all of Europe, but Stalin forbade eastern states to take part. The aid therefore helped Western Europe recover while deepening the division of the continent.
[hist-eur4-s1.10.b] Stalin saw Marshall aid as a way of drawing eastern Europe into the Western camp. He answered by tightening control over his allies and tying their economies to Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s1.10.0] The Soviet Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov left the Paris talks on the plan in July 1947. Czechoslovakia and Poland had wanted to attend, but withdrew under Soviet pressure in the same month.
[hist-eur4-s1.10.1] The 'Molotov Plan' was a name for the bilateral trade agreements that bound eastern Europe to Moscow from 1947. It was not a formal aid programme like Marshall's, so give it no figure.
[hist-eur4-s1.10.2] The Cominform (Communist Information Bureau) was founded in September 1947 at a meeting in Poland, linking nine communist parties, including the French and Italian, under Soviet direction.
[hist-eur4-s1.10.3] At the meeting Andrei Zhdanov gave his 'two camps' speech: the world was divided into an imperialist camp led by the USA and a democratic camp led by the Soviet Union. This left no room for neutrality.
[hist-eur4-s1.10.4] The Comintern (Communist International) had been dissolved in 1943. The Cominform was a different body, made up of parties and aimed at keeping them in line.
[hist-eur4-s1.10.5] Comecon, the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, was set up in January 1949 as the bloc's economic answer to the Marshall Plan.
[hist-eur4-s1.11.b] Sovietisation was the remaking of eastern European states on the Soviet model: one-party communist rule, secret police, a planned economy, collectivised farming and loyalty to Moscow, imposed with Red Army backing. It happened in stages and by a set of repeated methods.
[hist-eur4-s1.11.0] The historian Hugh Seton-Watson (The East European Revolution, 1950) described three stages: genuine coalition, bogus coalition and monolithic (single-party) regime. The takeovers were complete by 1948.
[hist-eur4-s1.11.1] In the first stage, communist parties governed through coalitions with peasant, socialist and liberal parties while taking key posts, such as the interior ministry (which controlled the police), for themselves.
[hist-eur4-s1.11.2] Salami tactics means removing opponents one slice at a time, so that no single step provoked a united resistance. The phrase is usually attributed to the Hungarian communist leader Mátyás Rákosi.
[hist-eur4-s1.11.3] The tools were the Red Army's presence, Soviet and local secret police, communist control of interior ministries and police, and land reform to win over peasants. They also included rigged elections, arrests and show trials, and forced mergers of socialist parties into communist ones.
[hist-eur4-s1.11.4] Land reform in 1945–47 broke up large estates in Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and the Soviet zone of Germany and gave land to small farmers, which helped the communists win support.
[hist-eur4-s1.11.5] A satellite state is formally independent but controlled by the Soviet Union in politics, economy and defence. By 1948 the states of eastern Europe had become satellites.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.b] Poland, where the Soviet Union had the strongest interest, and Hungary, where the communists began weak, show how the same methods worked in different conditions.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.0] Poland: the Soviet Union backed the communist-dominated Lublin government (formed in 1944), while Britain and the USA backed the London government-in-exile. Poland was the first flashpoint.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.1] Poland: leaders of the Polish underground were arrested in 1945 and tried in Moscow in June 1945 (the Trial of the Sixteen), which removed non-communist resistance leaders early.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.2] Poland: the election of January 1947 was rigged, and the Peasant Party leader and deputy prime minister Stanisław Mikołajczyk fled in October 1947.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.3] Poland: the Polish Workers' Party and the Polish Socialist Party merged into the Polish United Workers' Party (PZPR) in December 1948, ending independent socialist opposition. Bolesław Bierut became the leading communist, as president.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.4] Poland: Władysław Gomułka was removed as party leader in 1948 for 'nationalist deviation' and later arrested, showing that even communists who sought a separate path were purged.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.5] Hungary: in the free election of November 1945 the Smallholders won about 57 per cent and the communists only about 17 per cent. The communists therefore had to use police power rather than votes.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.6] Hungary: Rákosi used the police and interior ministry to break the Smallholders. Their leader Béla Kovács was arrested by Soviet forces in February 1947 and Prime Minister Ferenc Nagy was forced to resign in May 1947.
[hist-eur4-s1.12.7] Hungary: the August 1947 election was rigged and the communists became the largest party with about 22 per cent. Communists and social democrats merged in June 1948; the Hungarian People's Republic followed in 1949.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.b] In the rest of the region the pattern was the same: pressure from the Red Army and the police, then the removal of the leading opponent.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.0] Hungary: Cardinal József Mindszenty was arrested in December 1948, tried in February 1949 and given life imprisonment. This showed that the Church, a source of opposition, was also under attack.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.1] Hungary: László Rajk, a communist who had been interior minister (running the police) and then foreign minister, was tried in September 1949 and executed in October 1949, so even leading communists were show-trial victims.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.2] Romania: the Groza government was imposed under Soviet pressure in March 1945. The National Peasant leader Iuliu Maniu was tried in 1947 and imprisoned for life.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.3] Romania: King Michael was forced to abdicate on 30 December 1947 and a people's republic was proclaimed.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.4] Bulgaria: the Fatherland Front was a communist-dominated coalition. The monarchy was abolished after a referendum in September 1946 and Georgi Dimitrov became communist prime minister.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.5] Bulgaria: the Agrarian leader Nikola Petkov was hanged in September 1947, which removed the main opposition leader.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.6] Czechoslovakia: in the May 1946 election the communists were the largest party with about 38 per cent, and Klement Gottwald became prime minister. It was the only eastern state to stay democratic until 1948.
[hist-eur4-s1.13.7] Czechoslovakia: on 20 February 1948 non-communist ministers resigned over communist control of the police. President Edvard Beneš accepted a communist-dominated government on 25 February, and he resigned in June 1948.
[hist-eur4-s1.14.b] Two stories fit awkwardly into the pattern: the Soviet zone of Germany, which became a state of its own, and Yugoslavia and Albania, which had communist governments that did not depend on the Red Army.
[hist-eur4-s1.14.0] Jan Masaryk, the non-communist foreign minister, was found dead below his window on 10 March 1948. Whether it was murder or suicide is still disputed, but it shocked Western opinion. He was the son of Tomáš Masaryk, Czechoslovakia's founding president.
[hist-eur4-s1.14.1] In the Soviet zone of Germany the SED, formed in 1946, ruled alongside the dismantling of industry. Walter Ulbricht was the leading German communist, and the zone was on the road to becoming the GDR.
[hist-eur4-s1.14.2] Yugoslavia under Josip Broz Tito and Albania under Enver Hoxha had communist governments that came to power largely through their own wartime resistance, not the Red Army. Tito was a partisan leader.
[hist-eur4-s1.14.3] Because Tito did not owe his power to Moscow, he could resist Stalin. Yugoslavia was expelled from the Cominform in June 1948, showing that a communist state could break from Moscow without ceasing to be communist.
[hist-eur4-s1.14.4] Purges of 'Titoists' followed elsewhere, including Rajk in Hungary, Traicho Kostov, a Bulgarian communist executed in December 1949, and Gomułka in Poland. Slánský in Czechoslovakia followed later, in 1952.
[hist-eur4-s1.15.b] After 1948 the satellites were made to copy the Soviet Union in detail. The forces that might have resisted had by then been broken one by one.
[hist-eur4-s1.15.0] The Soviet model was copied: central planning, heavy industry, the start of collectivisation of farms, the cult of Stalin and further purges. This made the states satellites, not just friendly neighbours.
[hist-eur4-s1.15.1] Opposition came from peasant parties, the Church, socialists and, in Poland, an armed underground. Each was dealt with separately, which is why salami tactics worked: no group could count on the others.
[hist-eur4-s1.15.2] The peasant parties lost their leaders (Mikołajczyk, Kovács, Petkov, Maniu) and the socialists were merged into communist parties, such as the SED and the PZPR, so they lost their independence.
[hist-eur4-s1.15.3] The Church was attacked, as Mindszenty's trial shows. The Red Army and secret police stood behind the local communists, so open resistance risked Soviet force.
[hist-eur4-s1.15.4] Yugoslavia was the partial exception, because there was no Red Army occupation and Tito's own partisans held power.
[hist-eur4-s1.16.b] As Soviet and Western policies on Germany diverged, the Western powers decided to build a West German state and to revive its economy. The currency reform was the step that brought the crisis.
[hist-eur4-s1.16.0] The London Six-Power Conference (USA, Britain, France and the Benelux countries) met from February to June 1948 and recommended a West German state. This broke with the idea of treating Germany as a single unit.
[hist-eur4-s1.16.1] In March 1948 the Soviet delegate walked out of the Allied Control Council, ending four-power government of Germany.
[hist-eur4-s1.16.2] The French zone joined the Bizone in 1948–49 (Trizonia), creating a single western area.
[hist-eur4-s1.16.3] On 20 June 1948 a currency reform in the western zones introduced the Deutsche Mark, and it was extended to West Berlin.
[hist-eur4-s1.16.4] Moscow saw the reform as a threat because it signalled a lasting West German state.
[hist-eur4-s1.17.b] Stalin used Berlin's position inside the Soviet zone to try to reverse Western plans. The Western reply was to supply the city by air, which turned a Soviet pressure tactic into a Western propaganda success.
[hist-eur4-s1.17.0] The Soviet Union blockaded road, rail and canal links to West Berlin, and the blockade was complete from 24 June 1948. West Berlin had a population of over 2 million.
[hist-eur4-s1.17.1] Stalin's aims were to make the West drop the new currency and the West German state, or leave Berlin altogether.
[hist-eur4-s1.17.2] The Western powers organised an airlift from June 1948 to September 1949, called Operation Vittles by the Americans and Operation Plainfare by the British.
[hist-eur4-s1.17.3] Planes used three airfields: Tempelhof (American sector), Gatow (British) and Tegel (French, built during the airlift). At its peak about one plane landed a minute.
[hist-eur4-s1.17.4] About 2.3 million tons of supplies were delivered. Truman refused to withdraw.
[hist-eur4-s1.17.5] General Lucius Clay, the US military governor, and Ernst Reuter led the Western and Berlin response. Reuter led West Berlin and became its mayor in December 1948, and in September 1948 he appealed to the people of the world to look upon the city.
[hist-eur4-s1.17.6] The blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949, though the airlift continued until September 1949.
[hist-eur4-s1.18.b] The blockade failed, and by the end of 1949 Germany had two states. Berlin became a symbol and a flashpoint, and the crisis pushed the West towards a military alliance.
[hist-eur4-s1.18.0] The blockade was a Western propaganda victory: the West was seen as feeding a city that the Soviet Union tried to starve. It confirmed Germany's division and pushed the West towards NATO.
[hist-eur4-s1.18.1] The Federal Republic of Germany was founded on 23 May 1949 under a Basic Law, with Bonn as its capital. Konrad Adenauer, a Christian Democrat, became chancellor in September 1949.
[hist-eur4-s1.18.2] The German Democratic Republic was proclaimed on 7 October 1949 in the Soviet zone. Wilhelm Pieck was president and Otto Grotewohl prime minister, but Walter Ulbricht was the leading figure in the SED.
[hist-eur4-s1.18.3] East Berlin was the capital of the GDR, while West Berlin was run by the Western powers and closely tied to the Federal Republic, so Berlin stayed divided.
[hist-eur4-s1.19.b] Fear of Soviet power led the Western states to ally, and later the Soviet Union created a rival alliance. The two alliances made the division of Europe a military one as well as a political and economic one.
[hist-eur4-s1.19.0] The Brussels Treaty of 17 March 1948 linked Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg. It was the starting point for NATO.
[hist-eur4-s1.19.1] The North Atlantic Treaty was signed in Washington on 4 April 1949 by twelve states: the USA, Canada, Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Norway, Denmark, Iceland, Portugal and Italy.
[hist-eur4-s1.19.2] Article 5 says that an attack on one member is an attack on all. This tied US troops to Europe, so the USA was committed to defend it.
[hist-eur4-s1.19.3] Lord Ismay, NATO's first Secretary General (1952), is credited with saying its purpose was 'to keep the Russians out, the Americans in, and the Germans down.' Greece and Turkey joined in 1952.
[hist-eur4-s1.19.4] West Germany joined NATO in May 1955, after its rearmament was agreed; the European Defence Community had failed in 1954.
[hist-eur4-s1.19.5] The Warsaw Pact was signed in Warsaw on 14 May 1955, as the Soviet reply to West German entry into NATO. Its members were the Soviet Union, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, East Germany and Albania, with a Soviet commander and Soviet troops in member states.
[hist-eur4-s1.19.6] Albania stopped taking part in the early 1960s and formally left in 1968.
[hist-eur4-s1.20.b] Historians disagree about who was to blame, and the opening of Soviet archives after 1991 changed the terms of the debate. Good answers pair the views and finish with a judgement.
[hist-eur4-s1.20.0] Orthodox view: Western writers of the 1940s and 1950s blamed Stalin's drive to expand and the threat it posed to Western Europe. Evidence for this is the way eastern Europe was taken over by rigged elections, purges and one-party rule.
[hist-eur4-s1.20.1] Revisionist view: William Appleman Williams, in The Tragedy of American Diplomacy (1959), argued that US economic expansion and the atomic monopoly provoked Soviet hostility.
[hist-eur4-s1.20.2] Post-revisionist view: mutual misperception, each side reading the other's defensive moves as threats. John Lewis Gaddis later, after the archives partly opened, put more weight on Stalin in We Now Know (1997).
[hist-eur4-s1.20.3] Stalin's control of eastern Europe went far beyond a defensive buffer. Historians who stress the defensive side point to the wartime invasions of Russia and the huge war losses; those who stress the offensive side point to the speed with which communist rule was imposed in 1947–48.
[hist-eur4-s1.20.4] Western decisions such as the currency reform of June 1948 deepened the division of Germany, which Soviet leaders could regard as a threat; so each side's actions made the other's worse.
[hist-eur4-s1.21.b] By 1949 Stalin had built a bloc of communist states, but the bloc had limits and had provoked a strong Western reaction.
[hist-eur4-s1.21.0] Achievement: eight communist states were under Soviet leadership, with one-party rule in each by 1948.
[hist-eur4-s1.21.1] Achievement: the Cominform tied the parties to Moscow and Comecon tied the economies, so the bloc had political and economic institutions of its own.
[hist-eur4-s1.21.2] Achievement: purges of rivals, including Rajk and Kostov, kept the local communist leaders obedient.
[hist-eur4-s1.21.3] Limit: Tito's Yugoslavia broke away in 1948 and survived, so Soviet control was not complete.
[hist-eur4-s1.21.4] Limit: the West held Berlin through the airlift, so the Soviet attempt to push the West out failed.
[hist-eur4-s1.21.5] Limit: Marshall aid revived Western Europe and the Soviet bloc could not match it, and NATO (1949) united the West against Soviet pressure. Stalin's actions had therefore strengthened the West he feared.
