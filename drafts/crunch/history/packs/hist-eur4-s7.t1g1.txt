TARGET hist-eur4-s7: 7. Case study: the Oil Crisis, 1973, and the Western economies, 1973–1990; Margaret Thatcher
[hist-eur4-s7.0.b] The oil crisis struck an economy that had grown rapidly for almost thirty years on cheap energy and stable exchange rates. To judge how much damage the shock did, you need to know what was already shaky before October 1973.
[hist-eur4-s7.0.0] From 1945 to 1973 Western Europe enjoyed a post-war boom of rapid growth, full employment, Marshall Aid (American money for rebuilding), free trade and a growing welfare state. People came to expect that prosperity would simply continue.
[hist-eur4-s7.0.1] Keynesian economics was the guiding idea: governments should manage demand, by spending more or cutting taxes, to keep employment high. In Britain it produced a post-war 'consensus', meaning both main parties accepted full employment and the welfare state.
[hist-eur4-s7.0.2] Western European oil consumption had risen sharply since the 1950s, and most imported oil came from the Middle East. Oil became the main energy source in the 1950s and 1960s, supplying around half or more of Western Europe's energy by 1973.
[hist-eur4-s7.0.3] Oil was bought cheaply in dollars, at about three dollars a barrel in 1973. Because economies were built around cheap oil, any large price rise would hit every importing country.
[hist-eur4-s7.0.4] The Bretton Woods system fixed exchange rates, so that currencies kept a stable value against the dollar. It collapsed in 1971–73, when the USA ended dollar convertibility to gold in August 1971, and currency values became less predictable.
[hist-eur4-s7.0.5] Inflation, a general rise in prices, had begun to rise in the late 1960s. This is why many historians argue the boom was already weakening before the oil shock.
[hist-eur4-s7.1.b] The oil shock was a political act as much as an economic one. Arab oil producers had grievances over prices and over land Israel held, and in 1973 they found a way to turn oil into a weapon.
[hist-eur4-s7.1.0] Israel was created in 1948, and the Arab–Israeli conflict that followed shaped Middle East politics for decades.
[hist-eur4-s7.1.1] In the Six-Day War of June 1967 Israel occupied Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights. Arab states wanted this land back, so Israeli withdrawal from the occupied territories became the producers' main political demand.
[hist-eur4-s7.1.2] OPEC (the Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries) was formed in 1960 and includes producers of all regions. Its Arab members had long sought higher prices for their oil.
[hist-eur4-s7.1.3] OAPEC (the Organisation of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries) was formed in 1968 and includes Arab members only, so it could act on Arab political aims such as the occupied territories.
[hist-eur4-s7.1.4] An 'oil shock' means a sudden, large rise in the price of oil that hits the economies importing it. The crisis of 1973 was the first of two such shocks.
[hist-eur4-s7.2.b] On 6 October 1973 the Arab–Israeli conflict turned into open war. Within two weeks the oil producers had used the war as the occasion to raise prices and cut supply.
[hist-eur4-s7.2.0] The Yom Kippur War began on 6 October 1973 when Egypt and Syria attacked Israel. It was the trigger for the oil crisis.
[hist-eur4-s7.2.1] The USA resupplied Israel with arms during the war. This led the Arab states to use oil as a weapon against supporters of Israel.
[hist-eur4-s7.2.2] On 16 October 1973 OPEC's Gulf members raised the posted price (the official price set by the producers) by about 70 per cent. This rise affected every importing country, not just those later singled out.
[hist-eur4-s7.2.3] On 17 October OAPEC announced output cuts of 5 per cent a month, to continue until Israel withdrew from the occupied territories. Cutting supply pushed prices up further because buyers competed for less oil.
[hist-eur4-s7.3.b] The embargo was aimed at particular countries, while the price rise hit everyone. Together they ended the era of cheap oil.
[hist-eur4-s7.3.0] An embargo is a ban on supplying particular countries. One was placed on oil exports to the USA from 19–20 October 1973 and on the Netherlands from 21 October, as supporters of Israel.
[hist-eur4-s7.3.1] The Netherlands was targeted because its government supported Israel and because Rotterdam was Europe's main oil port, so cutting it off hurt Europe's whole supply network.
[hist-eur4-s7.3.2] The Yom Kippur War ended with a ceasefire in late October 1973, following UN Security Council Resolution 338 of 22 October. Western governments then reassessed their dependence on imported energy.
[hist-eur4-s7.3.3] The price was raised again at the end of 1973. Taken with the October rise, this meant that by early 1974 oil cost about four times its pre-war price, so prices roughly quadrupled.
[hist-eur4-s7.3.4] The embargo was lifted in March 1974, but the new higher prices stayed in place. This is why the economic damage outlasted the political crisis.
[hist-eur4-s7.4.b] Governments and households reacted to shortages and soaring prices by using less fuel. The measures were visible and are easy to illustrate with named examples.
[hist-eur4-s7.4.0] Car-free Sundays were introduced in the Netherlands and West Germany in the winter of 1973–74, so that private motoring, a big user of petrol, was cut.
[hist-eur4-s7.4.1] Governments reduced speed limits, because cars burn less fuel at lower speeds, and asked households to turn down their heating.
[hist-eur4-s7.4.2] The Netherlands also issued ration coupons for petrol, so rationing is a Dutch example.
[hist-eur4-s7.4.3] In Britain, Edward Heath's government introduced a three-day working week for industry from January to March 1974. A miners' overtime ban and dispute added to the power shortage, which cut electricity supplies.
[hist-eur4-s7.5.b] Higher oil prices fed through the whole economy. Western Europe faced rising prices and falling output at the same time, a combination that its economic thinking had not prepared it for.
[hist-eur4-s7.5.0] Inflation rose sharply, because oil is used in transport, heating and manufacturing, so higher energy costs pushed up the price of almost everything.
[hist-eur4-s7.5.1] Industrial production fell and unemployment rose from the low levels of the 1960s, with the sharpest increases in heavy industry.
[hist-eur4-s7.5.2] The balance of payments of oil-importing states went into deficit. That means they paid out more for imports than they earned from exports, because they now had to pay far more for oil.
[hist-eur4-s7.5.3] The Arab producers gained huge revenues, known as petrodollars (oil income in dollars), which were recycled through Western banks.
[hist-eur4-s7.5.4] Stagflation is the combination of stagnation (no growth, falling output and rising unemployment) with rising prices. It is different from an ordinary recession and from inflation on its own.
[hist-eur4-s7.5.5] Conventional Keynesian policies struggled with stagflation. Boosting demand to cut unemployment would push prices up further, but cutting demand to reduce inflation would deepen unemployment.
[hist-eur4-s7.6.b] A second shock in 1979 meant the first was not a one-off. Together they produced the long recession and unemployment of the early 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s7.6.0] The Iranian revolution of 1979 caused the second oil shock, which is different from the first shock of 1973–74 that followed the Yom Kippur War. Oil prices roughly doubled between 1978 and 1980.
[hist-eur4-s7.6.1] The second shock pushed many economies back into a second recession in 1980–82, just as they were recovering from the first.
[hist-eur4-s7.6.2] Unemployment across the EEC rose from low levels in the early 1970s to above 10 per cent by the mid-1980s. Spain and Ireland suffered much higher rates.
[hist-eur4-s7.6.3] The rise was worst in heavy industry and in the north of Britain, Scotland and Wales, where coal, steel and shipbuilding were concentrated. British unemployment reached about three million in 1982.
[hist-eur4-s7.6.4] Riots in English cities in 1981, including Brixton and Toxteth, came during this period of recession.
[hist-eur4-s7.6.5] Rising unemployment also strained the welfare state, because more people needed benefits while fewer paid taxes.
[hist-eur4-s7.7.b] Recession and costly energy exposed industries that could no longer compete. The result was deindustrialisation, the long-term decline of manufacturing and heavy industry and the jobs it provided.
[hist-eur4-s7.7.0] Britain's steel, coal and shipbuilding industries declined sharply, and the loss of these jobs changed the regional economies of the north and Scotland.
[hist-eur4-s7.7.1] Older industries such as coal, steel and shipbuilding were cut back across Europe, because demand fell and competitors elsewhere produced more cheaply.
[hist-eur4-s7.7.2] The EEC introduced steel quotas in 1980, known as the Davignon Plan after Étienne Davignon, the commissioner for industry. Quotas limited how much steel each producer could make.
[hist-eur4-s7.7.3] West Germany contrasts with Britain: its industry moved up-market into machinery and cars, which kept it competitive.
[hist-eur4-s7.8.b] The shock made energy independence a political goal. Governments looked for new sources and for ways to use less oil.
[hist-eur4-s7.8.0] France announced a nuclear power programme in 1974, the Messmer Plan, named after Prime Minister Pierre Messmer. By the late 1980s nuclear power gave about 70 per cent of French electricity.
[hist-eur4-s7.8.1] North Sea oil began to flow in the mid-1970s. By about 1980 Britain was self-sufficient in oil and Norway a major exporter, which cushioned both economies against the second oil shock.
[hist-eur4-s7.8.2] The International Energy Agency was set up in 1974 under the OECD to coordinate oil stocks and policy among importing states.
[hist-eur4-s7.8.3] Energy conservation and the development of coal and nuclear power were promoted as ways to reduce dependence on imported oil.
[hist-eur4-s7.9.b] The crisis ended the long boom, but historians disagree about how much of that was the oil shock's doing. Examiners ask how lasting the impact was.
[hist-eur4-s7.9.0] The crisis ended the long boom and exposed the dependence of Western prosperity on cheap energy. It shifted political attention from growth to inflation and unemployment.
[hist-eur4-s7.9.1] It strengthened the case for the EEC to act together, leading to the European Monetary System in 1979.
[hist-eur4-s7.9.2] Contemporaries blamed the Arab embargo and OPEC for the economic troubles.
[hist-eur4-s7.9.3] Many economists later argued that inflation, the end of Bretton Woods and falling productivity were already weakening the boom, so the oil shock hastened the end of the boom rather than solely causing it.
[hist-eur4-s7.9.4] The embargo also gave oil-producing states a new role in world politics and led Western states to look for energy independence, through nuclear power, North Sea oil and conservation.
[hist-eur4-s7.9.5] Its impact was lasting in some ways, as energy policy changed. Unemployment and industrial decline also remained long after the embargo ended.

[hist-eur4-s7.10.1] The welfare state is state provision of health, pensions, benefits and education. It was strained by unemployment and cost after 1973. Thatcher cut the growth in spending but did not dismantle its core.
[hist-eur4-s7.10.2] Monetarism is the policy of controlling inflation by controlling the money supply, the amount of money circulating in the economy. It is the opposite of Keynesianism, which manages demand.
[hist-eur4-s7.10.3] Privatisation means selling state-owned firms to private owners. Deregulation is different: it means removing rules, as in the City of London in 1986.
[hist-eur4-s7.10.4] Trade union laws were the Employment Acts of 1980 and 1982 and the Trade Union Act of 1984, which limited union power.

[hist-eur4-s7.12.4] Under her government, fighting inflation replaced full employment as the main priority, which marked a break with the post-war consensus.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s6: 6. The Western economies, 1945–1973: growth, Marshall Aid, free trade, immigration and the welfare state
[hist-eur4-s6.0.b] To understand the post-war boom you need to know what Europe was recovering from and what its governments were determined to avoid. They rebuilt a capitalist system, but one with a much bigger role for the state than before the war.
[hist-eur4-s6.0.0] The Second World War ended in 1945 with much of Europe in ruins, while the USA emerged as the leading economy in the world. From 1947 the Cold War (the hostile standoff between the USA and the Soviet Union) began to divide Europe.
[hist-eur4-s6.0.1] Post-war planners remembered the inter-war years: the Great Depression, protectionism (high tariffs to keep foreign goods out) and mass unemployment. Governments wanted growth, open trade and full employment so that this would not happen again.
[hist-eur4-s6.0.2] Capitalism means private ownership, competitive markets and profit. Western Europe stayed capitalist, in contrast to communism, but ran a mixed economy, with state planning, nationalisation (state ownership of key industries) and welfare provision.
[hist-eur4-s6.0.3] The welfare state means the state provides health care, pensions, unemployment benefit and education, paid for through taxation and insurance contributions.
[hist-eur4-s6.0.4] A common market means free movement of goods, services, capital and workers inside a group of countries, with a common external tariff (the same import tax on goods from outside the group).
[hist-eur4-s6.1.b] Western Europe emerged from the war with damaged cities, disrupted trade and food shortages, which made reconstruction the first priority. Its weakness also made the USA's economic and political help decisive.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.0] Much of the industrial capacity of Germany, France and Italy was damaged or destroyed, and transport networks, bridges and ports had to be rebuilt before trade could restart.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.1] Food and fuel were scarce, so rationing continued in several countries into the late 1940s.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.2] The winter of 1946–47 was exceptionally severe. Coal and fuel shortages slowed factories and transport across Western Europe, including British and continental industry.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.3] Europe suffered from a dollar gap: it needed American goods, such as food and machinery, but had no dollars to pay for them, so it ran a large trade deficit with the USA.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.4] Britain borrowed heavily from the USA, including an Anglo-American loan of about 3.75 billion dollars agreed in December 1945. It stayed dependent on dollars and devalued the pound from 4.03 dollars to 2.80 dollars in September 1949.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.5] Several million German refugees and expellees from the east settled in West Germany by the early 1950s. At first they were a burden because they worsened housing shortages, but they later supplied labour for industry.
[hist-eur4-s6.1.6] The Cold War shaped recovery. In 1947 France and Italy had strong communist parties, and American leaders feared that poverty would increase their support, so the Marshall Plan aimed to prevent economic collapse from strengthening communism.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.b] The USA offered large-scale aid to rebuild Western Europe, partly out of generosity but mainly to bring stability and block the spread of communism. The aid came with conditions that pushed European states to work together.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.0] US Secretary of State George Marshall offered aid in a speech at Harvard on 5 June 1947. The Soviet Union refused and pressed its satellites (the states under its control) to refuse, and Molotov left the Paris talks in July 1947.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.1] Sixteen states took part in the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC), set up on 16 April 1948, and received aid. Recipients had to draw up plans for recovery, and the OEEC coordinated the allocation of aid.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.2] The Economic Cooperation Act passed in April 1948. The European Recovery Programme ran from 1948 to 1952 and provided about 13 billion dollars, mostly as grants, which means money that did not have to be repaid.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.3] The largest recipients were Britain, France and West Germany. Aid paid for food, fuel, raw materials and machinery, which helped solve the dollar gap.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.4] Counterpart funds were local currency raised by selling the American goods inside each country. Governments used them to finance investment in industry and housing.
[hist-eur4-s6.2.5] The OEEC later became the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) in 1961, when the USA and Canada joined. Do not confuse the OEEC with the EEC.
[hist-eur4-s6.3.b] Marshall Aid was meant to do more than supply dollars. It tied recipients to cooperation and to open trade, and historians still argue about how much it really achieved.
[hist-eur4-s6.3.0] Aid was linked to conditions: removing trade barriers, controlling inflation and keeping finances stable. This pushed European states away from the protectionism of the 1930s.
[hist-eur4-s6.3.1] The European Payments Union of 1950 introduced multilateral clearing of members' trade balances. In other words, countries settled their trade accounts together instead of one pair at a time, which helped to revive trade between members.
[hist-eur4-s6.3.2] Aid also promoted American-style productivity methods, such as more efficient factory organisation and management.
[hist-eur4-s6.3.3] Aid gave the USA political leverage. It was meant to strengthen stability and weaken communism's appeal, which fits the American policy of containment (stopping the spread of communism).
[hist-eur4-s6.3.4] The historian Alan Milward, in The Reconstruction of Western Europe, 1945–51 (1984), argued that aid was a modest share of national investment and that domestic policy mattered more.
[hist-eur4-s6.3.5] The case for aid is that it supplied dollars and confidence when Europe was short of both. The case against is that its money was a small part of total investment, so recovery depended mostly on European effort.
[hist-eur4-s6.4.b] Alongside regional recovery, the Western powers rebuilt the rules of world money and trade. Their aim was stable exchange rates and falling tariffs, the opposite of the inter-war chaos.
[hist-eur4-s6.4.0] The Bretton Woods conference of July 1944 created the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank. It fixed currencies to the US dollar, which was convertible into gold at 35 dollars an ounce.
[hist-eur4-s6.4.1] The fixed exchange rate system gave Western economies stable conditions for trade and investment, because firms could trade without fearing sudden currency swings. It lasted until the strains of the 1960s undermined it.
[hist-eur4-s6.4.2] The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) was signed at Geneva on 30 October 1947 by 23 states and came into force on 1 January 1948. It provided a framework for reducing tariffs through successive rounds of negotiation.
[hist-eur4-s6.4.3] The GATT rounds, including the Kennedy Round (talks 1964–67, concluded 1967), reduced tariffs on industrial goods across the Western world.
[hist-eur4-s6.4.4] In December 1958 the main Western European currencies became convertible, which meant they could be freely exchanged for dollars. This ended the controls on trade payments that had survived from the war.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.b] Within Western Europe, states went further than GATT by forming trading blocs. Each one lowered barriers between its members in a different way.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.0] The Benelux customs union (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg) was agreed by a convention signed in London in 1944 and began operating on 1 January 1948. A customs union removes tariffs between members and sets a common tariff against outsiders.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.1] The European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) was created by the Treaty of Paris in 1951 and came into force in 1952. It covered trade in coal and steel only.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.2] The European Economic Community (EEC) was created by the Treaty of Rome, signed on 25 March 1957 and in force from 1 January 1958. Its six members were Belgium, France, West Germany, Italy, Luxembourg and the Netherlands.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.3] The EEC abolished internal industrial tariffs on 1 July 1968, 18 months early, and had the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) in operation from 1962. The CAP set common farm prices, so the 1968 date relates to industrial tariffs and common farm prices, not the start of the CAP.
[hist-eur4-s6.5.4] The European Free Trade Association (EFTA) was set up by the Stockholm Convention of 1960. Its members were Britain, Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Austria, Switzerland and Portugal. They removed tariffs on industrial goods, but not on farm produce, and had no common external tariff.
[hist-eur4-s6.6.b] West Germany's recovery was so rapid that it became known as an economic miracle. Reform of money and prices released the market, and a distinctive economic model kept it stable.
[hist-eur4-s6.6.0] The currency reform of 20 June 1948 introduced the Deutsche Mark in the Western zones. It was an Allied measure, not a German one, and it restored confidence in money.
[hist-eur4-s6.6.1] Days later Ludwig Erhard, then director of economic administration, ended most price controls. He was economics minister from 1949 to 1963. Do not confuse him with Adenauer, who was chancellor.
[hist-eur4-s6.6.2] Erhard's social market economy combined free markets with social insurance and trade union rights, so it gave West Germany a stable framework.
[hist-eur4-s6.6.3] Together these reforms created the basis of the economic miracle, the Wirtschaftswunder.
[hist-eur4-s6.6.4] West Germany's economy grew at more than 7 per cent a year on average in the 1950s. Unemployment was about 1.3 per cent in 1960 and under 1 per cent from 1961.
[hist-eur4-s6.7.b] France and Italy also grew rapidly but in different ways: France through state planning and Italy through industry and the movement of labour. Britain, by contrast, grew more slowly.
[hist-eur4-s6.7.0] In France the state planning system began with the Monnet Plan of 1946–47, led by the Commissariat général du Plan. Its priorities were coal, electricity, steel, cement, transport and farm machinery.
[hist-eur4-s6.7.1] The Commissariat set targets for key industries through successive national plans. The state also owned key firms, so Renault was nationalised in 1945.
[hist-eur4-s6.7.2] The French economy grew strongly through the 1950s and 1960s. The economist Jean Fourastié later coined the name trente glorieuses (the thirty glorious years, covering about 1945–75).
[hist-eur4-s6.7.3] Italy's boom ran roughly from 1958 to 1963 and was built on exports of Fiat cars, domestic appliances and textiles. The south (the Mezzogiorno) stayed poor, so many people moved to Turin, Milan and abroad, straining housing and services in the north.
[hist-eur4-s6.7.4] Britain grew more slowly than the continental economies, and balance-of-payments crises produced stop-go policy (swings between expansion and restraint).
[hist-eur4-s6.7.5] Common features were reconstruction, catching up with American methods, cheap oil from the Middle East, skilled and mobile labour, rising exports and rising domestic demand. The adoption of American production methods and the reintegration of trade sustained productivity growth.
[hist-eur4-s6.8.b] Fast growth created more jobs than local workers could fill, so Western Europe turned to workers from abroad. West Germany's guest worker system is the clearest example.
[hist-eur4-s6.8.0] Labour was short for several reasons. The war had killed many working-age men, growth created many new jobs, and rising education and living standards meant fewer locals wanted low-paid, heavy or dirty work.
[hist-eur4-s6.8.1] West Germany recruited guest workers, the Gastarbeiter, under agreements with Italy in 1955, Spain and Greece in 1960, Turkey in 1961 and Yugoslavia in 1968.
[hist-eur4-s6.8.2] Until the Berlin Wall was built in August 1961, workers had also come from East Germany. Once the Wall ended that flow, guest workers became more important to West German industry.
[hist-eur4-s6.8.3] Recruitment was halted in November 1973, by which time there were well over two million foreign workers.
[hist-eur4-s6.8.4] Many guest workers were expected to return home, but many stayed and their families joined them, which made Western Europe multicultural in the later decades.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.b] France and Britain drew many workers from their former empires, who often had citizenship or settlement rights, unlike temporary guest workers. This brought economic benefits and also social tensions.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.0] France drew workers from its former colonies in North Africa (Algeria, Morocco and Tunisia) and from Portugal, and Belgium from Italy and Spain. They worked in construction and car factories.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.1] Algeria became independent in 1962, and several hundred thousand pieds-noirs (settlers of European origin) moved to France.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.2] Britain admitted workers from the Commonwealth. The arrival of the Empire Windrush at Tilbury on 22 June 1948, with over 1,000 passengers, several hundred of them from the Caribbean, marked a major stage in Commonwealth migration.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.3] The British Nationality Act of 1948 gave Commonwealth citizens the right to enter and settle in Britain. The Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 restricted this right.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.4] Commonwealth migrants from the Caribbean, India, Pakistan and Bangladesh worked in the NHS, transport and textile factories. They faced discrimination in housing and employment.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.5] Immigration brought social tensions, and the Race Relations Act of 1965 was Britain's first law against racial discrimination in public places.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.6] The Netherlands received migrants from Indonesia, its former colony, from 1945 onwards, and Italy and Spain exported workers to northern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s6.9.7] Overall, migrants brought economic benefit by filling jobs and supporting growth, but they met discrimination and caused housing pressure. Settled families made the societies multicultural.
[hist-eur4-s6.10.b] The welfare state gave citizens protection against sickness, unemployment and old age.
[hist-eur4-s6.10.0] The Beveridge Report of December 1942, by Sir William Beveridge, identified five giant evils: want, disease, ignorance, squalor and idleness. It proposed a system of national insurance, in which workers pay contributions to get benefits.
[hist-eur4-s6.10.1] Clement Attlee was prime minister from 1945 to 1951. His Labour government passed the National Insurance Act of 1946.
[hist-eur4-s6.10.2] Britain's National Health Service (NHS) was founded on 5 July 1948 by Aneurin Bevan, the minister of health, and provided free treatment at the point of use.
[hist-eur4-s6.10.3] Attlee's government also nationalised the Bank of England (1946), coal (1947) and the railways (1948).
[hist-eur4-s6.10.4] The Education Act of 1944 made secondary education free, and universities grew in the 1960s.
[hist-eur4-s6.11.b] Other Western European states built their own welfare systems, often from older traditions. Welfare was accepted by parties of left and right, so it grew steadily.
[hist-eur4-s6.11.0] France's social security system was created by ordinances of October 1945. It combined health insurance, family allowances and pensions for workers.
[hist-eur4-s6.11.1] West Germany's welfare system was closely tied to the social market economy. Social insurance dates from Bismarck's laws of the 1880s and was revived, and it provided compulsory insurance funded by employers and employees.
[hist-eur4-s6.11.2] The Scandinavian countries developed universal welfare systems, meaning benefits for all citizens. In Sweden, Social Democratic governments from the 1930s to the 1970s combined social security with active labour market policies.
[hist-eur4-s6.11.3] Christian Democratic governments in West Germany, Italy and Belgium also supported social insurance, which showed that the welfare state was favoured by parties of both left and right.
[hist-eur4-s6.11.4] Welfare spending rose steadily across Western Europe in the 1950s and 1960s, and it became a permanent feature of the political consensus.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.b] The boom had many causes, and some things also slowed it. Group the factors by type, such as state action, foreign aid and trade, and labour and energy, then judge which mattered most.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.0] Helped, investment and labour: high investment in industry, the move of workers from farms into factories in Italy, France and West Germany, and the supply of labour from refugees and later guest workers.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.1] Helped, energy and technology: access to cheap oil, which kept factory costs low, and the catching up with American technology and management methods.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.2] Helped, trade and aid: Marshall Aid, freer trade and growing export markets, because exports paid for imports and gave factories steady demand.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.3] Helped, state action: governments accepted responsibility for full employment, using the ideas of John Maynard Keynes to manage demand, and political stability supported long-term investment.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.4] Helped, war: the Korean War boom of 1950–53 raised demand for European exports of steel and machinery.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.5] Hindered, early: war damage, which cut output, and the shortage of dollars in the late 1940s, and the cost of rearmament and Cold War defence spending.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.6] Hindered, Britain: weak productivity and frequent strikes, and balance-of-payments crises that led governments to alternate between expansion and restraint, known as stop-go policies.
[hist-eur4-s6.12.7] Hindered, late 1960s: inflation and pressure on currencies as wages rose, and Vietnam War spending and American deficits, which weakened the dollar.
[hist-eur4-s6.13.b] The boom of 1945–1973 is judged as the most prosperous period in Western Europe's modern history, but its causes and its end are debated. Weigh achievements against limits.
[hist-eur4-s6.13.0] Achievements: unprecedented growth, rising living standards, full employment in many countries, welfare provision, the spread of home ownership and a large expansion of education.
[hist-eur4-s6.13.1] Limits: dependence on cheap oil and on the dollar-based system, inflation in the late 1960s, uneven growth (Britain, southern Italy) and the difficult position of migrants.
[hist-eur4-s6.13.2] The Bretton Woods system broke down after US president Richard Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold on 15 August 1971, and by early 1973 currencies were floating (their values set by the market).
[hist-eur4-s6.13.3] Western Europe's growth rate between 1950 and 1973 was far higher than in any earlier period, but it slowed sharply after 1973.
[hist-eur4-s6.13.4] Historians disagree on how far Marshall Aid caused recovery. Some stress American aid and pressure for cooperation, while Milward and others stress domestic policy and economic structure.
[hist-eur4-s6.13.5] Contemporaries saw the boom as the result of good management. Later historians stress that it was partly a catching-up with the American economy that could not continue indefinitely.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s5: 5. Moves towards European unity and the EEC, 1945–1992
[hist-eur4-s5.0.b] In 1945 Western Europe was in ruins and its old rivalries had just caused a second world war. Leaders turned to unity for three reasons: to stop another war, to stand together against the Soviet Union and to rebuild their economies.
[hist-eur4-s5.0.0] The Second World War left Western Europe devastated and dependent on the USA. France and Germany had fought three wars in seventy years (1870-71, 1914 and 1939), so ending their rivalry was the first aim of unity.
[hist-eur4-s5.0.1] The Cold War split Europe by the Iron Curtain into a Soviet bloc in the East and a Western side, which formed NATO (the US-led military alliance) in April 1949. Integration here was Western European only, and its supporters saw it as strength against the Soviet threat.
[hist-eur4-s5.0.2] Marshall Aid (1948-52) and the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC, set up in 1948 to share it out) made the USA a sponsor of European cooperation.
[hist-eur4-s5.0.3] Economic recovery was a third motive: a larger market allows more efficient production, and the Benelux customs union (Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg, in force 1 January 1948) was an example that cooperation worked.
[hist-eur4-s5.0.4] The Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) was founded in 1949 under its first chancellor, Konrad Adenauer (in office 1949-63). How to rebuild and rearm it safely, the 'German question', lay behind both the Schuman Plan and the European Defence Community.
[hist-eur4-s5.0.5] Ideas mattered too. Altiero Spinelli, an Italian federalist, wrote the Ventotene Manifesto in 1941 while interned by Mussolini's regime.
[hist-eur4-s5.0.6] Christian Democrat leaders such as Robert Schuman (France), Adenauer and Alcide De Gasperi (Italy) shared a wish to bind their countries together, and Europe's decline as a world power made unity look necessary.
[hist-eur4-s5.1.b] The history of European unity is a debate between two ways of organising it, and a handful of terms describe the choices. Knowing them lets you say exactly what each treaty did and what each leader wanted.
[hist-eur4-s5.1.0] A federal Europe means a united Europe with a central, supranational government above the national governments. Federalists such as Spinelli, Spaak, Monnet, Hallstein and Delors wanted this.
[hist-eur4-s5.1.1] Supranational means that the institutions' decisions bind member states, even against their wishes. Examples are the ECSC High Authority, the Commission and the Court of Justice.
[hist-eur4-s5.1.2] Intergovernmental means cooperation decided by national governments, each keeping a veto (the power to block a decision). The Council of Europe, EFTA, de Gaulle's Europe of states and Thatcher's view all follow this model.
[hist-eur4-s5.1.3] A customs union has no tariffs (taxes on imports) between members and one common tariff on goods from outside. It is the first stage of a common market, which adds free movement of goods, services, capital and people. In Britain and Ireland the EEC was commonly called 'the Common Market'.
[hist-eur4-s5.1.4] The single market is the completion of the common market by removing physical (border), technical (standards) and fiscal (tax) barriers, the 1992 programme. Do not confuse it with the customs union, completed in 1968.
[hist-eur4-s5.1.5] Qualified majority voting means decisions in the Council of Ministers by a weighted majority rather than unanimity, so one state cannot block everything. The Single European Act extended it.
[hist-eur4-s5.1.6] The Six were France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg, the members of the ECSC and the founders of the EEC.
[hist-eur4-s5.2.b] Between 1946 and 1950 Europeans tried loose cooperation first. It produced a forum for debate, not a government, and its limits pushed the federalists to look for another route.
[hist-eur4-s5.2.0] Winston Churchill, then leader of the opposition, called at the University of Zurich on 19 September 1946 for 'a kind of United States of Europe' built on a partnership between France and Germany. He saw Britain and the Commonwealth as friends and sponsors outside it, not members.
[hist-eur4-s5.2.1] The Congress of Europe at The Hague (May 1948) brought together several hundred delegates, with Churchill as chairman. It called for a European assembly and a charter of human rights, and it led to the creation of the Council of Europe the following year.
[hist-eur4-s5.2.2] It split between federalists, who wanted real authority, and unionists, who wanted cooperation between governments, so it showed the argument that ran through the whole period.
[hist-eur4-s5.2.3] The Council of Europe was founded by the Treaty of London on 5 May 1949 by ten states: Belgium, Denmark, France, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Sweden and Britain. Its seat is Strasbourg.
[hist-eur4-s5.2.4] Its Consultative Assembly could only debate and recommend, so it was a forum rather than a source of binding law. This disappointed federalists, and Paul-Henri Spaak, the Assembly's first president (1949), resigned in 1951 in frustration at its lack of power.
[hist-eur4-s5.2.5] Its main achievement was the European Convention on Human Rights, signed in Rome on 4 November 1950 and in force in 1953. The European Court of Human Rights was set up in Strasbourg in 1959.
[hist-eur4-s5.2.6] The Council of Europe is not an EEC institution, and its Court of Human Rights is not the EEC's Court of Justice (which sits in Luxembourg). Do not confuse either with the Council of Ministers.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.b] Monnet was a French planner and persuader who designed the first European communities and is widely called a 'father of Europe'. He never held elected office, so he worked through politicians.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.0] He was born in Cognac, France, in 1888, worked in the family cognac business and then in international finance, and was deputy secretary-general of the League of Nations from 1919 to 1923.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.1] In the Second World War he coordinated Anglo-French war supplies (1939-40) and helped draft the offer of a Franco-British union in June 1940, which the French government did not accept before France's surrender. He later worked on war supplies in Washington.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.2] From 1946 he headed the French planning commission. The Monnet Plan of that year modernised coal, steel, electricity, transport and other basic industries through state investment, partly financed by Marshall Aid.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.3] He drafted the Schuman Declaration, announced by the foreign minister Robert Schuman on 9 May 1950. His method was to unite Europe sector by sector, 'through concrete achievements', so that each small step created a reason for the next.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.4] He was the first president of the ECSC High Authority (1952-55), based in Luxembourg. He then left it to campaign for further integration.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.5] In 1955 he founded the Action Committee for the United States of Europe, which brought together political parties and trade unions from the Six. It pressed for Euratom, the EEC and later British membership, so he kept shaping events without office.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.6] Contemporaries were divided. Federalists and American leaders admired him, while de Gaulle and the Gaullists opposed him because they rejected supranational institutions. He died in 1979.
[hist-eur4-s5.3.7] Historians call him the architect of the 'Monnet method' of gradual, functional integration, but debate how much credit he deserves compared with the national governments that took the decisions.
[hist-eur4-s5.4.b] The first supranational community pooled the coal and steel of France and West Germany.
[hist-eur4-s5.4.0] Coal and steel were the basis of war industries, and they were a sore point: the Ruhr (in West Germany) had been under an International Authority for the Ruhr since 1949, and the Saar was under French economic control after the war (it returned to West Germany in 1957).
[hist-eur4-s5.4.1] The Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950 proposed a joint authority for French and West German coal and steel, open to other states. It said pooling them would make war between France and Germany 'not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible'. 9 May is now celebrated as Europe Day.
[hist-eur4-s5.4.2] Reactions differed. Adenauer accepted at once, because the plan treated West Germany as an equal and offered an end to international control of the Ruhr (that authority was wound up only when the ECSC began in 1952). Italy and the Benelux countries joined.
[hist-eur4-s5.4.3] Britain's Labour government under Clement Attlee refused to join, because it would not hand control of its nationalised coal and steel industries to a supranational body.
[hist-eur4-s5.4.4] The Treaty of Paris was signed on 18 April 1951 by the Six (the signatories) and came into force on 23 July 1952, to run for fifty years.
[hist-eur4-s5.4.5] It had four institutions: the High Authority (a supranational executive), the Common Assembly, the Special Council of Ministers and the Court of Justice.
[hist-eur4-s5.4.6] The High Authority could take decisions binding on firms and governments, raise a levy on coal and steel production and fine firms that broke the rules.
[hist-eur4-s5.4.7] Results: a common market in coal and steel, an institutional model that the EEC later copied, and Franco-German reconciliation in practice, since the two countries now ran their war industries together.
[hist-eur4-s5.5.b] After success in coal and steel, the French tried to extend the same supranational method to defence. The attempt failed, and integration turned back to the economy.
[hist-eur4-s5.5.0] The Korean War (from June 1950) led the USA to press for West German rearmament. In October 1950 the French prime minister René Pleven proposed a European Defence Community (EDC), a European army, so that West Germany could rearm without having a national army.
[hist-eur4-s5.5.1] The EDC Treaty was signed on 27 May 1952 by the Six. A plan for a European Political Community (1953) depended on it and fell with it.
[hist-eur4-s5.5.2] The French National Assembly rejected the EDC on 30 August 1954, by a procedural vote. It was the Assembly, not the government, that killed a plan the French government itself had proposed.
[hist-eur4-s5.5.3] The reasons were fear of German rearmament, opposition from Gaullists and communists, and reluctance to lose sovereignty over the army. Britain had refused to join, and the death of Stalin in March 1953 and the Korean armistice of July 1953 had eased the sense of threat.
[hist-eur4-s5.5.4] West Germany was rearmed another way. The Paris Agreements of October 1954 extended the 1948 Brussels Treaty to West Germany and Italy, creating the Western European Union (from 1955), and West Germany joined NATO in May 1955.
[hist-eur4-s5.5.5] The result was that integration turned back to economics, and defence stayed national and Atlantic. This is why the Treaties of Rome were about trade, not armies.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.b] Having failed in defence, the Six returned to the economy in 1955. The result, the Treaties of Rome, created the EEC, which became the centre of European integration.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.0] The Messina conference of the six foreign ministers (1-3 June 1955) relaunched the project. It was prepared by the Benelux governments and chaired by Italy; Monnet was not its organiser but worked through his Action Committee.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.1] Spaak chaired the committee whose report (April 1956) proposed a common market and an atomic energy community. It was approved at Venice in May 1956.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.2] The Treaties of Rome were signed on 25 March 1957 by the Six and came into force on 1 January 1958. They created the European Economic Community (EEC) and the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom).
[hist-eur4-s5.6.3] The EEC was to build a customs union in stages over twelve years, with a common external tariff, and to bring free movement of goods, services, capital and workers (the 'four freedoms'). It also planned common policies in agriculture and transport.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.4] The preamble speaks of 'an ever closer union among the peoples of Europe', which shows that the long-term aim was political as well as economic.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.5] Euratom was for civil nuclear cooperation among the Six. It was less important than the EEC.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.6] Four institutions ran the EEC: the Commission (based in Brussels, which proposed legislation), the Council of Ministers, the Assembly and the Court of Justice. The Assembly renamed itself the European Parliament in 1962, and its members were appointed from national parliaments until direct elections in 1979.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.7] Walter Hallstein of West Germany, a federalist, was the first Commission president (1958-67). The Merger Treaty (signed 1965, in force 1 July 1967) merged the executives of the ECSC, EEC and Euratom into one Commission and one Council.
[hist-eur4-s5.6.8] The EEC was not the European Union or a single currency. Those date from Maastricht (1992, in force 1993) and the euro from 1999.
[hist-eur4-s5.7.b] In its first decade the EEC proved it could deliver. It built its customs union early and launched a common farm policy, while a rival group tried a looser path.
[hist-eur4-s5.7.0] The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) was agreed in January 1962 and operated from 1962, designed by the Dutch commissioner Sicco Mansholt. It gave farmers common guaranteed prices, protected them with a levy on cheaper imports and paid export subsidies, all financed from a common fund.
[hist-eur4-s5.7.1] Benefits were secure incomes for farmers and food security. Costs were that the CAP became by far the largest item in the Community budget, and guaranteed prices produced surpluses nicknamed 'butter mountains' and 'wine lakes'. Milk quotas were introduced in 1984 to limit them.
[hist-eur4-s5.7.2] The customs union was completed on 1 July 1968, eighteen months ahead of schedule, with the common external tariff in place the same day. Trade among the Six grew rapidly.
[hist-eur4-s5.7.3] The European Free Trade Association (EFTA) was set up by the Stockholm Convention in January 1960 (in force May 1960) by Britain, Austria, Denmark, Norway, Portugal, Sweden and Switzerland. It was a free-trade area in industrial goods only, with no common external tariff and no supranational institutions.
[hist-eur4-s5.7.4] EFTA was Britain's alternative to the EEC for states that rejected supranationalism. Its weakness was that it lacked the EEC's large common market and common policies. Britain, Ireland and Denmark applied to join the EEC in 1961.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.b] Charles de Gaulle, president of France from 1959 to 1969, wanted a Europe of cooperating states, not a supranational one. He used vetoes and a boycott to block British entry and to defend national sovereignty.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.0] De Gaulle favoured a Europe of states or nations, cooperation between sovereign governments, which contrasted with Monnet's supranational model.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.1] The Élysée Treaty of Franco-German friendship, signed on 22 January 1963 by de Gaulle and Adenauer, showed that he wanted a French-led partnership with West Germany at the heart of Europe.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.2] Britain applied to join in 1961 under Harold Macmillan (the negotiator was Edward Heath), as did Ireland under Seán Lemass and Denmark. De Gaulle announced his veto at a press conference on 14 January 1963.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.3] His reasons were Britain's ties to the USA (the Nassau agreement of December 1962 on American Polaris missiles for Britain) and to the Commonwealth, and its different farming and trading system. He feared Britain would weaken the Community and French leadership of it.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.4] He vetoed Britain, not Ireland. Ireland's application lapsed because its trade depended on the British market, so it would not join without Britain.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.5] Britain reapplied in 1967 under Harold Wilson, with Ireland and Denmark, and de Gaulle used his second veto on 27 November 1967.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.6] The empty chair crisis began when France stopped attending Council of Ministers meetings from 30 June / 1 July 1965. The causes were the financing of the CAP, plans to give the Commission and Assembly control of Community money, and the coming move to majority voting.
[hist-eur4-s5.8.7] The Luxembourg Compromise of January 1966 was an informal political agreement, not a treaty change. Where a member said very important national interests were at stake, the Council would try for unanimity. In practice a state could block such decisions, so unanimity became the norm for about twenty years.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.b] After de Gaulle resigned in April 1969, his successor Georges Pompidou allowed the Community to grow. The 1970s brought new members and new institutions, but also slow progress.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.0] The Hague summit of 1-2 December 1969 agreed on 'completion, deepening, enlargement': finishing the common policies, moving towards monetary union and admitting new members. Do not confuse it with the Hague Congress of 1948.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.1] Britain (prime minister Edward Heath), Ireland (Taoiseach Jack Lynch) and Denmark joined on 1 January 1973. Norway's voters rejected membership in September 1972.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.2] Irish voters approved membership in a referendum on 10 May 1972 by about 83 per cent. Britain confirmed its membership in a referendum on 5 June 1975 under Harold Wilson, when about two-thirds voted to stay in. Britain joined in 1973, not 1975.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.3] The European Regional Development Fund was set up in 1975 to help poorer regions. Ireland later became a large net beneficiary of the CAP and the structural funds.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.4] The European Monetary System (EMS) began on 13 March 1979, with the Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM), which kept currencies within bands, and the ECU, a unit of account. Roy Jenkins, Commission president 1977-81, promoted it, backed by Helmut Schmidt and Valery Giscard d'Estaing.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.5] Ireland joined the ERM, and the Irish pound's one-to-one link with sterling ended in March 1979. Britain stayed out until October 1990 and left in September 1992.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.6] The first direct elections to the European Parliament were held in June 1979, giving it a democratic mandate for the first time.
[hist-eur4-s5.9.7] Eurosclerosis was the name for the slow progress of the Community in the 1970s and early 1980s, during recession.
[hist-eur4-s5.10.b] In the 1980s the Community widened to twelve members and then deepened, setting a deadline for a true single market. Britain's budget quarrel had to be settled first.
[hist-eur4-s5.10.0] Greece joined on 1 January 1981, after military rule ended in 1974. Spain and Portugal joined on 1 January 1986, after the fall of dictatorship (Franco died in 1975; Portugal's dictatorship fell in 1974). Membership became twelve, and the Community supported these new democracies.
[hist-eur4-s5.10.1] At the Fontainebleau summit in June 1984 a permanent rebate for Britain on its budget contribution was agreed, which settled a dispute that had dogged the Community.
[hist-eur4-s5.10.2] The Single European Act was signed in February 1986 and came into force on 1 July 1987. Its entry into force was delayed by the Irish Supreme Court's Crotty judgment, which required a referendum, and the Irish referendum of May 1987 approved it.
[hist-eur4-s5.10.3] The Act set 31 December 1992 for the single market, extended qualified majority voting, gave the Parliament more say and put European Political Cooperation (foreign policy) on a treaty basis. Structural funds doubled afterwards.
[hist-eur4-s5.10.4] Germany reunified on 3 October 1990, and the former East Germany became part of the Community through the united Germany.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.b] Delors was the French socialist who led the European Commission for ten years and relaunched integration through the single market and the plan for monetary union.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.0] He was born in Paris in 1925 and worked at the Banque de France and in French planning. A socialist influenced by Catholic social thought, he joined the Socialist Party in 1974.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.1] He advised prime minister Jacques Chaban-Delmas (1969-72), was an MEP (1979-81) and was French minister of the economy and finance under President Mitterrand (1981-84), so he came to Brussels with government experience.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.2] He was president of the European Commission from January 1985 to January 1995, three terms, about ten years.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.3] The single market was his first project: the 1985 White Paper listed about 300 measures to remove physical, technical and fiscal barriers by 31 December 1992, and the Single European Act of 1986 gave it legal force.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.4] He added a social dimension, because he thought a single market needed protection for workers. In September 1988 he told the British Trades Union Congress to expect one, and the Social Charter was adopted in December 1989 by eleven of the twelve members, Britain refusing.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.5] In July 1988 he told the European Parliament that within ten years about 80 per cent of economic legislation would come from the Community.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.6] The Delors Report of April 1989 set out economic and monetary union in three stages. He took part in the Maastricht negotiations of 1991 and the Treaty of 1992, which built on his plan.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.7] Contemporaries credited him with relaunching the Community after Eurosclerosis. Thatcher saw him as the leader of a drive towards a European super-state, and the Sun newspaper's headline 'Up Yours Delors' (1990) showed British tabloid hostility.
[hist-eur4-s5.11.8] Historians rate him one of the most influential Commission presidents, but debate whether his federal ambitions outran what member states and voters wanted, as the Danish 'no' of 1992 suggested.
[hist-eur4-s5.12.b] Maastricht turned the Community into a union with the aim of a single currency. It also exposed how far voters and governments were willing to go.
[hist-eur4-s5.12.0] The Treaty on European Union was agreed at Maastricht in December 1991, signed on 7 February 1992 and in force on 1 November 1993. It created the European Union, so the term dates from here, not from Rome.
[hist-eur4-s5.12.1] The Union had three pillars: the European Communities, a Common Foreign and Security Policy, and cooperation in justice and home affairs. The principle of subsidiarity (decisions taken at the lowest suitable level) was added.
[hist-eur4-s5.12.2] Economic and monetary union would come in three stages, with a single currency by 1 January 1999 at the latest, and the treaty introduced European citizenship.
[hist-eur4-s5.12.3] Britain opted out of the single currency and the Social Chapter (the Maastricht protocol on social policy, not to be confused with the 1989 Social Charter declaration). Denmark later gained opt-outs.
[hist-eur4-s5.12.4] Danish voters rejected the treaty on 2 June 1992 (approving it in a second vote in 1993), while Irish voters approved it in June 1992.
[hist-eur4-s5.13.b] Throughout the period people disagreed about what unity should be. The split was between those who wanted a federal Europe and those who wanted cooperation between states.
[hist-eur4-s5.13.0] Federalists (Spinelli, Spaak, Monnet, Hallstein and Delors) wanted supranational institutions. Intergovernmentalists (de Gaulle, Thatcher and British governments generally) wanted states to keep control. This split explains most disputes in the period.
[hist-eur4-s5.13.1] Thatcher, British prime minister 1979-90, supported the single market and signed the Single European Act, but opposed federalism, the social dimension and a single currency. She demanded a refund of what she called Britain's own money.
[hist-eur4-s5.13.2] In her Bruges speech of 20 September 1988 she said Europe should be a family of sovereign nations and warned against 'a European super-state' exercising power from Brussels.
[hist-eur4-s5.13.3] Britain was called an 'awkward partner', the title of a book by the historian Stephen George (1990), because of its late entry, the referendum, the budget dispute and the opt-outs.
[hist-eur4-s5.14.b] Unity changed Western Europe's economy, politics and society, but not equally for everyone. Weigh the benefits against the costs.
[hist-eur4-s5.14.0] Economic: trade between members grew rapidly, markets and investment grew, farmers got guaranteed prices under the CAP, and the regional and structural funds helped poorer regions such as Ireland. The costs were CAP surpluses and food prices.
[hist-eur4-s5.14.1] Political: there was Franco-German reconciliation and no war between members of the Community in the period. Spain, Portugal and Greece joined as new democracies, and Europe gained a stronger voice in world trade.
[hist-eur4-s5.14.2] Social: free movement of workers and common rules on working conditions and equal pay (women's rights belong elsewhere) gave new opportunities to countries such as Ireland.
[hist-eur4-s5.14.3] Limits: unity was Western Europe only, there was no common defence, national vetoes remained, the CAP was costly, and there was a 'democratic deficit' (the Parliament had limited power over decisions). This is why unity did not reach every area.
[hist-eur4-s5.15.b] A good answer opens with a judgement. Economic unity went far, but political and defence unity did not, and Europe was supranational in coal, steel and trade but intergovernmental in most else.
[hist-eur4-s5.15.0] Achievements: peace between France and Germany, a customs union and single market, growth from six to twelve members and democracy anchored in the south.
[hist-eur4-s5.15.1] Limits: no common defence (the EDC failed), de Gaulle's vetoes and the empty chair, the CAP's costs, Britain's reluctance, and unity confined to Western Europe.
[hist-eur4-s5.15.2] Contemporary view: federalists hoped that Maastricht was the start of a political union, while critics such as Thatcher feared a super-state.
[hist-eur4-s5.15.3] Later view: historians stress that integration came from compromises between national governments, not from the triumph of the Monnet method alone.
[hist-eur4-s5.15.4] On Monnet and Delors, contemporaries saw them as builders of peace or as threats to sovereignty, while historians ask how far they or the governments deserve the credit and whether Delors's ambitions outran voters.
