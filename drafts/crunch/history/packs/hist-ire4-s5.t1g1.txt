TARGET hist-ire4-s5: 5. Case study: de Valera in America, June 1919–December 1920
[hist-ire4-s5.0.b] Éamon de Valera went to America in June 1919 as head of the Dáil government (the Irish republican parliament), after escaping from prison in England, to win recognition for the Irish Republic. To see why he was chosen and why he needed to go, start with the events that made him a leader.
[hist-ire4-s5.0.0] De Valera was born in New York in 1882 to an Irish mother and a Spanish father and was raised in Co. Limerick, so he had a link to America that few Irish leaders had.
[hist-ire4-s5.0.1] In the Easter Rising of 1916 he commanded the Volunteer garrison at Boland's Mill in Dublin. He was sentenced to death, but the sentence was commuted to penal servitude (imprisonment with hard labour), so he survived to lead the movement afterwards.
[hist-ire4-s5.0.2] Sinn Féin, the republican party that grew in strength after the Rising, won 73 of the 105 Irish seats in the general election of December 1918. Its MPs refused to sit at Westminster and instead met as Dáil Éireann.
[hist-ire4-s5.0.3] The first Dáil met on 21 January 1919 and declared an Irish Republic. A republic that no other state recognised had little standing abroad, which is why recognition became the goal of the mission.
[hist-ire4-s5.0.4] De Valera was arrested in May 1918 in the round-up that followed the so-called German Plot (the British claim that Sinn Féin was plotting with Germany), and he was held in Lincoln Gaol in England.
[hist-ire4-s5.0.5] He escaped from Lincoln Gaol on 3 February 1919, in an escape arranged by Michael Collins and Harry Boland. Collins and Boland were close allies who each played a major part in the months that followed.
[hist-ire4-s5.0.6] In April 1919 the Dáil elected him head of its ministry, as Príomh Aire, usually translated as President. This gave him the authority to speak for the Republic abroad.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.b] The mission had four aims, and each was shaped by what had already gone wrong at the Paris Peace Conference. Irish republicans had hoped the world's leaders would hear Ireland's case there, but they found the door closed.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.0] Self-determination (the principle that each people should choose its own government) was promoted by US President Woodrow Wilson at the Paris Peace Conference. Irish republicans appealed to it because it seemed to support an independent Irish state.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.1] The Dáil sent envoys to Paris, including Seán T. O'Kelly, but Wilson did not press Britain on Irish self-determination and the Irish delegates were not heard. This pushed the Dáil to turn to the American public instead.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.2] An Irish Race Convention in Philadelphia in February 1919 sent three Irish-American delegates, Frank P. Walsh, Edward F. Dunne and Michael J. Ryan, to Paris to ask Wilson to hear Ireland's case. They later visited Ireland.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.3] Irish America already had organisations to build on. The Friends of Irish Freedom, founded in 1916 and led by Judge Daniel Cohalan of New York and John Devoy, had supported Irish independence since then, so the ground was prepared.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.4] Aim 1: to win US recognition of the Irish Republic, meaning that the American government would formally accept it as an independent state.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.5] Aim 2: to raise funds for the Dáil government, which had no income of its own and was soon fighting a war.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.6] Aim 3: to influence the 1920 US presidential election, so that the next president would back the Republic.
[hist-ire4-s5.1.7] Aim 4: to put pressure on Britain, because American sympathy for Ireland would embarrass its wartime ally.
[hist-ire4-s5.2.b] De Valera had to leave Britain in secret because he was an escaped prisoner. His arrival in America was prepared in advance and launched his public campaign.
[hist-ire4-s5.2.0] He left Britain secretly on a ship from Liverpool as a stowaway, so that British officials would not catch him. He reached the USA in June 1919.
[hist-ire4-s5.2.1] Harry Boland had already gone ahead to prepare the way, arranging contacts and meetings with Irish-American organisations so that de Valera could start work at once.
[hist-ire4-s5.2.2] On 6 June 1919 the US Senate passed a resolution asking the Peace Conference to hear Irish representatives. It showed sympathy but did not commit the American government to anything.
[hist-ire4-s5.2.3] His first public appearance in New York came in late June 1919, and he stayed at the Waldorf-Astoria hotel. This launched the tour of the cities with the largest Irish communities.
[hist-ire4-s5.3.b] De Valera toured the cities where Irish Americans were most numerous, and they greeted him as the head of a nation. The crowds were large, but the American government and press were more guarded.
[hist-ire4-s5.3.0] On 29 June 1919 he addressed a huge rally at Fenway Park in Boston. His meetings in other cities such as Chicago and New York also drew large Irish-American crowds, who gave him receptions, parades and donations.
[hist-ire4-s5.3.1] The tour continued through 1919 and into 1920, and he also visited Irish communities in western states. Irish-American organisations and parishes arranged his meetings, and local branches raised money and sent subscriptions to Dublin.
[hist-ire4-s5.3.2] He was given honours as well as welcomes. He was given the title of chief of the Chippewa nation and presents of beaded work, an example of how warmly he was received.
[hist-ire4-s5.3.3] His message was always the same: recognition of the Republic, support for the Dáil, and funds for the struggle in Ireland.
[hist-ire4-s5.3.4] The reception was enthusiastic among Irish Americans. The American government and most of the press treated the mission with caution.
[hist-ire4-s5.3.5] British officials objected to the tour, which they regarded as propaganda against British rule in Ireland.
[hist-ire4-s5.4.b] Money was the aim the mission came closest to achieving. The Dáil government needed funds to run a state and support a war.
[hist-ire4-s5.4.0] The Dáil authorised a loan, and bond certificates were sold to Irish Americans in a campaign launched in the USA in January 1920. Supporters could thus invest in the struggle for independence.
[hist-ire4-s5.4.1] The target was five million dollars, and about that sum had been raised by 1921, though sources differ on the exact total. Say 'about', never 'exactly'.
[hist-ire4-s5.4.2] The drive used the networks of Irish-American organisations, parishes and clubs to reach donors in cities across the United States.
[hist-ire4-s5.4.3] Disputes arose over who controlled the money, and Irish-American leaders disagreed with de Valera about how the funds should be spent.
[hist-ire4-s5.4.4] The bond drive in the USA must not be confused with the National Loan raised in Ireland by Michael Collins as Minister for Finance. Together they gave the Dáil government a financial base at home and in the diaspora (Irish people living abroad).
[hist-ire4-s5.4.5] Francis M. Carroll's study of the Dáil loans and their finances, Money for Ireland (2002), stresses the importance of the money for the Republic's finances.
[hist-ire4-s5.5.b] De Valera's mission divided Irish America, and his clash with Devoy and Judge Cohalan weakened the unity of the movement. It was a quarrel over who was in charge.
[hist-ire4-s5.5.0] John Devoy and Judge Daniel Cohalan led the Friends of Irish Freedom, which had supported Irish independence since 1916. Devoy edited the Gaelic American, a newspaper founded in 1903.
[hist-ire4-s5.5.1] They quarrelled with de Valera over the control of funds and of the American movement, and the quarrel was carried out in the press.
[hist-ire4-s5.5.2] Devoy and Cohalan saw de Valera as trying to take control of an organisation they had built. De Valera wanted an organisation that answered to the Dáil, so the dispute was about Dáil authority against American independence.
[hist-ire4-s5.5.3] Harry Boland, who had prepared the American side of the mission, backed de Valera, and the two camps traded accusations in the Gaelic American and other papers.
[hist-ire4-s5.5.4] The split reduced the unity of Irish America. De Valera turned to a new organisation of his own, a rival to the Friends of Irish Freedom.
[hist-ire4-s5.5.5] The quarrel showed the difficulty of running a movement from America when the leaders in Ireland had their own priorities.
[hist-ire4-s5.6.b] De Valera's own public statements caused controversy among his supporters. In February 1920 he suggested a compromise that many republicans found hard to accept.
[hist-ire4-s5.6.0] In February 1920 an interview with de Valera appeared in the Westminster Gazette, a British newspaper. In it he suggested that Britain's security fears could be met by treating Ireland as the United States treated Cuba.
[hist-ire4-s5.6.1] Cuba was a state that accepted limits on its foreign policy, in effect American influence, while remaining independent. The Cuba analogy therefore offered Britain a guarantee against Ireland becoming a base for its enemies.
[hist-ire4-s5.6.2] The comparison caused controversy among republicans at home and abroad, who felt it offered a compromise on full independence.
[hist-ire4-s5.6.3] Cohalan and Devoy used the interview to attack him.
[hist-ire4-s5.6.4] De Valera himself argued that he was only showing American opinion that the Irish demand was reasonable.
[hist-ire4-s5.7.b] In 1920 de Valera tried to win a commitment to recognition from both major American parties, because the next president would decide whether the USA recognised the Republic. Neither party gave one.
[hist-ire4-s5.7.0] The Republican National Convention met in Chicago in June 1920, and the Democratic National Convention met in San Francisco in June and July 1920. Do not mix up the two cities.
[hist-ire4-s5.7.1] De Valera approached delegates and party leaders at both conventions, seeking a plank (a policy commitment in the party platform) recognising the Irish Republic.
[hist-ire4-s5.7.2] The Republican platform ignored the request, and the Democrats adopted only a resolution of sympathy with Irish aspirations for self-government. Sympathy was not recognition.
[hist-ire4-s5.7.3] The Republican candidate Warren G. Harding won the presidential election in November 1920, and his administration did not recognise the Republic.
[hist-ire4-s5.7.4] Irish-American anger at the Wilson administration's refusal to back Irish self-determination hurt the Democrats in November 1920. Neither Wilson nor Harding recognised the Republic, and neither party backed it.
[hist-ire4-s5.8.b] After the failure at the conventions the campaign was reorganised. At the same time, events in Ireland increased sympathy in America.
[hist-ire4-s5.8.0] The American Association for the Recognition of the Irish Republic (AARIR) was founded in November 1920 as de Valera's own campaigning body. The Friends of Irish Freedom continued, so the AARIR became a rival to them for de Valera's supporters.
[hist-ire4-s5.8.1] The new association grew rapidly and claimed several hundred thousand members, and it organised rallies and lobbied members of Congress. Do not give an exact membership figure, because sources vary.
[hist-ire4-s5.8.2] Terence MacSwiney, Lord Mayor of Cork, died on hunger strike on 25 October 1920. His death received wide American press coverage and helped the Irish cause.
[hist-ire4-s5.8.3] An American Commission on Conditions in Ireland was set up in late 1920 to investigate British reprisals in Ireland and increased American sympathy. It was a separate body and was not run by the AARIR.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.b] While de Valera was in America, the Anglo-Irish War (the guerrilla war between the IRA and British forces) intensified. Events at home affected how Americans saw the mission and how the Dáil was run.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.0] The Soloheadbeg ambush on 21 January 1919 marked the start of the Anglo-Irish War, which lasted until 1921.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.1] The British government banned the Dáil on 12 September 1919, and its ministers went underground.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.2] Michael Collins, as Minister for Finance and director of IRA intelligence, ran much of the war in Ireland while de Valera was in America.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.3] From March 1920 the British government reinforced the Royal Irish Constabulary (the police) with the Black and Tans and, from July 1920, the Auxiliaries. Reprisals against civilians were widely reported in American newspapers, which helped the Irish cause.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.4] Kevin Barry, an 18-year-old IRA Volunteer, was hanged in Dublin on 1 November 1920.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.5] On 21 November 1920, known as Bloody Sunday, IRA gunmen killed British officers and agents in Dublin, and British forces then fired on a crowd at Croke Park, killing 14. This is not the Bloody Sunday of 1972.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.6] Auxiliaries burned part of Cork city in December 1920.
[hist-ire4-s5.9.7] The Government of Ireland Act, passed in December 1920, created separate parliaments for Northern Ireland and Southern Ireland. De Valera returned to Ireland in December 1920, and Harry Boland stayed on in America.
[hist-ire4-s5.10.b] The mission secured publicity and money for the Republic, but not the recognition its leaders wanted. Its results were mixed, and they cost the movement something too.
[hist-ire4-s5.10.0] No American government recognised the Irish Republic during the mission or in the years that followed. This was the main aim, so its failure is the central fact in any judgement.
[hist-ire4-s5.10.1] The mission raised money for the Dáil government, which helped to pay for the war and the Dáil's administration.
[hist-ire4-s5.10.2] The mission raised the profile of the Irish Republic in American newspapers and at public meetings, so Americans heard the Irish case directly.
[hist-ire4-s5.10.3] The split among Irish-American organisations weakened the Friends of Irish Freedom and led to a new body, the AARIR.
[hist-ire4-s5.10.4] De Valera was away for about eighteen months, from June 1919 to December 1920. His absence left the Dáil government without its president at a time of war, and relations between him and Collins were strained.
[hist-ire4-s5.10.5] The mission was one of the first major efforts by the Irish Republic to win international recognition, and it shows both the possibilities and the limits of diaspora politics.
[hist-ire4-s5.10.6] Harry Boland remained in the United States after de Valera left, and the AARIR carried on the campaign into 1921.
[hist-ire4-s5.11.b] Whether the mission succeeded depends on which aim you judge. It won money and publicity but lost the recognition it was meant to win.
[hist-ire4-s5.11.0] Achievement: the mission brought the Irish Republic's case to large American audiences and raised about five million dollars for the Dáil government, which paid for the war and the Dáil's administration.
[hist-ire4-s5.11.1] Limit: neither the Wilson administration nor the Harding administration recognised the Republic, and neither party backed it. The Irish-American movement was divided by the mission.
[hist-ire4-s5.11.2] Aims achieved: funds and publicity. Aims not achieved: recognition and influence on the 1920 election.
[hist-ire4-s5.11.3] Other costs: de Valera's long absence from Ireland, about eighteen months, and strained relations with Collins.
[hist-ire4-s5.11.4] Contemporary view: supporters called the mission a triumph, while Devoy, Cohalan and other Irish-American critics resented his attempt to control the movement, and the British government and its allies called it dangerous and divisive.
[hist-ire4-s5.11.5] Contemporary view in Ireland: some nationalists argued that the time spent in America would have been better used at home.
[hist-ire4-s5.11.6] Later view: historians stress that the mission was a propaganda and fundraising success but a diplomatic failure, because the recognition it sought was never granted. Carroll's study stresses the importance of the money.

CANDIDATES hist-ire4-s4: 4. Irish nationalism and Ulster Unionism as international forces
[hist-ire4-s4.0.b] Irish nationalism and Ulster Unionism both looked abroad for help because millions of Irish people, and their descendants, lived outside Ireland. The background below is what the later events rest on.
[hist-ire4-s4.0.0] The Famine (1845 to 1852) and the mass emigration that followed created large Irish communities in the United States, Britain, Canada and Australia, and many emigrants carried bitter memories of British rule, which made them willing to fund nationalist causes.
[hist-ire4-s4.0.1] Home Rule (a self-governing parliament for Ireland within the United Kingdom) was pursued by the Irish Parliamentary Party. The Home Rule crisis of 1912 to 1914 pitted Ulster Unionists, who armed themselves in the Ulster Volunteer Force, against it.
[hist-ire4-s4.0.2] The Rising of 1916, the War of Independence and the Treaty of 1921 form the Irish setting for American and other overseas support for republicans.
[hist-ire4-s4.0.3] Many Ulster Presbyterians emigrated to North America in the eighteenth century, and their descendants kept a sense of Ulster origins, which later gave Unionists an overseas audience.
[hist-ire4-s4.0.4] The Orange Order is a Protestant fraternal society (a brotherhood with its own lodges and rituals), and it had lodges overseas as well as in Ulster, so it gave Unionists a ready-made network abroad.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.b] Irish nationalism became an international force through emigrant organisations in the United States, Canada and Britain, which raised money and planned rebellion. Emigrants who could not change Ireland by voting at home could pay for those who would fight.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.0] The Irish Republican Brotherhood (IRB) was founded in Dublin on 17 March 1858 by James Stephens. It aimed at an independent Irish republic won by armed force, because Fenians believed Britain would never give up Ireland peacefully.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.1] The Fenian Brotherhood was founded in New York in 1858 by John O'Mahony as the American wing of the movement. It drew on Irish emigrants and on veterans of the American Civil War, who had military training.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.2] Fenian money came from Irish emigrant workers in American cities and paid for preparations for the Irish rising of 1867, which failed.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.3] In June 1866 Fenians crossed the Niagara River into Canada and won a skirmish at Ridgeway, Ontario, before withdrawing. The US authorities arrested some of them and pressed the Fenians to stop, so the raid achieved nothing for Ireland.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.4] The raids failed and damaged the movement's reputation in America, and they are generally said to have helped Canadian confederation (1867) by alarming the Canadian colonies. A further raid in 1870 was defeated.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.5] Clan na Gael was founded in New York in 1867 as a secret republican society for Irish Americans. As the Fenian Brotherhood declined it became the strongest republican body in the United States.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.6] The Hougoumont, the last convict ship to Australia, carried about 60 Fenian prisoners to Western Australia in 1867 to 1868 (arriving in January 1868). Irish-American opinion supported campaigns for the release of imprisoned Fenians.
[hist-ire4-s4.1.7] Jeremiah O'Donovan Rossa's Skirmishing Fund, launched in New York in 1876, paid for the dynamite campaign in English cities in the early to mid 1880s. This extended the use of American money to pay for violence.
[hist-ire4-s4.2.b] John Devoy was the most important Irish-American republican of his generation, and his career links the Fenians of the 1860s with the Rising of 1916. The first half of his story runs from Ireland to exile and the Catalpa.
[hist-ire4-s4.2.0] John Devoy was born in Co. Kildare in 1842 and joined the IRB in the 1860s, where he recruited Fenians among Irish soldiers in the British Army.
[hist-ire4-s4.2.1] He was arrested in 1866 and sentenced to 15 years' penal servitude for treason-felony (the charge of plotting against the Crown). He was released in 1871 on condition of exile and sailed to New York on the Cuba with other prisoners.
[hist-ire4-s4.2.2] In America he became a leading figure in Clan na Gael and used Irish-American newspapers to argue for an independent Irish republic, because newspapers reached emigrants across many cities.
[hist-ire4-s4.2.3] In 1876 Devoy and Clan na Gael organised the Catalpa rescue, in which the American whaler Catalpa freed six Fenian prisoners from Western Australia.
[hist-ire4-s4.2.4] In 1878 he proposed the New Departure with Michael Davitt. This was a plan for Fenians to cooperate with Charles Stewart Parnell's constitutional nationalists (those who sought change through Parliament) on land reform and self-government.
[hist-ire4-s4.2.5] He founded the Gaelic American newspaper in New York in 1903 and edited it, so that he could keep Irish-American opinion behind the republican cause.
[hist-ire4-s4.3.b] Devoy's last great effort was to help bring about the Rising of 1916, and his later life was darkened by a quarrel with the new republican leadership.
[hist-ire4-s4.3.0] In 1914 he and Judge Daniel Cohalan met the German ambassador in Washington, Count Bernstorff, to ask for German help for a rising. Clan na Gael also sent money to the IRB before the Rising of 1916.
[hist-ire4-s4.3.1] After 1919 he quarrelled bitterly with de Valera, the republican leader, yet he supported the Treaty of 1921. He visited the Irish Free State in 1924 and died in 1928.
[hist-ire4-s4.3.2] Contemporaries judged him differently: British opinion regarded him as a dangerous revolutionary, many Irish Americans saw him as an elder statesman of the republican cause, and de Valera's supporters saw him as an obstinate old man.
[hist-ire4-s4.3.3] Historians such as Terry Golway, in his biography Irish Rebel (1998), present him as a central link between Fenian conspiracy in Ireland and organised Irish-American support for republicanism in the twentieth century. His memoir Recollections of an Irish Rebel was published in 1929.
[hist-ire4-s4.4.b] Irish nationalists raised money in America for the land agitation of 1879 to 1882, and American support gave the movement a wide base. The campaign was about land: tenants wanted lower rents and protection from landlords.
[hist-ire4-s4.4.0] Michael Davitt, a Fenian released from prison in 1877, visited America in 1878. His work with Devoy produced the New Departure of 1878, which linked physical-force republicans with constitutional nationalists and the land question.
[hist-ire4-s4.4.1] The Irish National Land League was founded in 1879, with Parnell as president and Davitt as a founder. It campaigned against rack-renting (charging tenants excessive rents) and eviction.
[hist-ire4-s4.4.2] Parnell toured the United States in early 1880, raising funds among Irish Americans and addressing the House of Representatives in Washington.
[hist-ire4-s4.4.3] Patrick Ford's newspaper the Irish World raised money for the Land League and for evicted tenants.
[hist-ire4-s4.4.4] The American Ladies' Land League was founded in New York in 1880 and led by Fanny Parnell. The Land League itself was suppressed in 1881.
[hist-ire4-s4.4.5] American money paid for relief to evicted tenants and sustained the League's campaign, which made American money a direct force in Irish agrarian politics.
[hist-ire4-s4.5.b] Irish nationalist organisations in Britain and Australia gave the movement support beyond the United States, though their influence was smaller.
[hist-ire4-s4.5.0] The Irish National League of Great Britain, founded in 1883, organised Irish voters in Liverpool, Glasgow and other British cities behind the Irish Parliamentary Party.
[hist-ire4-s4.5.1] The Irish Self-Determination League of Great Britain, founded in 1919, campaigned for the Irish Republic among Irish emigrants in Britain during the War of Independence.
[hist-ire4-s4.5.2] Irish Catholic communities in Australia supported Home Rule through their churches and press, so priests and newspapers spread the cause. John and William Redmond toured Australia in 1883 to raise funds for the cause.
[hist-ire4-s4.5.3] After 1916 Irish Australians, led by Archbishop Mannix, opposed conscription (compulsory military service) and supported Irish independence.
[hist-ire4-s4.5.4] The Fenian prisoners transported to Western Australia and the Catalpa rescue gave Australia a place in Fenian memory.
[hist-ire4-s4.6.b] Irish-American organisations gave money and support to the 1916 Rising, and their divisions shaped Irish politics after 1918.
[hist-ire4-s4.6.0] Clan na Gael supported the Rising with money and contacts, and Devoy and Cohalan sought German help through contact with the German ambassador in Washington.
[hist-ire4-s4.6.1] The Irish Race Convention held in New York in March 1916 launched the Friends of Irish Freedom, led by Judge Daniel Cohalan and Devoy.
[hist-ire4-s4.6.2] The Aud, a German arms ship sent for the Rising, was intercepted by the Royal Navy and scuttled off Cork in April 1916, so the weapons never reached the rebels.
[hist-ire4-s4.6.3] After the Rising and the executions of its leaders, Irish-American opinion moved strongly behind the Irish Republic, and the Friends of Irish Freedom raised money and lobbied Congress.
[hist-ire4-s4.6.4] An Irish Race Convention in Philadelphia in February 1919 appealed to the Paris Peace Conference and President Wilson to support Irish self-determination (the right of a people to choose its own government).
[hist-ire4-s4.6.5] Divisions among Irish-American leaders, especially between Devoy and Cohalan on one side and Éamon de Valera on the other, weakened the unity of the movement after 1919.
[hist-ire4-s4.7.b] After the partition of Ireland, nationalists sought international support against the border, and Irish America was a key source of lobbying. The campaign achieved no change.
[hist-ire4-s4.7.0] The Government of Ireland Act of 1920 created Northern Ireland. The border with the Irish Free State was confirmed when the Boundary Commission collapsed in 1925.
[hist-ire4-s4.7.1] The Anti-Partition League was formed in Northern Ireland in 1945 to unite nationalists, so that they could speak with one voice, and it sought support in Ireland, Britain and America.
[hist-ire4-s4.7.2] Éamon de Valera, then leader of the opposition, toured the USA and other countries in 1948 to press the anti-partition case, but no US government action followed.
[hist-ire4-s4.7.3] In September 1948 Taoiseach John A. Costello announced in Ottawa the intention to repeal the External Relations Act, and the Republic of Ireland Act came into force on 18 April 1949.
[hist-ire4-s4.7.4] Britain answered with the Ireland Act of 1949, which guaranteed that Northern Ireland would remain in the United Kingdom unless its parliament decided otherwise.
[hist-ire4-s4.7.5] Ireland declined to join NATO in 1949 because of partition, and Seán MacBride, as Minister for External Affairs, appealed to American politicians and press.
[hist-ire4-s4.7.6] Congressman John Fogarty of Rhode Island proposed in 1950 that American aid to Britain be linked to ending partition. The proposal failed.
[hist-ire4-s4.8.b] Ulster Unionists looked for support among emigrants of Ulster Protestant descent in North America and the Dominions (self-governing countries of the British Empire), and their appeal rested on a shared Ulster-Scots identity.
[hist-ire4-s4.8.0] Ulster-Scots (or Scotch-Irish) identity means descent from Ulster Presbyterian settlers. In the United States and Canada these descendants kept a sense of Ulster origins, and a Scotch-Irish Society of America held its first congress in 1889.
[hist-ire4-s4.8.1] Andrew Jackson and Woodrow Wilson, two US presidents, were of Ulster-Scots descent, which Unionists could point to as proof of the Ulster connection with America.
[hist-ire4-s4.8.2] The Ulster Covenant of 28 September 1912 was signed by nearly half a million men and women (men signed the Covenant and women a parallel declaration) to oppose Home Rule, and it became the symbol of loyalist resistance that was publicised abroad.
[hist-ire4-s4.8.3] The Orange Order, founded in Co. Armagh in 1795, had strong lodges in Canada, in Ontario and Toronto. They were politically significant and gave moral support to Ulster Unionism during the Home Rule crisis.
[hist-ire4-s4.8.4] In Australia, Orange lodges in Victoria and New South Wales supported the British connection and opposed Home Rule for Ireland.
[hist-ire4-s4.8.5] Unionist lobbying abroad relied on Scotch-Irish and Orange networks. It had moral and some financial value but little influence on British policy, which makes it far smaller in scale than the nationalist networks.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.b] Bonar Law was born in Canada to an Ulster Presbyterian father, and he led the Conservatives in the Home Rule crisis before becoming Prime Minister in 1922. He was Canadian-born but of Ulster descent, and he was not himself an emigrant organiser.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.0] Andrew Bonar Law was born in 1858 at Kingston, New Brunswick, the son of the Rev. James Law, an Ulster Presbyterian minister.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.1] The family moved to Glasgow in 1870, and Bonar Law went into the iron trade there before entering Parliament in 1900.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.2] He became leader of the Conservative Party in November 1911, succeeding Arthur Balfour, which put a Unionist sympathiser at the head of the opposition.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.3] At Blenheim Palace in July 1912, he said he could imagine no length of resistance to which Ulster could go that he would not support. This was two months before the Ulster Covenant.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.4] He was a leading Unionist in the Home Rule crisis of 1912 to 1914, and his support for the Ulster Unionists made the exclusion of Ulster a Conservative aim.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.5] He served in the wartime coalition and was Chancellor of the Exchequer from 1916 to 1919 in Lloyd George's coalition. He became Prime Minister in October 1922, resigned in May 1923 because of cancer and died in October 1923.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.6] Contemporary opinion was divided: Liberal and nationalist newspapers portrayed him as a reckless partisan, while unionists praised him as a loyal champion.
[hist-ire4-s4.9.7] Historians such as Robert Blake (The Unknown Prime Minister, 1955) stress his role in shaping Conservative policy on Ulster, and they debate how far his stand made partition more likely.

[hist-ire4-s4.10.1] Limit: the Fenian rising of 1867 and the raids into Canada failed, and the quarrel between Devoy and Cohalan on one side and de Valera on the other weakened support in America.
[hist-ire4-s4.10.2] Anti-partition campaigners won sympathy in America after 1945 but failed to end partition.
[hist-ire4-s4.10.3] Unionist networks won real sympathy through Orange and Scotch-Irish links, but they had little effect on British policy, and they were small beside the nationalist networks.
[hist-ire4-s4.10.4] Contemporary view: British officials regarded Irish-American networks as threats to security, while Irish-American leaders saw themselves as allies of the Irish cause.
[hist-ire4-s4.10.5] Later view: historians debate whether diaspora money or politics in Ireland and Britain decided events, and many stress that the decisive events happened in Ireland and Britain.

CANDIDATES hist-ire4-s3: 3. The Irish in the USA and Britain: anti-Irish sentiment and political participation
[hist-ire4-s3.0.b] Famine-era emigrants were mostly poor, Catholic and unskilled, and they settled in the cities of the north-east USA and industrial Britain, where host communities saw them at close quarters. Hostility rested on religion, poverty and competition for work, and it was reinforced by stereotypes that were widely printed. This is discrimination: unfair treatment of a group in jobs, housing, politics or public life.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.0] Religion: most Irish emigrants were Catholic, and Protestant Americans and Britons often saw Catholicism as foreign and a threat to their institutions, because they believed the Pope was a rival authority to the state.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.1] Poverty and disease: the Irish who arrived in the Famine years were poor and crowded into slums, so outbreaks of cholera and typhus (diseases that spread easily in overcrowded, dirty housing) were blamed on them.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.2] Crime and drink: Irish migrants were blamed for crime, drunkenness and disorder in the cities where they lived, and they were over-represented in arrest figures, which seemed to confirm the prejudice.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.3] Competition for work: Irish labourers and servants competed with native-born and other workers, and their willingness to accept low wages created resentment among those who feared being undercut.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.4] Racial stereotypes: the Irish were portrayed as a separate and inferior race, and in cartoons they were often drawn with apelike features, which made hostility seem natural rather than prejudiced.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.5] Political fear: Protestants feared that Catholic voters would take orders from their priests, and that Irish nationalists would drag the host country into Irish quarrels.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.b] American nativism, the movement of native-born Protestants against immigrants and Catholic influence, was strongest in the 1840s and 1850s and went on in cartoons and job notices afterwards. In the USA it grew into a political party.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.0] The Philadelphia riots of 1844 (May and July) were anti-Catholic disturbances in which nativist mobs attacked Irish Catholic homes and churches, after a dispute over which Bible, the Protestant King James Bible or the Catholic one, should be used in public schools.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.1] Nativism became a political force, for example: the Know-Nothings, formally the American Party, won major state elections in 1854, including Massachusetts.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.2] The Know-Nothings took their name from the secrecy of their members, who answered questions about the movement with 'I know nothing'.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.3] They wanted longer residence before immigrants could become citizens and vote (a 21-year wait was a common demand) and wanted to keep Catholics and the foreign-born out of public office. The movement split over slavery in the mid-1850s and faded before the Civil War (1861).
[hist-ire4-s3.1.4] 'No Irish Need Apply' (NINA) was said to appear on job advertisements and signs in the 19th century. Richard Jensen argued in 2002 that written signs were rare and the memory exaggerated, while Rebecca Fried in 2015 found real newspaper advertisements, so historians disagree on how common they were.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.5] Thomas Nast, in cartoons in Harper's Weekly in the 1870s, drew Irish Catholics, and Irish politicians especially, as ape-like and corrupt, which spread the stereotype to a mass audience.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.6] Irish Americans responded by building their own parishes, schools, newspapers and societies, so they no longer depended on a hostile society, and by using the vote.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.b] In Britain anti-Irish feeling was mixed with sectarian rivalry (hostility between Protestants and Catholics), and it flared at moments of Fenian threat. The Fenians were an Irish republican movement active in Ireland, Britain and America in the 1860s.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.0] Punch magazine caricatured the Irish in the 1840s and 1860s, presenting them as simian (ape-like), violent and backward, so a respectable magazine helped to spread the stereotype.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.1] The Stockport riots of June 1852 saw Irish Catholic homes and churches attacked in Stockport, Cheshire, after a Catholic procession in the town.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.2] The Murphy riots of 1867 followed the anti-Catholic lectures of William Murphy and brought attacks on Irish districts in English towns, chiefly Birmingham and also Wolverhampton.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.3] In Liverpool and Glasgow, Protestant Orange lodges (societies of Protestants loyal to the Crown) and Catholic Irish communities clashed, so sectarian rivalry shaped local politics and street life.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.4] Three Fenians, the Manchester Martyrs, were executed on 23 November 1867. Three weeks later, on 13 December 1867, a Fenian bomb at Clerkenwell prison in London killed about a dozen people, and fears grew that all Irish people in Britain were a security threat.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.5] Fenian scares led to heavy policing of Irish districts, and Irish nationalists claimed that the state treated the whole community as suspect.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.6] Writers blamed the Irish for typhus, low wages and crime in industrial towns, and the Victorian press often presented them as a separate race.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.b] Irish Americans turned their numbers into political power through the Democratic Party and the city machines. To vote a migrant needed citizenship (naturalisation), and city government ran through patronage, meaning jobs and favours given in return for support, between two parties, Democrats and Republicans.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.0] Most Irish Americans voted Democratic, because the party defended immigrants against nativist attacks and offered them jobs and help.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.1] Ward politics: local party organisers in each ward (a district of a city) gave immigrants jobs, housing and legal help in return for votes, so the party became part of daily life.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.2] Tammany Hall, the Democratic organisation in New York City, controlled city politics through patronage, jobs and contracts. John Kelly led it from 1872, the first Irish boss, after the Tweed era.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.3] William R. Grace, born in Ireland, was elected mayor of New York in 1880, the first Irish-born Catholic mayor, and took office in 1881. Hugh O'Brien became mayor of Boston in 1885.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.4] John F. Fitzgerald ('Honey Fitz', mayor 1906 to 1908 and 1910 to 1914) and James Michael Curley (mayor four times, first term from 1914) dominated Boston politics in the early 20th century.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.5] Al Smith, a Catholic partly of Irish descent, was governor of New York in the 1920s and the Democratic presidential candidate in 1928, when he was defeated by Herbert Hoover.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.6] Irish men were prominent in urban police forces and fire companies, and these jobs were a route into local politics because city jobs were handed out through the party.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.7] Irish-American political success was built on group loyalty, so it gave immigrant communities a voice in city government.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.b] Richard Croker turned Tammany Hall into the most powerful political machine in New York City, and his career shows both the opportunity and the corruption of machine politics.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.0] Richard Croker was born in Co. Cork in 1843 and was taken to New York as a child, where he grew up in a poor Irish district.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.1] He rose, for example, through the Fourth Avenue gang, the fire companies and the local Democratic clubs, and held minor city offices such as alderman and coroner.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.2] He led Tammany Hall from the mid-1880s until 1902, using patronage, jobs in city departments and contracts to win support among immigrants and the poor.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.3] He controlled Democratic nominations and so could place allies in office, including Robert Van Wyck, mayor of New York from 1898 to 1901.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.4] The Lexow Committee (1894, a state inquiry into police corruption) and the Mazet Committee (1899, an inquiry into Tammany government) exposed the system. Tammany lost the mayoral election of 1901, and Croker left the leadership in 1902.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.5] He retired to Ireland, where he lived at Glencairn near Dublin and bred racehorses; his horse Orby won the Epsom Derby in 1907.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.6] Contemporary reformers and much of the respectable press condemned Croker's corruption, while many Tammany voters regarded him as a friend of the poor.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.7] Historians now present him as the clearest example of machine politics, in which immigrant political success came with corruption, though they also see his career as a route of upward mobility for an immigrant.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.b] Joseph P. Kennedy showed how an Irish American could rise to the top of business and then government, and his career was marked by controversy.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.0] Joseph P. Kennedy was born in Boston in 1888, the son of Patrick J. Kennedy, a Boston politician and businessman, and he married Rose Fitzgerald, daughter of Mayor John F. Fitzgerald.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.1] He built a fortune, for example, in banking, the stock market and the film industry before entering government.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.2] He was the first chairman of the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) from 1934, a body set up to regulate the stock market after the crash of 1929, and he chaired the US Maritime Commission in 1937.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.3] President Franklin D. Roosevelt appointed him ambassador to Britain, 1938 to 1940, widely described as the first Irish Catholic in the post.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.4] As ambassador he supported Neville Chamberlain's policy of appeasement (giving way to Germany to avoid war) and doubted that Britain could win a war with Germany. He resigned in late 1940 after an interview that was read as defeatist about Britain's chances.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.5] Contemporary Irish-American opinion was divided because he was both a success story and a conservative: some were proud of his success, while others distrusted his conservative views and his closeness to the British government.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.6] Historians judge him a self-made businessman and an appeaser whose pessimism ended his public career, and a father whose ambitions shaped his sons, including John F. Kennedy.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.b] Paul O'Dwyer combined a career as a New York lawyer with campaigns for civil rights and for Irish causes.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.0] Paul O'Dwyer was born in Bohola, Co. Mayo, in 1907 and emigrated to New York in 1925, becoming a lawyer there in the 1930s. He was the brother of William O'Dwyer, mayor of New York from 1946 to 1950.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.1] For example, he defended trade unionists and took on civil rights cases and causes for working people and immigrants, and he supported the civil rights movement.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.2] He was a Democratic politician who was the party's candidate for the US Senate in 1968, opposed to the Vietnam War, and lost to Jacob Javits. He was president of the New York City Council from 1974 to 1977, in the mid-1970s.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.3] He was active in Irish causes, and in later decades he supported civil rights for Catholics in Northern Ireland and criticised British policy there.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.4] Contemporaries saw him as a liberal and a critic of the establishment, and because of this some thought his views on Northern Ireland too sympathetic to the republican cause.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.5] Historians place him in the tradition of liberal Irish-American politics, in which civil rights and Irish nationalism were linked.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.b] Irish people in Britain were British subjects after the Act of Union (1801), so they needed no naturalisation and their political voice took a different form from American machines. It worked through Irish nationalist organisation and then through Labour.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.0] The Irish National League of Great Britain, founded in 1883, organised Irish voters behind the Irish Parliamentary Party, led by Charles Stewart Parnell.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.1] In the general election of November and December 1885 Parnell told Irish voters in Britain to vote against the Liberals, which in effect meant voting Conservative.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.2] T. P. O'Connor was elected MP for Liverpool Scotland in 1885 and held the seat until 1929, as a nationalist and a journalist. He is usually described as the only Irish nationalist elected for a British seat.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.3] O'Connor founded newspapers in London, such as The Star (1888), and used the press to support Home Rule (self-government for Ireland) and Irish causes.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.4] After Gladstone, the Liberal prime minister, introduced the first Home Rule Bill in 1886 (it was defeated), Irish voters in Liverpool and Glasgow largely supported the Liberal Party as the party of Home Rule.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.5] From the early 20th century Irish voters in Britain increasingly backed the Labour Party, which also drew support from the working class.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.6] Irish-born men could vote and stand for Parliament as British subjects, though the franchise was limited by property qualifications until the reforms of 1867 and 1884. This made participation easier than in the USA, where naturalisation was needed.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.b] The Molly Maguires were a secret society among Irish miners in the Pennsylvania anthracite (hard coal) coalfields, whose conflict with mine owners ended in executions.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.0] The society operated in the anthracite coalfields of eastern Pennsylvania, especially Schuylkill County, in the 1860s and 1870s. It drew on the tradition of the Ribbonmen, an Irish agrarian secret society.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.1] Irish miners worked in dangerous conditions for low pay, which fed their grievances, and many members belonged to the Ancient Order of Hibernians, an Irish Catholic fraternal organisation.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.2] Violence against mine bosses and police was blamed on the Mollies, so they were treated as a criminal conspiracy, who were accused of murders and arson.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.3] Franklin B. Gowen, head of the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad and its coal company, hired the Pinkerton detective agency, and the detective James McParland infiltrated the society under the name James McKenna.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.4] The Long Strike (December 1874 to June 1875) failed and broke the miners' union, the Workingmen's Benevolent Association. The trials of 1876 to 1878 followed, with convictions depending heavily on the evidence of informers and of McParland.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.5] Twenty men were hanged between 1877 and 1879. Ten were hanged on a single day, 21 June 1877, known as the Day of the Rope, at Pottsville and Mauch Chunk, and the leader Jack Kehoe was hanged in December 1878.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.6] Labour historians see the Mollies as victims of an employer-backed frame-up, while others see a real secret society using violence. Kevin Kenny (Making Sense of the Molly Maguires, 1998) argued they were a genuine society rooted in Irish agrarian violence, but that the trials were unfair.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.7] Jack Kehoe was pardoned after his death, by the governor of Pennsylvania in 1979.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.b] Judgements on Irish political life in America and Britain turn on whether the Irish gained real influence despite discrimination, or only built machines and patronage. Assimilation, adopting the language, customs and habits of the new country, is the thread: political participation was one route to it.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.0] Achievement: Irish politicians built lasting power in American cities and in some British seats, so they gave immigrants a route into public life.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.1] Limit: machine politics relied on patronage and corruption, and reform movements challenged it from the 1880s onwards.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.2] Success against discrimination: by the early 20th century Irish Americans were prominent in the police and city government, and Al Smith and Joseph Kennedy had reached national prominence, a sign that discrimination was being overcome.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.3] Contemporary view: nativists and reformers condemned Irish politicians as corrupt, while Irish communities defended them as champions of immigrants.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.4] Later view: historians disagree on whether Irish political success advanced assimilation or delayed it, because machine politics kept communities separate.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.5] Britain versus the USA: the Irish in Britain had votes sooner, but their political voice was channelled into the Irish Parliamentary Party and Labour rather than into city machines.
