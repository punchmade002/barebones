[hist-ire4-s3.4.0] Richard Croker was born in Co. Cork in 1843 and was taken to New York as a child, where he grew up in a poor Irish district.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.1] He rose through the Fourth Avenue gang, the fire companies and the local Democratic clubs, and he held minor city offices such as alderman and coroner.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.2] He became the leader of Tammany Hall in the mid-1880s and ran it until 1902, using patronage, jobs in city departments and contracts to build support among immigrants and the poor.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.3] He controlled Democratic nominations and placed his allies in office, including Mayor Robert Van Wyck (1898 to 1901).
[hist-ire4-s3.4.4] Investigations by the Lexow Committee (1894) into police corruption and the Mazet Committee (1899) exposed the system, and he left the leadership after Tammany lost the mayoral election of 1901.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.5] He retired to Ireland, where he lived at Glencairn near Dublin and bred racehorses; his horse Orby won the Epsom Derby in 1907.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.6] Contemporary reformers and much of the respectable press condemned Croker's corruption, while many Tammany voters regarded him as a friend of the poor.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.7] Historians now present him as the clearest example of machine politics, in which immigrant political success came with corruption, though they also see his career as a route of upward mobility for an immigrant.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.b] Joseph P. Kennedy showed how an Irish-American could rise to the top of business and then government, and his career was marked by controversy.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.0] Joseph P. Kennedy was born in Boston in 1888, the son of Patrick J. Kennedy, a Boston politician and businessman, and he married Rose Fitzgerald, daughter of Mayor John F. Fitzgerald.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.1] He built a fortune in banking, the stock market and the film industry before entering government.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.2] He was the first chairman of the US Securities and Exchange Commission, from 1934, set up to regulate the stock market after the crash of 1929.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.3] In 1937 he led the US Maritime Commission, and in 1938 President Roosevelt appointed him ambassador to Britain, the first Irish Catholic to hold the post.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.4] As ambassador from 1938 to 1940 he supported Neville Chamberlain's policy of appeasement and doubted that Britain could win a war with Germany, and he resigned in late 1940 after an interview that was widely seen as defeatist about Britain.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.5] Contemporary Irish-American opinion was divided: some were proud of his success, while others distrusted his conservative views and his closeness to the British government.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.6] Historians judge him a self-made businessman and an appeaser whose pessimism damaged his career, and whose ambitions shaped his sons, including John F. Kennedy (see the notes on John F. Kennedy).
[hist-ire4-s3.6.b] Paul O'Dwyer combined a career as a New York lawyer with campaigns for civil rights and for Irish causes.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.0] Paul O'Dwyer was born in Bohola, Co. Mayo, in 1907 and emigrated to New York in 1925, becoming a lawyer there in the 1930s; he was the brother of William O'Dwyer, mayor of New York from 1946 to 1950.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.1] He defended trade unionists and took on civil rights cases and causes for working people and immigrants, and supported the civil rights movement.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.2] He was a Democratic politician who ran unsuccessfully for the US Senate in 1968 as an opponent of the Vietnam War and later served as president of the New York City Council in the mid-1970s.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.3] He was active in Irish causes, and in later decades he supported civil rights for Catholics in Northern Ireland and criticised British policy there.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.4] Contemporaries saw him as a liberal and a critic of the establishment, and some criticised his views on Northern Ireland as too sympathetic to the republican cause.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.5] Historians place him in the tradition of liberal Irish-American politics, in which civil rights and Irish nationalism were linked.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.b] The Irish vote in British cities was strongest in Liverpool and Scotland, and it was used by nationalist and Labour politicians.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.0] The Irish National League of Great Britain, founded in 1883, organised Irish voters behind the Irish Parliamentary Party.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.1] In the general election of 1885 Parnell told Irish voters in Britain to vote against the Liberals, and they voted Conservative.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.2] T. P. O'Connor was elected MP for Liverpool Scotland in 1885, and he held the seat until 1929 as a nationalist and a journalist.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.3] O'Connor founded newspapers in London, including The Star, and he used the press to support Home Rule and Irish causes.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.4] After Gladstone's first Home Rule Bill in 1886 Irish voters in Liverpool and Glasgow largely supported the Liberal Party as the party of Home Rule.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.5] From the early 20th century Irish voters in Britain increasingly backed the Labour Party, which also drew support from the working class.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.6] Irish-born men could vote and stand for Parliament as British subjects, so their political participation was easier than in the USA, where naturalisation was needed.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.b] The Molly Maguires were a secret society among Irish miners in the Pennsylvania anthracite coalfields, whose conflict with mine owners ended in executions.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.0] The society operated in the anthracite coalfields of eastern Pennsylvania, especially in Schuylkill County, in the 1860s and 1870s, and drew on the tradition of the Ribbonmen and the name of an Irish agrarian secret society.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.1] Irish miners worked in dangerous conditions for low pay, and many members belonged to the Ancient Order of Hibernians.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.2] Violence against mine bosses and police was blamed on the Mollies, who were accused of murders and arson.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.3] The owner of the Reading Railroad, Franklin B. Gowen, hired the Pinkerton detective James McParland, who infiltrated the society under the name James McKenna.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.4] After the failed Long Strike of 1875 the miners' union was broken, and the trials took place in 1876 to 1878, with convictions depending heavily on the evidence of informers and of McParland.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.5] Twenty men were hanged between 1877 and 1879; ten were hanged on a single day, 21 June 1877, known as the Day of the Rope, and the leader Jack Kehoe was hanged in 1878.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.6] Labour historians see the Mollies as victims of an employer-backed frame-up, while others see a real secret society using violence; Kevin Kenny (Making Sense of the Molly Maguires, 1998) argued that they were a genuine society, rooted in Irish agrarian violence, but that the trials were unfair.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.7] Jack Kehoe was pardoned after his death, by the Governor of Pennsylvania in 1979.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.b] Judgements on Irish political life in America and Britain turn on whether the Irish gained real influence despite discrimination, or only built machines and patronage.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.0] Achievement: Irish politicians built lasting power in American cities and in some British seats, and they gave immigrants a route into public life.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.1] Limit: machine politics relied on patronage and corruption, and reform movements challenged it from the 1880s onwards.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.2] Success against discrimination: by the early 20th century Irish Americans were prominent in the police and city government, a sign that discrimination was being overcome.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.3] Contemporary view: nativists and reformers condemned Irish politicians as corrupt, while Irish communities defended them as champions of immigrants.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.4] Later view: historians disagree on whether Irish political success advanced assimilation or delayed it, because machine politics kept communities separate.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.5] Britain versus the USA: the Irish in Britain had votes sooner, but their political voice was channelled into the Irish Parliamentary Party and Labour rather than into city machines.
