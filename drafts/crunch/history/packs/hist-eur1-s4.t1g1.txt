[hist-eur1-s6.3.1] Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte, nephew of Napoleon I, was elected president on 10 December 1848 with about three-quarters of the vote, beating Cavaignac.
[hist-eur1-s6.3.2] Louis-Napoléon had been imprisoned at Ham after an attempted rising at Boulogne in 1840, and he had lived in exile in Britain and elsewhere.
[hist-eur1-s6.3.3] The Party of Order, made up of monarchists and conservatives, won a large majority in the Legislative Assembly elections of May 1849.
[hist-eur1-s6.3.4] The Falloux Law (March 1850) gave the Catholic Church a major role in education, and in May 1850 the conservative majority restricted the franchise, removing about three million voters.
[hist-eur1-s6.3.5] Louis-Napoléon's campaign to change the constitution to allow his re-election failed in the Assembly in July 1851.
[hist-eur1-s6.4.0] On 2 December 1851 Louis-Napoléon carried out a coup d'état, arresting deputies and dissolving the Assembly, and armed resistance in Paris and the provinces was suppressed.
[hist-eur1-s6.4.1] A plebiscite in December 1851 approved the coup by a very large majority and a new constitution of January 1852 gave the president a ten-year term and wide powers; the plebiscite of November 1852 approved the restoration of the empire.
[hist-eur1-s6.4.2] Louis-Napoléon proclaimed the Second Empire on 2 December 1852 and took the name Napoleon III, with the title of Emperor of the French.
[hist-eur1-s6.4.3] The Second Empire in its early years was authoritarian: the press was censored, political opponents were exiled, and elections were controlled by official candidates.
[hist-eur1-s6.4.4] The Crédit Mobilier, founded in 1852 by the Pereire brothers, financed railways, industry and urban development, and rapid economic growth followed in the 1850s.
[hist-eur1-s6.4.5] The Paris Exposition of 1855 and the rebuilding of Paris under Haussmann showed the regime's commitment to modern economy and prestige.
[hist-eur1-s6.4.6] Napoleon III married Eugénie de Montijo in 1853, and the regime promoted railway building and large public works.
[hist-eur1-s6.5.0] The Cobden–Chevalier Treaty of 1860 with Britain reduced French tariffs, opening French markets to British goods and encouraging French industry to modernise.
[hist-eur1-s6.5.1] From 1860 the regime gave the legislature more freedom to debate, and in 1864 it legalised the right to strike, though trade unions remained illegal; the press law of 1868 relaxed censorship.
[hist-eur1-s6.5.2] France fought in the Crimean War (1854–56) as Britain's ally and it helped Piedmont in 1859, which led to the French gains of Nice and Savoy in 1860.
[hist-eur1-s6.5.3] Napoleon III's plan for a French-backed empire in Mexico under the Habsburg Archduke Maximilian collapsed, French troops withdrew by 1867, and Maximilian was executed by Mexican republicans.
[hist-eur1-s6.5.4] Napoleon III's government lost prestige after the Austrian war of 1866 and the Prussian victory, which had changed the balance of power in Europe.
[hist-eur1-s6.5.5] Émile Ollivier formed a liberal ministry in 1870, and a plebiscite in May 1870 approved the liberal reforms by a large majority.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.b] Napoleon III was a participant in the political events of 1848 to 1870 and a symbol of the French regime. Contemporaries and historians disagree about whether he was a modernising leader or an autocrat who led France into disaster.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.0] Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte was born in Paris in 1808, the son of Louis Bonaparte, King of Holland, and the nephew of Napoleon I.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.1] He attempted coups in Strasbourg (1836) and Boulogne (1840), both of which failed, and he spent about six years in prison at Ham, where he wrote on social questions, before escaping in 1846.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.2] As president of the Second Republic he won the election of December 1848 by appealing to peasants and to the memory of his uncle, and he was supported by conservatives who expected to use him.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.3] His coup of December 1851 and the Second Empire that followed made him the central political figure in France until 1870.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.4] In foreign policy he backed Piedmont in 1859, joined the Crimean War, supported the Mexican adventure and then led France into war with Prussia in 1870.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.5] He was captured at Sedan on 2 September 1870, was held in Germany and then went into exile in England, where he died in 1873.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.6] Contemporaries were divided: supporters praised economic growth and Paris, while republicans and liberals such as Victor Hugo denounced him as a usurper.
[hist-eur1-s6.6.7] Hugo and Marx, in Napoléon le Petit and The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, depicted him as a usurper and a parody of his uncle; revisionist historians such as Theodore Zeldin and Roger Price stress his interest in social reform, his economic programme and his liberalising steps, though they judge his foreign policy a failure.
[hist-eur1-s6.7.b] Hugo was a politician as well as a writer. His political role in this period was as a deputy and an opponent of the Empire; his literary work is covered in the notes on cultural nationalism.
[hist-eur1-s6.7.0] Victor Hugo, born in 1802, was a leading Romantic writer and a monarchist in the 1820s, and he had become a republican by 1848.
[hist-eur1-s6.7.1] He was elected to the Constituent Assembly of 1848 and the Legislative Assembly, where he defended freedom of the press and education and opposed the restriction of the franchise.
[hist-eur1-s6.7.2] He supported Louis-Napoléon's candidacy in 1848 but broke with him, and by 1851 was a leader of the democratic opposition in the Legislative Assembly.
[hist-eur1-s6.7.3] After the coup of December 1851 he was forced to leave France, first to Brussels, then to Jersey (1852–55) and Guernsey (1855–70).
[hist-eur1-s6.7.4] His pamphlet Napoléon le Petit (1852) and his satirical poems Les Châtiments (1853) attacked Napoleon III and made him a symbol of republican resistance.
[hist-eur1-s6.7.5] He refused the amnesty offered in 1859 and stayed in exile on Guernsey as a protest against the regime.
[hist-eur1-s6.7.6] Hugo returned to Paris on 5 September 1870, the day after the Third Republic was proclaimed; during the Commune he criticised its excesses but opposed the repression and offered asylum to fugitives in Brussels.
[hist-eur1-s6.7.7] Contemporaries saw him as a republican hero, while critics saw his shifts from royalism to republicanism as inconsistent; his funeral in 1885 drew huge crowds, and historians now see him as a principled defender of liberty and democracy.
[hist-eur1-s6.8.0] After Sedan the Empire collapsed, and a republic was proclaimed in Paris on 4 September 1870 by deputies from Paris, including Léon Gambetta.
[hist-eur1-s6.8.1] A Government of National Defence continued the war against Prussia, and Paris was besieged from September 1870 until the armistice of January 1871; Gambetta left the city by balloon to organise resistance in the provinces.
[hist-eur1-s6.8.2] The National Assembly elected in February 1871 was dominated by monarchists, with Adolphe Thiers as head of the executive, and it sat at Bordeaux and then at Versailles, not in Paris.
[hist-eur1-s6.8.3] The Paris Commune of 18 March to 28 May 1871 began when Thiers's troops tried to seize the National Guard's cannon on Montmartre; it was a revolutionary government of Parisian radicals and socialists, suppressed by government troops in the Bloody Week with many thousands killed.
[hist-eur1-s6.8.4] The Third Republic was confirmed in 1875 by constitutional laws, which established a parliamentary system with a president.
[hist-eur1-s6.9.0] Five regimes between 1814 and 1871 (the Bourbon Restoration, the July Monarchy, the Second Republic, the Second Empire and the Third Republic) reflected the lack of agreement about the basis of government.
[hist-eur1-s6.9.1] The Bourbon and July regimes were limited by narrow franchises, and the exclusion of most men from political life made opposition likely.
[hist-eur1-s6.9.2] The Second Republic showed that universal male suffrage could bring a president who would destroy the republic, and it deepened the divide between the urban poor and the conservative countryside.
[hist-eur1-s6.9.3] The Second Empire gave France economic growth and prestige but depended on the personal authority of Napoleon III, and its foreign policy failures weakened it.
[hist-eur1-s6.9.4] Overall: France's regimes were unstable because each one lacked a broad consensus, and the authority of the state rested on the personal position of its rulers rather than on a settled constitutional system.
