[hist-ire2-s5.0.b] Unionism was a coalition of Protestants of all classes in Ulster with landlords and business people elsewhere in Ireland, united by opposition to a Dublin parliament.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.0] Ulster had a slight Protestant majority (about 56 per cent in 1911), concentrated in the east: Antrim, Down, Armagh and Londonderry had Protestant majorities, while Tyrone and Fermanagh were narrowly Catholic.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.1] Protestants of every class were unionist: landlords and gentry, industrialists, shopkeepers, Presbyterian tenant farmers and Protestant workers in Belfast.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.2] Southern unionists were a small minority (Protestants were about a tenth of the population outside Ulster), made up of landlords, professionals, businessmen and Church of Ireland clergy, strongest in Dublin and parts of Munster and Leinster.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.3] Most Ulster Presbyterians had supported the Liberals before 1886, and Gladstone’s conversion to Home Rule pushed many into the unionist camp.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.4] Many Protestants had been alarmed by Gladstone’s disestablishment of the Church of Ireland (1869) and by the Land Acts, and saw Home Rule as the next step.
[hist-ire2-s5.0.5] Colonel Edward Saunderson led the Irish Unionist MPs at Westminster from 1886 until his death in 1906, and was followed by Walter Long, then Carson in 1910.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.b] Unionists opposed Home Rule for three linked reasons: religion, economics and identity.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.0] Religion: Protestants feared that a parliament elected by a Catholic majority would be under Catholic Church influence; the slogan was that Home Rule is Rome Rule.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.1] The Catholic hierarchy’s role in the Irish Party’s candidate selection in 1885, and later the Ne Temere decree of 1908, strengthened the argument.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.2] Economy: Belfast’s linen, shipbuilding, engineering and rope-making depended on British and imperial markets and raw materials, and unionists feared tariffs and higher taxes under a Dublin parliament.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.3] The agricultural south and west had a different economy from the industrial north-east, so unionists argued that an Irish parliament would favour agricultural interests.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.4] Identity and empire: Ulster Protestants saw themselves as British, were proud of the empire, and believed that Home Rule was the first step towards separation.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.5] Protestants also feared for their position as a minority in a self-governing Ireland, and many believed nationalists would dispossess them, as in the Land War.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.6] Liberal unionists such as Chamberlain argued that Home Rule would threaten the union of the kingdoms, not only the Ulster minority.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.7] Protestants held a disproportionate share of Ireland’s land, wealth and professions, and feared losing status in a state run by the Catholic majority.
[hist-ire2-s5.1.8] Many Ulster unionists believed that the Land Acts and the Irish Party’s links with Fenians showed that nationalists would not respect property.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.b] From 1885 unionists built organisations to match the National League.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.0] The Orange Order, founded in 1795, revived in the 1880s and gave Ulster unionism a mass base of lodges across the province.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.1] The Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union was founded in 1885 by southern unionists; the Ulster Loyalist Anti-Repeal Union followed in 1886.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.2] The Belfast riots of 1886 sharpened sectarian division (see the notes on the 1885–86 elections).
[hist-ire2-s5.2.3] The Irish Unionist Alliance was formed in 1891 to unite the Irish unionist bodies.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.4] The Ulster Convention, held in the Botanic Gardens in Belfast in June 1892 with about 12,000 delegates, protested against the Second Home Rule Bill; a Dublin convention followed.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.5] The Ulster Unionist Council was founded in March 1905 as a central body for Ulster, bringing together Orange lodges, constituency associations and unionist MPs.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.6] Opposition to the Irish Reform Association’s devolution proposals in 1904–05 convinced Ulster unionists that they needed their own permanent body.
[hist-ire2-s5.2.7] The Ulster Women’s Unionist Council, formed in January 1911 under Lady Londonderry, mobilised tens of thousands of women for the unionist cause.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.b] Carson, a Dublin-born barrister, led the unionist campaign against the Third Home Rule Bill and made the threat of armed resistance credible.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.0] Edward Carson (1854–1935) was born in Dublin, educated at Trinity College, and became a leading barrister, Solicitor General for Ireland in 1892 and Solicitor General for England in 1900–06.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.1] He was MP for Dublin University from 1892, and in 1895 he cross-examined Oscar Wilde as counsel for the Marquess of Queensberry in the libel case that ruined Wilde.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.2] In 1910 he became leader of the Irish Unionist parliamentary party, and he was the chief unionist campaigner from 1911 to 1914.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.3] He was a participant: he led the Ulster Day rally on 28 September 1912, signed the Covenant first at Belfast City Hall, and led the Provisional Government plan of 1913.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.4] His southern, Dublin background made him an unlikely leader of Ulster, but he spoke for all Irish unionists, which was the point of his campaign.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.5] Contemporary unionists revered him as the defender of the Union; nationalists and many Liberals thought him a man ready to defy parliament, and Redmond denounced the threat of force.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.6] Historians differ: some say he never intended to fight and bluffed to protect the Union; others that his threat made partition inevitable, and that he abandoned southern unionists.
[hist-ire2-s5.3.7] He resigned the leadership in 1921, becoming a law lord, and lamented that unionism had been sacrificed in the creation of Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.b] After the Parliament Act of 1911 removed the Lords’ veto, unionists organised a campaign of mass resistance with Conservative backing.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.0] The Parliament Act (August 1911) meant that the Third Home Rule Bill would become law within about two years, even if the Lords rejected it.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.1] James Craig, a wealthy Belfast businessman and MP for East Down, became Carson’s chief organiser and planned the campaign.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.2] A huge unionist demonstration was held at Craigavon, outside Belfast, in September 1911, with Carson as speaker.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.3] Andrew Bonar Law, the Conservative leader since November 1911, spoke at Blenheim Palace on 27 July 1912, pledging the party to support Ulster in any resistance.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.4] The Third Home Rule Bill was introduced on 11 April 1912; an amendment to exclude four Ulster counties was defeated in June 1912.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.5] On Ulster Day, 28 September 1912, about 237,000 men signed the Solemn League and Covenant, pledging to use all means to defeat Home Rule, and about 234,000 women signed a separate declaration.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.6] The total of about 471,000 signatures included many in Britain; some, especially younger Protestants, signed in their own blood or at the church door.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.7] The Commons passed the Third Home Rule Bill in January 1913, the Lords rejected it, and the same happened in July 1913; a third Commons passage in 1914 overrode the Lords under the Parliament Act.
[hist-ire2-s5.4.8] A British Covenant organised by the Union Defence League in 1914 gathered a very large number of signatures in Britain (about two million is often quoted).
[hist-ire2-s5.4.9] The Twelfth of July Orange demonstrations and Presbyterian and Church of Ireland ministers gave the campaign a religious character, with many ministers joining Covenant services.
[hist-ire2-s5.5.b] In 1913 unionist leaders organised an armed force and prepared to take over the government of Ulster.
[hist-ire2-s5.5.0] The Ulster Volunteer Force was formed in January 1913 from earlier local drilling clubs, with Lieutenant-General Sir George Richardson as commander.
[hist-ire2-s5.5.1] By 1914 the UVF had perhaps 90,000 to 100,000 men, organised in battalions, with many drilling openly and wearing uniform.
[hist-ire2-s5.5.2] In September 1913 the Ulster Unionist Council announced that it would set up a Provisional Government of Ulster, with Carson as chairman, if Home Rule became law.
[hist-ire2-s5.5.3] Unionists said that they were loyal to the king but not to a parliament they believed to be illegitimate.
[hist-ire2-s5.5.4] The government did not prosecute, and Redmond warned that the failure to act encouraged separatist counter-organisation (the Irish Volunteers, founded November 1913).
[hist-ire2-s5.6.b] The crisis peaked in the first half of 1914, with the army’s reluctance and the unionist arms landing.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.0] On 9 March 1914 Asquith proposed that Ulster counties could vote themselves out of Home Rule for six years; Carson rejected this as a stay of execution.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.1] On 20 March 1914, in the Curragh incident, Brigadier-General Hubert Gough and many of his officers offered to resign rather than act against Ulster; the Secretary for War, John Seely, and Sir John French resigned afterwards.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.2] On the night of 24–25 April 1914 the UVF landed about 25,000 rifles and several million rounds at Larne, Donaghadee and Bangor, organised by Fred Crawford, and the police and army did not intervene.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.3] The Larne landing prompted nationalists to organise their own gun-running at Howth and Kilcoole in July–August 1914.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.4] The Buckingham Palace Conference (21–24 July 1914), opened by George V and chaired by the Speaker, brought Asquith, Lloyd George, Redmond, Dillon, Carson, Craig, Lansdowne and Bonar Law together.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.5] The Conference failed over the area to be excluded, especially Tyrone and Fermanagh, with mixed populations.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.6] The Home Rule Act received the royal assent on 18 September 1914 but was suspended for the duration of the war, leaving Ulster’s status unsettled.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.7] The rifles had been bought in Germany by Fred Crawford and brought to Larne by the ship Clyde Valley, then distributed by car across Ulster in a night.
[hist-ire2-s5.6.8] Redmond rejected permanent exclusion of Ulster as partition, while Carson rejected temporary exclusion; the gap between them was never closed before the war.
[hist-ire2-s5.7.b] Judgements on unionism compare its success in blocking Home Rule before 1914 with its failure to hold the whole of Ireland in the Union.
[hist-ire2-s5.7.0] Achievement: unionists defeated the first two Home Rule Bills, built effective organisation and by 1914 had forced the government to accept the principle of exclusion for Ulster.
[hist-ire2-s5.7.1] Achievement: the UVF, the Covenant and Conservative backing showed that Home Rule could not be imposed on Ulster by force, and made Carson and Craig decisive in later negotiations.
[hist-ire2-s5.7.2] Limit: unionists could not stop the Third Home Rule Bill becoming law, and southern unionists had no protection.
[hist-ire2-s5.7.3] Limit: the Conservative alliance meant unionism was exposed when the Conservatives themselves compromised, as they did in 1920–21.
[hist-ire2-s5.7.4] Contemporary nationalists thought unionist arms threatened democracy and parliament; unionists thought them a legitimate defence of rights.
[hist-ire2-s5.7.5] Historians such as A.T.Q. Stewart (The Ulster Crisis, 1967) show how close Britain came to civil war in 1914; Alvin Jackson stresses unionist resilience and the divisions between Ulster and southern unionists.
