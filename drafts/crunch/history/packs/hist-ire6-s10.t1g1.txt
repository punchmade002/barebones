TARGET hist-ire6-s10: 10. Case study: the impact of RTÉ, 1962–1972
[hist-ire6-s10.0.b] Irish television began late, after a long debate about whether and how the state should run it.
[hist-ire6-s10.0.0] Radio had been run by the state since 2RN opened in Dublin in 1926, and by the 1950s it was called Radio Éireann and was part of the Department of Posts and Telegraphs.
[hist-ire6-s10.0.1] Before 1961 viewers in the east, the north and along the coast could pick up BBC and Ulster Television signals from Britain and Northern Ireland, and these showed that Irish demand for television was growing.
[hist-ire6-s10.0.2] The government decided to set up a national service, financed by licence fees and advertising, and the Broadcasting Authority Act 1960 created the Radio Éireann Authority, soon known as the Radio Telefís Éireann (RTÉ) Authority, to run radio and television.
[hist-ire6-s10.0.3] The members of the Authority were appointed by the government, which also set the framework in law.
[hist-ire6-s10.0.4] Telefís Éireann began broadcasting on 31 December 1961; President de Valera spoke at the opening and warned that television had a great power for good and evil.
[hist-ire6-s10.0.5] Television reached most of the country within a few years as transmitters were built, and by the early 1970s most households had a set.
[hist-ire6-s10.1.0] The Minister for Posts and Telegraphs could direct the Authority in certain matters under the 1960 Act, and Section 31 allowed him to require it to refrain from broadcasting specified material.
[hist-ire6-s10.1.1] RTÉ was a statutory body, not a government department, and the Act required it to be impartial in news and current affairs; how far it was independent in practice was a source of conflict.
[hist-ire6-s10.1.2] Seán Lemass saw television as a means of national development and public information, and he said that RTÉ was an instrument of public policy, which meant it should support the national goals of the government without being its mouthpiece.
[hist-ire6-s10.1.3] Critics, including many in RTÉ, held that a broadcaster could serve the public only if it was free to criticise the government.
[hist-ire6-s10.1.4] The tension between the two views shaped the history of RTÉ in the 1960s and the crisis of 1971 and 1972.
[hist-ire6-s10.2.0] Much early output was imported from Britain and the United States, including westerns, comedies and films, and imported programmes gave Irish viewers a view of life elsewhere.
[hist-ire6-s10.2.1] Home-produced programmes covered news, sport, current affairs, drama, light entertainment and Irish-language material; coverage of Gaelic games drew large audiences.
[hist-ire6-s10.2.2] The Riordans, a drama about a farming family that began in 1965, was a hit with viewers, especially in rural Ireland, and it dealt with real issues of farming life and change.
[hist-ire6-s10.2.3] Telefís Scoile, which began on 4 February 1964, broadcast lessons for secondary school pupils and was one of the earliest schools television services in Europe.
[hist-ire6-s10.2.4] Irish-language programmes and news bulletins in Irish were broadcast from the start, though they took a small share of airtime and often had a small audience.
[hist-ire6-s10.2.5] RTÉ also raised standards for Irish drama and music, and helped writers, actors and entertainers to reach a national audience.
[hist-ire6-s10.3.b] The Late Late Show, first broadcast in July 1962, became the best-known programme on Irish television and a forum for national debate.
[hist-ire6-s10.3.0] The programme was a live Friday night talk show with guests, music and audience participation, and it started as a summer filler before it became a fixture.
[hist-ire6-s10.3.1] Gay Byrne presented it from 1962 until 1999, and his skills as a chat-show host brought viewers an open-minded, humorous but probing style.
[hist-ire6-s10.3.2] The show discussed subjects that were rarely talked of in public, including sex, marriage, contraception, unmarried mothers and the church.
[hist-ire6-s10.3.3] In 1966 a quiz item for married couples led to the episode known as the Bishop and the Nightie: when Gay Byrne asked a wife, Eileen Fox, what she had worn on her honeymoon night, she said she had not worn a nightdress; the remark caused uproar.
[hist-ire6-s10.3.4] Bishop Thomas Ryan of Clonfert condemned the programme, and other clergy and some viewers complained, while many viewers defended Byrne and the show.
[hist-ire6-s10.3.5] The incident showed that television could provoke public arguments about morality and the limits of church authority, and it brought the Late Late Show to national attention.
[hist-ire6-s10.4.0] Seven Days, a current affairs series launched in 1966, used young producers and reporters to investigate housing, poverty, emigration and other social issues.
[hist-ire6-s10.4.1] Ministers resented programmes that criticised policy, and complaints were made to the RTÉ Authority, which was under pressure to restrain current affairs.
[hist-ire6-s10.4.2] A Seven Days programme on illegal moneylending in 1969 led to a government-ordered inquiry and a row about RTÉ's independence, after which current affairs coverage was reorganised.
[hist-ire6-s10.4.3] From the end of the 1960s the Troubles made coverage of Northern Ireland a major source of conflict between RTÉ and the government, which feared that broadcasts would encourage violence.
[hist-ire6-s10.4.4] The government held financial and legal power over the broadcaster, as it appointed the Authority and could change the law.
[hist-ire6-s10.5.0] Television altered how evenings were spent, since people stayed at home to watch rather than go to dances or visit neighbours, and it reduced the isolation of rural communities.
[hist-ire6-s10.5.1] It brought national and international news and sport into every home, and gave people in the countryside access to the same culture and entertainment as city people.
[hist-ire6-s10.5.2] Programmes on social issues exposed viewers to arguments about contraception, divorce and the church that censorship had kept out of print.
[hist-ire6-s10.5.3] Church leaders such as McQuaid were concerned about the effects of television on faith and morals, but the church also used television to broadcast Mass, the Angelus and religious programmes.
[hist-ire6-s10.5.4] Politicians had to learn to use the medium: party leaders appeared on television to speak directly to voters, and campaigns began to focus on television appearances and image.
[hist-ire6-s10.5.5] Viewers' reactions varied: many welcomed the new window on the world, while others, including some clergy and traditionalists, saw it as a threat to Irish values.
[hist-ire6-s10.6.b] Coverage of the Northern conflict led to the first serious use of the government's powers over broadcasting.
[hist-ire6-s10.6.0] On 1 October 1971 the Minister for Posts and Telegraphs, Gerry Collins, issued the first order under Section 31, directing RTÉ not to broadcast material that could promote or incite to crime or tend to undermine the authority of the State.
[hist-ire6-s10.6.1] RTÉ journalists protested that the order restricted reporting of the Troubles and argued that the public had a right to hear different views.
[hist-ire6-s10.6.2] On 19 November 1972 the radio programme This Week broadcast an interview by Kevin O'Kelly with Seán Mac Stíofáin, chief of staff of the Provisional IRA, who was arrested the same day.
[hist-ire6-s10.6.3] The government regarded the broadcast as a breach of the Section 31 order, and in late November 1972 Collins dismissed the whole RTÉ Authority and appointed a new one.
[hist-ire6-s10.6.4] O'Kelly was later jailed for refusing to identify Mac Stíofáin's voice in court.
[hist-ire6-s10.6.5] The sacking caused protests by journalists and in the Oireachtas, and it raised the question of whether a state broadcaster could report freely.
[hist-ire6-s10.6.6] Section 31 restrictions were extended and widened in the 1970s and 1980s, and they were not ended until 1994.
[hist-ire6-s10.7.b] Byrne was the presenter of The Late Late Show and the best-known broadcaster in Ireland for decades.
[hist-ire6-s10.7.0] Gay Byrne was born in Dublin in 1934, worked in Britain in the 1950s and returned to join Radio Éireann in the late 1950s, becoming a presenter on Telefís Éireann in its first years.
[hist-ire6-s10.7.1] He presented The Late Late Show from 1962 to 1999 and a morning radio programme from 1973, which gave him a wide audience in every part of the country.
[hist-ire6-s10.7.2] He was a participant in the change of the 1960s: his questions to guests and audience on sex, marriage and religion set the agenda for debate.
[hist-ire6-s10.7.3] He generally avoided taking sides, presenting himself as a neutral host, but his willingness to air subjects that the church or the government preferred to avoid had a liberalising effect.
[hist-ire6-s10.7.4] Contemporaries were divided, with many welcoming him as a modern voice and others, including some clergy, seeing his programme as an attack on morals.
[hist-ire6-s10.7.5] Historians and commentators regard him as a major influence on how Irish people talked about sexuality, the church and social issues, while noting that television reflected changes that were already under way; he died in 2019.
[hist-ire6-s10.8.0] The government and Lemass: RTÉ should help to modernise the country, explain official policy and support national aims, and ministers expected a degree of cooperation from its Authority.
[hist-ire6-s10.8.1] RTÉ journalists and producers: a national broadcaster should be free to investigate and criticise, and many saw ministerial pressure as censorship.
[hist-ire6-s10.8.2] The Catholic Church: television was a powerful influence on the young that needed to be watched, and bishops and priests objected to some programmes, though the church also valued the airtime given to religion.
[hist-ire6-s10.8.3] Language activists: Irish-language output was too small a share of the schedule, and imported English-language programmes, mostly from Britain and the United States, risked weakening the language further.
[hist-ire6-s10.8.4] Rural viewers: programmes such as The Riordans showed their own lives, while others thought the city-based producers misrepresented the countryside.
[hist-ire6-s10.8.5] Politicians outside government: opposition parties complained of bias in favour of the government, and some felt that the Authority was too ready to give way to ministers.
[hist-ire6-s10.8.6] Historians such as Robert Savage, in his study of television and Irish society in these years, show that RTÉ both reflected changes already under way and helped to speed them, and that its relationship with the state was complicated rather than simply one of control.
[hist-ire6-s10.9.0] Entertained: it brought sport, drama, light entertainment and music to a national audience, and many programmes, such as The Riordans and The Late Late Show, were hugely popular.
[hist-ire6-s10.9.1] Challenged: current affairs and chat shows questioned authority and exposed issues that were taboo, such as contraception, poverty and the Northern conflict.
[hist-ire6-s10.9.2] Changed: viewers had a wider view of the world, attitudes to church and state became more questioning, and the habit of national debate grew.
[hist-ire6-s10.9.3] Limits: the government held strong powers, Section 31 and the dismissal of the Authority showed that independence was fragile, and Irish-language programmes struggled for audiences.
[hist-ire6-s10.9.4] Contemporary view: viewers welcomed the entertainment and new voices, while the church and government saw risks in the programmes.
[hist-ire6-s10.9.5] Later view: historians argue that television both entertained and challenged, and that it speeded up changes in attitudes to religion, sexuality and authority, while acknowledging that other factors such as education, emigration and EEC entry mattered too.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s2: 2. Economic planning and the move to free trade; case study: the First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958–1963
[hist-ire6-s2.0.b] By 1957 the policy of protection that Fianna Fáil had begun in the 1930s was widely seen to have run out of steam.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.0] Tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934 had created many small factories that sold only on the home market, and the home market was too small to support efficient industry.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.1] The Control of Manufactures Acts required that a majority of the shares in a new company be held by Irish citizens, which discouraged foreign firms from setting up in Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.2] Exports were mostly live cattle and farm produce sold to Britain, so the economy depended on one customer.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.3] Net emigration in the 1950s was about 400,000 and the population was falling, while the rest of western Europe was growing fast.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.4] The balance of payments crises of 1951–52 and 1955–56 had been met by deflation, which cut demand without improving productive capacity (see the notes on alternating governments).
[hist-ire6-s2.0.5] Fianna Fáil returned to government in March 1957 with Seán Lemass at Industry and Commerce and James Ryan at Finance, and they were ready to listen to new ideas from officials.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.b] Economic Development was a report by civil servants, led by Whitaker, that changed the direction of policy.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.0] Thomas Kenneth Whitaker became Secretary of the Department of Finance in 1956 at the age of 39, and he gathered a group of younger officials to examine the economy.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.1] In May 1958 he published Economic Development, a study prepared in the Department of Finance and presented under the authority of the government.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.2] It argued that protection had failed to produce growth or jobs and that emigration was a symptom of an economy that was not expanding.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.3] It recommended shifting public investment from social projects such as housing and hospitals towards directly productive investment in agriculture, industry and tourism.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.4] It welcomed foreign capital and technology, a break with the Control of Manufactures Acts, and it argued for export-led growth.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.5] The government accepted its analysis, and in November 1958 it published the Programme for Economic Expansion, usually called the First Programme, which turned the proposals into a policy with targets.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.b] The First Programme ran for five years from 1959 to 1963 and set out an agenda for growth.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.0] Aim: to raise national output and living standards and to halt emigration by creating jobs in export industry and farming.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.1] Target: a modest rise in gross national product of about 2 per cent a year, set deliberately low so that it could be achieved.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.2] The Industrial Development (Encouragement of External Investment) Act 1958 and related measures removed the rule that Irish citizens must hold most of the shares in new firms, opening the way to foreign-owned factories.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.3] Export Profits Tax Relief, first introduced in 1956, was extended so that profits from increased exports were exempt from tax.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.4] Grants were offered through the IDA, which was given a larger role, to firms setting up new plant, with the best terms in the poorer western areas.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.5] The Shannon Free Airport Development Company was set up in 1959 and built an industrial estate at Shannon to attract foreign firms.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.6] In agriculture, An Foras Talúntais (the agricultural research institute) was established in 1958 and farmers were encouraged to raise productivity and exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.7] The state shifted its capital spending towards productive investment, though spending on housing and health rose again as growth took hold.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.b] Judged by its own targets, the First Programme succeeded, and it did so by a wide margin.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.0] Real national output grew by about 4 per cent a year between 1958 and 1963, roughly double the target, and the growth was faster than in the previous decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.1] Exports of manufactured goods rose sharply, and industrial production grew as new, mostly foreign-owned firms began to operate.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.2] Net emigration fell to about 80,000 in 1961–66, from about 200,000 in 1956–61.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.3] The 1966 census recorded a population of about 2.88 million, up from 2.82 million in 1961 and the first rise since the early 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.4] Unemployment fell but remained a serious problem, and total employment grew only modestly because jobs in farming continued to decline.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.5] The balance of payments remained a concern, and by the mid-1960s wage claims and inflation were raising difficulties.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.6] Part of the success lay outside Ireland: the late 1950s and 1960s were years of strong growth in Britain and Europe, which raised the demand for Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.0] The Programme was the start of regular economic planning in Ireland, followed by a Second Programme published in 1963 and a Third Programme for 1969–72.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.1] Contemporaries welcomed it as a sharp break with the pessimism of the 1950s, and the idea of a plan for growth was supported across the parties and by the trade unions and employers.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.2] Those who see it as a turning point stress the end of protection, the openness to foreign investment and the change in public confidence.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.3] Sceptical historians stress that the world economy was booming, that the Programme built on measures begun by the Inter-Party governments, such as Export Profits Tax Relief, and that its targets were modest.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.4] Critics on the left argued that foreign firms took profits out of the country and that the growth was uneven, with the west benefiting least.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.5] Whitaker himself stressed that the Programme was a framework and that its success depended on private investors and on a favourable international climate.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.b] Lemass became Taoiseach in June 1959, when de Valera was elected President, and he carried the Programme through.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.0] Lemass had been a minister since 1932 and a pragmatic modernizer; he was Taoiseach until November 1966, when he was succeeded by Jack Lynch.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.1] His style was practical and businesslike: he built a closer relationship with the trade unions and employers, and the national wage rounds, in which unions, employers and the government bargained over general pay increases, were an example.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.2] The Second Programme for Economic Expansion, published in 1963, aimed for growth of about 4 per cent a year to 1970, but it was abandoned in 1967 after wage rises and a balance of payments deficit.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.3] The government set up the Committee on Industrial Organisation and, from 1963, adaptation councils to help industries prepare for competition from free trade.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.4] Ireland joined the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) in 1967, which committed it to lower tariffs.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.5] He used the new television service, Telefís Éireann, to explain government policy and to project an image of a modern state (see the notes on the impact of RTÉ).
[hist-ire6-s2.5.6] He is also remembered for easing the state's attitude to Northern Ireland, as in his meetings with Terence O'Neill.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.b] Lemass saw that the future of Irish industry lay in competing in open markets, not in protection.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.0] Ireland applied to join the European Economic Community on 31 July 1961, nine days before Britain did, because Britain was the market for most Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.1] President de Gaulle vetoed British entry on 14 January 1963, which ended the negotiations for the time being, although Ireland's application remained on the table (see the notes on the UN and the EEC).
[hist-ire6-s2.6.2] With EEC entry delayed, Lemass turned to a trade agreement with Britain: the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement was signed on 14 December 1965 and took effect on 1 July 1966.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.3] The agreement provided for the removal of tariffs on trade between the two countries over ten years and kept access for Irish farm exports to the British market.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.4] Free trade threatened protected Irish firms in textiles, footwear and other consumer goods, and many small firms closed over the next decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.5] Supporters argued that free trade would bring cheaper inputs, larger markets and new investment, and that protected industries could not survive in the long run.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.6] Lemass visited Terence O'Neill at Stormont on 14 January 1965, and O'Neill visited Dublin on 9 February 1965; these were the first meetings of the leaders of the two parts of Ireland since partition (see the notes on Anglo-Irish relations).
[hist-ire6-s2.7.b] Whitaker was a civil servant, not a politician, and the most influential public official of his generation.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.0] Whitaker was born in 1916 in Rostrevor, Co. Down, and joined the civil service in 1934.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.1] As Secretary of the Department of Finance from 1956 to 1969 he was the main author of Economic Development and the First Programme, working with Lemass and with ministers such as James Ryan.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.2] He was a participant in the change of policy, using the authority of the Department of Finance, which had traditionally resisted spending, to argue for investment.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.3] He later served as Governor of the Central Bank from 1969 to 1976, as a senator from 1977 to 1982 and as chairman of several commissions, including the Constitution Review Group of the 1990s; he died in 2017 at the age of 100.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.4] Contemporaries praised his clarity and courage in questioning protection, though some in his own department doubted how far growth could be planned.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.5] Historians see him as the architect of the new approach, while stressing that Lemass and de Valera supplied the political will, and some argue that his role has been overstated by the idea that planning alone made the economy grow.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.b] Lemass was the minister and then Taoiseach who turned the new thinking into government policy.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.0] Lemass was born in Dublin in 1899, fought in the Rising in 1916 and on the anti-Treaty side in the Civil War, and his brother Noel was killed in 1923.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.1] He was a founder of Fianna Fáil in 1926 and was Minister for Industry and Commerce from 1932 to 1939, from 1941 to 1948, from 1951 to 1954 and from 1957 to 1959, and Minister for Supplies from 1939 to 1945.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.2] As the architect of the protectionist industrial policy of the 1930s, he was able later to argue that protection had served its purpose and should now give way to competition.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.3] As Taoiseach he backed the First Programme, applied to join the EEC and signed the Free Trade Agreement.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.4] Contemporaries saw him as a practical, impatient leader who got things done, though some in Fianna Fáil mistrusted his break with protection.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.5] Historians and biographers judge him a modernizer who changed the tone of Irish politics, but they note that he left rural poverty, urban housing problems and partition unresolved.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.0] Whitaker supplied the analysis and Lemass the political leadership; the Programme needed both, and their partnership is the central reason for the change of direction.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.1] Whitaker was cautious by temperament and was more sceptical than Lemass about the Second Programme's ambitious targets, which were abandoned in 1967.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.2] Achievements of the First Programme: growth about twice the target, falling emigration, rising exports and a shift in confidence.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.3] Limits: total employment barely increased, farming and the west lagged, and the balance of payments remained fragile.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.4] Contemporary view: the Programme was a success, and Fianna Fáil regained an overall majority in the 1965 election.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.5] Later view: historians accept that it was a turning point but debate how much of the growth came from policy and how much from the European boom.

[hist-ire6-s9.0.b] The language was the first official language of the state, but it was losing ground as a community language, and by the 1960s public attitudes were turning against compulsion.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.0] The 1937 Constitution declared Irish the national and first official language, and successive governments backed its revival through schools, the civil service and the Gaeltacht.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.1] Irish was a compulsory subject in school, a pass in Irish was required for the Leaving Certificate and Irish was needed for entry to the civil service.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.2] Gael Linn, founded in 1953, tried to promote the language through popular means, including records, the Amharc Éireann newsreel from 1956 and the feature film Mise Éire in 1959.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.3] The Gaeltacht was in decline in the 1950s because of emigration, and the state set up Gaeltarra Éireann in 1957 to encourage industry in Irish-speaking areas.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.4] The Commission on the Restoration of the Irish Language reported in 1963, and a government White Paper of 1965 accepted the aim of restoration but proposed a more flexible approach.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.5] The Language Freedom Movement, founded in 1966, campaigned against compulsory Irish, arguing that it turned pupils against the language, and it provoked counter-campaigns.
[hist-ire6-s9.0.6] In 1973 the Fine Gael-Labour government, with Richard Burke as Minister for Education, ended the rule that students must pass Irish to gain the Leaving Certificate, and the requirement for civil service entry was also relaxed.

[hist-ire6-s9.1.3] Údarás na Gaeltachta, created in 1980, took over responsibility for economic and social development in the Gaeltacht.

[hist-ire6-s9.2.5] Contemporaries valued him as a bridge between the Gaeltacht and the English-speaking world and as a rare Irish-language writer with a wide readership.
[hist-ire6-s9.2.6] Later critics and his biographer Liam Mac Con Iomaire see him as one of the most accomplished bilingual writers and broadcasters of his generation.

[hist-ire6-s9.4.b] The Second Vatican Council, held in Rome between October 1962 and December 1965, changed Catholic worship and the church's relationship with other Christians and the modern world.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.0] Pope John XXIII opened the council on 11 October 1962 and it closed on 8 December 1965 under Paul VI; Irish bishops, including McQuaid, attended.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.1] The council allowed the Mass to be said in the vernacular instead of Latin, and the first Masses in English in Ireland were celebrated in 1965.

[hist-ire6-s9.4.5] Many Irish Catholics welcomed the changes, but others, mostly older, felt unsettled by the loss of Latin and old devotions.
[hist-ire6-s9.4.6] Vatican II also encouraged people to think for themselves on moral questions, which contributed to a questioning of church authority.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.b] McQuaid, Archbishop of Dublin from 1940 to 1972, was the most powerful churchman in Ireland and was cautious about the changes brought by the council.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.0] McQuaid attended Vatican II and, on returning in December 1965, assured Dubliners that the council would not disturb their faith, a message of reassurance in tune with his conservative outlook.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.1] He implemented liturgical reforms in Dublin, but slowly, and he was wary of ecumenism, which he regarded as risky for Catholic doctrine, and of lay initiatives outside the control of the clergy.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.2] He was a participant in church-state relations, with direct access to ministers, and had been involved in the Mother and Child dispute of 1951 (see the notes on alternating governments).
[hist-ire6-s9.5.3] He was also involved in education, social work and the building of new churches and schools in the Dublin suburbs, and founded charitable bodies such as the Catholic Social Service Conference.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.4] He retired in January 1972 and died in 1973; he was succeeded by Dermot Ryan.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.5] Contemporaries admired his efficiency and devotion to the church, but critics complained of his authoritarian style and his readiness to intervene in politics and in people's lives.
[hist-ire6-s9.5.6] Historians, notably John Cooney in his biography of 1999, portray him as the ruler of Catholic Ireland whose style came to seem out of date; other historians stress his role in building schools and welfare services and argue that the decline of church power was the result of social change as much as of his policies.
[hist-ire6-s9.6.0] A survey in 1973 and 1974 found about nine in ten Catholics attending Mass weekly, so practice remained high even as other signs of change appeared.
[hist-ire6-s9.6.1] Pope Paul VI's encyclical Humanae Vitae in 1968 reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception, and many Irish married Catholics found it difficult to accept.
[hist-ire6-s9.6.2] Vocations to the priesthood and religious life began to fall from the late 1960s, and fewer young people entered seminaries and convents.
[hist-ire6-s9.6.3] Pope John Paul II visited Ireland from 29 September to 1 October 1979, and a mass in the Phoenix Park drew more than a million people, but the visit did not halt the long-term shift.
[hist-ire6-s9.6.4] Secularisation, the decline in the influence of religion on public life and private behaviour, advanced through the 1970s and 1980s, helped by education, television, emigration and contact with Europe.
[hist-ire6-s9.6.5] Pluralism, the idea that the state should respect different beliefs and not enforce one, was at the centre of the 1983 referendum on abortion and the 1986 referendum on divorce, and the results showed that traditional views remained strong (see the notes on the leadership of the 1970s and 1980s).
[hist-ire6-s9.7.0] The Censorship of Publications Act 1929 set up a board that could ban books for being indecent or obscene, and the Act of 1946 added an appeals board; thousands of books were banned, including works by leading Irish writers.
[hist-ire6-s9.7.1] John McGahern's novel The Dark was banned in 1965, and McGahern lost his teaching job after the Archbishop of Dublin's intervention, which became a symbol of the censorship of the time.
[hist-ire6-s9.7.2] The Censorship of Publications Act 1967, introduced by the Minister for Justice, Brian Lenihan, provided that a ban lasts twelve years, after which a book is freed unless banned again, and thousands of titles were released.
[hist-ire6-s9.7.3] Film censorship became more liberal during the 1960s and 1970s, as television and cinema showed material from abroad.
[hist-ire6-s9.7.4] Contraceptives and publications about them were covered by separate laws until the Health (Family Planning) Act 1979 (see the notes on the status of women).
[hist-ire6-s9.7.5] Censorship in broadcasting took a different form, through the powers of the minister over RTÉ (see the notes on the impact of RTÉ).
[hist-ire6-s9.8.0] The number of cars on the road rose several times over between 1960 and 1980, and better roads, bus services and air travel reduced rural isolation.
[hist-ire6-s9.8.1] Telephone services, once poor and slow to expand, were improved and in 1984 Bord Telecom Éireann was set up to run them as a separate state company.
[hist-ire6-s9.8.2] Television reached most households in the 1960s and early 1970s, and RTÉ 2 started in 1978, bringing more programmes and choice; British channels could be received in much of the east and north (see the notes on the impact of RTÉ).
[hist-ire6-s9.8.3] The Irish Times under the editor Douglas Gageby, from 1963, became a forum for new ideas, and magazines such as Magill, founded in 1977, expanded political journalism.
[hist-ire6-s9.8.4] Pirate radio stations grew in the 1970s and 1980s, challenging RTÉ's monopoly, and the Radio and Television Act 1988 licensed independent local radio.
[hist-ire6-s9.8.5] Computers were adopted in business and government from the 1970s, and American firms such as Digital opened plants in Ireland, laying the base for the electronics industry.
[hist-ire6-s9.8.6] These changes exposed people to a wider range of ideas and lifestyles, and helped to break down the isolation on which traditional authority had rested.
[hist-ire6-s9.9.0] Achievements: the Irish language gained broadcasting and institutions, traditional music was revived and reached a world audience, the church embraced liturgical reform and ecumenism, and censorship was relaxed.
[hist-ire6-s9.9.1] Limits: the language continued to decline as a community language, the compulsion debates left many pupils indifferent, and the church's leadership, especially under McQuaid, was slow to adapt.
[hist-ire6-s9.9.2] Contemporary view: traditionalists feared that television and liberal reform threatened Irish identity and Catholic values, while reformers saw the changes as overdue.
[hist-ire6-s9.9.3] Later view: historians see the period as the start of the shift to a more secular and pluralist society, with the church retaining strong influence on law and public life well into the 1980s.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s4: 4. Economic and social challenges of the 1970s and 1980s: Lynch, Haughey and FitzGerald
[hist-ire6-s4.0.b] The decade that began with EEC entry brought a series of external shocks that exposed the weaknesses of the Irish economy.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.0] Ireland imported almost all of its oil, so it was badly exposed when the Arab members of OPEC cut supplies and raised prices after the Yom Kippur War of October 1973.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.1] The oil price rise of 1973 pushed up inflation in the mid-1970s and ended the long post-war boom in Europe, reducing demand for Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.2] The Fine Gael-Labour coalition of 1973 to 1977 under Liam Cosgrave faced recession, rising unemployment and a large balance of payments deficit.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.3] The Iranian Revolution of 1979 caused a second oil shock, which raised fuel costs and prices again, and inflation reached about 20 per cent in 1981.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.4] Ireland joined the European Monetary System in March 1979 and broke its link with sterling for the first time since the 1820s; the link had been a feature of the Irish pound since the 1920s.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.5] Governments responded to rising unemployment by raising public spending and borrowing, since the state hoped that growth would return, and the national debt rose rapidly.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.6] By the early 1980s a large share of tax revenue was being spent on interest on the national debt (see the notes on unemployment and emigration in the 1980s).
[hist-ire6-s4.1.b] Lynch succeeded Lemass and led Fianna Fáil through the first years of the Troubles, then returned to power in 1977.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.0] John Mary Lynch was born in Cork in 1917 and became a famous hurler, winning six All-Ireland medals in a row; he qualified as a barrister before entering the Dáil in 1948.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.1] He was Minister for Education from 1957, for Industry and Commerce from 1959 and for Finance from 1965, and became Taoiseach on 10 November 1966 as a compromise choice between rival candidates.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.2] He won the 1969 general election with a majority of seats but lost power in 1973 to the National Coalition of Fine Gael and Labour.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.3] In August 1969, after violence in Derry and Belfast, he said in a broadcast that the state could not stand by, and the army set up field hospitals near the border, but he ruled out military intervention.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.4] His policy on Northern Ireland was to seek unity by consent and to oppose violence; he condemned internment in August 1971 and recalled the Irish ambassador from London after Bloody Sunday in January 1972.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.5] In December 1972, after bombs in Dublin, his government secured passage of amendments to the Offences Against the State Act to deal with the IRA.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.6] Strengths: he was popular and conciliatory, kept the party together and stopped the state from being drawn into the conflict in the North.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.7] Weaknesses: critics said he was indecisive, that he was slow to remove Haughey and Blaney from his cabinet, and that the 1977 programme he led was financially reckless.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.8] Contemporaries admired his decency and moderate approach but doubted his firmness; historians assess him as a skilful manager of the party, whose time was dominated by the crisis in the North and the Arms Crisis.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.b] The Arms Crisis was the most serious split in Fianna Fáil in the Troubles and tested Lynch's authority.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.0] In April 1970 the Special Branch told Lynch that senior ministers might be involved in a plan to import arms for Northern nationalists.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.1] On 6 May 1970 Lynch dismissed Charles Haughey, Minister for Finance, and Neil Blaney, Minister for Agriculture, and Kevin Boland resigned from the cabinet in protest.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.2] Haughey, Blaney, Captain James Kelly of army intelligence, John Kelly from Belfast and the Belgian businessman Albert Luykx were charged with conspiracy to import arms illegally.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.3] The charge against Blaney was dropped before the trial, and Haughey and the other accused were acquitted in October 1970.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.4] Haughey always maintained that the government had known about the plan; Lynch's version was that he acted as soon as he had evidence; the question of who knew what is still disputed by historians.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.5] The crisis left a lasting division between Lynch's supporters and Haughey's in Fianna Fáil, which continued into the 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.0] Fianna Fáil's manifesto for the June 1977 general election promised to abolish domestic rates and the tax on cars and to create jobs by increasing spending.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.1] The party won 84 of the 148 seats, the largest majority in the history of the state.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.2] The new government cut taxes and increased public spending, financed by borrowing, in the hope of lifting growth; the current budget deficit and the national debt rose sharply.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.3] Growth was strong at first, but inflation, a second oil crisis and the cost of the manifesto turned the position into a crisis by 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.4] Fianna Fáil lost two by-elections in Cork in November 1979, which weakened Lynch's authority, and he announced his resignation in December 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.5] Haughey defeated George Colley for the leadership of the party by 44 votes to 38 and became Taoiseach on 11 December 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.b] Haughey dominated Irish politics in the 1980s and divided opinion more than any other leader.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.0] Charles Haughey was born in 1925 in Castlebar, Co. Mayo, trained as an accountant, married Maureen Lemass, a daughter of Seán Lemass, and became very wealthy; he was Minister for Justice, for Agriculture and, from 1966 to 1970, for Finance.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.1] He was dismissed in the Arms Crisis, spent the 1970s rebuilding his support in the party and returned as Minister for Health and Social Welfare in 1977.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.2] In a television address on 9 January 1980 he told the country that it was living beyond its means and called for sacrifice, but his government's spending and borrowing continued to rise.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.3] His first government lasted until June 1981; he returned briefly in March 1982 and held office again from March 1987.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.4] In 1987 he led a minority government that, with the support of Fine Gael under Alan Dukes (the Tallaght Strategy), cut public spending sharply under his Minister for Finance Ray MacSharry.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.5] The Programme for National Recovery, agreed in October 1987 with the unions, employers and farmers, held pay increases to low levels in return for a commitment to reduce the deficit.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.6] Strengths: charisma, drive, loyalty from followers and a talent for tactics; weaknesses: secrecy, extravagance and the use of power for personal advantage.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.7] Contemporaries divided between those who saw him as a bold nationalist who got things done and those who saw him as a corrupting influence; the tribunals of the 1990s and 2000s later found that he had received large sums from businessmen.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.8] Historians now generally judge him a gifted but flawed leader whose record on the public finances was poor before 1987, and whose later cuts, along with the PNR, helped to prepare recovery in the 1990s.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.b] Three general elections in eighteen months produced weak governments and a sense of crisis.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.0] The June 1981 election produced a Fine Gael-Labour coalition under Garret FitzGerald, which depended on independent TDs.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.1] That government fell in January 1982 when its budget, which proposed VAT on children's clothes and shoes, was defeated, and a new election followed in February 1982.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.2] Haughey returned to office in March 1982 with the support of the Workers' Party, some independents and Tony Gregory, who secured investment for inner-city Dublin in return.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.3] In 1982 a series of scandals followed: phone-tapping of journalists, a leadership challenge against Haughey, and the arrest in August of the murderer Malcolm MacArthur in the apartment of the Attorney General, Patrick Connolly.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.4] A description of these events as grotesque, unbelievable, bizarre and unprecedented was turned into the acronym GUBU by Conor Cruise O'Brien, and it became shorthand for the political culture of the time.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.5] Haughey's government fell in November 1982 when the Workers' Party and Tony Gregory withdrew their support, and the third election in eighteen months brought FitzGerald back to power.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.b] FitzGerald, an economist and Fine Gael leader, headed two coalitions with Labour and tried to modernise the state.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.0] Garret FitzGerald was born in 1926, the son of Desmond FitzGerald, a minister in the first Free State government; he worked as an economist and lecturer before becoming Minister for Foreign Affairs from 1973 to 1977.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.1] He became leader of Fine Gael in 1977 and was Taoiseach from June 1981 to March 1982 and from December 1982 to March 1987.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.2] In 1981 he announced a constitutional crusade to make the Republic more pluralist and thereby more attractive to Northern unionists, with divorce reform and the removal of Catholic influences from the law.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.3] The crusade had limited results: the 1983 referendum inserted a constitutional ban on abortion, the Eighth Amendment, which FitzGerald had not wished to see written in that form, and the 1986 divorce referendum was defeated.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.4] In 1985 his government liberalised the law on contraceptives, allowing sale without prescription to people over 18.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.5] He established the New Ireland Forum in 1983, and negotiated the Anglo-Irish Agreement with Margaret Thatcher in 1985 (see the notes on Anglo-Irish relations).
[hist-ire6-s4.6.6] His economic record was weak: his coalition tried to reduce the deficit but unemployment and the debt kept rising, and Labour left the government in January 1987 over spending cuts.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.7] Strengths: intellect, integrity and a vision of a modern, tolerant Ireland; weaknesses: a tendency to overreach and difficulty in holding a coalition together on economic policy.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.8] Contemporaries admired his honesty and energy but saw him as unworldly; historians credit him with helping to open Irish politics to new ideas, but judge his economic record the weakest part.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.0] The Eighth Amendment, which recognised the right to life of the unborn, was approved on 7 September 1983 by about two to one in a referendum.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.1] It was promoted by anti-abortion groups, and both Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael leaders gave way to the pressure, although FitzGerald had concerns about the wording.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.2] The divorce referendum of 26 June 1986 sought to remove the constitutional ban on divorce and was defeated by about two to one.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.3] The results showed that the church still had great influence over social questions in the 1980s, and that liberalisation had not progressed as FitzGerald had hoped.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.4] Debates about pluralism and the Constitution in the 1980s are an important background to the changes of the 1990s (see the notes on culture and religion).
[hist-ire6-s4.8.0] Unemployment rose from about 7 per cent in 1979 to over 15 per cent by the mid-1980s, with the numbers on the live register approaching a quarter of a million.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.1] Emigration returned on a large scale, with about 200,000 more people leaving than arriving over the decade, many of them well-educated young people heading for Britain, the United States and continental Europe.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.2] The public debt rose to more than 100 per cent of national income by 1987, and interest payments absorbed a large part of tax revenue.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.3] Tax rates rose to pay for services, which discouraged investment and made the burden on workers heavy.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.4] Cuts in spending in 1987 and the PNR brought the deficit down, laying the base for the recovery of the 1990s.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.0] Lynch: kept the state out of the Northern conflict and held the party together, but left the economy in difficulty after 1977.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.1] Haughey: showed drive, and in 1987 presided over a turn towards financial discipline, but his earlier government was marked by deficits and scandal.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.2] FitzGerald: put pluralism and the Northern question on the agenda, but was unable to deliver social change in referendums or economic recovery.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.3] Contemporary view: voters repeatedly turned governments out, partly because each had to impose or defend painful measures.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.4] Later view: historians describe the 1980s as a lost decade for growth, though they see the late 1980s as the beginning of the recovery, and debate how far credit belongs to Haughey, MacSharry, Dukes and the European funds.
