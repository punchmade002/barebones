TARGET hist-ire6-s7: 7. The impact of EEC membership; case study: the impact of the EEC on fisheries
[hist-ire6-s7.0.b] By 1973 Ireland had already spent fifteen years moving away from protection, and it still depended heavily on the British market. Those two facts explain both why entry was sought and why its effects were mixed.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.0] Before 1958 Ireland was protectionist (it shielded home industry with tariffs, taxes on imports) and its economy was stagnant. The First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958 to 1963, and Seán Lemass's policies began to move it towards free trade.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.1] The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement of 1965 cut tariffs on trade with Britain, so protected Irish industries already faced competition before 1973. This is why entry deepened an existing trend rather than starting it.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.2] Ireland depended heavily on the British market for exports, above all farm produce.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.3] Ireland's application of 1961 fell when de Gaulle vetoed British entry in 1963, and he vetoed it again in 1967. Ireland and Britain were finally admitted together.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.4] The 1972 referendum approved entry, with about 83 per cent voting yes, under a Fianna Fáil government led by Jack Lynch.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.5] The economic problems of the 1970s and 1980s (oil price shocks, inflation, unemployment, debt and emigration) are the setting in which the gains and costs of membership have to be judged.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.b] The European Economic Community (EEC) was built as a common market, meaning its members traded with one another as if they were a single economy. Several technical terms recur through this topic, so each is defined here as it is used.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.0] A common market means free trade among its members, a common external tariff (one shared tax on goods from outside), and free movement of goods, workers and capital across member borders.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.1] The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) was the EEC's system of farm support. Intervention meant the EEC buying surplus produce into storage at a guaranteed price, so that market prices could not fall below a floor.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.2] A quota is a limit on how much a farmer or fishing state may produce or land. A derogation is a temporary exemption from a general rule, such as the one Ireland won on fishing limits.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.3] A total allowable catch (TAC) is the whole limit set for a fish species. Relative stability is the rule that divides a TAC into national quotas according to each state's past catches. Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM) is the Irish state fisheries board.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.4] The European Regional Development Fund (ERDF) gave money to poorer regions. A directive is an EEC instruction that member states must turn into their own national law.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.b] On 1 January 1973 Ireland joined the EEC, together with Britain and Denmark, which raised the Community from six members to nine. Membership changed how Ireland traded, who made its laws and where its money came from.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.0] Tariffs on industrial trade with other members were phased out over a transition period ending in 1977, and farm prices were brought into line in stages over about five years. Ireland also adopted the common external tariff on goods from outside the Community.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.1] EEC law took precedence over Irish law in the areas covered by the treaties. The Third Amendment to the Constitution, approved by referendum in May 1972, made this possible by allowing the state to join and giving EEC law effect in Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.2] Irish ministers sat on the Council of Ministers, Irish members sat in the European Parliament, and Patrick Hillery was the first Irish member of the Commission, serving from 1973 to 1976. The other main institution was the European Court of Justice.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.3] Ireland held the presidency of the Council of Ministers for the first time from January to June 1975, which gave a small state a turn at chairing the Community's business.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.4] Membership reduced dependence on Britain. Britain took well over half of Irish exports in the early 1970s and only about a third by the late 1980s, because trade with the continent grew.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.5] Net payments from the EEC budget to Ireland (what it received minus what it paid in) amounted to several per cent of national income by the 1980s. This helped to pay for farm support, training and infrastructure.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.6] Ireland joined the European Monetary System in March 1979, a scheme to keep member currencies stable against one another. Britain stayed out, so the Irish pound broke its link with sterling.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.b] Farming was the sector with most to gain from entry, because the CAP promised farmers prices that Irish producers had never been guaranteed. The gains were large, but they were not shared equally and they brought a new problem of surplus.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.0] The CAP guaranteed minimum prices for products such as beef, milk and cereals, and bought surplus produce into intervention stores. Farmers therefore knew they could sell at a fixed floor price.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.1] Irish farm prices were raised towards EEC levels during the transition. Farm incomes rose sharply in the early years of membership, especially for beef and dairy farmers.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.2] Land values rose, and farmers invested in machinery, buildings and drainage, helped by grants, because higher guaranteed prices made the investment worth making.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.3] The benefits were uneven. Larger and more productive farms in the east and south gained most, while small farms on poor land in the west gained less, because they had less produce to sell at the higher prices.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.4] Guaranteed prices encouraged overproduction, because farmers produced more whatever the demand. In 1984 the EEC introduced milk quotas, limiting output on Irish dairy farms.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.5] The number of people working on the land continued to fall, and agriculture's share of employment and output declined, even though farm incomes had risen.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.b] Alongside farm support, the EEC redistributed money to poorer places and people. Ireland was one of the poorer members, so it received a large share compared with its size.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.0] The European Regional Development Fund was created in 1975, and Ireland, as one of the poorer members, was a leading beneficiary relative to its size.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.1] The European Social Fund paid for training and employment schemes for young and unemployed people. It helped to pay for the training schemes of AnCO, the state training authority.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.2] The funds were spent on roads, water and sewerage schemes, telecommunications and industrial estates, mainly under the direction of the Irish government.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.3] A reform of the structural funds (the regional and social funds together) in 1988, linked to the Single European Act of 1987, roughly doubled the money available. Ireland's programme ran from 1989 to 1993.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.b] Membership opened the EEC market to anyone who produced inside Ireland, but it also stripped away the tariffs that had sheltered older Irish firms. New industry grew while old industry shrank.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.0] American and other foreign firms set up factories in Ireland to sell into the EEC, drawn by Industrial Development Authority (IDA) grants, a young English-speaking workforce, low taxes and access to the EEC market.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.1] From 1981 manufacturing profits were taxed at 10 per cent, which made Ireland even more attractive to firms looking for a low-tax base.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.2] Electronics, pharmaceuticals, chemicals and engineering became leading sectors. Digital, for example, opened a plant in Galway in 1971, before entry, which shows the kind of foreign investment the IDA attracted.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.3] Protected Irish industries such as clothing, textiles and footwear lost their tariff protection, and many firms closed with heavy job losses, because they could not compete with cheaper imports.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.4] Employment in traditional manufacturing fell. The rise in unemployment of the late 1970s and 1980s showed that membership did not by itself solve the economy's weaknesses.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.5] Irish exports became more diversified, and manufactured goods became the larger share of exports, overtaking farm produce in value.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.b] Membership changed Irish law and outlook as well as incomes. Some changes were required by EEC directives, while others coincided with entry.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.0] The equal pay directive of 1975 and the equal treatment (employment) directive of 1976 required changes in Irish law. They reinforced the Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 and the Employment Equality Act 1977.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.1] The marriage bar (the rule forcing women to resign from the civil service on marrying) was lifted in 1973, the year of entry. It followed the Commission on the Status of Women rather than an EEC directive.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.2] A directive on equal treatment in social welfare, adopted in 1978, forced Ireland to change payments to married women in the 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.3] Irish citizens gained the right to live and work in other member states, which eased emigration, and young people had more contact with continental Europe, so attitudes became more outward-looking.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.4] Irish judges were obliged to apply EEC law, and the European Court of Justice became a route for individuals to challenge Irish law.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.5] Critics argued that membership cost Ireland some of its independence. The debate over sovereignty returned in the Crotty case and in the later referendums on European treaties.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.b] Irish fishing was small and under-developed, but it was important to coastal communities and became a sensitive issue in the accession negotiations. Knowing its state before entry explains why the terms mattered and why they were disputed.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.0] Irish waters were rich in fish, but the industry was small. Most boats were old, small and inshore, and the fleet lacked the range to fish far from land.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.1] Fishing employed only a few thousand full-time fishermen, concentrated in coastal counties, and many combined it with farming.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.2] Irish consumers ate little fish outside Fridays, so the home market was small and the industry exported little.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.3] Foreign trawlers fished close to the Irish coast under historic rights (long-established claims to fish there), because the exclusive Irish limit was narrow.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.4] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM), the state fisheries board, was created in 1952 to develop the industry through grants and loans for boats, training and marketing.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.5] In the 1960s and early 1970s BIM helped to build a more modern fleet, including larger boats able to catch herring, whitefish and shellfish.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.6] Fish made up a tiny share of Irish exports and national output before 1973, which is why governments treated the fishing terms as less important than the farming terms.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.b] Fishing rules went through three stages: an equal-access principle in 1970, a temporary Irish exemption in 1973 and the Common Fisheries Policy of 1983, which was to be reviewed after ten years. Keeping the dates and terms straight is the key to this case study.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.0] In 1970 the EEC agreed the principle of equal access for all members' fleets to Community waters, just as the applicant states opened negotiations.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.1] Irish negotiators argued that fishing mattered to poor coastal communities. Ireland obtained a derogation: an exclusive six-mile zone for Irish boats, and a 12-mile zone in parts of the coast, for ten years to the end of 1982.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.2] Other members could fish beyond those limits, and Irish boats gained access to British and Danish waters. Critics said the government gave away too much in order to secure the farming terms, and that fishing was treated as expendable.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.3] In 1976, at The Hague, member states agreed to extend fishing limits to 200 nautical miles from 1 January 1977 and gave Ireland (and Britain) preferential treatment, known as the Hague preferences. This is not the 1969 Hague summit of EEC leaders.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.4] Negotiating a new common policy took until January 1983, when member states agreed on the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP), which replaced the temporary derogation that ended at the close of 1982.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.5] The CFP set annual total allowable catches (TACs) for the main species, shared out as national quotas on the principle of relative stability, meaning shares based on past catches, with some preference for Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.6] It reserved a 12-mile coastal belt for local fishermen, and the arrangements were to be reviewed after ten years.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.7] Irish fishermen and ministers complained that Ireland's quotas were small compared with the value of the fish in its waters.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.b] Grants turned a small inshore industry into a more modern one centred on a few ports, while foreign fleets and quota disputes kept the industry in the news.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.0] With EEC and state grants, BIM financed new and larger vessels, and the Irish fleet grew in the late 1970s and 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.1] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, developed into Ireland's leading fishing port, with a fleet of larger trawlers and pelagic boats (which catch fish such as herring that swim near the surface), processing factories and a growing service industry.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.2] Castletownbere, Co. Cork, became a major whitefish port, with boats landing fish for sale and export.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.3] Other ports, namely Dunmore East (Co. Waterford), Howth (Co. Dublin), Rossaveal (Co. Galway) and Kilmore Quay (Co. Wexford), also received investment in piers and harbours.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.4] Foreign fleets fished in Irish waters, and the Irish Naval Service did fishery protection, inspecting and arresting vessels that broke the rules.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.5] Spain and Portugal joined the EEC on 1 January 1986, after which Spanish vessels had a legal but limited route to Irish waters. Before then Spain was not a member.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.6] Disputes over illegal fishing, over the size of quotas and over the shares given to other states recurred through the 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s7.10.b] Judging the EEC's impact on fishing means weighing real investment against real grievances. The two sides in the argument were the fishermen and the government, each with a case.
[hist-ire6-s7.10.0] Benefits: investment in harbours, boats and processing plants created jobs in some coastal towns, and the 200-mile limit gave Ireland a large area of sea to manage.
[hist-ire6-s7.10.1] Fish processing and exports to continental markets expanded through the 1980s, which widened the market for an industry that had once sold little abroad.
[hist-ire6-s7.10.2] Costs: quotas limited what Irish boats could land, small inshore fishermen often lost out to larger vessels and fewer ports, and young people left declining harbours.
[hist-ire6-s7.10.3] Fishermen's organisations claimed that Ireland received a small share of the fish caught in its own waters, and argued that the government treated fisheries as secondary to farming.
[hist-ire6-s7.10.4] The government's side was that it had obtained the best terms it could, the 12-mile zone and the Hague preferences, and that fishing mattered little beside farming.
[hist-ire6-s7.10.5] Supporters of EEC membership pointed to the new money for the industry. Opponents of entry, such as the Common Market Defence Campaign (1972), warned mainly of lost sovereignty in general.
[hist-ire6-s7.10.6] The fishing industry remained a source of grievance, and its history became a standard example in debates on the costs of membership.
[hist-ire6-s7.11.b] Examiners reward a clear judgement backed by paired points, so weigh what membership achieved against what it failed to do, and say who gained and who lost.
[hist-ire6-s7.11.0] Achievements: secure and better-priced markets for farmers, large transfers from Europe, foreign investment in new industries, equality legislation and a wider international outlook.
[hist-ire6-s7.11.1] Limits: protected firms closed, unemployment rose in the 1980s, small farms and the west benefited least, and the fishing industry gained less than farming.
[hist-ire6-s7.11.2] Contemporary view: supporters said membership was the only way to modernise an economy that relied on protection and emigration; opponents said it threatened sovereignty and traditional industries.
[hist-ire6-s7.11.3] Later view: historians generally accept that membership underpinned the transformation of the economy and society, while stressing that the full benefits came later, from the 1990s, and that farmers and fishermen did not all gain equally.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s6: 6. Increasing international involvement: the UN and the EEC
[hist-ire6-s6.0.b] To see why Ireland's entry to international bodies was slow and then fast, you need three pieces of background: its record in the League of Nations, its neutrality, and the Cold War split of the world into two camps.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.0] Ireland was a member of the League of Nations (the interwar association of states) from 1923 to 1946, and was neutral in the Second World War (1939-45), which Irish people called 'the Emergency'. This is why it was not a stranger to international bodies in 1946.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.1] That wartime neutrality shaped how other states saw Ireland in 1946, which is why it became an obstacle when Ireland first asked to join the United Nations.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.2] The Cold War was the rivalry between the United States-led West and the Soviet bloc. NATO, the Western military alliance, was founded in April 1949. This is why the Soviet Union and the West treated each new UN member as a point scored.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.3] The UN Security Council has five permanent members (the United States, Soviet Union, Britain, France and China), and each holds a veto, meaning one of them can block a decision on its own. This power decided Ireland's first application.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.4] Ireland declined to join NATO in 1949, giving partition (the division of the island) as its reason, and kept to a policy of military neutrality: belonging to no military bloc.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.5] In the 1950s Britain was Ireland's main market for exports, so any British decision about Europe would force an Irish one. This mattered once the EEC appeared.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.b] Ireland wanted to join the UN as soon as it was founded, but the Cold War kept it out for nearly a decade. It was outside the UN until 1955, even though it had been in the League of Nations and other European bodies.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.0] Ireland applied for UN membership in 1946, but the Soviet Union used its veto in the Security Council, citing Ireland's neutrality in the Second World War and the lack of diplomatic relations between the two states.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.1] The deadlock over new members lasted until 1955, when a package deal in the General Assembly (the UN's body of all member states) allowed sixteen states to be admitted together, so neither side had to approve a state it disliked on its own.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.2] Ireland was admitted on 14 December 1955, alongside states as varied as Italy, Spain, Austria, Hungary and Jordan. It was a package, so Ireland came in with states from both sides of the Cold War, such as Italy and Hungary.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.3] Ireland was neutral in the Cold War sense of belonging to no military bloc, but it was a Western, Christian democracy and generally sympathetic to the West on issues of communism.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.4] Membership gave a small state a voice on world affairs, recognition, and a means of showing independence of Britain.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.5] Do not confuse the vetoes: the Soviet Union vetoed Ireland in 1946 in the Security Council, whereas de Gaulle later vetoed Britain's EEC entry, a different veto in a different body.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.b] Frank Aiken, Minister for External Affairs from 1951 to 1954 and from 1957 to 1969, used the UN to pursue a policy not tied to the Western powers. His line was independent, but it was not pro-Soviet.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.0] Aiken was a veteran of the War of Independence and Civil War who had been in de Valera's governments since 1932, and he led the Irish delegation to the General Assembly nearly every year. This is why he had the standing to set policy independently of the Western powers.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.1] From 1957 Ireland would not support the American-led move to block debate on the seat of the People's Republic of China, a position that irritated Washington because it broke with the usual Western line.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.2] In 1959 Ireland and Malaya sponsored a resolution at the UN criticising the suppression of human rights in Tibet, which shows the independent line was not simply pro-communist.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.3] Aiken took an anti-colonial line on some issues, reflecting a small, formerly colonised state's sympathy with peoples seeking independence.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.4] His main contribution was the effort to stop the spread of nuclear weapons (non-proliferation): Irish resolutions were introduced from 1958.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.5] Resolution 1665 of 4 December 1961 called on all states to agree not to transfer or acquire nuclear weapons. It led to negotiations on the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.6] The treaty opened for signature on 1 July 1968, and Ireland was the first state to sign it.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.7] Critics said the Irish line was naive or inconsistent with Ireland's position in the West; supporters said it showed that a small state could influence world opinion.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.b] Peacekeeping means soldiers sent by the UN to separate fighting sides and hold a peace, rather than to fight a war. Irish soldiers served in it from 1958, and the Congo was the first major test.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.0] Irish officers served as UN observers in Lebanon from 1958. This was a different, earlier mission from the later force of 1978. It was a small observer mission, so it is not the same as the larger force that came later.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.1] The Belgian Congo became independent on 30 June 1960 and descended into disorder, with Katanga province declaring secession in July 1960. The UN sent a force, ONUC, and Irish troops joined it in July 1960.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.2] On 8 November 1960 an Irish patrol was ambushed at Niemba in Katanga and nine soldiers were killed, the first big loss of Irish life in UN service.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.3] In September 1961, 155 men of A Company, 35th Battalion, under Commandant Pat Quinlan, were besieged at Jadotville by Katangese forces and foreign mercenaries. After about five days, with ammunition and water short, they surrendered, and were released after some weeks.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.4] About 6,000 Irish soldiers served in the Congo until 1964, and twenty-six lost their lives. The loss of twenty-six lives was heavy for a small army, which is why it counts in judgements on the cost of peacekeeping.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.5] Irish troops joined the UN force in Cyprus (UNFICYP) from 1964, to keep apart the Greek and Turkish Cypriot communities. The two communities were in conflict, so a neutral force was needed between them.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.6] From 1978 an Irish battalion served with the UN Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL), set up after Israel's invasion of southern Lebanon.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.7] Peacekeeping linked Ireland's identity as a neutral state to practical service, and it gave the Defence Forces an important role and good reputation abroad.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.b] Peacekeeping needed a legal basis at home, and it fitted Ireland's neutrality: a neutral state could serve the UN without joining a military alliance.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.0] Ireland refused to join NATO in 1949, with partition as the stated reason, and kept to a policy of military neutrality in the Cold War.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.1] The Defence (Amendment) Act 1960 allowed the government to send Irish contingents abroad on service under the United Nations, which provided the legal basis for the Congo mission.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.2] Peacekeeping allowed a neutral state to take part in world affairs without joining an alliance, and it became a source of national pride. For example, Irish soldiers could serve in the Congo without any alliance commitment.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.3] Opponents of EEC entry raised in 1972 the question of whether membership would affect neutrality, and the government replied that membership did not require Ireland to join a military alliance.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.b] Frederick Boland was a diplomat rather than a minister, and in 1960 his career brought a small, new member state a high honour at the UN.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.0] Frederick Boland, formerly Secretary of the Department of External Affairs (the senior civil servant) and ambassador in London, was Ireland's permanent representative at the UN from 1956.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.1] He was elected President of the fifteenth session of the General Assembly, which began in September 1960, an honour for a state that had joined only five years earlier. This is why the election is read as a sign of the respect Ireland had won.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.2] The session was dominated by the crisis in the Congo and by Cold War tension, and in October 1960 Boland broke his gavel while trying to restore order during a heated exchange involving the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.3] Boland's election showed the respect Ireland had gained, and the session brought more African states into the UN as colonies became independent. Many colonies were becoming independent, so the Assembly was changing in make-up.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.4] Ireland also served on the Security Council in 1962, the first of several terms.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.b] The European Economic Community (EEC) was a common market, and Irish policy-makers soon saw that staying outside it carried risks. A common market is a group of states that trade freely without tariffs among themselves, apply a common tariff to outsiders, and allow free movement of goods, workers and capital.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.0] The Treaty of Rome in 1957 created the EEC. Its six founding states were France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg, with a customs union and a common external tariff (one set of import taxes on goods from outside).
[hist-ire6-s6.6.1] Britain was Ireland's main market, so when it considered joining, Irish ministers feared that British entry would leave Ireland outside a market where its farm exports would face barriers. After 1958 Ireland had also turned from protection to freer trade.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.2] Ireland formally applied on 31 July 1961 under Taoiseach Seán Lemass, and the Dáil, the main parties and farmers' groups supported the application. The support of the Dáil, parties and farmers' groups meant the application had wide backing at home.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.3] Supporters hoped to gain access to a large market for beef and dairy goods and to attract investment, and Lemass argued that membership would help to modernise Irish industry.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.4] French president Charles de Gaulle vetoed British entry on 14 January 1963, which stopped negotiations, although the Irish application remained on the table. A veto by one member was enough, which means one man could halt Britain's entry and Ireland's with it.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.5] To prepare, the government set up bodies to improve industrial competitiveness and signed the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement with Britain in 1965. This was to make Irish firms competitive, because they would face open competition inside the EEC.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.b] After the first veto, Ireland tried again, and only a change of French leadership opened the way to entry. The steps run from veto to summit to treaty to referendum to membership, and they are easy to muddle.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.0] Ireland, Britain, Denmark and Norway applied again in 1967, but de Gaulle vetoed British entry a second time in November 1967. Ireland was again tied to Britain's fate, which is why de Gaulle's veto stopped all of them.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.1] After de Gaulle resigned in 1969, the Hague summit of December 1969 agreed to enlarge the community, and negotiations opened in Luxembourg in 1970. His resignation removed the main obstacle, so enlargement could go ahead.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.2] Patrick Hillery, Minister for External Affairs from 1969 to 1973, led the Irish negotiating team; Ireland won a five-year transitional period for industry (time to adjust before facing full competition) and agreed terms on farm prices.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.3] The transition was so that Irish industry could adjust gradually rather than face full competition at once.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.4] The Treaty of Accession was signed in Brussels on 22 January 1972.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.5] The government held a referendum on 10 May 1972 (the Third Amendment) to amend the Constitution to allow membership; about 83 per cent of those who voted were in favour, on a turnout of about 71 per cent.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.6] A referendum was needed because the Constitution had to be changed before Ireland could join.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.7] Ireland, Britain and Denmark became members on 1 January 1973, with Jack Lynch as Taoiseach; Norway's voters had rejected membership in September 1972.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.8] Hillery became Ireland's first member of the European Commission in 1973.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.b] Ireland's bid to join the EEC rested on economic and political hopes, and it met organised opposition. The size of the final yes vote shows which side persuaded the public.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.0] Economic motives: the failure of protection (shielding home industry with tariffs), the need for markets for beef and dairy goods beyond Britain, and the hope of attracting industry looking for access to a large market. This is why joining looked like the answer to the problems of a protected economy.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.1] Political motives: membership would end the dependence on the British market and give a small state a seat among the major European powers. Relying on one customer left Ireland exposed, so a wider market meant more independence.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.2] The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), which would pay guaranteed prices to farmers, was the strongest attraction for farmers and the Irish Farmers' Association, which supported entry. Guaranteed prices meant farmers could plan with confidence.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.3] Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael and farmers' and employers' organisations supported entry and campaigned for a yes vote.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.4] Opponents, grouped in the Common Market Defence Campaign, argued that entry would cost sovereignty (the state's freedom to make its own laws) and neutrality, force small firms and farms out of business, and give control of the economy to foreign companies.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.5] For example, they feared small firms could not compete with larger European companies.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.6] Labour opposed entry in 1972, as did Sinn Féin and some trade unionists.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.7] Raymond Crotty, an economist, was a prominent critic of the EEC.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.8] The size of the yes vote in 1972 showed how strongly the public linked membership to economic hopes.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.b] In the 1980s a new European treaty raised the question of how far Ireland could hand over power without asking the people. A court case then forced a second EEC referendum, in 1987.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.0] The Single European Act, signed in February 1986, aimed to complete the single market (free movement of goods, services, people and capital) by the end of 1992 and extended qualified majority voting, where a decision passes without every state agreeing, to more decisions.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.1] This is why it was seen as a step beyond a simple trading arrangement.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.2] It also gave a legal basis to European political cooperation, the effort of member states to coordinate foreign policy. Ireland ratified it in 1987, after the Crotty case.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.3] Raymond Crotty, an economist and critic of the EEC, challenged the government's plan to ratify the Act. He lost in the High Court but won in the Supreme Court. He argued that ratifying would give away Irish freedom of action, so the courts were asked to decide.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.4] On 9 April 1987 the Supreme Court ruled that the provisions on political cooperation limited the state's freedom to conduct its foreign policy, so ratification required an amendment to the Constitution.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.5] The Tenth Amendment, approved by referendum on 26 May 1987 by about 70 per cent to 30 per cent, on a low turnout of about 44 per cent, allowed ratification. The amendment was needed because the Supreme Court had ruled the Constitution blocked ratification.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.6] The Crotty judgment established that major transfers of power to the EEC and the EU would require a referendum in Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s6.10.b] Both organisations ended Ireland's isolation, but they did so in different ways. A strong answer sets them side by side and reaches a clear judgement.
[hist-ire6-s6.10.0] The UN gave Ireland international recognition, a platform for independent policies on China, Tibet and nuclear weapons, and a respected peacekeeping role. It also shaped Irish identity and foreign policy.
[hist-ire6-s6.10.1] The EEC had greater practical effect: it gave farmers guaranteed prices and access to a large market, brought regional and social funds, attracted foreign investment and reduced Ireland's dependence on Britain.
[hist-ire6-s6.10.2] Contemporary view: supporters of the UN saw it as the best guarantee of Irish independence and neutrality, because it let a small state act without joining a bloc.
[hist-ire6-s6.10.3] Contemporary view: supporters of the EEC saw it as the only way to modernise the economy and end dependence on Britain, since the home market and protection had failed.
[hist-ire6-s6.10.4] Later view: historians generally judge that EEC membership had the deeper impact on Irish society and economy.
[hist-ire6-s6.10.5] Later view: the UN involvement shaped Ireland's self-image and foreign policy and has remained an important part of its identity.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s2: 2. Economic planning and the move to free trade; case study: the First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958–1963
[hist-ire6-s2.0.b] Four ideas run through this subsection. Together they describe the shift from a closed, state-shielded economy to one that planned for growth and competed abroad.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.0] Economic planning means the government sets targets for several years ahead and directs public investment and incentives towards them, so that growth is pursued on purpose rather than left to chance. The First Programme was Ireland's first plan of this kind.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.1] Protectionism is the use of tariffs (taxes on imported goods) and controls to shield home industry from foreign competition. Fianna Fáil used it from the 1930s in the hope of making Ireland more self-sufficient.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.2] Free trade is the removal of tariffs and other barriers between countries, so that firms sell across borders on equal terms. It is the opposite of protection, and Ireland moved towards it in the 1960s.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.3] The balance of payments is the gap between what a country earns abroad, mainly from exports, and what it spends abroad on imports. A deficit (spending more than it earns) forced governments to cut back at home, so it shaped policy throughout the period.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.b] By 1957 the policy of protection that Fianna Fáil had begun in the 1930s was widely seen to have run out of steam. Small factories, one export customer and mass emigration showed that the economy was not growing.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.0] Tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934 had created many small factories that sold only on the home market. The home market was too small to support efficient industry, so firms stayed small and costly to run.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.1] The Control of Manufactures Acts required that a majority of the shares in a new company (51 per cent) be held by Irish citizens. This discouraged foreign firms from setting up in Ireland, since they could not keep control of their own businesses.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.2] Exports were mostly live cattle and farm produce sold to Britain, so the economy depended on one customer and on farming.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.3] Net emigration in the 1950s was about 400,000 (over the decade 1951–61), and the population fell to about 2.82 million in 1961, while the rest of western Europe was growing fast.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.4] Balance of payments crises in 1951–52 and 1955–56 were met by deflation (cutting spending and demand to reduce imports). This cut demand without improving productive capacity.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.5] Because each crisis was met by deflation and then by recovery, the economy moved in a repeating stop-go pattern.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.6] Fianna Fáil returned to government in March 1957, with Seán Lemass at Industry and Commerce (and as Tánaiste, the deputy head of government) and James Ryan at Finance. They were ready to listen to new ideas from officials.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.b] Economic Development was a study by civil servants, led by Whitaker, that changed the direction of policy. It is the link between the failure of the 1950s and the First Programme.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.0] Thomas Kenneth Whitaker became Secretary of the Department of Finance (its top civil servant) in 1956 at the age of 39. He gathered a group of younger officials to examine the economy, and the department that had long resisted spending changed its attitude.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.1] Economic Development was written in the Department of Finance under Whitaker. It was completed in May 1958 and submitted to Ministers, and it was published to the public later in 1958, around the time of the Programme.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.2] It argued that protection had failed to produce growth or jobs, and that emigration was a symptom of an economy that was not expanding.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.3] It recommended shifting public investment from social projects such as housing and hospitals towards directly productive investment in agriculture, industry and tourism.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.4] It welcomed foreign capital and technology, a break with the Control of Manufactures Acts, and it argued for export-led growth (growth driven by selling more abroad).
[hist-ire6-s2.2.5] The government accepted its analysis, and in November 1958 it published the Programme for Economic Expansion, usually called the First Programme, which turned the proposals into a policy with targets.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.b] The First Programme ran for five years, from 1959 to 1963, and set out an agenda for growth. Its measures aimed to attract investment and to reward firms that sold abroad.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.0] Aim: to raise national output and living standards and to halt emigration, because jobs in export industry and farming would give people a reason to stay.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.1] Target: a rise of about 2 per cent a year in gross national product (the total value of what the country's people produce). It was set deliberately modest, so that it could be achieved and confidence rebuilt.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.2] The Industrial Development (Encouragement of External Investment) Act 1958 ended the rule that Irish citizens must hold most of the shares in new firms, so it opened the way to foreign-owned factories.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.3] Export Profits Tax Relief, which exempted profits from increased exports from tax, was introduced in 1956 by the Second Inter-Party Government. Fianna Fáil kept it and widened it, so it began before the Programme.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.4] The Industrial Development Authority (IDA), set up in 1949, was given a larger role and larger grants in the late 1950s to firms setting up new plant. Incentives favoured less developed areas, such as the west.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.5] The Shannon Free Airport Development Company was set up in 1959 and built an industrial estate at Shannon to attract foreign firms.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.6] In agriculture, An Foras Talúntais (the agricultural research institute) was set up in 1958, and farmers were encouraged to raise productivity and exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.7] The state shifted its capital spending (spending on lasting assets such as roads and equipment) towards productive investment, though spending on housing and health rose again as growth took hold.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.b] Judged by its own targets, the First Programme succeeded, and it did so by a wide margin. The results are best shown as target against outcome, with the limits and the outside help set beside them.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.0] Real national output grew by about 4 per cent a year between 1958 and 1963, about double the 2 per cent target and faster than in the previous decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.1] Exports of manufactured goods rose sharply, and industrial production grew because new, mostly foreign-owned firms began to operate.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.2] Net emigration fell to about 80,000 in 1961–66, from about 200,000 in 1956–61, so the haemorrhage of people had slowed.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.3] The 1966 census recorded a population of about 2.88 million, up from about 2.82 million in 1961. This was the first rise since the early 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.4] Unemployment fell but remained a serious problem, and total employment barely rose because jobs in farming continued to decline.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.5] The balance of payments remained fragile, and by the mid-1960s wage claims and inflation were raising difficulties.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.6] Part of the success lay outside Ireland. The late 1950s and 1960s were years of strong growth in Britain and Europe, which raised the demand for Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.b] Historians agree that the First Programme mattered, but they disagree about how much of the change it caused. The question is whether it was a turning point or part of a longer story.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.0] The Programme was the start of regular economic planning in Ireland, so it was followed by a Second Programme published in 1963 and a Third Programme covering 1969–72.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.1] Contemporaries welcomed it as a sharp break with the pessimism of the 1950s. The idea of a plan for growth was supported across the parties and by the trade unions and employers.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.2] Those who see it as a turning point stress the end of protection, the openness to foreign investment and the change in public confidence.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.3] Sceptical historians stress that the world economy was booming, which lifted Irish exports whatever the policy. They add that the Programme built on measures begun by the Inter-Party governments, such as Export Profits Tax Relief, and that its targets were modest.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.4] Critics on the left argued that foreign firms took profits out of the country, so Ireland kept less of the gain, and that growth was uneven, with the west benefiting least.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.5] Whitaker himself stressed that the Programme was a framework, and that its success depended on private investors and on a favourable international climate.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.b] Lemass became Taoiseach (head of government) in June 1959, when de Valera was elected President, and he carried the Programme through. His record is a mix of modernizing steps and unfinished business.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.0] Lemass became Taoiseach on 23 June 1959 and held the office until 10 November 1966, when Jack Lynch succeeded him. He had been a minister since 1932 and was a pragmatic modernizer.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.1] His style was practical, businesslike and impatient for change, in contrast to de Valera, the older leader he succeeded.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.2] He built closer links with the trade unions and employers, called social partnership, so that pay could be settled by agreement. The national wage rounds, in which unions, employers and the government bargained over general pay increases, began in 1964.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.3] The government set up the Committee on Industrial Organisation in the early 1960s and, from 1963, adaptation councils to help industries prepare for competition from free trade, since protected firms would soon lose their tariff shelter.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.4] The Second Programme for Economic Expansion, published in 1963, aimed for growth of about 4 per cent a year to 1970, but it was abandoned in 1967 after wage rises and a balance of payments deficit. A Third Programme followed for 1969–72.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.5] He used the new television service, Telefís Éireann, to explain government policy and to project an image of a modern state. There is more on this in the RTÉ case study.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.6] He is also remembered for easing the state's attitude to Northern Ireland, as in his 1965 meetings with Terence O'Neill, who was Prime Minister of Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.b] Lemass saw that the future of Irish industry lay in competing in open markets, not in protection. Britain was both the main customer and the key to any trade deal.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.0] Ireland applied to join the European Economic Community (EEC, the common market of western European states) on 31 July 1961, nine days before Britain did, because Britain was the market for most Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.1] President de Gaulle of France vetoed British entry on 14 January 1963, which ended the negotiations for the time being. Ireland's application remained on the table.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.2] With EEC entry delayed, Lemass turned to a trade agreement with Britain. The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement was signed on 14 December 1965 and took effect on 1 July 1966.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.3] The agreement removed tariffs on trade between the two countries over ten years and kept access for Irish farm exports to the British market.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.4] Free trade threatened protected Irish firms in textiles, footwear and other consumer goods, and many small firms closed over the next decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.5] Supporters argued that free trade would bring cheaper inputs, larger markets and new investment, and that protected industries could not survive in the long run.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.6] Ireland joined the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT, an international agreement to lower tariffs) in 1967, the year after Lemass left office. It was the end point of the move to lower tariffs, not an act of his government.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.7] Lemass visited Terence O'Neill at Stormont on 14 January 1965, and O'Neill visited Dublin on 9 February 1965. These were the first meetings between the heads of the two governments since the 1920s.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.b] Whitaker was a civil servant, not a politician, and the most influential public official of his generation. He supplied the ideas behind the First Programme.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.0] Whitaker was born in 1916 in Rostrevor, Co. Down, and joined the civil service in 1934.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.1] As Secretary of the Department of Finance from 1956 to 1969 he was the main author of Economic Development and the First Programme, working with Lemass and with ministers such as James Ryan.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.2] He shaped events by using the authority of the Department of Finance, which had traditionally resisted spending, to argue for investment. This carried weight because Finance had always been the department that said no.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.3] He later served as Governor of the Central Bank from 1969 to 1976, as a senator from 1977 to 1982 and as chairman of several commissions, including the Constitution Review Group of the mid-1990s. He died in 2017 at the age of 100.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.4] As author of the report, he was a participant in events. He later said that he and his colleagues did the work without extra pay.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.5] Contemporaries praised his clarity and courage in questioning protection, though some in his own department doubted how far growth could be planned.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.6] Historians see him as the architect of the new approach, while stressing that Lemass and de Valera supplied the political will. Some argue that his role has been overstated by the idea that planning alone made the economy grow.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.b] Lemass was the minister and then Taoiseach who turned the new thinking into government policy. He is unusual because he built the system of protection and then helped to end it.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.0] Lemass was born in Dublin in 1899, fought in the Rising in 1916 and on the anti-Treaty side in the Civil War, and his brother Noel was killed in 1923.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.1] He co-founded Fianna Fáil in 1926. He was Minister for Industry and Commerce from 1932 to 1939, 1941 to 1948, 1951 to 1954 and 1957 to 1959, and Minister for Supplies from 1939 to 1945.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.2] As the architect of the protectionist industrial policy of the 1930s, he was able later to argue that protection had served its purpose and should now give way to competition. The failure of protection had convinced a former protectionist.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.3] As Taoiseach he backed the First Programme because he now believed in growth through exports, applied to join the EEC and signed the Free Trade Agreement.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.4] Contemporaries saw him as a practical, impatient leader who got things done, so he was respected for results, though some in Fianna Fáil mistrusted his break with protection.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.5] Historians and biographers judge him a modernizer who changed the tone of Irish politics, but they note that he left rural poverty, urban housing problems and partition unresolved.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.b] The change of direction needed both an adviser with ideas and a leader with the will to act. The achievements and limits below give the material for a balanced judgement.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.0] Whitaker supplied the analysis and Lemass the political leadership. The Programme needed both, and their partnership is the central reason for the change of direction.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.1] Compared directly, Whitaker was a civil servant and Lemass a politician. Whitaker was cautious and analytical by temperament, and Lemass was driven and impatient for results.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.2] Achievements of the First Programme: growth about twice the target, so it beat its aim, falling emigration, rising exports, a shift in confidence and the habit of planning.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.3] Limits: total employment barely increased because farm jobs kept falling, farming and the west lagged, the balance of payments remained fragile and wage claims raised inflation. The Second Programme was abandoned in 1967, and partition and rural poverty were left unresolved.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.4] Contemporary view: the Programme was seen as a success, and Fianna Fáil did well in the 1965 election and stayed in office.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.5] Later view: historians accept that it was a turning point but debate how much of the growth came from policy and how much from the European boom.
