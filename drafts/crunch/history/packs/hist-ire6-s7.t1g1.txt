[hist-ire6-s7.0.b] From 1 January 1973 Ireland was part of a common market with free movement of goods and a common external tariff, and a member of its institutions.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.0] Tariffs on trade with other member states were phased out over a transitional period that ended in 1977, and Ireland adopted the common external tariff on goods from outside the community.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.1] EEC law took precedence over Irish law in the areas covered by the treaties, and the Third Amendment to the Constitution, approved in 1972, made this possible.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.2] Irish ministers sat on the Council of Ministers, Irish members sat in the European Parliament, and Patrick Hillery served as the first Irish member of the Commission from 1973.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.3] Ireland held the presidency of the Council of Ministers for the first time in the first half of 1975.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.4] Membership reduced dependence on Britain: the share of Irish exports going to Britain fell as trade with the continent grew.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.5] Net payments from the EEC budget to Ireland amounted to several per cent of national income by the 1980s, which helped to pay for farm support, training and infrastructure.
[hist-ire6-s7.0.6] Membership was the context for Ireland's decision in March 1979 to join the European Monetary System, which ended the link between the Irish pound and sterling.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.0] The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) guaranteed minimum prices for products such as beef, milk and cereals, and bought surplus produce into intervention stores.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.1] Irish farm prices were raised towards EEC levels during the transition, and farm incomes rose sharply in the early years of membership, especially for beef and dairy farmers.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.2] Land values rose and farmers invested in machinery, buildings and drainage, helped by grants.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.3] The benefits were uneven: larger and more productive farms in the east and south gained most, while small farms on poor land in the west gained less.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.4] Guaranteed prices encouraged overproduction, and in 1984 the EEC introduced milk quotas, limiting output on Irish dairy farms.
[hist-ire6-s7.1.5] The number of people working on the land continued to fall, and agriculture's share of employment and output declined.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.0] The European Regional Development Fund was created in 1975, partly because Britain, Ireland and Italy pressed for help for poorer regions, and Ireland received a considerable share of it.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.1] The European Social Fund paid for training and employment schemes for young and unemployed people.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.2] Funds were spent on roads, water and sewerage schemes, telecommunications and industrial estates, mainly under the direction of the Irish government.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.3] A reform of the structural funds in 1988, linked to the Single European Act, doubled the money available, with Ireland as a major beneficiary of the programmes for 1989 to 1993.
[hist-ire6-s7.2.4] The Social Fund helped to pay for the training schemes of AnCO, the state training authority.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.0] American and other foreign firms set up factories in Ireland to sell into the EEC, drawn by IDA grants, a young English-speaking workforce and low taxes; in 1981 a 10 per cent rate of corporation tax on manufacturing profits was introduced.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.1] Electronics, pharmaceuticals, chemicals and engineering became leading sectors, with firms such as Digital in Galway and, later, Intel at Leixlip.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.2] Protected Irish industries such as clothing, textiles and footwear lost their tariff protection and many firms closed, with heavy job losses.
[hist-ire6-s7.3.3] Employment in traditional manufacturing fell, and the rise in unemployment of the late 1970s and 1980s showed that membership did not by itself solve the economy's weaknesses (see the notes on the economic challenges of the 1970s and 1980s).
[hist-ire6-s7.3.4] Irish exports became more diversified, with manufactured goods overtaking farm produce in the value of exports.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.0] EEC directives on equal pay (1975) and equal treatment in employment (1976) required changes in Irish law, and they reinforced the Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 and the Employment Equality Act 1977 (see the notes on the status of women).
[hist-ire6-s7.4.1] The marriage bar in the public service was lifted in 1973, and a directive on equal treatment in social welfare, adopted in 1978, forced changes to payments to married women in the 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.2] Irish citizens gained the right to live and work in other member states, which eased emigration, and young people had more contact with continental Europe.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.3] Irish judges were obliged to apply EEC law, and the European Court of Justice became a route for individuals to challenge Irish law.
[hist-ire6-s7.4.4] Critics argued that membership cost Ireland some of its independence, and the debate over sovereignty returned in the Crotty case and the later referendums on European treaties.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.b] Irish fishing was small and under-developed, but it was important to coastal communities and became a sensitive issue in the accession negotiations.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.0] Irish waters were rich in fish, but the industry was small: most boats were old, small and inshore, and the fleet lacked the capacity to fish far from land.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.1] Fishing employed only a few thousand full-time fishermen, concentrated in Donegal, Kerry, Cork, Galway and parts of the east coast, and many combined it with farming.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.2] Irish consumers ate little fish outside Fridays, and the home market was small, so the industry exported little.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.3] Foreign trawlers from France, Spain, Belgium, the Netherlands and Britain fished close to the Irish coast under historic rights, because the exclusive Irish limit was narrow.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.4] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM), the state fisheries board, was created in 1952 to develop the industry through grants and loans for boats, training and marketing.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.5] In the 1960s and early 1970s BIM helped to build a more modern fleet, including larger boats able to catch herring, whitefish and shellfish.
[hist-ire6-s7.5.6] Fish made up a tiny share of Irish exports and national output before 1973, which is why governments treated the fishing terms as less important than the farming terms.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.0] The EEC had agreed a principle of equal access for all member states' fleets to community waters in 1970, just before the applicant states opened negotiations.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.1] Irish negotiators argued that fishing was important to poor coastal communities, and Ireland obtained a derogation: an exclusive six-mile zone for Irish boats and a 12-mile zone in parts of the coast, for ten years to the end of 1982.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.2] Critics said the government gave away too much in order to secure the farming terms, and that the fishing industry was treated as expendable.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.3] In 1976, at The Hague, member states agreed to extend their fishing limits to 200 nautical miles from 1977 and recognised that Ireland needed preferential treatment.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.4] Negotiating a new common policy took until January 1983, when member states agreed on a Common Fisheries Policy (CFP).
[hist-ire6-s7.6.5] The CFP set annual total allowable catches (TACs) for the main species, divided into national quotas according to the principle of relative stability, based on past catches, with some preference for Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.6] It kept a 12-mile coastal belt reserved for local fishermen, and the arrangements were to be reviewed after ten years.
[hist-ire6-s7.6.7] Irish fishermen and ministers complained that Ireland's quotas were small compared with the value of the fish in its waters.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.0] With EEC and state grants, BIM financed new and larger vessels, and the Irish fleet grew in the late 1970s and 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.1] Killybegs, Co. Donegal, developed into Ireland's leading fishing port, with a fleet of larger trawlers and pelagic boats and with processing factories and a growing service industry.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.2] Castletownbere, Co. Cork, became a major whitefish port, with boats landing fish for sale and export.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.3] Other ports such as Dunmore East, Howth, Rossaveel and Kilmore Quay also received investment in piers and harbours.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.4] Foreign fleets from Spain, France and elsewhere fished in Irish waters, and the Irish Naval Service was used to inspect and arrest vessels that broke the rules.
[hist-ire6-s7.7.5] Disputes over illegal fishing, over the size of quotas and over the shares given to other states recurred through the 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.0] Benefits: investment in harbours, boats and processing plants created jobs in some coastal towns, and the 200-mile limit gave Ireland a large area of sea to manage.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.1] Costs: quotas limited what Irish boats could land, small inshore fishermen often lost out to larger vessels and fewer ports, and young people left declining harbours.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.2] Fishermen's organisations claimed that Ireland received a small share of the fish caught in its own waters, and argued that the government treated fisheries as secondary to farming.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.3] The government replied that the 12-mile zone and the preferences obtained at The Hague were the best terms available and that Ireland, as a small state, could not manage the waters alone.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.4] Supporters of EEC membership pointed to the new money for the industry; critics, including the Common Market Defence Campaign, pointed to the loss of control over a national resource.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.5] The fishing industry remained a source of grievance, and its history became a standard example in debates on the costs of membership.
[hist-ire6-s7.8.6] Fish processing and exports to continental markets expanded through the 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.0] Achievements: secure and better-priced markets for farmers, large transfers from Europe, foreign investment in new industries, equality legislation and a wider international outlook.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.1] Limits: protected firms closed, unemployment rose in the 1980s, small farms and the west benefited least, and the fishing industry gained less than farming.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.2] Contemporary view: supporters said membership was the only way to modernise an economy that relied on protection and emigration; opponents said it threatened sovereignty and traditional industries.
[hist-ire6-s7.9.3] Later view: historians generally accept that membership underpinned the transformation of the economy and society, while stressing that the full benefits came later, from the 1990s, and that farmers and fishermen did not all gain equally.

[hist-ire6-s6.0.b] Ireland had been outside international organisations since the League of Nations and wished to join the UN as soon as it was founded.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.0] Ireland applied for UN membership in 1946, but the Soviet Union used its veto in the Security Council, citing Ireland's neutrality in the Second World War and the lack of diplomatic relations between the two states.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.1] The deadlock over new members lasted until 1955, when a package deal in the General Assembly allowed sixteen states to be admitted together.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.2] Ireland was admitted on 14 December 1955, alongside states as varied as Italy, Spain, Austria, Hungary and Jordan.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.3] Ireland was neutral in the Cold War sense of belonging to no military bloc, but it was a Western, Christian democracy and generally sympathetic to the West on issues of communism.
[hist-ire6-s6.0.4] Membership gave a small state a voice on world affairs and a means of showing independence of Britain.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.b] Frank Aiken, Minister for External Affairs from 1951 to 1954 and from 1957 to 1969, used the UN to pursue a policy not tied to the Western powers.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.0] Aiken was a veteran of the War of Independence and Civil War who had been in de Valera's governments since 1932, and he led the Irish delegation to the General Assembly nearly every year.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.1] From 1957 Ireland would not support the American-led move to block debate on the seat of the People's Republic of China, a position that irritated Washington.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.2] In 1959 Ireland and Malaya sponsored a resolution at the UN criticising the Chinese suppression of human rights in Tibet.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.3] Ireland backed discussion of Algeria's self-determination and later opposed apartheid in South Africa, which reflected an anti-colonial approach.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.4] Aiken's main contribution was in the effort to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons: Irish resolutions were introduced from 1958, and Resolution 1665 of 4 December 1961 called on all states to agree not to transfer or acquire nuclear weapons.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.5] That resolution led to negotiations on the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, which opened for signature in 1968, and Ireland was the first state to sign it.
[hist-ire6-s6.1.6] Critics said the Irish line was naive or inconsistent with Ireland's position in the West; supporters said it showed that a small state could influence world opinion.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.b] Irish soldiers served in UN peacekeeping from 1958, and the Congo was the first major test.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.0] Irish officers served as UN observers in Lebanon from 1958, and Irish troops joined the UN force in the Congo (ONUC) in July 1960, soon after the Congo gained independence from Belgium and fell into disorder.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.1] On 8 November 1960 an Irish patrol was ambushed at Niemba in Katanga, and nine soldiers were killed, the first big loss of Irish life in UN service.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.2] In September 1961, 155 men of A Company, 35th Battalion, under Commandant Pat Quinlan, were besieged at Jadotville by Katangese forces and foreign mercenaries; after five days, with ammunition and water short, they surrendered, and were held for weeks before being released (see the notes on the decolonisation of Africa).
[hist-ire6-s6.2.3] About 6,000 Irish soldiers served in the Congo until 1964, and twenty-six lost their lives.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.4] Irish troops joined the UN force in Cyprus (UNFICYP) from 1964, to keep apart the Greek and Turkish Cypriot communities.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.5] From 1978 an Irish battalion served with the UN Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL), set up after Israel's invasion of southern Lebanon.
[hist-ire6-s6.2.6] Peacekeeping linked Ireland's identity as a neutral state to practical service, and it gave the Defence Forces an important role and good reputation abroad.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.0] Frederick Boland, formerly Secretary of the Department of External Affairs and ambassador in London, was Ireland's permanent representative at the UN from 1956.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.1] He was elected President of the fifteenth session of the General Assembly, which began in September 1960, an honour for a small state that had joined only five years earlier.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.2] The session was dominated by the crisis in the Congo and by Cold War tension, and in October 1960 Boland broke his gavel while trying to restore order during a heated exchange involving the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.3] Boland's election showed the respect Ireland had gained, and the session brought more African states into the UN as colonies became independent.
[hist-ire6-s6.3.4] Ireland also served on the Security Council in 1962, the first of several terms.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.b] The Treaty of Rome in 1957 created a common market of six states, and Irish policy-makers soon saw that staying outside it carried risks.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.0] The European Economic Community was founded by France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg in 1957, with a customs union and a common external tariff.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.1] When Britain, Ireland's main market, applied to join, Irish ministers feared that a British entry would leave Ireland outside a market where its farm exports would face barriers.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.2] Ireland formally applied on 31 July 1961, and the Dáil and the main parties and farmers' groups supported the application.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.3] Supporters hoped to gain access to a large market for beef and dairy goods and to attract investment, and Lemass argued that membership would help to modernise Irish industry.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.4] French president Charles de Gaulle vetoed British entry on 14 January 1963, which stopped negotiations, although the Irish application remained on the table.
[hist-ire6-s6.4.5] To prepare, the government set up bodies to improve industrial competitiveness and signed the free trade agreement with Britain in 1965 (see the notes on economic planning).
[hist-ire6-s6.5.0] Ireland, Britain, Denmark and Norway applied again in 1967, but de Gaulle vetoed British entry a second time in November 1967.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.1] After de Gaulle resigned in 1969, the Hague summit of December 1969 agreed to enlarge the community, and negotiations opened in Luxembourg in 1970.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.2] Patrick Hillery, as Minister for External Affairs, led the Irish negotiating team; Ireland won a five-year transitional period for industry and agreed terms on farm prices and a limited fisheries arrangement.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.3] The Treaty of Accession was signed in Brussels on 22 January 1972.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.4] The government held a referendum on 10 May 1972 to amend the Constitution to allow membership; about 83 per cent of those who voted were in favour, on a turnout of about 71 per cent.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.5] Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael and farmers' organisations supported entry, while Labour, Sinn Féin and some trade unionists opposed it on the grounds of loss of sovereignty and neutrality.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.6] Ireland, Britain and Denmark became members on 1 January 1973; Norway's voters had rejected membership in September 1972.
[hist-ire6-s6.5.7] Hillery became Ireland's first member of the European Commission in 1973.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.0] The Single European Act, signed in February 1986, aimed to complete the single market by the end of 1992 and extended qualified majority voting to more decisions.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.1] It also gave a legal basis to European political cooperation, the effort of member states to coordinate foreign policy.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.2] Raymond Crotty, an economist, challenged the government's plan to ratify the Act, and the High Court rejected his case on 12 February 1987.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.3] On 9 April 1987 the Supreme Court ruled that the provisions on political cooperation limited the state's freedom to conduct its foreign policy, and that ratification therefore required an amendment to the Constitution.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.4] The Tenth Amendment, approved by referendum on 26 May 1987 by about 70 per cent to 30 per cent, on a low turnout of about 44 per cent, allowed ratification.
[hist-ire6-s6.6.5] The Crotty judgment established that major transfers of power to the EEC and the EU would require a referendum in Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.0] Ireland refused to join NATO in 1949, with partition as the stated reason, and kept to a policy of military neutrality in the Cold War.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.1] The Defence (Amendment) Act 1960 allowed the government to send Irish contingents abroad on service under the United Nations, which provided the legal basis for the Congo mission.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.2] Irish troops served only under UN command and with the approval of the Dáil, a condition that later became formalised in policy.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.3] Peacekeeping allowed a neutral state to take part in world affairs without joining an alliance, and it became a source of national pride.
[hist-ire6-s6.7.4] The question of whether EEC membership would affect neutrality was raised by opponents in 1972, and the government replied that membership did not require Ireland to join a military alliance.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.0] Economic motives: the failure of protection, the need for markets for beef and dairy goods beyond Britain, and the hope of attracting industry looking for access to a large market.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.1] Political motives: membership would end the dependence on the British market and give a small state a seat among the major European powers.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.2] The Common Agricultural Policy, which would pay guaranteed prices, was the strongest attraction for farmers and the Irish Farmers' Association.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.3] Opponents, grouped in the Common Market Defence Campaign, argued that entry would cost sovereignty and neutrality, force small firms and farms out of business and give control of the economy to foreign companies.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.4] Labour opposed entry in 1972, as did Sinn Féin and parts of the trade union movement, but the main parties and employers' and farmers' organisations campaigned for a yes vote.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.5] Raymond Crotty, who later challenged the Single European Act, was a prominent economist critic of the EEC.
[hist-ire6-s6.8.6] The size of the yes vote in 1972 showed how strongly the public linked membership to economic hopes.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.0] The UN gave Ireland international recognition, a platform for independent policies on China, nuclear weapons and decolonisation, and a respected peacekeeping role.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.1] The EEC had greater practical effect: it gave farmers guaranteed prices and access to a large market, brought regional and social funds, attracted foreign investment and reduced Ireland's dependence on Britain.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.2] Contemporary view: supporters of the UN saw it as the best guarantee of Irish independence and neutrality; supporters of the EEC saw it as the only way to modernise the economy and end dependence on Britain.
[hist-ire6-s6.9.3] Later view: historians generally judge that EEC membership had the deeper impact on Irish society and economy, while the UN involvement shaped Ireland's self-image and foreign policy and has remained an important part of its identity.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s2: 2. Economic planning and the move to free trade; case study: the First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958–1963
[hist-ire6-s2.0.b] By 1957 the policy of protection that Fianna Fáil had begun in the 1930s was widely seen to have run out of steam.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.0] Tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934 had created many small factories that sold only on the home market, and the home market was too small to support efficient industry.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.1] The Control of Manufactures Acts required that a majority of the shares in a new company be held by Irish citizens, which discouraged foreign firms from setting up in Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.2] Exports were mostly live cattle and farm produce sold to Britain, so the economy depended on one customer.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.3] Net emigration in the 1950s was about 400,000 and the population was falling, while the rest of western Europe was growing fast.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.4] The balance of payments crises of 1951–52 and 1955–56 had been met by deflation, which cut demand without improving productive capacity (see the notes on alternating governments).
[hist-ire6-s2.0.5] Fianna Fáil returned to government in March 1957 with Seán Lemass at Industry and Commerce and James Ryan at Finance, and they were ready to listen to new ideas from officials.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.b] Economic Development was a report by civil servants, led by Whitaker, that changed the direction of policy.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.0] Thomas Kenneth Whitaker became Secretary of the Department of Finance in 1956 at the age of 39, and he gathered a group of younger officials to examine the economy.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.1] In May 1958 he published Economic Development, a study prepared in the Department of Finance and presented under the authority of the government.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.2] It argued that protection had failed to produce growth or jobs and that emigration was a symptom of an economy that was not expanding.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.3] It recommended shifting public investment from social projects such as housing and hospitals towards directly productive investment in agriculture, industry and tourism.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.4] It welcomed foreign capital and technology, a break with the Control of Manufactures Acts, and it argued for export-led growth.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.5] The government accepted its analysis, and in November 1958 it published the Programme for Economic Expansion, usually called the First Programme, which turned the proposals into a policy with targets.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.b] The First Programme ran for five years from 1959 to 1963 and set out an agenda for growth.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.0] Aim: to raise national output and living standards and to halt emigration by creating jobs in export industry and farming.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.1] Target: a modest rise in gross national product of about 2 per cent a year, set deliberately low so that it could be achieved.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.2] The Industrial Development (Encouragement of External Investment) Act 1958 and related measures removed the rule that Irish citizens must hold most of the shares in new firms, opening the way to foreign-owned factories.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.3] Export Profits Tax Relief, first introduced in 1956, was extended so that profits from increased exports were exempt from tax.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.4] Grants were offered through the IDA, which was given a larger role, to firms setting up new plant, with the best terms in the poorer western areas.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.5] The Shannon Free Airport Development Company was set up in 1959 and built an industrial estate at Shannon to attract foreign firms.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.6] In agriculture, An Foras Talúntais (the agricultural research institute) was established in 1958 and farmers were encouraged to raise productivity and exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.7] The state shifted its capital spending towards productive investment, though spending on housing and health rose again as growth took hold.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.b] Judged by its own targets, the First Programme succeeded, and it did so by a wide margin.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.0] Real national output grew by about 4 per cent a year between 1958 and 1963, roughly double the target, and the growth was faster than in the previous decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.1] Exports of manufactured goods rose sharply, and industrial production grew as new, mostly foreign-owned firms began to operate.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.2] Net emigration fell to about 80,000 in 1961–66, from about 200,000 in 1956–61.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.3] The 1966 census recorded a population of about 2.88 million, up from 2.82 million in 1961 and the first rise since the early 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.4] Unemployment fell but remained a serious problem, and total employment grew only modestly because jobs in farming continued to decline.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.5] The balance of payments remained a concern, and by the mid-1960s wage claims and inflation were raising difficulties.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.6] Part of the success lay outside Ireland: the late 1950s and 1960s were years of strong growth in Britain and Europe, which raised the demand for Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.0] The Programme was the start of regular economic planning in Ireland, followed by a Second Programme published in 1963 and a Third Programme for 1969–72.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.1] Contemporaries welcomed it as a sharp break with the pessimism of the 1950s, and the idea of a plan for growth was supported across the parties and by the trade unions and employers.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.2] Those who see it as a turning point stress the end of protection, the openness to foreign investment and the change in public confidence.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.3] Sceptical historians stress that the world economy was booming, that the Programme built on measures begun by the Inter-Party governments, such as Export Profits Tax Relief, and that its targets were modest.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.4] Critics on the left argued that foreign firms took profits out of the country and that the growth was uneven, with the west benefiting least.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.5] Whitaker himself stressed that the Programme was a framework and that its success depended on private investors and on a favourable international climate.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.b] Lemass became Taoiseach in June 1959, when de Valera was elected President, and he carried the Programme through.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.0] Lemass had been a minister since 1932 and a pragmatic modernizer; he was Taoiseach until November 1966, when he was succeeded by Jack Lynch.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.1] His style was practical and businesslike: he built a closer relationship with the trade unions and employers, and the national wage rounds, in which unions, employers and the government bargained over general pay increases, were an example.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.2] The Second Programme for Economic Expansion, published in 1963, aimed for growth of about 4 per cent a year to 1970, but it was abandoned in 1967 after wage rises and a balance of payments deficit.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.3] The government set up the Committee on Industrial Organisation and, from 1963, adaptation councils to help industries prepare for competition from free trade.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.4] Ireland joined the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) in 1967, which committed it to lower tariffs.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.5] He used the new television service, Telefís Éireann, to explain government policy and to project an image of a modern state (see the notes on the impact of RTÉ).
[hist-ire6-s2.5.6] He is also remembered for easing the state's attitude to Northern Ireland, as in his meetings with Terence O'Neill.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.b] Lemass saw that the future of Irish industry lay in competing in open markets, not in protection.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.0] Ireland applied to join the European Economic Community on 31 July 1961, nine days before Britain did, because Britain was the market for most Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.1] President de Gaulle vetoed British entry on 14 January 1963, which ended the negotiations for the time being, although Ireland's application remained on the table (see the notes on the UN and the EEC).
[hist-ire6-s2.6.2] With EEC entry delayed, Lemass turned to a trade agreement with Britain: the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement was signed on 14 December 1965 and took effect on 1 July 1966.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.3] The agreement provided for the removal of tariffs on trade between the two countries over ten years and kept access for Irish farm exports to the British market.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.4] Free trade threatened protected Irish firms in textiles, footwear and other consumer goods, and many small firms closed over the next decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.5] Supporters argued that free trade would bring cheaper inputs, larger markets and new investment, and that protected industries could not survive in the long run.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.6] Lemass visited Terence O'Neill at Stormont on 14 January 1965, and O'Neill visited Dublin on 9 February 1965; these were the first meetings of the leaders of the two parts of Ireland since partition (see the notes on Anglo-Irish relations).
[hist-ire6-s2.7.b] Whitaker was a civil servant, not a politician, and the most influential public official of his generation.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.0] Whitaker was born in 1916 in Rostrevor, Co. Down, and joined the civil service in 1934.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.1] As Secretary of the Department of Finance from 1956 to 1969 he was the main author of Economic Development and the First Programme, working with Lemass and with ministers such as James Ryan.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.2] He was a participant in the change of policy, using the authority of the Department of Finance, which had traditionally resisted spending, to argue for investment.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.3] He later served as Governor of the Central Bank from 1969 to 1976, as a senator from 1977 to 1982 and as chairman of several commissions, including the Constitution Review Group of the 1990s; he died in 2017 at the age of 100.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.4] Contemporaries praised his clarity and courage in questioning protection, though some in his own department doubted how far growth could be planned.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.5] Historians see him as the architect of the new approach, while stressing that Lemass and de Valera supplied the political will, and some argue that his role has been overstated by the idea that planning alone made the economy grow.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.b] Lemass was the minister and then Taoiseach who turned the new thinking into government policy.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.0] Lemass was born in Dublin in 1899, fought in the Rising in 1916 and on the anti-Treaty side in the Civil War, and his brother Noel was killed in 1923.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.1] He was a founder of Fianna Fáil in 1926 and was Minister for Industry and Commerce from 1932 to 1939, from 1941 to 1948, from 1951 to 1954 and from 1957 to 1959, and Minister for Supplies from 1939 to 1945.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.2] As the architect of the protectionist industrial policy of the 1930s, he was able later to argue that protection had served its purpose and should now give way to competition.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.3] As Taoiseach he backed the First Programme, applied to join the EEC and signed the Free Trade Agreement.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.4] Contemporaries saw him as a practical, impatient leader who got things done, though some in Fianna Fáil mistrusted his break with protection.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.5] Historians and biographers judge him a modernizer who changed the tone of Irish politics, but they note that he left rural poverty, urban housing problems and partition unresolved.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.0] Whitaker supplied the analysis and Lemass the political leadership; the Programme needed both, and their partnership is the central reason for the change of direction.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.1] Whitaker was cautious by temperament and was more sceptical than Lemass about the Second Programme's ambitious targets, which were abandoned in 1967.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.2] Achievements of the First Programme: growth about twice the target, falling emigration, rising exports and a shift in confidence.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.3] Limits: total employment barely increased, farming and the west lagged, and the balance of payments remained fragile.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.4] Contemporary view: the Programme was a success, and Fianna Fáil regained an overall majority in the 1965 election.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.5] Later view: historians accept that it was a turning point but debate how much of the growth came from policy and how much from the European boom.
