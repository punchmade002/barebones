[hist-ire1-s1.0.b] The Act of Union (1800) abolished the Irish Parliament; from 1 January 1801 Ireland was part of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, governed by Westminster and a separate executive in Dublin.
[hist-ire1-s1.0.0] Ireland sent 100 MPs to the House of Commons, a small minority of its roughly 650 members, so Irish measures depended on British votes.
[hist-ire1-s1.0.1] Ireland was represented in the House of Lords by 28 Irish representative peers elected for life by the Irish peerage, and by four Church of Ireland bishops sitting in rotation.
[hist-ire1-s1.0.2] The Union also united the Church of England and the Church of Ireland, so the Protestant Church of Ireland remained the established church of a mainly Catholic country.
[hist-ire1-s1.0.3] William Pitt had hoped to follow the Union with Catholic Emancipation, but George III refused, and Pitt resigned in 1801; this broken expectation was the root of the Catholic question.
[hist-ire1-s1.0.4] The Lord Lieutenant (viceroy), usually a British peer, headed the Irish executive and lived in Dublin Castle and the Viceregal Lodge in the Phoenix Park.
[hist-ire1-s1.0.5] The Chief Secretary, a politician who sat in the Commons, ran Irish business in Parliament and became the most powerful figure in Irish government; Robert Peel held the post from 1812 to 1818.
[hist-ire1-s1.0.6] The Under-Secretary was the senior permanent official in Dublin Castle, the name used for the whole Irish administration, which stayed separate from Whitehall.
[hist-ire1-s1.1.0] Grand juries, chosen by the county sheriff from the leading Protestant landowners, levied the county cess (a local tax on land) and paid for roads, bridges, jails and courthouses.
[hist-ire1-s1.1.1] Unpaid magistrates (justices of the peace), nearly all Protestant landowners, tried minor cases at petty sessions and quarter sessions, so most Catholics met the law through their landlords.
[hist-ire1-s1.1.2] Town corporations such as Dublin Corporation were closed, self-electing Protestant bodies, which kept Catholics out of municipal office until the reforms of 1840.
[hist-ire1-s1.1.3] Rural unrest by agrarian secret societies such as the Whiteboys and Ribbonmen led Peel to create the Peace Preservation Force in 1814, a paid force sent into disturbed districts at local expense.
[hist-ire1-s1.1.4] The Irish Constabulary Act of 1822 set up a county constabulary under four provincial inspectors-general, Ireland's first regular rural police.
[hist-ire1-s1.1.5] The Constabulary Act of 1836 merged these forces into the Irish Constabulary, an armed, centrally controlled national police under an inspector-general in Dublin; it became the Royal Irish Constabulary in 1867.
[hist-ire1-s1.1.6] Ireland was policed more heavily and more centrally than Britain, which showed that the Union rested on coercion as well as consent.
[hist-ire1-s1.2.0] The Relief Act of 1793 had already given Catholics the vote on the same terms as Protestants, entry to the bar and to junior army commissions, and the right to sit on juries.

[hist-ire1-s1.2.4] They were also barred from the senior judiciary, the rank of King's Counsel, high government office and the senior ranks of the army and navy.

[hist-ire1-s1.2.6] The Irish bishops rejected the veto, and in 1814 the Quarantotti rescript from Rome, which accepted it, was repudiated in Ireland; O'Connell led lay opposition, and the issue split the Catholic leadership and stalled the cause after 1815.

[hist-ire1-s1.9.2] Political dimension: Catholics could now enter Parliament and office, and a disciplined, peaceful mass movement had forced a reluctant government to change the constitution.
[hist-ire1-s1.9.3] Political limits: the £10 franchise disfranchised most of the voters who had won the victory, and Protestants still dominated the magistracy, grand juries, corporations and Dublin Castle.
[hist-ire1-s1.9.4] Religious dimension: the campaign fused Catholic and national identity and gave the Church a central place in Irish politics, which strengthened sectarian division.
[hist-ire1-s1.9.5] Religious limits: the Church of Ireland stayed established and Catholics still paid tithes to it, the grievance behind the Tithe War of the 1830s.
[hist-ire1-s1.9.6] Contemporary British view: a dangerous surrender to agitation; Irish Catholic view: a triumph that proved organised peaceful pressure worked.
[hist-ire1-s1.9.7] Later view: the Emancipation campaign was O'Connell's greatest success because its aim was limited and could be granted within the Union, unlike Repeal later.
