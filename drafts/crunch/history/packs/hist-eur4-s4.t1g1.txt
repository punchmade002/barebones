[hist-eur4-s2.0.10] On 14 October 1964 the Central Committee removed him from the posts of First Secretary and chairman of the Council of Ministers, while he was on holiday in the Black Sea.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.11] Leonid Brezhnev became First Secretary of the party and Alexei Kosygin chairman of the Council of Ministers.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.12] His critics cited the Cuban humiliation, the failed agricultural and economic reforms, and his erratic style of leadership.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.13] He was pensioned off and lived in retirement, largely at a dacha outside Moscow, until his death in 1971.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.14] His fall confirmed that the Soviet leadership would not tolerate a single figure dominating policy, which shaped the more collective style of the Brezhnev years.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.b] Khrushchev's domestic programme loosened Stalinist controls but its economic experiments produced mixed results.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.0] De-Stalinisation released many political prisoners, reduced the role of the secret police and allowed more open discussion in literature.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.1] The Virgin Lands campaign from 1954 ploughed up grassland in Kazakhstan and Siberia to grow grain, and it produced good harvests in its early years.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.2] Droughts and poor management later brought failures, most seriously in 1963, when the harvest fell and grain had to be imported.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.3] In 1957 industry was decentralised into regional economic councils, a reform that was later reversed by his successors.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.4] The Novocherkassk strike of June 1962 over food prices and wages was suppressed by troops who fired on the crowd, a sign of the limits of his reforms.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.5] Space achievements boosted prestige: Sputnik 1 was launched on 4 October 1957, and Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on 12 April 1961.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.b] The crises before Khrushchev set the pattern of confrontation without direct war.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.0] Berlin Blockade (1948–49): the first major crisis, ended by the airlift and followed by the founding of NATO.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.1] Korean War (1950–53): the North Korean invasion of the South in June 1950 persuaded Western governments that communism was expansionist, and led to the rearmament of NATO, the creation of a unified NATO command under Eisenhower and the push to rearm West Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.2] Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 opened a more flexible phase; an armistice in Korea followed in July 1953.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.b] Berlin was the most dangerous flashpoint in Europe, because West Berlin was an enclave inside East Germany and a window to the West.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.0] On 27 November 1958 Khrushchev issued an ultimatum giving the Western powers six months to agree to a settlement that would end their occupation of West Berlin.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.1] Khrushchev's visit to the USA in September 1959, including talks with Eisenhower at Camp David, produced a brief thaw that the U-2 incident later ended.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.2] The Paris summit of May 1960 collapsed after the U-2 spy plane piloted by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union on 1 May 1960.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.3] At the Vienna summit in June 1961 Khrushchev pressed the new US president John F. Kennedy on Berlin, and the crisis deepened over the summer.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.4] East German forces began building the Berlin Wall on 13 August 1961, closing the border between the two halves of the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.5] The Western powers did not use force against the Wall, accepting it as the price of avoiding war; Kennedy is reported to have said that a wall was better than a war.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.6] Between 1961 and 1989 the Wall and the border fortifications around it were guarded, and people who tried to cross were shot or arrested.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.b] The Cuban Missile Crisis was the closest the Cold War came to nuclear war, and it was resolved by a compromise that Europeans watched closely.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.0] In October 1962 US reconnaissance photographs showed Soviet medium-range missiles being installed in Cuba, within range of much of the USA.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.1] President Kennedy announced a naval quarantine of Cuba on 22 October 1962 and demanded the removal of the missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.2] For about a week the two superpowers were on the brink of war; Kennedy sent former secretary of state Dean Acheson to brief the French president de Gaulle, who gave firm support, while most European governments learned of the crisis only as it unfolded.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.3] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed to withdraw the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.4] The secret part of the deal was a US promise to remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey, which was not made public at the time.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.5] The crisis shaped later arms control: a Moscow–Washington hotline was set up in 1963, and the Partial Test Ban Treaty followed in August 1963.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.6] Khrushchev's motives were to defend Castro's Cuba after the Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961, to offset US missiles in Turkey and Italy, and to narrow the Soviet Union's weakness in long-range missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.7] The crisis began on 16 October 1962 when Kennedy was shown U-2 photographs; Kennedy's advisers (ExComm) weighed an air strike against a naval blockade and chose the blockade, described publicly as a quarantine.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.8] Seen from Europe, the crisis showed that Berlin and Cuba were linked, and made West European leaders more aware that decisions on nuclear war would be taken in Washington and Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.b] Each superpower relied on nuclear weapons to deter attack, and limited agreements grew out of the fear that deterrence could fail.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.0] Nuclear deterrence meant that each side kept enough weapons to destroy the other after a first strike, a condition later called mutually assured destruction.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.1] The Soviet Union tested the 50-megaton Tsar Bomba on 30 October 1961, the largest nuclear explosion ever set off.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.2] The Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed in Moscow on 5 August 1963 by the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union, banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.3] The Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968 committed non-nuclear states not to acquire nuclear weapons, while the nuclear powers agreed to pursue disarmament.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.4] Western Europe depended on the US nuclear umbrella under NATO, and debates in France and Britain over independent deterrents reflected doubts about that protection.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.5] France tested its first atomic bomb in 1960 and developed its own deterrent, which it kept outside NATO's military command from 1966.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.6] The ideological dispute with Mao's China grew from the late 1950s and became open in 1960, when Soviet advisers were withdrawn from China.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.7] The Sino-Soviet split meant the Soviet Union faced two rival communist powers and could not count on Chinese support in Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.8] Albania sided with China and in 1968 became the only member state to leave the Warsaw Pact.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.9] Soviet leaders regarded the Berlin Wall as a means of stabilising the German Democratic Republic, which had lost about 2.7 million people to the West since 1945.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.b] Détente was a period of easing tension, from about 1969 to the late 1970s, in which the superpowers and European states signed agreements on arms and borders.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.0] Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik sought better relations with Eastern Europe; the Treaty of Moscow of 12 August 1970 renounced the use of force between the Federal Republic and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.1] The Four Power Agreement on Berlin of 3 September 1971 guaranteed access between West Berlin and West Germany and confirmed the Western presence in the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.2] The Basic Treaty of 1972 established relations between the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic, which led to both joining the United Nations in 1973.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.3] SALT I, signed on 26 May 1972, limited anti-ballistic missile systems and placed interim limits on strategic offensive missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.4] The Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe produced the Helsinki Final Act on 1 August 1975, signed by 35 states including the USA, Canada and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.5] The Final Act recognised existing borders in Europe and committed signatories to respect human rights, a commitment that later dissidents used to press their governments.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.b] Détente broke down in the late 1970s, and a new arms race in Europe led to the deployment of intermediate-range missiles in the early 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.0] On 24 December 1979 Soviet forces invaded Afghanistan, and the USA responded with a grain embargo and a boycott of the Moscow Olympics in 1980.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.1] NATO's double-track decision of 12 December 1979 agreed to deploy Pershing II and cruise missiles in Western Europe unless arms talks with the Soviet Union succeeded.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.2] The Soviet Union had deployed SS-20 intermediate-range missiles in the late 1970s, which threatened Western Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.3] Large peace demonstrations took place in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands in 1981–83 against the deployment of the new missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.4] The Soviet leadership's fear of a Western first strike led to a serious misreading during the NATO exercise Able Archer 83 in November 1983.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.5] The crisis was defused by arms negotiations, and the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty was signed in December 1987 under Gorbachev.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.b] Examiners ask which events changed the course of the Cold War, so know the order and what each changed.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.0] Berlin Blockade, 1948–49: confirmed the division of Germany and led to NATO.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.1] Hungary, 1956: showed that the Soviet Union would use force to keep its bloc together.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.2] Berlin Wall, 1961 and Cuba, 1962: the high points of confrontation, after which both sides sought safeguards such as the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.3] Prague Spring, 1968: the invasion of Czechoslovakia confirmed the Brezhnev Doctrine but did not end détente.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.4] Helsinki, 1975: the high point of détente, which gave dissidents in the East a human rights standard.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.5] Afghanistan and the Euromissiles, 1979: ended détente and began the second Cold War.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.6] Reagan's election in 1980 brought a hard line: in March 1983 he called the Soviet Union an evil empire, and announced the Strategic Defense Initiative.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.7] Gorbachev from 1985, the Reykjavik summit of October 1986 and the INF Treaty of 1987 reversed the arms race, and 1989 ended Soviet control of eastern Europe (see the notes on reform movements and the collapse of communism).
[hist-eur4-s2.9.b] Khrushchev's record is judged by contrasting his bold moves with the setbacks that ended his rule.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.0] Achievements: de-Stalinisation, the Partial Test Ban Treaty, the hotline, the Austrian State Treaty of 1955 and the space successes.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.1] Failures: the Virgin Lands harvest crises, the reversal of his administrative reforms, and the Cuban settlement, which Soviet leaders saw as a retreat.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.2] Contemporary Western views often focused on his unpredictability, while his Soviet colleagues attacked his rash 'hare-brained schemes' and his boasting.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.3] Later historians have judged him as a reformer who opened the system but never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.4] The Berlin Wall, which his policy helped to produce, was for many observers the most lasting symbol of his era, and later assessments weigh it heavily against his reform record.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s3: 3. Case study: the Hungarian Uprising, 1956
[hist-eur4-s3.0.b] Nagy was a Hungarian communist who became the symbol of reform inside the party and was executed after the uprising.
[hist-eur4-s3.0.0] Born in 1896, Nagy was a communist who spent much of the 1930s and early 1940s in the Soviet Union and returned to Hungary with the Red Army in 1944, serving as minister of agriculture in 1945 when land was distributed to peasants.
[hist-eur4-s3.0.1] He was prime minister from 1953 to 1955, pursuing a New Course of consumer goods, relief for farmers and looser controls against the Stalinist policies of Mátyás Rákosi.
[hist-eur4-s3.0.2] Rákosi had him removed in April 1955 and expelled from the party later that year, after Nagy had criticised collectivisation and heavy industry.
[hist-eur4-s3.0.3] During the uprising he was a participant: he was reappointed prime minister on 24 October 1956, announced Hungary's neutrality and its withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact on 1 November, and appealed to the West for help.
[hist-eur4-s3.0.4] Contemporaries in the Soviet bloc saw him as a traitor; Kádár's government branded him a counter-revolutionary, and he was tried in secret.
[hist-eur4-s3.0.5] Nagy was executed on 16 June 1958, together with Pál Maléter and others, and buried in an unmarked grave; historians now treat him as a national martyr and a reformer who was the uprising's political leader.
[hist-eur4-s3.0.6] He was reburied with a state ceremony on 16 June 1989, the thirty-first anniversary of his execution, as the regime was collapsing.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.b] Hungary under Rákosi was one of the most rigidly Stalinist states of the Soviet bloc, and events in the Soviet Union and Poland in 1956 weakened its leadership.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.0] Mátyás Rákosi led the Hungarian communists from the late 1940s and ran a one-party state with show trials, a powerful secret police (the AVH) and forced collectivisation of farms.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.1] The Hungarian economy was run on Soviet lines, with heavy industry favoured and living standards falling, which fuelled resentment among workers and peasants.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.2] Khrushchev's secret speech of 25 February 1956 exposed the crimes of Stalin's era and gave Hungarian reformers a precedent for criticising their own leaders.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.3] Rákosi was forced to resign in July 1956 and was succeeded by Ernő Gerő, a Stalinist who lacked popular support.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.4] The Polish October of 1956, when Władysław Gomułka returned to power after pressure from Poland's workers and party, encouraged Hungarian intellectuals in the Petőfi Circle and the Writers' Union to demand change.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.5] Cardinal József Mindszenty, the head of the Hungarian Catholic Church, was jailed in 1949 in a show trial; the regime's attack on the Church added to resentment.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.6] Nagy's New Course of 1953 followed Soviet pressure on the Hungarian leaders after Stalin's death; Rákosi stayed as party leader and used his control of the party to undermine Nagy, which divided the communists.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.7] The AVH secret police, which spied on and arrested ordinary citizens, was the best-known instrument of Rákosi's rule and the main target of popular hatred.
[hist-eur4-s3.1.8] Hungary also had large economic burdens: Soviet reparations in the late 1940s and obligations to supply the Soviet Union, which kept living standards low.
[hist-eur4-s3.2.b] A student demonstration in Budapest turned into a national uprising within a day.
[hist-eur4-s3.2.0] On 22 October students in Budapest drew up a list of sixteen demands, including free elections, the withdrawal of Soviet troops and the return of Imre Nagy as prime minister.
[hist-eur4-s3.2.1] On 23 October a large crowd gathered at the statue of Stalin, which was pulled down, and a delegation tried to broadcast the demands on Radio Budapest.
[hist-eur4-s3.2.2] The AVH secret police opened fire on the crowd outside the radio building, which triggered fighting across Budapest.
[hist-eur4-s3.2.3] Soviet troops entered the city, and the Gerő government asked Moscow for help, which it received on the same night.
[hist-eur4-s3.2.4] Armed civilians and army units joined the fighting, and the uprising spread to other towns in the following days.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.b] For about a week Hungary had a reformed communist government and Soviet troops were pulling back, before the second intervention.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.0] Nagy was reinstated as prime minister on 24 October 1956 and sought a ceasefire; he ordered Soviet troops out of Budapest, and the Soviet government announced on 30 October that it was prepared to negotiate the withdrawal of its forces.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.1] On 30 October Nagy formed a coalition with non-communist parties, including the Smallholders and the Social Democrats, and the Communist Party was reduced to a minority role.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.2] Workers' councils formed in factories and in Budapest, and some of them organised strikes and demanded genuine workers' control.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.3] On 1 November Nagy declared Hungary neutral and announced its withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact, and he appealed to the United Nations.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.4] Kádár, who had joined Nagy's government and become party leader on 25 October, left Budapest on 1 November, held talks with the Soviet leaders, and with their backing formed a rival government that was proclaimed on 4 November and based at Szolnok in central Hungary.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.5] The Soviet leadership decided on 31 October to intervene again; fresh Soviet troops crossed the border from 1 November, and the main attack, Operation Whirlwind, began on 4 November, even as Soviet delegates were negotiating a withdrawal.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.6] On 30 October a crowd attacked the party headquarters at Republic Square in Budapest and lynched several AVH men and party officials; the revolution's violence was used later by Soviet propaganda.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.7] Cardinal Mindszenty was freed from house arrest on 30 October and returned to Budapest, and political prisoners were released.
[hist-eur4-s3.3.8] Khrushchev and the Soviet Presidium at first leaned towards a negotiated settlement, but the declaration of neutrality, the end of one-party rule and the Suez crisis helped to convince them to intervene.
[hist-eur4-s3.4.b] On 4 November 1956 Soviet forces returned in strength, crushed the fighting in Budapest and removed Nagy's government.
[hist-eur4-s3.4.0] The second intervention began on 4 November 1956 with a large Soviet force that attacked Budapest and the provincial centres.
[hist-eur4-s3.4.1] Nagy broadcast an appeal to the nation and the world that Soviet troops were attacking Hungary; he then took refuge in the Yugoslav embassy in Budapest.
[hist-eur4-s3.4.2] Pál Maléter, the Hungarian minister of defence, was arrested while negotiating with the Soviet commanders and later executed alongside Nagy.
[hist-eur4-s3.4.3] Kádár became prime minister on 4 November and formed a government that was backed by Soviet troops.
[hist-eur4-s3.4.4] Nagy left the Yugoslav embassy on 22 November after a Soviet promise of safe passage, and he was taken away by Soviet forces that same day.
[hist-eur4-s3.4.5] Fighting in Budapest continued until mid-November, with workers' councils calling a general strike that paralysed the country for weeks.
[hist-eur4-s3.5.b] The uprising ended with a Soviet victory and a long repression, and the scale of the casualties and flight is the most widely quoted measure of its cost.
[hist-eur4-s3.5.0] Commonly cited estimates put the Hungarian dead at about 2,500 to 3,000 and the Soviet dead at several hundred (often given as about 700); figures vary between sources and are approximate.
[hist-eur4-s3.5.1] About 200,000 Hungarians fled to Austria and beyond, among them many young and skilled people; sources give varying figures, so the number is approximate.
[hist-eur4-s3.5.2] Many thousands were arrested in the months after the uprising, and hundreds were executed in the years that followed under Kádár's government.
[hist-eur4-s3.5.3] Nagy was lured from the Yugoslav embassy, taken to Romania, then returned to Hungary and tried in secret with Maléter and others; he was executed on 16 June 1958, with the verdict decided in advance.
[hist-eur4-s3.5.4] Kádár's regime later moved to a policy of consolidation and limited reform, which in time became known as goulash communism.
[hist-eur4-s3.6.b] The uprising coincided with the Suez crisis, and the Western powers did not intervene, which shaped Soviet calculations.
[hist-eur4-s3.6.0] Israel invaded Egypt on 29 October 1956, and Britain and France bombed Egypt from 31 October and landed at Port Said on 5–6 November, drawing American and international attention away from Hungary at the crucial moment.
[hist-eur4-s3.6.1] Radio Free Europe broadcast messages of encouragement to Hungarians, and Western politicians used the language of liberation, but no Western government intervened militarily.
[hist-eur4-s3.6.2] The UN Security Council debated the Hungarian question but the Soviet Union vetoed action; the General Assembly condemned the Soviet intervention, but no UN force was sent.
[hist-eur4-s3.6.3] Eisenhower publicly ruled out military intervention, reflecting the risk of a wider war and US caution over Soviet nuclear power.
[hist-eur4-s3.6.4] The refugees who reached Austria were welcomed in the West, and their arrival was a propaganda victory for the West, though it did not change the outcome in Hungary.
[hist-eur4-s3.6.5] Radio Free Europe's broadcasts were widely taken by Hungarians as promises of Western help, and the station was criticised afterwards for raising false hopes.
[hist-eur4-s3.6.6] Western communist parties lost members and intellectuals after Hungary, and the uprising damaged the Soviet Union's reputation among the left.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.b] The uprising was crushed because the Soviet Union was willing to use overwhelming force and the West would not act, but it left a lasting legacy in Hungary.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.0] Soviet willingness to use force was decisive, because Moscow regarded Hungary's departure from the Warsaw Pact as unacceptable to its security.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.1] The uprising had no organised armed leadership beyond local groups, and the negotiations with Soviet commanders failed to produce a lasting arrangement.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.2] Its significance was that it confirmed the limits of de-Stalinisation in eastern Europe and showed that Soviet control would be defended by force.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.3] Kádár's later policy of compromise (summed up in his 1961 saying that whoever is not against us is with us) and the New Economic Mechanism of 1968 allowed Hungarians more consumer goods and some market elements, as the price of political obedience.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.4] The reburial of Nagy on 16 June 1989 and the later recognition of 23 October as Hungary's national day showed the collapse of communist authority in Hungary.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.5] Contemporary views differed: Soviet and Kádár propaganda described a counter-revolution led by fascist elements, while Western reporting presented a national and largely anti-communist rising.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.6] Hungary's declaration of neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact crossed the line that the Soviet leaders would tolerate; Poland's Gomułka had stayed within the alliance and was allowed to survive.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.7] Tito, who was consulted by Khrushchev before the second intervention, accepted the need to defeat the uprising but disliked Kádár's methods.
[hist-eur4-s3.7.8] The memory of 1956 shaped later events: Czechoslovak reformers in 1968 promised to stay in the Warsaw Pact to avoid Hungary's fate, and Soviet leaders drew the lesson that force might be needed again.
[hist-eur4-s3.8.b] Historians disagree about the uprising's character, and the answer shapes judgement of Nagy and of the West.
[hist-eur4-s3.8.0] Achievements: within days the uprising forced Soviet withdrawal, a multi-party government and a declaration of neutrality, which no other satellite had achieved.
[hist-eur4-s3.8.1] Limits: the uprising relied on the goodwill of Soviet commanders, and it had no means of defending itself once Moscow chose to use force.
[hist-eur4-s3.8.2] Later historians differ between those who see a popular anti-communist revolution and those who see a reformist communist revolt that grew beyond its leaders.
[hist-eur4-s3.8.3] Nagy's reputation was rehabilitated after 1989, but judgements of his communism, his tactics and his naivety about Soviet intentions remain debated.
