TARGET hist-ire5-s8: 8. The impact of the Troubles on the economy and society
[hist-ire5-s8.0.b] The Troubles (the conflict in Northern Ireland that began in 1969 and continued into the 1990s and beyond) caused thousands of deaths and injuries. Here the focus is the human cost and where it fell, not the causes or events.
[hist-ire5-s8.0.0] Between 1969 and 1993 the Troubles caused about 3,000 deaths. The total for the whole conflict to about 2001 is about 3,500 or more, and figures vary by source, so always say "about".
[hist-ire5-s8.0.1] 1972 was the deadliest year of the Troubles, with nearly 500 deaths.
[hist-ire5-s8.0.2] About half of the dead were civilians. Members of the security forces (the army and police), republican paramilitaries and loyalist paramilitaries also died in large numbers.
[hist-ire5-s8.0.3] Tens of thousands of people were injured, and the harm was psychological as well as physical, because survivors and the bereaved lived with trauma.
[hist-ire5-s8.0.4] The violence was concentrated in Belfast, especially north and west Belfast, in Derry and in the border counties. Working-class districts such as Ardoyne, the Falls and the Shankill in Belfast, and the Bogside in Derry, suffered most.
[hist-ire5-s8.0.5] Most deaths took place in Northern Ireland, though bombings in Britain and the Republic also caused deaths, including the Dublin and Monaghan bombings of 1974 in the Republic.
[hist-ire5-s8.1.b] Violence made Northern Ireland look like a risky place to do business or take a holiday. Investment and tourism fell, and the cost of insurance and security rose for businesses across the province.
[hist-ire5-s8.1.0] Foreign investors were reluctant to open or expand factories in Northern Ireland because of the risk of bombing, disruption and bad publicity, so jobs that might have replaced declining industries never arrived.
[hist-ire5-s8.1.1] Tourism fell sharply because visitors avoided the province. This hurt hotels, shops and the wider local economy that depended on visitors' spending.
[hist-ire5-s8.1.2] Bomb attacks damaged city-centre trade. From 1972 a security zone with barriers and gates surrounded central Belfast, popularly called the ring of steel, and the checks discouraged shoppers.
[hist-ire5-s8.1.3] Businesses faced high insurance premiums to cover bomb damage and had to spend on security. Some companies closed or moved their operations to Britain or the Republic, which put further pressure on local jobs.
[hist-ire5-s8.1.4] Security measures, such as checkpoints and searches, increased the time and cost of moving goods and people.
[hist-ire5-s8.1.5] The government paid compensation for criminal damage under official schemes, which added to public spending.
[hist-ire5-s8.2.b] Before 1969 the economy depended heavily on shipbuilding, linen and engineering, and in the 1960s new industries, including man-made fibres, were promoted to modernise it. Manufacturing then kept shrinking while public-sector and security jobs grew, so the province became more dependent on the British state.
[hist-ire5-s8.2.0] Manufacturing employment continued to decline, so many workers lost their jobs, with shipbuilding, linen, engineering and man-made fibres all shedding jobs through the 1970s and 1980s.
[hist-ire5-s8.2.1] This decline began before 1969 and had international causes, including wider recession, so the Troubles worsened it rather than caused it alone.
[hist-ire5-s8.2.2] Harland and Wolff, the Belfast shipyard, was taken into public ownership in 1975 because the government wished to protect jobs.
[hist-ire5-s8.2.3] Public-sector employment (jobs paid for by government, such as health, education and local administration) grew as a share of the workforce.
[hist-ire5-s8.2.4] The security sector also provided many jobs: the Ulster Defence Regiment (formed 1970 and merged into the Royal Irish Regiment in 1992), the RUC (the police), the prison service, the army and private security.
[hist-ire5-s8.2.5] British subvention, the public money transferred from the Treasury (the British finance ministry) to cover the gap, kept public services and living standards above what local output could pay for.
[hist-ire5-s8.2.6] Because the public sector was so large, Northern Ireland's economy was exposed to any change in British government spending. Subvention is not the same as British security spending, though the two are related.
[hist-ire5-s8.3.b] The De Lorean factory at Dunmurry, south-west Belfast, was a government-backed attempt to bring in investment and jobs. It collapsed in 1982 and cost the taxpayer heavily.
[hist-ire5-s8.3.0] The DeLorean Motor Company built a factory at Dunmurry to make a stainless-steel sports car. Production began in 1981, funded by British government grants and loans of tens of millions of pounds.
[hist-ire5-s8.3.1] The factory was intended to create about 2,000 jobs in an area of high unemployment. The site lay between Catholic and Protestant housing estates, in the hope of drawing both communities into work, though the workforce was never as mixed as hoped.
[hist-ire5-s8.3.2] The car sold poorly, so the company went into receivership (was taken over by an administrator because it could not pay its debts) in February 1982.
[hist-ire5-s8.3.3] John De Lorean, the man behind the company, was arrested in the USA in October 1982 on a drug-trafficking charge and was acquitted in August 1984. His case is separate from the company's failure.
[hist-ire5-s8.3.4] The collapse left the British government with heavy losses. It showed both the government's willingness to subsidise investment and the difficulty of attracting investors to Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s8.4.b] Unemployment stayed well above the UK average, and it fell unequally: Catholics were out of work far more often than Protestants. Governments responded with fair employment law, and campaigners abroad with the MacBride Principles.
[hist-ire5-s8.4.0] Unemployment in Northern Ireland was above the UK average through the 1970s and 1980s.
[hist-ire5-s8.4.1] Catholic unemployment was consistently higher than Protestant unemployment, roughly twice as high. Causes include discrimination, location in the west of the province, and the sectors where Catholics worked.
[hist-ire5-s8.4.2] The Fair Employment (Northern Ireland) Act 1976 set up a Fair Employment Agency to investigate discrimination in employment. It could not compel employers to monitor their workforces, so its power was limited.
[hist-ire5-s8.4.3] The Fair Employment (Northern Ireland) Act 1989 replaced the Agency with a Fair Employment Commission, required employers to monitor their workforce by religion, and brought stronger enforcement.
[hist-ire5-s8.4.4] The MacBride Principles of 1984, named after Sean MacBride, were a code of fair employment for US firms operating in Northern Ireland, promoted by Irish-American campaigners.
[hist-ire5-s8.4.5] West Belfast and Derry suffered the highest deprivation (poor housing, income and opportunity), with families who had lost jobs and had few alternative sources of income.
[hist-ire5-s8.4.6] Historians link unemployed young men, especially young Catholic men in the 1980s, to recruitment to paramilitaries. This is a link they raise, not a proven single cause.
[hist-ire5-s8.5.b] Catholic complaints about housing allocation had been central to the civil rights movement before 1969. The Housing Executive was set up to answer them, but violence pushed communities apart faster than policy could bring them together.
[hist-ire5-s8.5.0] The Northern Ireland Housing Executive was established in 1971 to take over public housing from the local councils and the Northern Ireland Housing Trust.
[hist-ire5-s8.5.1] It allocated houses by a points system based on need, so that religion no longer decided who got a house, to end the discrimination that the civil rights movement had criticised.
[hist-ire5-s8.5.2] Intimidation (threats and violence meant to force people out) drove families from mixed areas into areas where their own community was the majority. Tens of thousands of people moved home in Belfast between 1969 and 1973.
[hist-ire5-s8.5.3] Peace walls (also called peace lines) are barriers between Protestant and Catholic streets. They were first built in Belfast in 1969, grew in number in the 1970s and 1980s, and were later built in Derry and elsewhere.
[hist-ire5-s8.5.4] Peace walls were built to protect people, but they also reinforced the segregation that intimidation had created.
[hist-ire5-s8.5.5] Segregation made communities more distinct, so it made it harder for people from different backgrounds to meet.
[hist-ire5-s8.5.6] Segregation cut across the Executive's allocation policy in practice, because families moved from mixed areas into segregated estates.
[hist-ire5-s8.6.b] Schools remained largely divided by religion throughout the Troubles. A small, parent-led integrated movement began in the 1970s, and the state gave it legal support in 1989.
[hist-ire5-s8.6.0] Most children went to either Catholic maintained schools or mainly Protestant controlled schools, so the school system mirrored the religious divisions of the province.
[hist-ire5-s8.6.1] The Catholic Church controlled its schools and defended its right to do so, so Catholic voluntary schools kept state support, and the system stayed divided.
[hist-ire5-s8.6.2] Teacher training was also separate: Catholic teachers trained mainly at St Mary's College and Protestants at Stranmillis College, both in Belfast.
[hist-ire5-s8.6.3] The integrated education movement began when parents in both communities formed All Children Together in 1974 to campaign for mixed schools.
[hist-ire5-s8.6.4] Lagan College, opened in Belfast in September 1981 with 28 pupils, was the first integrated secondary school in Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s8.6.5] The Education Reform (Northern Ireland) Order of 1989 gave statutory (legal) support to integrated schools. It placed a duty on the Department of Education to encourage and facilitate them.
[hist-ire5-s8.6.6] Integrated schools were few by 1993 and at first depended on parents and charitable funding, so most children remained in separate schools.
[hist-ire5-s8.7.b] The health service had to treat the wounded of a violent conflict, and the strain of living through it left lasting psychological harm.
[hist-ire5-s8.7.0] The Royal Victoria Hospital on the Falls Road in Belfast developed world-leading expertise in treating gunshot wounds and blast injuries, and its surgeons' experience was used elsewhere.
[hist-ire5-s8.7.1] Because the casualties were so many, hospitals and ambulance crews had to treat large numbers of injured civilians and members of the security forces, which placed heavy demands on staff.
[hist-ire5-s8.7.2] Ambulance crews and hospital staff faced danger when responding to bomb incidents.
[hist-ire5-s8.7.3] Mental health problems, such as stress and trauma, were widespread among people who had lived through bombings, shootings, bereavement and displacement.
[hist-ire5-s8.7.4] Security measures and fear of attack affected access to health care, through delays and restrictions on movement.
[hist-ire5-s8.7.5] In 1973 health and personal social services were reorganised into four Health and Social Services Boards (Eastern, Northern, Southern and Western). This was an administrative reform, not a result of the Troubles.
[hist-ire5-s8.8.b] Beyond the headline events, the Troubles changed ordinary routines. Security measures and community division shaped how people shopped, travelled and grew up, especially in Belfast and Derry.
[hist-ire5-s8.8.0] Because of the threat of bombs, security checkpoints, searches and vehicle checks were a feature of daily life in Belfast and Derry, and they changed the routines of shopping and travel.
[hist-ire5-s8.8.1] Town centres were sealed or controlled after bombings, with security gates, so shoppers had to pass through checks to enter them.
[hist-ire5-s8.8.2] Car bombs prompted security checks and car-park controls in city-centre shopping areas.
[hist-ire5-s8.8.3] In divided towns and estates, some people avoided areas where the other community lived, which limited social contact.
[hist-ire5-s8.8.4] Children grew up, for example, with soldiers and barriers around them and with memories of violence in their local areas.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.b] Examiners reward a judgement on how serious the impact was. Weigh the costs against the reforms, and compare how people at the time and historians since have seen it.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.0] Cost: investment, tourism and manufacturing were damaged, and the economy came to rely on public money and the security sector.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.1] Cost: segregation in housing and schooling deepened the social divisions between the communities.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.2] Achievement: the Housing Executive, the Fair Employment Acts and integrated schools were real reforms towards equal treatment in housing, jobs and education.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.3] Limit: the Catholic-Protestant gap in unemployment and deprivation persisted, which means reforms were incomplete, and integrated schools were few.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.4] Contemporary view: unionists pointed to public investment and the British subvention as proof that the state supported the province.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.5] Contemporary view: nationalists argued that discrimination and deprivation were rooted in state policy and that the Troubles had made them worse.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.6] Later view: historians stress that the Troubles were both a cause and a consequence of inequality, and that the costs fell on both communities, most heavily on working-class areas.
[hist-ire5-s8.9.7] The impact was uneven: some areas, and middle-class suburbs, were largely untouched.

CANDIDATES hist-ire5-s4: 4. The outbreak of the Troubles: from 1969 to Direct Rule, 1972
[hist-ire5-s4.0.b] By 1969 Northern Ireland had been governed for almost fifty years by one party, and many Catholics felt shut out of the state. Knowing what had built up before August 1969 explains why the violence broke out so quickly once it started.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.0] The Government of Ireland Act 1920 set up the Northern Ireland parliament (Stormont), and the Unionist Party governed from 1921 to 1972. Westminster kept sovereignty but in practice rarely interfered in Northern Ireland's internal affairs.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.1] The Ireland Act 1949 guaranteed that Northern Ireland would stay in the United Kingdom unless its parliament (later its people) consented, which is why unionists treated any talk of change as a threat to the border.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.2] Catholics complained of discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting. The Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association (NICRA), founded in 1967, campaigned on these grievances through marches.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.3] A NICRA march in Derry on 5 October 1968 and the attack on a People's Democracy march at Burntollet in January 1969 deepened tension, and the Prime Minister, Terence O'Neill, resigned on 28 April 1969. Such marches mattered because they brought the grievances onto the streets.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.4] The RUC (Royal Ulster Constabulary) was the regular police, armed and mostly Protestant. The B Specials (the Ulster Special Constabulary) were a part-time reserve, almost entirely Protestant, and Catholics distrusted both.
[hist-ire5-s4.0.5] The IRA's border campaign of 1956-62 had failed, and Stormont had used internment (detention without trial) against it in the 1950s, so the IRA was weak and divided in 1969.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.b] Four terms run through this period, and each is used in a specific way in this topic. Defining them first makes the events easier to follow.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.0] Terrorism means violence against civilians and the state for political aims. In this topic it applies to both republican and loyalist paramilitaries, and examiners reward answers that name both sides.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.1] Sectarianism means hostility between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance, so an attack on a Catholic or Protestant as such, not as a soldier or politician, is sectarian.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.2] Internment means detaining suspects without trial, so people could be held on suspicion alone. It was introduced in August 1971 and applied almost entirely to Catholics.
[hist-ire5-s4.1.3] Direct Rule means that Northern Ireland was governed directly from Westminster, through a Secretary of State, instead of by the Stormont parliament. It began on 30 March 1972.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.b] The Apprentice Boys' parade in Derry in August 1969 led to street fighting, and British troops were sent in to restore order. The crisis then forced London to take a hand in policing and reform.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.0] On 12 August 1969 the parade of the Apprentice Boys of Derry passed close to the Bogside (a Catholic area of Derry), and stone-throwing and clashes with the RUC began.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.1] The Battle of the Bogside lasted from 12 to 14 August 1969. Residents put up barricades, which created the 'Free Derry' area.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.2] British troops were deployed in Derry on 14 August 1969 and in Belfast shortly afterwards, on 15 August. This was the start of the army's long involvement in internal security. The UK Prime Minister was Harold Wilson (Labour).
[hist-ire5-s4.2.3] Rioting in Belfast on 14 and 15 August 1969 saw Catholic streets such as Bombay Street in the Lower Falls burned and several people killed, most of them Catholics.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.4] Thousands of families, most of them Catholic, fled their homes in Belfast, and refugee centres were set up in the Republic and in Catholic parishes. August 1969 is also often treated as the start of the Troubles in their most violent form.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.5] The Downing Street Declaration of 19 August 1969, issued by Wilson and Chichester-Clark, restated that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of its people, and promised reforms.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.6] The Hunt Report, published on 10 October 1969 by a committee led by Lord Hunt, recommended a reformed, unarmed RUC under a police authority, so that the police would no longer look like a partisan armed force. It also said the B Specials should be replaced.
[hist-ire5-s4.2.7] The B Specials (Ulster Special Constabulary) were stood down in April 1970. The Ulster Defence Regiment, a part-time regiment of the British army formed in 1970, took over their local security duties.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.b] Chichester-Clark's two years as Prime Minister saw reform under pressure from London and a rising tide of violence. He could satisfy neither unionist hardliners nor nationalists.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.0] James Chichester-Clark became Prime Minister on 1 May 1969, defeating Brian Faulkner in the party vote by 17 votes to 16, a very narrow margin that left Faulkner as his rival.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.1] He presided over the Battle of the Bogside and the disbanding of the B Specials, and London pressed him to carry out the reforms of the Hunt Report.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.2] His government announced a points system for housing allocation, which made the awarding of council houses fairer, the creation of a Housing Executive (established in 1971) and reform of local government.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.3] Unionist hardliners saw these steps as surrender, while nationalists said they came too late, so the reforms won him little credit from either side.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.4] He resigned on 20 March 1971 after London would not give him the security measures he wanted.
[hist-ire5-s4.3.5] Brian Faulkner succeeded him as Prime Minister on 23 March 1971, so the man he had narrowly beaten in 1969 took over because security policy had failed.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.b] August 1969 convinced many Catholics that the IRA had failed to protect them, and the movement split. At the same time new political parties appeared, offering constitutional alternatives to violence.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.0] The IRA split in December 1969 into the Provisional IRA, whose chief of staff was Seán Mac Stíofáin, and the Official IRA, led by Cathal Goulding.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.1] The split followed the failure to defend Catholic areas in August 1969, which discredited the old leadership, and a dispute over abstentionism (refusing to take seats in parliaments) and the political, socialist direction.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.2] The Provisionals argued that the IRA must defend Catholic areas and that British forces should be driven out, in pursuit of a united Ireland. This position gained support after August 1969.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.3] Provisional Sinn Féin was set up in January 1970 to match the new military wing, so the Provisionals had a political party as well as an army.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.4] The Falls curfew of 3 to 5 July 1970 saw troops search houses for weapons in the Lower Falls, and four civilians were killed. The operation alienated Catholics from the army.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.5] The Alliance Party was founded in April 1970 as a cross-community party, and it later chose Oliver Napier as leader.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.6] The Social Democratic and Labour Party (SDLP) was founded on 21 August 1970 by nationalist politicians including Gerry Fitt (leader), John Hume, Austin Currie and Paddy Devlin. It stood for constitutional nationalism, seeking change by political means.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.7] The SDLP withdrew from Stormont in July 1971 after the army shot dead two men in Derry, Séamus Cusack and Desmond Beattie.
[hist-ire5-s4.4.8] The Official IRA, which stressed a left-wing political strategy, declared a ceasefire in May 1972, which left the Provisionals as the main republican force.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.b] Loyalist paramilitary groups grew in response to the republican campaign and to the fear that the Union was under threat. They attacked Catholic civilians and republicans, which fed the cycle of reprisal.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.0] The Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) was revived in 1966 and killed three people that year. In the early years of the Troubles it carried out attacks on Catholic targets. It was a small, secretive killer group.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.1] The Ulster Defence Association (UDA), a loose umbrella of loyalist vigilante groups, was formed in 1971 and grew rapidly in working-class Protestant areas. It was a legal organisation until 1992, which marks it out from the UVF.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.2] The bombing of McGurk's Bar in Belfast on 4 December 1971 killed 15 Catholic civilians. The police first blamed the IRA, but the Police Ombudsman's report of 2011 found that the UVF was responsible.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.3] Loyalist groups claimed that their violence defended the Union and Protestant areas, but most of their victims were Catholic civilians, which is why their violence is called sectarian.
[hist-ire5-s4.5.4] William Craig founded the Ulster Vanguard movement in 1972, which held large rallies against the loss of Stormont's powers.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.b] Faulkner was the Prime Minister who introduced internment and then saw his government collapse under the pressure of violence and British policy. His career is followed here only up to March 1972.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.0] Brian Faulkner, born in 1921, was a Unionist businessman and politician. He was Minister of Home Affairs in 1959-63 and Minister of Commerce in 1963-69, and he resigned from O'Neill's cabinet in 1969.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.1] He lost the party vote to Chichester-Clark by 17 to 16 in 1969, but became Prime Minister on 23 March 1971, winning the party vote against William Craig.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.2] He asked London to authorise internment, which began on 9 August 1971 under the UK Prime Minister, Edward Heath. Faulkner defended it as necessary to stop the IRA, but it hardened Catholic opinion.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.3] In June 1971 he offered the opposition committee chairmanships at Stormont. The SDLP did not take up the offer and withdrew from Stormont in July 1971 after the Derry shootings, so his attempt to draw nationalists in failed.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.4] He talked with Heath about control of security and insisted that Stormont should keep its powers over policing and order. He and his ministers resigned on 24 March 1972 because he refused London's demand to transfer security powers.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.5] He was shaped by events: the violence, the SDLP withdrawal, Bloody Sunday and London's demand for security powers ended his government.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.6] Contemporary unionists praised his firmness on security, while nationalists saw internment as a deliberate attack on the Catholic community.
[hist-ire5-s4.6.7] He was a participant who wrote of events from the inside in his memoirs, published in 1978 after his death in 1977. Historians generally see him as a pragmatic, able, reforming politician whose attempts at reform were swamped by violence he could not contain.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.b] Internment was introduced on 9 August 1971 to break the IRA, but it was applied almost entirely to republicans, and the violence that followed killed civilians.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.0] Internment, the detention of suspects without trial, began on 9 August 1971 in Operation Demetrius, which arrested about 340 people in its first sweep. The intelligence used was poor, so many of those arrested had no paramilitary links.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.1] Those arrested were almost entirely Catholic, and no loyalist was interned until 1973. This is why nationalists saw internment as proof of bias.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.2] Interrogation methods known as the five techniques, which included hooding and sleep deprivation, were used on fourteen internees, known as the Hooded Men.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.3] The Compton and Parker inquiries examined the methods, and in 1978 the European Court of Human Rights ruled that the techniques were inhuman and degrading treatment, but not torture.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.4] At Ballymurphy in west Belfast, eleven civilians died during army operations of 9 to 11 August 1971, including a priest, Father Hugh Mullan. A coroner's inquest in 2021 found that those killed were innocent.
[hist-ire5-s4.7.5] Internment increased recruitment to the Provisional IRA. Separately, 1971 was the first year in which Troubles deaths passed 100.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.b] A civil rights march in Derry against internment ended with British paratroopers killing thirteen civilians, an event that deepened the conflict. Many nationalists lost confidence in the army and the state.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.0] The NICRA march of 30 January 1972 in Derry protested against internment and was banned by the authorities.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.1] Soldiers of the 1st Battalion, Parachute Regiment, entered the Bogside and fired on marchers and bystanders, killing thirteen people on the day, so a banned but peaceful protest became a massacre in nationalist eyes.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.2] A fourteenth man who was wounded died some months later, and most of the dead were shot while unarmed or while fleeing.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.3] The Widgery Report of 19 April 1972, by the Lord Chief Justice, largely accepted the army's account, and nationalists widely rejected it as a whitewash.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.4] The Saville Inquiry, reporting on 15 June 2010, found that soldiers of 1 PARA caused the deaths and injuries and that no casualty had posed a threat of causing death or serious injury.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.5] In his statement to the Commons that day, the Prime Minister, David Cameron, called the killings 'unjustified and unjustifiable' and apologised.
[hist-ire5-s4.8.6] The killings led to the burning of the British embassy in Dublin on 2 February 1972, and they increased recruitment to the Provisional IRA, because nationalist confidence in the army and the state broke down.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.b] Stormont was suspended and government was taken over by Westminster after Faulkner's government refused to hand security powers to London. This was the end of the Unionist government that had ruled since 1921.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.0] London, under Edward Heath, demanded that control of security be transferred to Westminster. Faulkner's government refused.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.1] Faulkner and his ministers resigned on 24 March 1972 in protest, and Westminster suspended the Stormont parliament from 30 March.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.2] The Northern Ireland (Temporary Provisions) Act 1972 introduced Direct Rule from 30 March 1972, placing the province under a Secretary of State.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.3] William Whitelaw was the first Secretary of State for Northern Ireland and governed with civil servants, two ministers of state and the army. The Stormont parliament was formally abolished in 1973 by the Northern Ireland Constitution Act.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.4] Whitelaw granted special category status to paramilitary prisoners in June 1972. He met leaders of the Provisional IRA in London on 7 July 1972 during a short ceasefire, but the talks failed.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.5] Unionists reacted with anger because they felt abandoned, and Craig's Vanguard held large rallies against the loss of Stormont's powers, while nationalists welcomed the end of Stormont.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.6] Direct Rule was meant to be temporary, but apart from the executive of 1 January to 28 May 1974 it continued until the 1990s, because the parties could not agree a power-sharing settlement acceptable to both communities.
[hist-ire5-s4.9.7] Direct Rule lasted because unionists would not accept an assembly with nationalist participation, nationalists would not accept a return to majority rule, and London wished to avoid a return to the old system.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.b] The IRA bombings of 21 July 1972 killed nine people in Belfast, and ten days later the army's Operation Motorman broke the IRA's no-go areas. Violence in 1972 reached its peak.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.0] On 21 July 1972, the Provisional IRA set off about twenty bombs in Belfast in the space of about an hour and a half, a day that became known as Bloody Friday.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.1] Nine people died in the bombings and about 130 were injured, and the attacks shocked public opinion in Britain and Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.2] Operation Motorman, launched on 31 July 1972 with about 12,000 soldiers, moved into the no-go areas (districts the army and police could not enter) in Belfast and Derry, including the Creggan and the Bogside.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.3] The operation cleared the barricades and restored government control, so areas that the IRA had controlled came back under the state.
[hist-ire5-s4.10.4] The IRA's campaign continued after Motorman, which is why 1972 was the deadliest year of the Troubles, with nearly 500 deaths.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.b] The Troubles had long-term causes, short-term triggers and factors that kept them going. A good answer separates these and judges which mattered most.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.0] Discrimination in housing, jobs and local voting gave the civil rights movement a sustained base of grievance, and unionist fear of change made reform hard.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.1] The state's security forces, the B Specials and the RUC, were seen by Catholics as partisan, which turned protest into a security issue.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.2] British misjudgement also played a part, because London had long left Northern Ireland's affairs alone and it misjudged the situation.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.3] The Provisional IRA gave the Catholic community a military organisation, and its campaign drew the British army into the conflict.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.4] Loyalist paramilitaries defended Protestant areas and attacked Catholic civilians, which increased the cycle of reprisal.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.5] The army and internment alienated Catholics, and internment and Bloody Sunday hardened attitudes on both sides and made political compromise harder.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.6] Stormont's inability to share power with nationalists meant there was no political institution capable of containing the violence.
[hist-ire5-s4.11.7] The decline of Stormont had many causes: civil rights protest, the failure to reform fast enough, the IRA campaign, and the British government's decision to take over security.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.b] Direct Rule can be judged as an achievement or a failure, and contemporaries judged it differently from later historians. Examiners reward paired points on both sides.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.0] Achievement: the end of Stormont removed an institution that nationalists had rejected since 1921, and it opened the way for later settlements.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.1] Failure: the Stormont government could not contain the violence, and its reliance on internment alienated the Catholic community.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.2] Contemporary view: unionists saw the suspension of Stormont as a betrayal by Westminster, while nationalists welcomed it as the end of an unfair system, and were relieved.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.3] Contemporary view: the British government saw Direct Rule as a temporary arrangement, but it proved to be a long-term one.
[hist-ire5-s4.12.4] Later view: historians argue that the 1972 settlement was forced by violence, and that the Unionist government's decline was a product of its own policies as well as republican action.

CANDIDATES hist-ire5-s7: 7. Republican and Loyalist terrorism and the Republic's responses to the Troubles, 1969–1993
[hist-ire5-s7.0.b] Three ideas run through the whole story of violence in the Troubles, and they rest on the divide between Northern Ireland's two communities. Knowing the terms makes it easier to judge each attack and each response.
[hist-ire5-s7.0.0] Northern Ireland had two main communities: unionists or loyalists, mostly Protestant, who wanted to stay in the United Kingdom, and nationalists or republicans, mostly Catholic, who wanted a united Ireland. Loyalists are unionists who accept violence, and republicans seek a united Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s7.0.1] Terrorism means violence against civilians and the state for political aims. Both republican and loyalist groups used it, which is why bombings and shootings are judged as political acts and not only as crimes.
[hist-ire5-s7.0.2] Sectarianism means hostility and violence between Protestants and Catholics, based on religious identity and political allegiance. It explains why so many victims were ordinary civilians killed only because of their community.
[hist-ire5-s7.0.3] Propaganda means murals, publications, broadcasts and statements used to win opinion, and also the broadcast bans that tried to deny paramilitaries a voice. Each side needed public support, so the fight for opinion ran alongside the fighting.
[hist-ire5-s7.0.4] The Troubles began in 1968 to 1969, and the British army was deployed in August 1969. Direct Rule, meaning government of Northern Ireland straight from London, came in March 1972.
[hist-ire5-s7.1.b] The Provisional IRA was the biggest republican paramilitary group and the one that killed the most people in the Troubles. It came out of a split in the older IRA and set itself the aim of ending British rule by force.
[hist-ire5-s7.1.0] The IRA split in December 1969 into the Provisional IRA, led by Seán Mac Stíofáin, and the Official IRA, led by Cathal Goulding. The Provisionals rejected the Officials' policy and took up armed defence of Catholic areas, and then a campaign against British rule.
[hist-ire5-s7.1.1] The Provisionals' aims were to end British rule in Northern Ireland and to bring about a united Ireland through a campaign of armed attacks, because they believed only force would make Britain withdraw.
[hist-ire5-s7.1.2] Their targets were British soldiers, members of the RUC (the Royal Ulster Constabulary, Northern Ireland's police) and the UDR (the Ulster Defence Regiment, a locally recruited army regiment), and also civilians.
[hist-ire5-s7.1.3] Their methods were bombings, shootings and car bombs.
[hist-ire5-s7.1.4] The Provisional IRA was responsible for the largest number of killings in the Troubles, followed by the loyalists of the UDA and UVF, so it dominates any account of who caused the deaths.
[hist-ire5-s7.2.b] The Provisional IRA's campaign in Northern Ireland hit soldiers, police, civilians and, in some attacks, Protestant communities. Several of the worst attacks also caused public revulsion, which damaged republican claims to be defending their community.
[hist-ire5-s7.2.0] Bloody Friday, 21 July 1972, saw more than twenty bombs go off in Belfast in about an hour and a half, killing nine people.
[hist-ire5-s7.2.1] On 27 August 1979 Lord Mountbatten was killed with three others, one of them a fourteen-year-old grandson, by a bomb on his boat at Mullaghmore, County Sligo.
[hist-ire5-s7.2.2] On the same day eighteen British soldiers were killed in two bombs at Warrenpoint, County Down. Mullaghmore and Warrenpoint were separate attacks, so keep them apart in an answer.
[hist-ire5-s7.2.3] The Enniskillen bombing, 8 November 1987, killed eleven people at a Remembrance Day ceremony. Gordon Wilson, whose daughter Marie died, publicly forgave the bombers, and this made the attack even more shocking.
[hist-ire5-s7.2.4] Attacks on Protestants included the La Mon House Hotel firebomb near Belfast in February 1978, which killed twelve people, and Teebane, County Tyrone, in January 1992, where eight Protestant workmen were killed.
[hist-ire5-s7.2.5] At Kingsmill, South Armagh, in January 1976, ten Protestant workmen were shot dead. The killings were attributed to republicans, were claimed under a cover name, and followed the killing of Catholics by loyalists.
[hist-ire5-s7.2.6] The Shankill Road fish shop bomb of 23 October 1993 killed ten people, including one of the two bombers. The target was thought to be a loyalist meeting upstairs, but the dead were people in the shop.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.b] From 1974 the IRA took its campaign to England, bombing pubs, London parks, a department store and a party conference. These attacks aimed to put pressure on the British public and government, and two of them led to wrongful convictions.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.0] The Guildford pub bombings of 5 October 1974 killed five people. The Guildford Four, convicted of them, were freed in 1989 when their convictions were overturned.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.1] The Birmingham pub bombings of 21 November 1974 killed 21 people. The IRA never formally claimed them, and the Birmingham Six, convicted of them, were freed in 1991 when their convictions were quashed.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.2] The IRA bombed Hyde Park and Regent's Park in London on 20 July 1982, killing eleven soldiers and bandsmen, who were chosen as military targets in public places.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.3] A bomb at Harrods in December 1983 killed six people, and it showed that civilian shoppers in central London could be caught up in the campaign.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.4] The Brighton bombing of 12 October 1984 killed five people. It was aimed at the Conservative government at its party conference, and Margaret Thatcher survived.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.5] The Warrington bombing of 20 March 1993 killed two boys, Johnathan Ball, aged 3, and Tim Parry, aged 12, and injured many more. Warrington was an IRA bomb, not a loyalist one.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.b] The Provisional IRA was not the only republican group, and it needed weapons and money to keep going. Arms from abroad and funds from the United States kept the campaign supplied.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.0] The Irish National Liberation Army (INLA) was a republican splinter group of the mid-1970s, separate from both the Provisional and Official IRA.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.1] The INLA killed 17 people in the Droppin' Well bar bombing at Ballykelly, County Londonderry, in December 1982. 11 of the dead were soldiers.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.2] A cargo of arms from Libya on the ship Eksund was seized by French customs in November 1987, which showed how the IRA was supplied for its campaign from abroad.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.3] NORAID, an Irish-American fundraising committee formed in 1970, gave republicans funds and publicity in the United States.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.b] Loyalist paramilitaries rose to defend the Union and to oppose a united Ireland and power sharing, meaning the sharing of government between unionists and nationalists. In practice most of their victims were Catholic civilians.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.0] The Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) was revived in 1966 and carried out attacks in the early years of the Troubles, including bombings and shootings.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.1] The Ulster Defence Association (UDA) was formed in 1971 as an umbrella for local loyalist groups. The Ulster Freedom Fighters (UFF), formed in 1973, was the UDA's cover name for killings.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.2] Their aims were to defend the Union with Britain, and to oppose a united Ireland and power sharing.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.3] Loyalist paramilitaries claimed to be defending the Union, but most of their victims were Catholic civilians with no paramilitary links. This is why their violence is described as sectarian.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.4] UVF and UDA gunmen attacked Catholic bars and homes in Belfast during the 1970s, and Catholic workers on the roads, because any Catholic could be chosen as a target.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.b] Loyalist attacks hit Catholic civilians in bars, in a Dublin street and on a country road. They became more frequent again in the early 1990s, when loyalists answered republican attacks with attacks on Catholic crowds.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.0] The bombing of McGurk's Bar in Belfast on 4 December 1971 killed 15 Catholic civilians, so the attack was sectarian. Police first blamed the IRA, but the Police Ombudsman reported in 2011 that the UVF was responsible.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.1] On 17 May 1974 car bombs in Dublin and Monaghan killed 33 people and an unborn child. This was the largest loss of life in a single day of the Troubles, and the attack was attributed to the UVF.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.2] The Miami Showband killings of 31 July 1975 saw UVF men kill three members of a popular band at a fake army checkpoint in County Down.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.3] On 5 February 1992 the UFF killed five Catholics at Sean Graham's bookmakers on the Ormeau Road, Belfast. Loyalist killing rose sharply in the early 1990s.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.4] The Greysteel massacre of 30 October 1993 saw the UFF kill eight people at the Rising Sun bar, at a Halloween dance. It came a week after the Shankill bomb, and was the UFF's response to it, not an IRA reprisal.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.b] Some loyalist groups carried out sectarian murders of Catholics that were marked by torture and brutality, and the Shankill Butchers were the most notorious. Their crimes shocked people in both communities.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.0] The Shankill Butchers were a UVF gang from the Shankill Road in west Belfast, who abducted Catholics at night and killed them between 1975 and 1977.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.1] Many victims were killed with knives and hatchets, which led to widespread revulsion in both communities because the killings were so cruel.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.2] The gang was led by Lenny Murphy, and eleven members were convicted in 1979 of a series of murders, receiving many life sentences.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.3] The gang's activities showed how sectarian violence could develop outside the formal paramilitary chains of command, and Murphy himself was shot dead in 1982.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.b] Counting the dead shows how widely responsibility for killing was spread. It also shows that most of the violence came from paramilitaries and not the state.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.0] About 3,500 people were killed between 1969 and 2001, about 3,000 of them by 1993. About half of the dead were civilians.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.1] Republican paramilitaries were responsible for about 60 per cent of the deaths, loyalist paramilitaries for about 30 per cent and the security forces for about 10 per cent.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.2] The worst year was 1972, with nearly 500 deaths, and the number of deaths fell sharply in the 1980s.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.3] Loyalist killing rose sharply in the early 1990s, with many attacks on Catholics, so loyalist groups became a larger share of the violence late in the period.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.b] The army and RUC fought the IRA with searches, arrests and surveillance, and with some armed ambushes. Several incidents led to allegations that the security forces had used lethal force unlawfully.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.0] The British army and RUC carried out searches, arrests and surveillance operations that disrupted IRA units, but the IRA continued to recruit and attack, so security pressure alone did not end the campaign.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.1] Republicans argued that the army's arrests and searches in Catholic areas increased recruitment to the IRA, because they alienated people who might otherwise have stayed out of the conflict.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.2] At Loughgall, County Armagh, on 8 May 1987, the SAS (the army's special forces) killed eight IRA men attacking an RUC station. A civilian who happened to drive by was also killed.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.3] The Gibraltar shootings of 6 March 1988 saw the SAS kill three unarmed IRA members, Mairéad Farrell, Daniel McCann and Seán Savage, and they raised questions about the use of lethal force.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.4] Loughgall and Gibraltar fed an allegation of a 'shoot to kill' policy, meaning that suspects were killed instead of arrested. It was an allegation, and the incidents are not proof of an official policy.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.5] The Stalker inquiry, from 1984, and the Sampson inquiry followed RUC shootings of unarmed men in 1982, and they added to the 'shoot to kill' controversy.
[hist-ire5-s7.10.b] Both republicans and loyalists used murals, publications and political statements to shape public opinion. The British and Irish governments responded by trying to deny the paramilitaries publicity on air.
[hist-ire5-s7.10.0] Republican murals celebrated IRA volunteers, so they became a central feature of the political landscape, and loyalist murals in Protestant areas celebrated the UVF and UDA and presented their violence as a defence of loyal communities.
[hist-ire5-s7.10.1] Republicans used publications such as An Phoblacht to present their case, and their propaganda stressed the British occupation.
[hist-ire5-s7.10.2] Irish-American support for republicans provided funds and publicity for the republican campaign, so the cause was heard beyond Ireland.
[hist-ire5-s7.10.3] In October 1988 the British government banned the broadcast of the voices of spokesmen for eleven organisations, including Sinn Féin, the IRA and loyalist groups. Broadcasters used actors' voices, and the ban was lifted in 1994.
[hist-ire5-s7.10.4] The British ban was intended to deny publicity to terrorist groups, but critics said it restricted freedom of expression, and the use of actors showed how easy it was to get round.
[hist-ire5-s7.10.5] In the Republic, Section 31 of the Broadcasting Authority Act 1960 was used through ministerial orders that barred broadcast interviews with the IRA and later Sinn Féin. It was allowed to lapse in 1994, the same year as the British ban.
[hist-ire5-s7.11.b] The Republic's government (the Taoiseach is its prime minister) answered the violence of August 1969 with words, aid and a border presence. The Arms Crisis of 1970 then split the governing party, Fianna Fáil, and Bloody Sunday brought an angry public reaction.
[hist-ire5-s7.11.0] On 13 August 1969 the Taoiseach Jack Lynch broadcast that the government 'can no longer stand by' while Catholics were attacked in Belfast and Derry, and he asked for a United Nations peacekeeping force.
[hist-ire5-s7.11.1] The Irish army set up field hospitals near the border. Refugees from Belfast and Derry were housed in army camps such as Gormanston, County Meath.
[hist-ire5-s7.11.2] In May 1970 Lynch dismissed Charles Haughey and Neil Blaney on 6 May, after allegations that money for relief was being used to import arms for Northern nationalists. This was the Arms Crisis, and it divided Fianna Fáil.
[hist-ire5-s7.11.3] At the Arms Trial in autumn 1970, Haughey, Captain James Kelly, John Kelly and Albert Luykx were tried for conspiracy to import arms. They were acquitted in October 1970.
[hist-ire5-s7.11.4] After Bloody Sunday, 30 January 1972, when 13 were killed in Derry, the Irish government declared a day of mourning and recalled its ambassador from London for consultations.
[hist-ire5-s7.11.5] The British embassy in Merrion Square, Dublin, was burned on 2 February 1972 during a demonstration, as anger over Bloody Sunday boiled over.
[hist-ire5-s7.12.b] As the Northern conflict spread south, the Republic tightened its laws against the IRA. The coalition of Liam Cosgrave also backed the Sunningdale settlement, to show Dublin's support for cross-border cooperation.
[hist-ire5-s7.12.0] The IRA was declared an unlawful organisation in 1936, and the Offences against the State Act 1939 created the Special Criminal Court, which has no jury and three judges.
[hist-ire5-s7.12.1] The Special Criminal Court was revived in 1972 to try paramilitary offences.
[hist-ire5-s7.12.2] The Offences against the State (Amendment) Act 1972 was passed in December 1972, after loyalist car bombs in Dublin. It allowed a chief superintendent's statement that he believed a person to be an IRA member to be accepted as evidence.
[hist-ire5-s7.12.3] Cosgrave's Fine Gael and Labour coalition, in power from 1973 to 1977, signed the Sunningdale Agreement in December 1973, which included a Council of Ireland. Garda (Irish police) cooperation with the RUC increased.
[hist-ire5-s7.12.4] The Criminal Law (Jurisdiction) Act 1976 allowed certain offences committed in Northern Ireland to be tried in the Republic, so suspects who fled south could still face a court.
[hist-ire5-s7.12.5] The IRA killed the British ambassador, Christopher Ewart-Biggs, in July 1976, and the Emergency Powers Act 1976 followed, giving the state stronger powers in response.
[hist-ire5-s7.12.6] President Cearbhall Ó Dálaigh referred the Emergency Powers bill to the Supreme Court. He resigned in October 1976 after a government minister criticised him.
[hist-ire5-s7.13.b] In the 1980s the Republic's governments worked with Britain on a political settlement while extradition, the handing over of wanted people to another state, stayed a source of tension.
[hist-ire5-s7.13.0] Charles Haughey, Taoiseach in 1979 to 1981, 1982 and 1987 to 1992, held a summit with Margaret Thatcher in Dublin in December 1980 to study 'the totality of relationships' between the two islands.
[hist-ire5-s7.13.1] Garret FitzGerald, Taoiseach in 1981 to 1982 and 1982 to 1987, pursued the New Ireland Forum of 1983 to 1984. He signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement with Thatcher at Hillsborough on 15 November 1985, which set up an Intergovernmental Conference.
[hist-ire5-s7.13.2] Unionists complained that the Republic gained too much influence through the Intergovernmental Conference. The Agreement also put cross-border security cooperation on a formal footing.
[hist-ire5-s7.13.3] Fianna Fáil under Haughey voted against the Agreement in 1985, but kept it in place when it returned to power in 1987.
[hist-ire5-s7.13.4] Extradition was a recurring dispute between Dublin and London. The Extradition (European Convention on the Suppression of Terrorism) Act 1987 was passed in December 1987, after the Enniskillen bombing.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.b] Judging terrorism means weighing what it did against what it set out to achieve. The best answers state a view, pairing impact with failure and contemporary opinion with later judgement.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.0] Impact: terrorism killed large numbers of civilians and security force members, and it made political compromise harder by hardening attitudes on all sides.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.1] Impact: republican and loyalist campaigns shaped British and Irish policy, because security measures and political initiatives were both shaped by the need to contain violence.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.2] Failure: neither the IRA nor the loyalist groups achieved their stated aims, a united Ireland or the defence of the Union, and the main IRA and loyalist ceasefires came in 1994, after the period covered here.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.3] Failure: terrorism deepened sectarian divisions, and its victims in both communities created lasting grievances.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.4] Contemporary view: the British and Irish governments condemned terrorism and sought to isolate paramilitaries from political influence.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.5] Contemporary view: republicans argued that the IRA was defending the Catholic community and fighting an occupying power, while loyalists claimed to defend the Union.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.6] Later view: historians point out that most of the killing was done by paramilitary groups, not the state, and that the long campaign persuaded many in each group that violence could not win.
[hist-ire5-s7.14.7] Later view: the Republic's responses leaned on security measures, such as the Special Criminal Court and extradition, while its political aims were pursued through Sunningdale and the Anglo-Irish Agreement.
