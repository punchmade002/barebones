TARGET hist-eur3-s6 (part 1 of 2): 6. France: the Third Republic, 1920-1940, and the Vichy state
[hist-eur3-s6.0.b] The Third Republic was France's parliamentary democracy from 1870 to 1940. Its institutions and parties explain why so much of the politics of the 1920s and 1930s looked the way it did.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.0] The Republic was proclaimed in 1870, after France's defeat by Prussia, and its constitutional laws date from 1875. Democracy here means government by elected representatives answerable to the voters, with free elections, parties and civil rights, although only men could vote.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.1] Parliament had two houses: an elected Chamber of Deputies and an indirectly elected Senate, which could block laws, so reforms needed to pass both. Women had no vote until 1944-45.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.2] The President, elected for seven years by the Chamber and Senate meeting together, had a largely ceremonial role. Real power lay with the Prime Minister (called the President of the Council) and the ministers.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.3] Governments needed a majority in the Chamber and fell when they lost a vote, so ministers lived at the mercy of the deputies.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.4] No single party ever won a majority, so every government was a coalition. The right was made up of conservative and Catholic groups, and the Radical Party stood in the centre-left.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.5] The Radical Party, anticlerical (opposed to the Church's influence) and strong among small-town and rural voters, was the hinge of most coalitions because governments needed its votes whether they leaned left or right. It was the largest force in most governments of the 1920s and early 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.6] The left consisted of the Socialists (the SFIO) and, from 1920, the Communists (the PCF), founded at the Congress of Tours in December 1920 after a split in the Socialist party.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.7] French society was deeply divided: left against right, Catholic against secular (anticlerical) France, workers against employers, Paris against the provinces. These divisions shaped every crisis of the period, because each camp distrusted the other's government.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.b] France won the First World War but paid heavily for it, and its politics after 1919 were dominated by the demand that Germany pay. The first post-war government, the Bloc National, was built around that aim.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.0] About 1.4 million French soldiers were killed in 1914-1918, and the north-east, where much of the fighting took place, was devastated, with many villages and farms destroyed. A falling birth rate and heavy war debts added to the damage.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.1] Alsace-Lorraine returned to France by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919. Even so, France felt a deep fear of Germany, so it wanted the treaty enforced in full.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.2] Reconstruction of the devastated areas was financed by borrowing, in the expectation that German reparations (payments for war damage) would repay the loans. The slogan of the early 1920s, 'Germany will pay' ('L'Allemagne paiera'), summed up this hope.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.3] The Bloc National, a coalition of conservative, centre and Catholic parties, won the November 1919 election and governed until 1924. It took a firm line on Germany and against the left.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.4] Clemenceau left politics, his career ended, after he was defeated in the presidential election of January 1920 by Paul Deschanel.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.5] Budget deficits, large debts and a falling franc marked the early and mid 1920s. This made the government's reliance on German payments a financial gamble as well as a political one.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.b] When Germany fell behind with reparations, France chose force. The result showed the limits of French power and left it short of friends.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.0] Raymond Poincaré was Prime Minister from January 1922 to June 1924 and took a hard line towards Germany, which suited the parties of the Bloc National.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.1] In January 1923, after Germany defaulted on reparations (failed to pay what was due), French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, a German industrial area.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.2] Germany answered with passive resistance, refusing to co-operate with the occupiers. Britain disapproved openly and refused to take part.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.3] The Dawes Plan of 1924 reorganised German payments, so the French troops were no longer needed and were withdrawn by 1925.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.4] The occupation brought some payments, but also isolation, cost and a weaker franc, so it ended without securing full reparations.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.b] While governments argued about money, French foreign policy pursued one aim: protection against a revival of German power.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.0] France made an alliance with Poland in 1921 and a treaty with Czechoslovakia in 1924, to gain friends against Germany in eastern Europe.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.1] Aristide Briand was Foreign Minister for most of the years 1925 to 1932 and favoured agreement with Germany rather than force, in contrast to Poincaré's hard line.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.2] The Locarno treaties of 1925, which aimed to keep the peace in western Europe, and the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, in which states renounced war, were his best-known achievements.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.3] Briand shared the 1926 Nobel Peace Prize with the German statesman Stresemann.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.4] France also decided in this period to build a fortified line along the German border, the Maginot Line, as a further guarantee of security.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.b] Voters turned left in 1924, but a financial crisis undid the new government. Poincaré's return then restored confidence and gave France a few calm, prosperous years.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.0] The Cartel des Gauches, a coalition of Radicals and Socialists, won the May 1924 election, and Édouard Herriot, a Radical, was Prime Minister from June 1924 to April 1925.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.1] The Cartel fell in April 1925 after a run on the franc (people rushing to sell francs) and a financial crisis. It was called the 'wall of money': banks and investors had lost confidence in a left-wing government.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.2] A run of short governments followed in the next years, and the franc crisis of July 1926 brought Poincaré back at the head of a Government of National Union.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.3] Poincaré raised taxes and cut spending, which restored confidence, so the franc was stabilised in practice by late 1926.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.4] The 'Poincaré franc' was fixed in law in June 1928 at about one-fifth of its 1914 gold value. The late 1920s were prosperous, and Poincaré retired in July 1929 because of ill health.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.b] Governments in the Third Republic were short-lived, which made steady policy difficult. Several features of the system, working together, explain why.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.0] There were many parties and no majority party, so governments were fragile coalitions that lasted only as long as every partner agreed.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.1] The Radicals switched between left and right partners, so withdrawing their support could topple a government quickly.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.2] The Chamber held frequent votes of no confidence, and governments changed very often, many lasting only months. Ministers were often replaced after a few months in office.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.3] The President had the power to dissolve the Chamber, but it was not used after 1877, so deputies could bring down governments without facing an election themselves.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.4] The Senate blocked reforms, so reforming governments lost momentum, and financial crises, especially in 1924-26, brought governments down.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.5] The President's role was largely ceremonial, so policy was decided by the Prime Minister and the parliamentary majority, and when the majority broke up there was no one else to hold the government together.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.b] France's economy was less affected by the Depression at first than Britain's or Germany's, but the slump arrived later and was prolonged by government policy.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.0] France's economy rested on small family farms and small family firms, and the country held large gold reserves, which cushioned it against the immediate effects of the Wall Street Crash.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.1] The slump hit in earnest from 1931, with industrial production falling sharply. It then lasted longer than in Britain or Germany, and recovery was slow until the late 1930s. Farmers and small businesses suffered most.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.2] Official unemployment stayed lower than in Britain or Germany, partly because foreign workers left and some unemployed returned to the land. It still remained a serious problem.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.3] Governments kept the franc at its 1928 gold value, and France led the 'gold bloc' of countries that stayed on gold. To defend the currency they used deflation, cutting spending, wages and prices, which deepened the slump and hurt industry and farming.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.4] Pierre Laval, Prime Minister from June 1935 to January 1936, issued decree-laws in 1935 that cut public spending and salaries, so the policy of deflation continued.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.5] The franc was devalued (its gold value lowered) only under the Popular Front in autumn 1936, after which exports recovered slightly.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.6] The left (Radicals and Socialists) won the May 1932 election and Herriot was Prime Minister again, but governments fell in quick succession. Contempt for parliament and 'the politicians' grew, which prepared the ground for the leagues.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.b] As faith in parliament fell, leagues offered an alternative. These were anti-parliamentary, nationalist and mostly right-wing movements.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.0] Action française was founded in 1899 and led by Charles Maurras. It was monarchist and anti-Semitic, had opposed the republic since before 1914, and its street militants were called the Camelots du Roi.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.1] The Jeunesses Patriotes were led by Pierre Taittinger, and the Solidarité française was funded by the perfume maker François Coty, so the leagues had wealthy backers as well as street militants.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.2] The Croix-de-Feu was a veterans' league led by Colonel François de La Rocque. It was the largest league, with hundreds of thousands of members by 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.3] Francisme, led by Marcel Bucard, was a smaller group that was openly fascist, which made it different from the larger leagues that claimed only to defend the nation.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.4] Economic hardship and fear of war split French opinion into left and right camps, so the leagues found support on the right while Communists and Socialists gained from the unity forged after February 1934.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.5] Historians disagree on how far the leagues threatened democracy. René Rémond argued that they belonged to France's traditional right, while Zeev Sternhell (Neither Right nor Left, 1983) argued that fascist ideas had deep roots in France.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.6] After the leagues were dissolved in 1936, the Croix-de-Feu re-formed as the Parti Social Français, which grew into a large party.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.b] A financial scandal in January 1934 gave the leagues their chance. The riot that followed brought down a government and pushed the left to unite.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.0] Alexandre Stavisky was a swindler with political protection who sold worthless bonds through the municipal pawnshop (the Crédit municipal) of Bayonne.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.1] He was found shot dead in January 1934. The police called it suicide, but left and right both suspected he had been silenced, and the affair suggested corruption reaching into the Radical Party.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.2] The scandal forced the resignation of the Radical Prime Minister Camille Chautemps in January 1934, and the leagues used it to attack the republic as corrupt.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.3] The new Prime Minister, Édouard Daladier, dismissed Jean Chiappe, the Paris Prefect of Police, who was favoured by the right. This angered the right further.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.4] On 6 February 1934 the leagues, including the Croix-de-Feu, Action française and the Solidarité française, and veterans' groups marched on the Chamber of Deputies. Police opened fire and about 15 people were killed and over a thousand injured.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.5] Daladier resigned on 7 February, the first government brought down by street violence. A government of national union under Gaston Doumergue, with Pétain as War Minister, was formed to restore order.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.6] The left read 6 February as an attempted fascist coup. It responded with a general strike and mass demonstration on 12 February 1934, which led to left unity.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.b] Fear of fascism did what years of argument had not, and brought Socialists, Communists and Radicals together. Their alliance, the Popular Front, won power in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.0] The Socialist-Communist unity-of-action pact of July 1934 was possible because the Comintern, the international body of Communist parties directed from Moscow, had reversed the Communists' hostility to other left parties. Its Seventh Congress confirmed the popular front line in 1935.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.1] The Radicals joined in 1935, shown at the mass rally of 14 July 1935, and a joint programme followed in January 1936. The slogan was 'Bread, Peace and Liberty' ('Pain, Paix, Liberté').
[hist-eur3-s6.9.2] The Popular Front won a majority in the elections of 26 April and 3 May 1936, and Léon Blum became Prime Minister on 4 June 1936. Socialists and Radicals sat in the cabinet.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.3] The Communists supported the government in parliament but took no ministries, so they should not be listed as ministers.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.4] Blum was the first Socialist and the first Jewish Prime Minister, but not the first left-of-centre one, because Herriot, a Radical, had led the Cartel in 1924.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.5] Blum was attacked by the anti-Semitic right and was beaten up by Action française supporters in February 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.9.6] Three women became under-secretaries, Cécile Brunschvicg, Irène Joliot-Curie and Suzanne Lacore, although women could not yet vote.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.b] Workers expected a Popular Front government to improve their lives, and they pressed the point with strikes. The employers gave way, and the Matignon Agreements and the June laws were the biggest social gains of the period.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.0] The strikes began in May 1936 (from about 11 May), before Blum took office. The main wave and the factory occupations came after he formed his government on 4 June, and they peaked in early-to-mid June.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.1] Many were sit-down strikes, in which workers occupied their factories instead of leaving them, and about two million workers were out at the height of the wave.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.2] The pressure of the wave pushed the employers into the Matignon Agreements, reached on 7-8 June 1936 in the middle of the wave to end it. They were signed at the Hôtel Matignon, the Prime Minister's residence, with Blum chairing the talks between employers and unions.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.3] The agreements gave wage rises of about 7 to 15 per cent, union recognition (the right of unions to represent workers), collective agreements and elected workers' delegates.
[hist-eur3-s6.10.4] Laws that month brought in the 40-hour week and two weeks' paid holiday, which meant that for the first time many workers could take a holiday.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.b] Beyond Matignon, the government used its majority to reshape the economy, farming, schools and leisure, and to deal with the leagues.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.0] The leagues were dissolved in June 1936, because the government feared the threat they had shown on 6 February 1934.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.1] The Wheat Office was set up in 1936 to stabilise farm prices, which helped farmers hurt by deflation, for example wheat growers.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.2] The state gained greater control of the Bank of France, and the arms industry was part-nationalised (partly taken into state ownership).
[hist-eur3-s6.11.3] The school-leaving age was raised to 14 in 1936 by the education minister Jean Zay.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.4] Léo Lagrange, under-secretary for sport and leisure, developed a leisure and sport policy that included cheap rail tickets for holidays.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.5] A rearmament programme began in September 1936, and the franc was devalued in autumn 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.11.6] The plans for public works spending were limited by financial pressure and capital flight.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.b] The Popular Front's gains were soon eroded by economic trouble, and the Spanish Civil War split the coalition itself.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.0] Capital fled abroad because investors distrusted the government, and rising prices ate up the wage gains of workers.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.1] Industrial output was slow to recover, and the 40-hour week limited production, including the production of arms.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.2] Blum adopted a policy of non-intervention in the Spanish Civil War in August 1936. This angered the Communists and much of his own party, but Britain backed non-intervention and the Radicals opposed intervening, so he feared splitting the coalition and the country.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.3] Roger Salengro, the Interior Minister, killed himself in November 1936 after a campaign against him in the right-wing press.
[hist-eur3-s6.12.4] The anti-Semitic right hated Blum, and a phrase attributed to parts of the right said they preferred Hitler to Blum. The origin of the phrase is unclear, so do not treat it as anyone's recorded words.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.b] Economic pressure and the Senate's opposition ended the Popular Front's reforming period. Its social gains were then undone one by one.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.0] Blum announced a pause in reform in February 1937, because financial pressure made further spending impossible.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.1] He resigned in June 1937 when the Senate refused him emergency financial powers. Governments under Chautemps followed.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.2] Blum led a brief second government in March and April 1938, and Daladier became Prime Minister in April 1938 (he held office until March 1940), which ended the coalition.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.3] The finance minister Paul Reynaud's decree-laws of November 1938 undid the 40-hour week.
[hist-eur3-s6.13.4] The general strike of 30 November 1938 failed, and its failure ended the Popular Front era.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.b] France's defences in the 1930s reflected memories of the First World War and a fear of losing another generation of young men. The result was a defensive strategy with fatal weaknesses.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.0] The Maginot Line was named after André Maginot, the War Minister. A law funding it passed in January 1930, and it was built mainly in the 1930s, so it was funded from 1930 and built mainly in the 1930s. It was very expensive.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.1] It was a system of concrete forts and underground works along the Franco-German border, designed to hold an invader while the army mobilised.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.2] It reflected defensive thinking after Verdun and the shortage of manpower, because France could not afford the huge casualties of attacking again.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.3] It did not extend along the Belgian border, because Belgium was an ally until it declared neutrality in 1936. It was also weak opposite the Ardennes, a wooded region thought unsuitable for tanks.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.4] The army's doctrine was slow, methodical and defensive. Tanks were spread among infantry units instead of being concentrated.
[hist-eur3-s6.14.5] De Gaulle's book of 1934, Vers l'armée de métier (in English The Army of the Future), argued for a professional armoured force. It was ignored.
[hist-eur3-s6.15.b] As Hitler's Germany rearmed, France looked for allies but did not act alone. Its caution helped to make war more likely.
[hist-eur3-s6.15.0] France signed the Franco-Soviet Pact in May 1935, because it hoped for an ally against Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.15.1] When Hitler remilitarised the Rhineland on 7 March 1936, France took no action, and its reliance on Britain grew.
[hist-eur3-s6.15.2] Daladier signed the Munich Agreement on 29-30 September 1938, which showed how far France followed Britain's policy of appeasement.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.b] France declared war in September 1939 but did little fighting for eight months. When the German attack came in May 1940, the French plan collapsed within weeks.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.0] France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939, after the invasion of Poland. During the Phoney War (the drôle de guerre) there was little fighting, and the army under General Maurice Gamelin, the commander-in-chief, waited behind the Maginot Line.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.1] The Communist Party was banned in September 1939 after the Nazi-Soviet Pact, which the Communists had to defend. Paul Reynaud replaced Daladier as Prime Minister in March 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.2] The German attack began on 10 May 1940. Gamelin and Pétain thought the Ardennes forest difficult terrain for tanks, so the main French and British forces moved into Belgium on the Dyle plan.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.3] German armoured divisions came through the Ardennes and crossed the Meuse at Sedan on 13 May and reached the Channel coast at Abbeville on 20 May, cutting off the northern armies.
[hist-eur3-s6.16.4] Maxime Weygand replaced Gamelin on 19 May. The British Expeditionary Force and many French troops, about 338,000 Allied troops in all, were evacuated from Dunkirk between 26 May and 4 June.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.b] Once the northern armies were cut off, resistance collapsed. Within six weeks the government had asked for peace and the Republic was at an end.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.0] Italy declared war on France on 10 June 1940. Paris was declared an open city (undefended) and occupied on 14 June, and millions of civilians fled south in the 'exodus'.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.1] The government left Paris for Bordeaux, and Reynaud resigned on 16 June. Pétain became Prime Minister and asked Germany for an armistice on 17 June.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.2] The armistice was signed with Germany on 22 June 1940 at Compiègne, in the same railway carriage as the 1918 armistice, so that Germany's humiliation of 1918 was reversed. A separate armistice with Italy followed on 24 June.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.3] France was divided into an occupied zone, the north and the Atlantic coast, about three-fifths of the country, and an unoccupied zone in the south. Alsace-Lorraine was in practice annexed by Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.4] France had to pay the costs of occupation, at first 400 million francs a day, and prisoners of war were to stay in Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.5] The armistice army was limited to 100,000 men, and the fleet was to be disarmed but kept by France.
[hist-eur3-s6.17.6] About 1.8 million French soldiers were taken prisoner in 1940, and well over a million were still in Germany years later.
[hist-eur3-s6.18.b] The Third Republic did not fall to a coup. Its own parliament voted away its powers, which makes 10 July 1940 a key date in the story of democracy in France.
[hist-eur3-s6.18.0] After the armistice the government settled at Vichy, a spa town in the unoccupied zone, in July 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.18.1] On 10 July 1940 the National Assembly, the Chamber and Senate meeting together in the Grand Casino at Vichy, voted by 569 to 80, with about 20 abstentions, to give full powers to Marshal Philippe Pétain to draw up a new constitution.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s3: 3. The Nazi state in peace, 1933-1939, and Hitler's foreign policy
[hist-eur3-s3.0.b] Everything the Nazi state did between 1933 and 1939 makes sense only against what Hitler wanted and the position he started from. He came to power through a deal with conservatives, but he had a programme that he had already written down.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.0] Hitler was appointed Chancellor on 30 January 1933 by President Hindenburg, at the head of a coalition. Only three Nazis (Hitler, Frick and Göring) sat in the first cabinet, so Papen, the Vice-Chancellor, and the conservatives expected to control him.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.1] In Mein Kampf (two volumes, 1925 and 1926, mostly written in Landsberg prison after the Munich Putsch) Hitler set out his aims: overturn Versailles, unite all Germans in one state, win Lebensraum (living space) in the east, and fight a racial struggle.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.2] The Treaty of Versailles (1919) limited the German army to 100,000 men and banned conscription, an air force, tanks and submarines, which is why rearmament was Hitler's first goal. The Rhineland was demilitarised and the Saar placed under League of Nations rule for 15 years from 1920.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.3] Versailles also forbade union (Anschluss) with Austria, made Danzig a Free City under the League, and used the Polish Corridor to separate East Prussia from the rest of Germany. Each of these became a Hitler target between 1933 and 1939.
[hist-eur3-s3.0.4] While Germany was weak, Hitler was cautious: he needed time to rebuild the army and economy, so his first steps were presented as demands for fair treatment, not as plans for conquest.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.b] Within months of his appointment Hitler removed the legal and political limits on his power, using emergency decrees, intimidation and a vote of the Reichstag itself. Each step built on the one before.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.0] The Reichstag fire of 27 February 1933 was blamed on a Communist plot, because Marinus van der Lubbe, a young Dutch communist (later executed), was found in the building. This gave the Nazis the excuse they needed to attack their left-wing opponents.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.1] The Decree for the Protection of People and State (the Reichstag Fire Decree) of 28 February 1933 suspended freedom of speech, press and assembly and allowed arrest without trial, so opponents could be locked up with no legal protection.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.2] Thousands of Communists were arrested after the fire, and the Communist Party was effectively destroyed in early 1933, removing one of the Nazis' main rivals before the next vote.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.3] In the election of 5 March 1933, despite terror and propaganda, the Nazis won only about 44 per cent of the vote. They had a majority only with their Nationalist (DNVP) allies, so Hitler still needed another way to rule freely.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.4] The Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 (formally the Law for the Removal of the Distress of People and Reich) let the cabinet make laws without the Reichstag, even ones departing from the constitution, for four years.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.5] The Act passed 444 to 94. The Communist deputies had been arrested or had fled, the Catholic Centre Party voted for it, and only the Social Democrats voted against, which shows how far intimidation had worked.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.6] Do not mix up the two measures: the Reichstag Fire Decree suspended people's rights, while the Enabling Act removed the Reichstag's law-making role.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.b] After the Enabling Act Hitler used Gleichschaltung (coordination: bringing every institution into line with Nazi aims) to take over the rest of German life. It ended in a state where only one party and one organisation for workers were allowed.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.0] Reich governors (Reichsstatthalter) were appointed over the German states in April 1933, and the Law for the Reconstruction of the Reich of 30 January 1934 abolished the state parliaments, so power was concentrated in Berlin.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.1] The Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service of 7 April 1933 removed Jews and political opponents from the civil service. This put the officials who ran the country under Nazi control and made loyalty the test for a job.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.2] Gleichschaltung also brought the courts, universities and cultural bodies under Nazi control, which meant that teaching, law and the arts could no longer act independently of the regime.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.3] On 2 May 1933 the SA and SS occupied trade union offices and the unions were dissolved. Their members were not abolished by law but seized and absorbed, with workers then pushed into the German Labour Front, set up in May 1933 under Robert Ley.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.4] The Law against the Formation of New Parties of 14 July 1933 made the NSDAP the only legal party. The Communists and Social Democrats had already been banned, and the other parties dissolved themselves in June and July 1933.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.b] By 1934 Hitler's remaining rival at the heart of the movement was his own storm-troop army, the SA. Removing its leadership in one weekend won him the army's support and cleared the way to supreme power.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.0] Ernst Röhm, the SA chief of staff, led an organisation of several million members by 1934, far more than the 100,000-man army. He talked of a "second revolution" and wanted the SA to form the core of a new people's army.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.1] Conservatives were alarmed by SA violence. Vice-Chancellor Papen's Marburg speech of 17 June 1934 criticised that violence and the calls for a second revolution, adding to the pressure on Hitler to act.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.2] Between 30 June and 2 July 1934, Hitler ordered the murder of Röhm and other SA leaders. The killings were carried out by the SS and Gestapo with army help.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.3] Hitler also used the purge to settle other scores: Gregor Strasser (a former Nazi leader), Kurt von Schleicher (former Chancellor) and his wife, Gustav von Kahr, Erich Klausener and Edgar Jung (author of the Marburg speech) were killed.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.4] At least 85 people were killed, possibly more, because the true total is uncertain.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.5] On 3 July 1934 the Law on Measures of State Self-Defence declared the killings legal after the event, which gave murder a cover of legality.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.6] The army leadership, under Defence Minister Werner von Blomberg, approved the purge in return for the removal of the SA as a rival force, which tied the army to Hitler.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.b] Hindenburg's death gave Hitler the last piece of formal power. From then on the state rested on his person, and the law, the army and the party all swore obedience to him.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.0] President Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934. The offices of President and Chancellor were merged and Hitler took the title Führer and Reich Chancellor. He was never called President. A plebiscite on 19 August 1934 approved the change, with about 90 per cent in favour.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.1] On 2 August 1934 soldiers swore an oath of unconditional obedience to Hitler personally, as Führer, rather than to the constitution. This tied the armed forces to Hitler personally.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.2] The Führer principle (Führerprinzip) meant authority flowed from the top down, with absolute obedience upward, and propaganda built a cult of Hitler's personal leadership around it.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.3] In practice the state was a polycracy: overlapping party and state bodies (ministries, Gauleiters, the SS, the Four Year Plan office) competed for power and for Hitler's favour. This rivalry meant decisions could be chaotic.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.4] A dictatorship is rule by one person or party without legal limits or free elections. By August 1934 Hitler had removed the Reichstag's law-making role, rival parties and independent organisations and so fitted this definition.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.b] The regime did not rely on consent alone. A police system outside the law, led by Heinrich Himmler, gave it the power to detain anyone it chose.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.0] Heinrich Himmler was Reichsführer-SS (head of the SS, the party's elite force) from 1929. By 1934 he controlled the political police of almost all the states, and in June 1936 he became Chief of German Police, which merged party and state police power in his hands.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.1] Reinhard Heydrich led the SD (Sicherheitsdienst), the SS Security Service, which watched for opponents and gathered intelligence on the population.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.2] The Gestapo (Secret State Police) was founded in April 1933 in Prussia by Hermann Göring. Himmler and Heydrich took it over in April 1934.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.3] The Gestapo could arrest people without trial or appeal, a procedure the regime called protective custody (Schutzhaft). Because there was no legal challenge, nobody could force the police to explain or end a detention.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.4] Historians such as Robert Gellately have shown that the Gestapo was small and relied heavily on denunciations by ordinary Germans. Its reach therefore depended partly on people reporting their neighbours, not on police numbers alone.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.5] Do not confuse the organisations: the SA were the brownshirts under Röhm, sidelined after 1934; the SS was the party elite force under Himmler; the SD was SS intelligence under Heydrich; the Gestapo was the secret state police.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.b] Detention and the courts completed the system. Camps held people the regime wanted out of sight, and the courts were changed so political cases could be decided without legal protection.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.0] Dachau, near Munich, opened in March 1933 and was the first concentration camp run by the SS and the model for later camps. The SA also ran makeshift early camps in 1933, which is why Dachau is called the first SS camp.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.1] Other camps followed: Sachsenhausen in 1936 and Buchenwald in 1937, so the system grew as the regime's controls hardened.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.2] The first prisoners were political opponents such as Communists, trade unionists and Social Democrats. Later Jehovah's Witnesses, "asocials", homosexuals and Jews were held too, so the camps widened as the idea of who threatened the community widened.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.3] Special Courts (Sondergerichte) were set up in March 1933 and the People's Court (Volksgerichtshof) in 1934 for treason cases. Political verdicts came with little legal protection for defendants, so courts reinforced terror instead of limiting it.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.b] The regime's popularity depended on jobs, and its foreign aims depended on arms. Its economic policy tried to deliver both, and hide how much it was spending on rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.0] Hjalmar Schacht was Reichsbank president from March 1933 and Economics Minister from August 1934. He financed rearmament through Mefo bills, credit notes issued through a dummy company (Mefo) to pay arms firms, which kept military spending off the budget.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.1] The New Plan of September 1934 controlled foreign trade through bilateral trade agreements and exchange controls (limits on buying foreign money), which gave priority to imports of raw materials for rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.2] Public works, including autobahns, housing and military building, created jobs. The first autobahn section, Frankfurt to Darmstadt, opened in May 1935. The Nazis expanded and publicised the autobahns, but plans and a first stretch (Cologne to Bonn, 1932) predate 1933.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.3] The Reich Labour Service (RAD) became compulsory for young men in June 1935, with six months' work, and conscription was reintroduced in March 1935. Both took young men out of the job market, which helped to lower the unemployment figures.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.b] Unemployment fell dramatically, but the figures hide a more complicated story, and from 1936 the economy was steered openly towards war.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.0] Registered unemployment was about six million in January 1933 and under one million by 1937. By 1938-39 labour shortages had replaced unemployment, because rearmament and conscription absorbed workers.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.1] The figures hide a lot: women and Jews were removed from them, conscription and the Labour Service took men out of the labour market, wages were held down and there were no free unions to bargain.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.2] Recovery had begun before 1933 and world trade was recovering, so Nazi policy alone did not end the Depression in Germany. Historians therefore debate how much credit the regime deserves.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.3] The Four Year Plan, announced in September 1936 with Hermann Göring as Plenipotentiary, aimed to have the army ready for action and the economy fit for war within four years. This priority is often paraphrased as guns before butter.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.4] It also aimed at autarky (self-sufficiency, so Germany would not depend on imports in wartime), through synthetic oil from coal, synthetic rubber (buna) and the state-owned Reichswerke Hermann Göring (1937), which mined low-grade German iron ore.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.5] The plan missed its targets, and Germany still relied on imports in 1939.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.6] Schacht resigned as Economics Minister in November 1937 after clashing with Göring over the pace and cost of rearmament. He was dismissed from the Reichsbank in January 1939, and by 1939 the economy was strained.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.b] The Volksgemeinschaft (people's community) was the idea of a racially pure national community that stood above class. The regime tried to win workers and shape children so that they would belong to it.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.0] The German Labour Front under Robert Ley replaced the free unions, so workers had an organisation controlled by the party instead of one that could bargain for them.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.1] Its Strength Through Joy (Kraft durch Freude) section, set up in November 1933, offered cheap holidays, cruises, concerts, theatre and sport, so workers felt rewarded. Its Beauty of Labour section improved factory canteens, lighting and washrooms.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.2] The Volkswagen ("people's car", the KdF-Wagen) scheme had workers pay weekly instalments, but the factory turned to war production and no savers received a car.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.3] The Hitler Youth, led by Baldur von Schirach (Reich Youth Leader from 1933), had four sections: the Jungvolk (boys 10-14), the Hitler Youth (boys 14-18), the Jungmädelbund (girls 10-14) and the League of German Girls, the BDM (girls 14-18).
[hist-eur3-s3.9.4] The Hitler Youth Law of 1 December 1936 made the Hitler Youth the only legal youth organisation, and membership became compulsory from 1939, so by the end of the decade almost no young person could avoid its training.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.5] Schools taught racial science and nationalist history, and textbooks were rewritten. Teachers were pressed to join the National Socialist Teachers' League, and teachers who were Jewish or politically unreliable were dismissed.
[hist-eur3-s3.10.b] The national community was defined by who it left out as much as who it included. Women were given a fixed role, those judged outsiders were excluded, and the regime relied on consent as well as terror.
[hist-eur3-s3.10.0] Nazi ideology assigned women the roles of wife and mother, often summed up as "Kinder, Küche, Kirche" (children, kitchen, church). The slogan is older than the Nazis, who did not coin it, but it describes their policy.
[hist-eur3-s3.10.1] Marriage loans from 1933 (Law for the Reduction of Unemployment, June 1933) gave 1,000 marks, with a quarter cancelled for each child born. The wife was expected to give up work, which also freed jobs for men.
[hist-eur3-s3.10.2] The Cross of Honour of the German Mother, instituted in December 1938, rewarded large families: bronze for four children, silver for six and gold for eight.
[hist-eur3-s3.10.3] Married women were pushed out of civil service posts from 1933, and from 1936 women could not be judges or public prosecutors. But labour shortages in the late 1930s drew many women back into work.
[hist-eur3-s3.10.4] The regime excluded those it judged inferior. The law of 14 July 1933 on hereditarily diseased offspring led to about 400,000 people being forcibly sterilised by 1945, and the Nuremberg Laws of 15 September 1935 took citizenship from Jews and banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans.
[hist-eur3-s3.10.5] Opponents and "asocials" were sent to camps, so belonging to the community depended on conforming.
[hist-eur3-s3.10.6] The regime was also popular. Jobs, order and foreign successes won consent, while opposition was limited and mostly passive. After the Anschluss, the slogan "One People, One Reich, One Leader" summed up the mood.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.b] Hitler's first foreign moves rejected the Versailles restrictions step by step. Each time the western powers protested but did not act, which taught him how far he could go.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.0] On 14 October 1933 Germany left the Geneva Disarmament Conference and the League of Nations, claiming unequal treatment. A plebiscite in November 1933 approved the move, with about 95 per cent in favour.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.1] The German-Polish non-aggression pact of 26 January 1934, for ten years.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.2] On 25 July 1934 Austrian Nazis murdered Chancellor Dollfuss in a failed coup in Vienna. Mussolini moved troops to the Brenner Pass, so Hitler backed off and disowned the coup. Kurt Schuschnigg succeeded Dollfuss.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.3] The Saar, governed by the League since 1920, held a plebiscite on 13 January 1935 in which about 90 per cent voted to return to Germany. It was reunited on 1 March 1935, a major propaganda success.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.4] In March 1935 Göring revealed the existence of the Luftwaffe (the German air force) to foreign journalists, and on 16 March Hitler announced conscription, with the army to grow to 36 divisions (about 550,000 men). Both breached Versailles.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.5] The Stresa Front of Britain, France and Italy condemned German rearmament in April 1935 and promised to uphold Locarno and Austrian independence, but took no effective action and broke down after Italy invaded Abyssinia in October 1935.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.6] The Anglo-German Naval Agreement of 18 June 1935 allowed Germany a surface fleet up to 35 per cent the size of the British. Britain accepted it without consulting France, so a Versailles limit was broken with British consent.
[hist-eur3-s3.11.7] The agreement gave Hitler the diplomatic recognition he had sought and weakened the united front against him, which encouraged him to test the western powers further.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.b] From 1936 Hitler moved from undoing Versailles to open expansion, supported by new friends and a growing army. The western powers' failure to resist the Rhineland set the pattern for what followed.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.0] On 7 March 1936 German troops reoccupied the demilitarised Rhineland (the reoccupation), breaking Versailles and the Locarno Treaties of 1925. France, in the middle of an election campaign, and Britain did not resist; the League only condemned it. Hitler offered new peace pacts the same day.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.1] It was a gamble, and the generals were nervous, but its success increased Hitler's popularity at home and his confidence that Britain and France would not fight.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.2] The Rome-Berlin Axis (agreed in October 1936 and named by Mussolini in a speech on 1 November 1936) and the Anti-Comintern Pact between Germany and Japan (25 November 1936, with Italy joining in November 1937) aligned Germany with Italy and Japan.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.3] The German Condor Legion (air and ground units) fought for Franco in the Spanish Civil War from 1936 to 1939 and bombed Guernica in April 1937.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.4] At the Hossbach meeting of 5 November 1937 Hitler told Blomberg, Fritsch, Raeder, Göring and Neurath that Germany's need for space could be solved only by force, at the latest by 1943-45 and earlier if chances arose, starting with Austria and Czechoslovakia.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.5] The minutes were written up days later by Colonel Friedrich Hossbach, so the meeting is known as the Hossbach Memorandum.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.6] In February 1938 Blomberg and Fritsch were removed after personal scandals. Hitler took personal command of the armed forces through a new High Command (OKW), and Joachim von Ribbentrop replaced Neurath as Foreign Minister.
[hist-eur3-s3.12.7] The Anschluss followed: Hitler pressed Schuschnigg at Berchtesgaden in February 1938 to admit Austrian Nazis, including Seyss-Inquart as Interior Minister. Schuschnigg announced a plebiscite for 13 March, cancelled it under pressure and resigned on 11 March; German troops entered Austria on 12 March 1938.
[hist-eur3-s3.13.b] After Austria, Hitler turned to Czechoslovakia. The Sudeten crisis showed how far Britain and France would go to avoid war and how Czechoslovakia was left to pay the price.
[hist-eur3-s3.13.0] Austria was declared part of the Reich on 13 March 1938, and a plebiscite on 10 April 1938 showed over 99 per cent approval, a result produced in an atmosphere of Nazi control.
[hist-eur3-s3.13.1] Konrad Henlein's Sudeten German Party, backed and funded by Berlin, demanded autonomy and then union with Germany for the three million Sudeten Germans, giving Hitler a pretext to threaten Czechoslovakia.
[hist-eur3-s3.13.2] Chamberlain flew to meet Hitler at Berchtesgaden on 15 September 1938 and again at Bad Godesberg on 22 September, where Hitler raised his demands to immediate occupation.
[hist-eur3-s3.13.3] The Munich conference met on 29 September 1938 and the agreement was signed in the early hours of 30 September by Chamberlain, Daladier (France), Hitler and Mussolini. Czechoslovakia was not represented and the Soviet Union, which had a treaty with Czechoslovakia, was not invited.
[hist-eur3-s3.13.4] Czechoslovakia had to hand over the Sudetenland to Germany between 1 and 10 October 1938, and lost its frontier fortifications, leaving it defenceless.
[hist-eur3-s3.13.5] Chamberlain and Hitler also signed a declaration that Britain and Germany would never go to war again. On his return Chamberlain said it was "peace for our time".
[hist-eur3-s3.13.6] Churchill told the Commons in October 1938 that Munich was "a total and unmitigated defeat".
[hist-eur3-s3.14.b] Hitler's seizure of Prague in 1939 broke the logic of appeasement, and within months the road to war was open.
[hist-eur3-s3.14.0] On 15 March 1939 German troops occupied Prague. Bohemia and Moravia became the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, and Slovakia, which declared independence on 14 March, became a German client state.
[hist-eur3-s3.14.1] Memel was taken from Lithuania in March 1939, and Italy invaded Albania in April 1939.
[hist-eur3-s3.14.2] Prague broke the Munich promises and was Hitler's first seizure of non-German land. It ended the belief that his claims were limited to Germans and led Britain to abandon appeasement.
[hist-eur3-s3.14.3] Hitler demanded Danzig and a road and rail link across the Polish Corridor. Britain announced a guarantee of Polish independence on 31 March 1939, with France joining it, and the Anglo-Polish alliance followed on 25 August 1939.
[hist-eur3-s3.14.4] The Nazi-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact (the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact) was signed in Moscow on 23 August 1939 by Ribbentrop and Molotov. It promised ten years of non-aggression, and a secret protocol divided Poland and placed the Baltic states and Bessarabia in spheres of influence.
[hist-eur3-s3.14.5] The pact removed the danger of a war on two fronts and was surprising because the two regimes were ideological enemies.
[hist-eur3-s3.14.6] Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and Britain and France declared war on 3 September.
[hist-eur3-s3.15.b] Hitler's foreign policy was driven by racial ideas and by the goal of Lebensraum in the east. Historians argue over whether this meant a plan for war from the start or opportunism.
[hist-eur3-s3.15.0] Lebensraum (living space) was Hitler's goal: Germany needed land in Eastern Europe, to be settled by a racially pure population, with the Slavs to be subjugated. This made war with the east, not just revision of Versailles, the aim.
[hist-eur3-s3.15.1] Herrenvolk (master race) was the idea that Germanic people were racially superior. It underpinned both domestic policy and plans for the conquest and resettlement of Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur3-s3.15.2] Totalitarianism and dictatorship: Hitler's ideology claimed to reach every part of life, which is why the regime tried to control work, schools and families as well as politics.
[hist-eur3-s3.15.3] Appeasement was British and French policy to satisfy Germany's grievances through concessions, based on the belief that Versailles had been unfair and that war must be avoided.
[hist-eur3-s3.15.4] Historians disagree on whether Hitler planned a general war from 1933 or responded to opportunities. Trevor-Roper argued Hitler pursued consistent war aims set out in Mein Kampf (the plan or programme view).
[hist-eur3-s3.15.5] A.J.P. Taylor's The Origins of the Second World War (1961) argued that Hitler was an opportunist with traditional German aims and that the war was a blunder. The book was widely attacked.
[hist-eur3-s3.15.6] The terms intentionalist (Hitler's plan) and functionalist (circumstances and rival agencies) are borrowed from the wider debate about the Nazi state.
[hist-eur3-s3.16.b] Appeasement was the policy of giving way to Hitler's demands to keep the peace. It was supported for several reasons and judged very differently at the time and afterwards.
[hist-eur3-s3.16.0] Britain and France appeased Hitler for several reasons: guilt over Versailles, a horror of another war, a fear of bombing, rearmament that was not ready, reluctant dominions, a belief that communism was the greater threat, and the cost to the economy.
[hist-eur3-s3.16.1] Neville Chamberlain, Prime Minister from May 1937 (succeeding Stanley Baldwin) to May 1940, was its main practitioner, so Munich is linked to his name. Churchill, out of office in the 1930s, was its main critic.
[hist-eur3-s3.16.2] At the time appeasement was popular: Chamberlain was cheered on his return from Munich.
[hist-eur3-s3.16.3] Later it was condemned: the pamphlet Guilty Men (1940), by "Cato" (Michael Foot and others), blamed the appeasers, but some historians defend it as buying time to rearm.
[hist-eur3-s3.17.b] The war gave the regime new radical powers, and Germany's economy and society were geared to total war.
[hist-eur3-s3.17.0] Albert Speer, Hitler's architect, became Minister for Armaments and Munitions in February 1942 after the death of Fritz Todt. Arms output rose sharply until 1944 despite bombing.
[hist-eur3-s3.17.1] After the surrender at Stalingrad, Goebbels called for total war in his Sportpalast speech in Berlin on 18 February 1943. In January 1943 women aged 17 to 45 were required to register for war work.
[hist-eur3-s3.17.2] Millions of foreign forced labourers and prisoners of war worked in Germany by 1944.
[hist-eur3-s3.17.3] On 20 July 1944 Colonel Claus Schenk von Stauffenberg planted a bomb at Hitler's headquarters, the Wolf's Lair near Rastenburg, East Prussia. Hitler was only injured, the coup in Berlin collapsed that night and Stauffenberg was shot.
[hist-eur3-s3.17.4] Thousands were arrested and many executed after the July plot.
[hist-eur3-s3.17.5] Hitler stayed in command to the end and killed himself in his Berlin bunker on 30 April 1945.
[hist-eur3-s3.18.b] Hitler's rule from 1933 to 1939 combined a consolidated dictatorship at home with a foreign policy of rearmament, expansion and war. He directed each stage himself.
[hist-eur3-s3.18.0] Hitler was born on 20 April 1889 at Braunau am Inn, Austria. He served in a Bavarian regiment from 1914 to 1918 and won the Iron Cross First Class in 1918.
[hist-eur3-s3.18.1] He led the Nazi Party from July 1921, attempted the Munich (Beer Hall) Putsch in November 1923, wrote Mein Kampf in Landsberg prison in 1924, and became Chancellor on 30 January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s3.18.2] From 1933 to 1939 he built a dictatorship, rearmed, left the League, took the Rhineland, achieved the Anschluss with Austria, received the Sudetenland under the Munich Agreement, occupied Prague and invaded Poland.
[hist-eur3-s3.18.3] He set the aims and took the key decisions on the purge, the Rhineland, the Anschluss, Czechoslovakia and Poland. He was a participant who directed each stage, not a witness.
[hist-eur3-s3.18.4] His style of rule was to rise late, avoid paperwork and encourage rivalry between subordinates, who competed to anticipate his wishes: what Ian Kershaw calls "working towards the Führer".
[hist-eur3-s3.18.5] At home, he was popular after the Saar, the Rhineland and the Anschluss. Abroad, David Lloyd George visited him in September 1936 and praised him, and many saw a reasonable revisionist, but Churchill warned repeatedly of German rearmament and saw a threat.
[hist-eur3-s3.18.6] Historians' views have changed. Alan Bullock (Hitler: A Study in Tyranny, 1952) saw an opportunist adventurer seeking power, a view later revised. Trevor-Roper stressed consistent war aims, and Taylor argued Hitler was an opportunist.
[hist-eur3-s3.18.7] Hans Mommsen called Hitler a "weak dictator", and Martin Broszat (The Hitler State) saw a chaos of rival agencies. Kershaw's two volumes (1998 and 2000) combine the views through "working towards the Führer".
[hist-eur3-s3.19.b] Examiners reward judgement more than narrative. For each theme, set the achievements against the limits, and be ready to weigh Hitler's own responsibility against other causes.
[hist-eur3-s3.19.0] Totalitarian state: totalitarian aims and terror (SS, Gestapo, camps, courts) show control, but polycracy, a small Gestapo dependent on denunciations, and consent won by jobs and success show it was less total than it looked.
[hist-eur3-s3.19.1] Economy, achievement: unemployment was cut, recovery was achieved and rearmament was built up.
[hist-eur3-s3.19.2] Economy, limit: hidden unemployment, controlled wages, strain and shortages by 1939, and a recovery that had begun before 1933.
[hist-eur3-s3.19.3] Foreign policy, achievement: by 1939 Hitler had overturned Versailles and gained territory without war.
[hist-eur3-s3.19.4] Foreign policy, limit: it led to a general war that Germany was not ready for in 1939 and to the destruction of Germany.
[hist-eur3-s3.19.5] Causes of the war: Hitler's aims and decisions (Mein Kampf, Hossbach, Prague, Poland) made war likely, while Versailles resentment, the weak League, appeasement, the Nazi-Soviet Pact and Mussolini shaped when and how it came.
[hist-eur3-s3.19.6] Plan or opportunism: the usual balanced verdict is fixed long-term aims with opportunist timing and methods, which fits both Trevor-Roper's programme and Taylor's opportunism.
[hist-eur3-s3.19.7] Munich: contemporaries felt relief and cheered Chamberlain in 1938, but it is now generally judged a failure that gave away Czechoslovakia's defences and left Hitler convinced that the western powers would not fight.
