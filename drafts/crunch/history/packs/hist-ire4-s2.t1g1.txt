[hist-ire4-s1.0.b] Emigration was already an established feature of Irish life before 1845, but it changed in who left and where they went.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.0] Ulster Presbyterians were the first large group of Irish emigrants to North America, sailing to the American colonies through the 18th century.

[hist-ire4-s1.0.2] Pre-Famine emigrants were often Protestant small farmers, weavers and tradesmen from Ulster and the midlands who could pay their own fares and had some capital.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.3] From the 1830s Catholic emigrants from the south and west made up a growing share, and they were poorer and less skilled than earlier emigrants.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.4] British North America (Canada) was the main transatlantic destination before 1845 because fares there were the cheapest, and many emigrants then walked or sailed on into the United States.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.5] Britain received the largest number of the poorest emigrants, because the crossing to Liverpool or Glasgow cost only a few shillings.
[hist-ire4-s1.0.6] The 1841 census counted about 420,000 Irish-born people in Britain, concentrated in Liverpool, Glasgow, Manchester and London.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.0] Seasonal migration: tens of thousands of labourers from Mayo, Donegal, Galway and other western counties crossed each summer to work on English and Scottish harvests and returned home with cash to pay the rent.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.1] Irish labourers in Britain worked as navvies on canals and railways, as dockers, as building labourers and in textile mills.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.2] Irish districts grew in the poorest parts of British cities, such as Little Ireland in Manchester and St Giles in London.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.3] In North America Irish men dug canals such as the Erie Canal (opened 1825) and built roads and early railways, while Irish women went into domestic service.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.4] Push factors: subdivision of holdings into tiny plots, a population that had risen to about 8.2 million by 1841, dependence on the potato, the decline of domestic linen and wool spinning, and evictions for rent arrears.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.5] Pull factors: higher wages and demand for labour in industrial Britain and North America, cheap land in Canada, and letters from earlier emigrants describing work abroad.
[hist-ire4-s1.1.6] Timber ships carried Canadian timber to Britain and Ireland and returned westwards nearly empty, so they took emigrants cheaply as human ballast.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.b] Repeated failures of the potato turned a steady flow into a mass exodus.

[hist-ire4-s1.2.3] Between 1.5 and 2 million people emigrated between 1845 and 1855 (estimates vary), the great majority to the United States, with several hundred thousand to British North America and to Britain.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.4] The census showed Ireland's population falling from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.5 million in 1851.
[hist-ire4-s1.2.5] Famine emigrants were mostly Catholic, poor, unskilled and Irish-speaking in many cases, and came heavily from the west and south-west; many travelled as whole families rather than as single young adults.
