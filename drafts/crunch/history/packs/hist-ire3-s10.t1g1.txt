[hist-ire3-s10.0.b] Both parts of Ireland were brand new in the early 1920s, and each government used language, religion, schools and culture to build or defend a sense of who its people were. The South promoted a Gaelic and Catholic identity, the North a Protestant and British one.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.0] Northern Ireland was created in 1920-21 under the Government of Ireland Act 1920, and the Free State came into being in December 1922, so both states had to decide what kind of society they wanted.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.1] In the South the cultural programme came from the Gaelic revival before 1914, led by the Gaelic League (which promoted the Irish language) and the GAA (the Gaelic Athletic Association, which promoted Irish games). The Church was the dominant institution, and after 1922 the Protestant minority was small.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.2] In the North the state was run by Unionists, who wanted to keep the link with Britain, and Catholics were about a third of the population, so they were a large minority and not a tiny one.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.3] Conformity means pressure to follow the accepted values, here Catholic and Gaelic ones, enforced through law, schools and public opinion. This is why those who did not fit, such as Protestants or writers, could feel left out.
[hist-ire3-s10.0.4] Censorship is state control of what may be shown or read, carried out by film and publications censors who had the power to ban. Discrimination is unequal treatment of one group; in the North it was aimed at Catholics, including in school funding and control.

[hist-ire3-s10.1.2] Ernest Blythe, Minister for Finance from 1923 to 1932, was a leading advocate of Irish, and the civil service was required to learn it.

[hist-ire3-s10.2.3] Irish remained a symbol of national identity, so the state kept it as a school subject even where it failed as a spoken language.

[hist-ire3-s5.0.0] The Treaty, signed on 6 December 1921, gave Ireland dominion status (self-government within the Commonwealth, as Canada had), an oath to the Crown, British retention of three ports, and partition, because Northern Ireland could opt out and a Boundary Commission would fix the border.

[hist-ire3-s5.0.5] The argument was mainly over the oath and the Crown, that is, republic against dominion status, and not mainly over partition.

[hist-ire3-s5.14.1] Fianna Fáil was founded on 16 May 1926 at the La Scala Theatre, Dublin. De Valera called the oath ‘an empty political formula’, so it could be sworn in order to enter the Dáil.
[hist-ire3-s5.14.2] In the June 1927 election Cumann na nGaedheal won 47 seats and Fianna Fáil 44. In the September 1927 election Cumann na nGaedheal won 62 and Fianna Fáil 57.
[hist-ire3-s5.14.3] Fianna Fáil TDs signed the oath book and entered the Dáil on 12 August 1927, under the threat of the Electoral Amendment Act. If they had refused, they would have been disqualified, so they had to choose between entering the Dáil and leaving politics.
[hist-ire3-s5.14.4] By entering the Dáil, the main opposition accepted the Free State's parliamentary system, which gave the state legitimacy.
[hist-ire3-s5.15.b] The real test of democracy came when the government that won the war lost an election. In 1932 power passed peacefully to the other side of the Civil War.
[hist-ire3-s5.15.0] The world Depression from 1929 and falling farm prices hurt the Free State. Cumann na nGaedheal's cuts and balanced budgets lost it support.
[hist-ire3-s5.15.1] Fianna Fáil promised protective tariffs, an end to the land annuity payments to Britain and the removal of the oath.
[hist-ire3-s5.15.2] In the February 1932 general election Fianna Fáil won 72 seats, Cumann na nGaedheal 57 and Labour 7.
[hist-ire3-s5.15.3] De Valera was elected President of the Executive Council on 9 March 1932 with Labour support.
[hist-ire3-s5.15.4] Cosgrave's government handed over power peacefully, and the army and police accepted the change. This was the first democratic transfer of power in the new state, just nine years after a civil war.
[hist-ire3-s5.16.b] The war ended in 1923, but its effects lasted much longer. It shaped the party system and left memories that many people preferred not to discuss.
[hist-ire3-s5.16.0] The two sides became the two main parties. Fine Gael was formed in 1933 from Cumann na nGaedheal, the Centre Party and the Blueshirts, with Eoin O'Duffy as first leader. Fianna Fáil came from the anti-Treaty side.
[hist-ire3-s5.16.1] In the August 1923 election Cumann na nGaedheal won 63 seats and the republicans (Sinn Féin) 44. The republicans abstained, so they did not take their seats.
[hist-ire3-s5.16.2] De Valera was arrested at Ennis on 15 August 1923 and released on 16 July 1924.
[hist-ire3-s5.16.3] Thousands of republican prisoners were held. A hunger strike from October to November 1923 ended without success, and two prisoners died.
[hist-ire3-s5.16.4] Both sides committed atrocities, and families and communities were divided, because neighbours and relatives had fought on opposite sides, and this made the war a subject of silence in Irish public life.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.b] Cosgrave led the pro-Treaty side from 1922 to 1932 and built the state's institutions. He is judged as a cautious administrator who secured democracy but did little about the Depression.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.0] William Thomas Cosgrave was born in Dublin on 6 June 1880, the son of a publican in James's Street. He was a Sinn Féin member of Dublin Corporation from 1909.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.1] In 1916 he was in the South Dublin Union garrison. His death sentence was commuted to penal servitude.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.2] He won the Kilkenny City by-election in August 1917 and was Minister for Local Government in the Dáil ministry from April 1919.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.3] He became head of the government in August 1922, as chairman of the Provisional Government, and was President of the Executive Council from 6 December 1922 to 9 March 1932.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.4] As a participant, he led the government through the Civil War executions, the Mutiny, the Boundary Commission crisis, the assassination of O'Higgins and the 1932 handover. The war and the killings of Hales and O'Higgins pushed him toward harsher measures.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.5] His cautious style suited a state that needed stability, but it was less suited to the Depression.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.6] Contemporaries: supporters admired his honesty and defence of law and stability, but critics called him conservative and authoritarian, described his government as ‘the Treaty government’, and attacked the executions, the emergency laws and economic conservatism.
[hist-ire3-s5.17.7] Historians: Tom Garvin (1922: The Birth of Irish Democracy, 1996) praises him for defending democracy against militarism, while John M. Regan (The Irish Counter-Revolution, 1921-1936, 1999) calls the government a conservative, elitist counter-revolution. Michael Laffan (Judging W.T. Cosgrave, 2014) gives a balanced assessment.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.b] Judgements depend on whether the test is survival and legitimacy or social and economic performance. Examiners reward paired points: achievements against limits, and contemporary against later views.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.0] Achievement: the state survived civil war, mutiny and assassination, and had working institutions by 1927, such as the Garda, the courts and the civil service.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.1] Achievement: the army was brought under civil control after the Mutiny of 1924, which meant the state's policy would be decided by elected ministers.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.2] Achievement: the Shannon Scheme and the ESB gave the state a modern electricity system.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.3] Achievement: the peaceful transfer of power in 1932, accepted by the army and police, showed that democratic institutions had become accepted.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.4] Limit: the government used military courts, executions and public safety laws that opponents described as repression.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.5] Limit: its economic conservatism, based on low spending and balanced budgets, did little for the Depression's hardship, and the Boundary Commission outcome of 1925 showed that hopes of the Treaty on the north were unrealistic. The Treaty symbols, the oath and the Governor-General, were also left in place.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.6] Contemporary view: supporters saw the government as the defender of law and stability, while opponents called it ‘the Treaty government’.
[hist-ire3-s5.18.7] Later view: historians disagree over whether it was a successful counter to militarism (Garvin) or a conservative counter-revolution (Regan).

CANDIDATES hist-ire3-s3: 3. Partition and Northern Ireland under the Unionist Party, 1912–1949
[hist-ire3-s3.0.b] Partition (the division of Ireland into Northern Ireland and Southern Ireland) came about because unionists would not accept Home Rule (self-government for Ireland within the United Kingdom), British governments failed to find an all-Ireland settlement, and the 1918 vote showed a divided island.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.0] Unionist resistance came first: the Ulster Covenant (signed 28 September 1912) and the Ulster Volunteer Force, or UVF (an armed unionist militia formed in January 1913), showed that Home Rule for all Ireland could be imposed only by force.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.1] Exclusion debates followed, all assuming that some Ulster counties would be left out of Home Rule. These were the county option (Asquith's proposal of March 1914, letting Ulster counties opt out for six years), the failed Buckingham Palace Conference (July 1914) and Lloyd George's 1916 proposals.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.2] The 1916 plan collapsed over whether exclusion would be permanent or temporary: nationalists were told it would be temporary, while unionists were told it would be permanent. Unionist and Conservative members of the cabinet, including Lansdowne and Long, then insisted on permanence.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.3] The First World War and the 1916 Rising changed nationalist politics, and the Irish Convention of 1917–18 failed to produce agreement. Sinn Féin, the rising republican party, boycotted it.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.4] The 1918 general election (December) confirmed the divide, because Sinn Féin won 73 seats, the Irish Parliamentary Party 6, and unionists 26, mostly in the north-east.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.5] The six counties were chosen, not all nine of Ulster. Only Antrim, Down, Armagh and Londonderry had unionist majorities, Tyrone and Fermanagh had narrow nationalist majorities, and Donegal, Cavan and Monaghan were left out. Six counties gave a safer unionist majority than nine would have.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.6] Northern Ireland had about 1.25 million people (1926 census), of whom about two-thirds were Protestant and about one-third Catholic. This one-third minority is why the new state's treatment of Catholics mattered so much later.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.7] British policy made partition the practical answer. Lloyd George's coalition, with a large Conservative element, was unwilling to coerce Ulster, so the Government of Ireland Act of 1920 gave the unionists the offer of their own parliament.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.b] The Act of 1920 created two Home Rule parliaments, but only the northern one worked. The Treaty then let Northern Ireland stay out of the Free State, and the Boundary Commission failed to move the border.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.0] The Government of Ireland Act (royal assent December 1920) set up a parliament for the six counties of Northern Ireland and one for the 26 counties of Southern Ireland. A Council of Ireland was meant to link the two, but it never worked.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.1] In the Northern election of 24 May 1921 the Unionists won 40 of 52 seats. Sinn Féin and the Nationalists won six each but refused to take part, an abstention (staying away from the parliament).
[hist-ire3-s3.1.2] Craig became Prime Minister on 7 June 1921, and George V opened the Northern parliament in Belfast on 22 June 1921, which gave the new state royal approval.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.3] Southern Ireland's parliament never worked, because Sinn Féin won 124 of 128 seats in the same election (the other four went to Dublin University unionists) and treated it as an election to the Dáil, the republican assembly.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.4] Under the Treaty (the 1921 agreement creating the Irish Free State) Northern Ireland could opt out of the Free State. It did so on 7 December 1922, the day after the Free State came into being on 6 December.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.5] The Treaty also provided for a Boundary Commission (set up 1924) to review the border. Richard Feetham, a South African judge, was chair, with Eoin MacNeill for the Free State and J.R. Fisher for Northern Ireland, appointed by Britain because Craig refused to nominate.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.6] The commission's expected report was leaked to the Morning Post in November 1925, and MacNeill resigned. On 3 December 1925 the three governments (British, Free State and Northern Ireland) agreed in the Tripartite Agreement that the border would stay as it was.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.7] The Tripartite Agreement also released the Free State from its share of the imperial (UK) debt and abolished the powers of the Council of Ireland. So the border was fixed, and the Council had no further role.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.b] The new state was born amid sectarian rioting and an IRA campaign. The government answered with a Protestant-recruited police force and exceptional powers that stayed in place long after the crisis.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.0] Expulsions of Catholic workers, and of some Protestant labour supporters, from the Belfast shipyards began in July 1920 amid IRA violence and unionist anger. Thousands of people were affected.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.1] Riots in Derry in June 1920 and in Belfast through 1920–22 killed about 500 people, most of them Catholics, and drove many families from their homes.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.2] The Dáil organised a boycott of Belfast goods in 1920. It hurt Catholic workers and shopkeepers in Belfast more than it hurt unionist business.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.3] The Ulster Special Constabulary, formed in late 1920, had full-time A Specials, part-time B Specials and reserve C Specials. It was recruited almost entirely from Protestants.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.4] The Royal Ulster Constabulary (RUC) replaced the older Royal Irish Constabulary (RIC) in June 1922. A third of its places were notionally reserved for Catholics, but Catholics never filled them.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.5] The Craig–Collins pacts of January and March 1922 failed. After the IRA offensive on the border in May 1922 the government interned republicans (imprisoned them without trial).
[hist-ire3-s3.2.6] The Civil Authorities (Special Powers) Act of April 1922 allowed internment, curfews, the banning of meetings and organisations, and arrest without warrant. It was renewed each year and made permanent in 1933.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.7] Some of the interned men were held on the prison ship Argenta in 1922.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.b] The Ulster Unionist Party governed without interruption from 1921. It also changed the electoral rules in ways that kept it in power, while nationalists took little part in the system.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.0] Nationalists abstained from Stormont (the Northern Ireland parliament, named after its later building) at first. The Nationalist Party under Joe Devlin took its seats in 1925, but Sinn Féin remained abstentionist.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.1] In 1922 Tyrone and Fermanagh county councils, which refused to recognise the Belfast government, were dissolved and replaced by commissioners (officials appointed by the government), as were other nationalist-controlled councils.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.2] The Local Government Act of 1922 abolished proportional representation, or PR (a voting system that gives parties seats in proportion to their votes), in local elections, and boundaries were redrawn.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.3] PR for Stormont elections was abolished in 1929 and replaced by single-member constituencies (one winner per area). This helped the Unionists and hurt Labour and independents.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.4] Derry Corporation stayed under unionist control from 1923 to 1969 although Catholics were a majority of the city's population. This is the best-known case of gerrymandering, drawing electoral boundaries to favour one side.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.5] The Unionist Party won every Stormont election from 1921 to 1969 by large margins. A bloc vote on the border issue kept Protestant workers within the party.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.6] Stormont Parliament Buildings, on the outskirts of Belfast, were opened by the Prince of Wales in 1932. Before then the parliament had sat in the Presbyterian Assembly's College in Belfast, so the new building was a symbol of the permanence of the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.b] Discrimination means unequal treatment of Catholics in jobs, housing, policing and local government. Nationalists said that the state practised it, while unionists denied it and argued that Catholics refused to take part in the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.0] Catholics were about a third of the population but held a small share of senior posts, for example in the civil service, the judiciary and local government.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.1] The shipyards and heavy industry employed few Catholics. Protestant workers' networks and the Orange Order (a Protestant fraternal society) helped to secure jobs for their own members, which kept the pattern in place.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.2] Local councils controlled by unionists allocated public housing, so nationalists argued that houses went to Protestant families first.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.3] In speeches in the 1930s (in 1933 and 1934) Basil Brooke urged unionists not to employ Catholics. His remarks are widely quoted as evidence of the attitudes of leading unionists.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.4] On 24 April 1934 Craig told the Stormont parliament, in reply to claims that the South was a Catholic state, that he boasted of “a Protestant Parliament and a Protestant State”. It is the best-known statement of the attitudes of the unionist government.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.5] Historians disagree. Michael Farrell, in The Orange State (1976), sees systematic discrimination, while others accept discrimination in some fields but stress that it was uneven and not centrally planned.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.6] Unionists defended the system by pointing to nationalist non-participation. Abstention in the early years and the Catholic Church's separate schools left the minority with a limited voice in the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.b] Northern Ireland began with high-value industries that depended on export markets. The post-war slump and then the Depression hit them hard, so poverty and unemployment shaped the state's first two decades.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.0] Shipbuilding at Harland and Wolff and linen were the two main industries. Both fell back after the post-war boom ended in 1920–21, because they depended on export markets.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.1] Unemployment was about 20 per cent for much of the 1920s and above 25 per cent in the early 1930s, among the highest in the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.2] Agriculture remained important because small farms could still sell goods, such as cattle and eggs, to Britain.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.3] The government followed a policy of ‘step by step’ with Britain, keeping welfare benefits such as pensions and unemployment pay in line with British levels. This was financed by the Imperial Contribution (Northern Ireland's payment towards UK-wide costs).
[hist-ire3-s3.5.4] Housing in the slums of Belfast was poor and overcrowded because the government built little until the late 1930s.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.5] In October 1932 unemployed Catholic and Protestant workers joined together in the Outdoor Relief strike and riots in Belfast against the harsh relief rules (the payments to the unemployed). Police shot dead two people, and relief rates were increased.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.b] Partition broke up an economy that had been built on all-Ireland trade, and it weakened both parts. It also left a lasting political issue in the border.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.0] Customs controls on the border from 1923 interrupted the flow of goods and cattle. The Economic War (1932–38) between the Free State and Britain then made smuggling common.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.1] Belfast's linen, shipbuilding and engineering had sold to a UK and world market, so they lost the southern market they had counted on.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.2] The South lost most of the island's large-scale industry, which was in the north-east, so it was left with an economy dependent on agriculture.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.3] Railways were also split by the border. The Great Northern Railway, for example, crossed it.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.4] Society divided as well. Catholics in the North were cut off from the southern state's institutions, while the Southern Protestant minority declined and had a smaller place in public life.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.5] Politically the border became the main theme of Northern nationalism and of southern foreign policy. Articles 2 and 3 of the 1937 Constitution claimed the whole island.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.b] Craig organised unionist resistance to Home Rule and became the first Prime Minister of Northern Ireland. He is judged both as the builder of a stable state and as the leader of a sectarian one.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.0] Craig (1871–1940) was born in Belfast, the son of a wealthy whiskey distiller, and worked as a stockbroker. He served in the Boer War and became MP for East Down in 1906.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.1] Before partition he organised the UVF and the Larne gun-running of April 1914 with Carson (the unionist leader). From 1916 to 1921 he held junior ministerial posts in the British government, the last as Parliamentary and Financial Secretary to the Admiralty (1920–21).
[hist-ire3-s3.7.2] He succeeded Carson as Unionist leader in February 1921 and became Prime Minister of Northern Ireland in June 1921, serving until his death on 24 November 1940. He was made Viscount Craigavon in 1927.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.3] He met Collins in January and March 1922 (the pacts) and negotiated with Lloyd George, but insisted that the border was settled. He also refused to nominate a member to the Boundary Commission.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.4] His government created the RUC and the Special Powers Act and abolished PR, and he told Stormont in 1934 that it was a Protestant parliament and state. Critics therefore see him as a sectarian figure. His government also kept welfare in line with Britain's step by step.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.5] Events shaped him: the violence of 1920–22, the Boundary Commission and the Depression all shaped him. His defenders say he held the state together against IRA violence and economic crisis.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.6] Contemporaries: unionists revered him as the founder of their state, while nationalists considered him responsible for their exclusion and for sectarian rule.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.7] Historians: Patrick Buckland's biography James Craig (1980) portrays a cautious, conservative leader who made no attempt to win Catholic consent. Others likewise criticise the lack of any attempt to win minority consent.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.b] Bates was the minister who ran the machinery of control for more than 20 years. His name is tied to the security system and the electoral changes.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.0] Richard Dawson Bates (1876–1949) was a Belfast solicitor. He was secretary of the Ulster Unionist Council (the governing body of the unionist movement) from 1906 to 1921 and helped to organise the UVF.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.1] He was Minister of Home Affairs from 1921 to 1943, which made him responsible for the RUC, the Special Constabulary, the Special Powers Act, local government and elections.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.2] He introduced the Special Powers Act and the Local Government Act of 1922, which abolished PR for local elections, and he was the minister responsible for the 1929 change for Stormont elections.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.3] He used his powers to ban republican parades and organisations, to arrest and intern suspects and to censor some publications.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.4] Events shaped him: the security crisis of 1921–22 created the powers that he then used for over twenty years. He left office in 1943 in ill health.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.5] Contemporaries: nationalists blamed his department for the system of control, and many unionists praised his efficiency.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.6] Historians: Patrick Buckland, in The Factory of Grievances: Devolved Government in Northern Ireland, 1921–39 (1979), describes how his department's decisions, even if cautious, left a legacy of grievances.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.b] Judgements depend on whether the state is measured by survival and stability or by its treatment of the minority. Good answers set paired points against each other and reach a verdict.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.0] Achievement: it survived the violence of 1920–22 and the Depression, and secured the position of Northern Ireland within the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.1] Achievement: through its step-by-step parity policy it maintained British standards in education, health and welfare.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.2] Limit: it relied on emergency powers, a largely Protestant police and manipulated electoral rules, so the minority never accepted the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.3] Limit: a one-party government with a permanent majority did not develop real opposition, and its economic and housing policy was timid.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.4] Contemporary view: unionists regarded the state as a bulwark against a Catholic all-Ireland state, while nationalists saw a Protestant state.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.5] Later view: historians disagree on how far the system was planned discrimination, but accept that its legacy shaped the Troubles.

CANDIDATES hist-ire3-s6: 6. De Valera in power, Anglo-Irish relations and from Free State to Republic, 1932–1949
[hist-ire3-s6.0.b] The Treaty of December 1921 left the Free State short of full sovereignty (complete independent authority over its own affairs). The Cosgrave governments accepted it but used the Commonwealth to widen the Free State's independence step by step, by agreement with Britain.
[hist-ire3-s6.0.0] The Treaty, signed on 6 December 1921, gave the Free State dominion status: a self-governing place in the British Commonwealth, but still tied to the Crown. The Civil War followed between those who accepted it and those who rejected it.
[hist-ire3-s6.0.1] The Treaty's symbols of British authority were the oath of allegiance (a promise of loyalty to the Crown), the Governor-General (the King's representative in Ireland), the Crown itself and British use of three Treaty ports: Berehaven (Bantry Bay), Spike Island (Cork Harbour, Cobh) and Lough Swilly.
[hist-ire3-s6.0.2] The Free State joined the League of Nations in 1923 and registered the Treaty there in 1924, treating it as an international agreement rather than an internal British matter. Britain objected.
[hist-ire3-s6.0.3] The Boundary Commission crisis (the dispute over the border with Northern Ireland) ended with the Tripartite Agreement of December 1925, which released the Free State from its share of the imperial debt, so the border question was set aside.
[hist-ire3-s6.0.4] The Imperial Conference of 1926 produced the Balfour Declaration, which described Britain and the dominions as autonomous communities, equal in status and freely associated. Kevin O'Higgins took a leading part for the Free State.
[hist-ire3-s6.0.5] The Statute of Westminster (December 1931) gave the dominions legislative independence by removing the British Parliament's power to override dominion laws. It was the legal foundation for what de Valera did next.
[hist-ire3-s6.0.6] Cosgrave's government held that independence should come by agreement with Britain, so it left the oath, the Governor-General and the Treaty ports in place.
[hist-ire3-s6.0.7] The Free State also kept paying the land annuities (the repayments tenant farmers owed for land bought under the Land Acts) to the British Exchequer, under arrangements of the 1920s.
[hist-ire3-s6.1.b] De Valera had led the anti-Treaty side in the Civil War, and Fianna Fáil's pledge was to remove the oath and the Treaty's symbols. First he had to win power and then secure it.
[hist-ire3-s6.1.0] De Valera left Sinn Féin and founded Fianna Fáil in 1926, so that the anti-Treaty republican cause could be pursued through elections. The party entered the Dáil in 1927.
[hist-ire3-s6.1.1] Cumann na nGaedheal, led by W.T. Cosgrave, had governed from 1922 until 1932, which is why Fianna Fáil's victory in 1932 marked a real change of direction.
[hist-ire3-s6.1.2] In the election of February 1932 Fianna Fáil won 72 seats, short of a majority, and governed with the support of Labour.
[hist-ire3-s6.1.3] De Valera became President of the Executive Council (the head of government, the title used until 1937) on 9 March 1932. He was also Minister for External Affairs, so he personally ran relations with Britain.
[hist-ire3-s6.1.4] He then called a snap election in January 1933. Fianna Fáil won 77 seats, an overall majority, so it no longer depended on Labour and could pass the Treaty-dismantling laws that followed.
[hist-ire3-s6.2.b] De Valera removed the Treaty's symbols one at a time by legislation, relying on the Statute of Westminster and his own Dáil majority. Learn these steps in date order, because each one builds on the last.
[hist-ire3-s6.2.0] In autumn 1932 he removed James McNeill as Governor-General after a public dispute with the government. In November 1932 he appointed Domhnall Ua Buachalla, a Fianna Fáil figure and former TD, and gave him only a minimal role, so the office lost its importance.
[hist-ire3-s6.2.1] The Constitution (Removal of Oath) Act, May 1933, ended the oath of fidelity to the King for members of the Oireachtas (the Irish parliament). This is a different thing from the Treaty's oath of allegiance (the oath to the Free State and the King).
[hist-ire3-s6.2.2] The Constitution (Amendment No. 22) Act 1933 ended appeals to the Privy Council, the British body that had been the final court of appeal for Irish cases, so Irish courts now had the last word.
[hist-ire3-s6.2.3] The Seanad (the Senate, the upper house) was abolished in 1936.
[hist-ire3-s6.2.4] Edward VIII abdicated in December 1936, which gave de Valera his chance. On 11 December the Constitution (Amendment No. 27) Act removed the Crown and the Governor-General from the Constitution.
[hist-ire3-s6.2.5] On 12 December 1936 the Executive Authority (External Relations) Act brought back a limited role for the King: he acted for the state only in appointing diplomats and signing international agreements.
[hist-ire3-s6.2.6] Britain took no military action and did not accept that the Free State had left the Commonwealth. The Statute of Westminster supported de Valera's constitutional argument, and Britain did not want a confrontation.
[hist-ire3-s6.3.b] De Valera's decision to stop paying the land annuities (the repayments tenant farmers owed for land bought under the Land Acts) became a trade conflict between the two states. It was only ended by the agreements of 1938.
[hist-ire3-s6.3.0] The annuities amounted to about £3 million a year. De Valera withheld them from mid-1932, arguing that they were owed to the Irish state, not to Britain.
[hist-ire3-s6.3.1] Britain held that the arrangements of the 1920s bound Ireland to pay. The two sides read those arrangements differently, and this disagreement was the cause of the war.
[hist-ire3-s6.3.2] From July 1932 Britain imposed special duties (extra import taxes) on Irish cattle, beef, butter and other exports, which hit the Irish farmers who depended on the British market.
[hist-ire3-s6.3.3] The Free State responded with duties on British goods such as coal, cement and iron and steel products, and began a policy of self-sufficiency, meaning producing more of what the country needed rather than importing it.
[hist-ire3-s6.3.4] The Coal–Cattle Pact of 1935 exchanged Irish cattle for British coal and slightly reduced the damage, but it did not end the dispute.
[hist-ire3-s6.3.5] Fianna Fáil argued that the conflict was the price of sovereignty, so in rural constituencies it kept its voters. Cosgrave's party attacked the policy as damaging.
[hist-ire3-s6.4.b] The first years of Fianna Fáil rule brought a challenge from the right. It strengthened the government's hand and led to the creation of a new opposition party.
[hist-ire3-s6.4.0] The Army Comrades Association (ACA) was founded in February 1932 by pro-Treaty ex-officers to protect Cumann na nGaedheal meetings from disruption by the IRA.
[hist-ire3-s6.4.1] In February 1933 de Valera dismissed Eoin O'Duffy as Garda Commissioner (head of the police). O'Duffy took over the ACA in July 1933, when it adopted a blue shirt and a stiff-armed salute, which is why its members became known as the Blueshirts.
[hist-ire3-s6.4.2] The movement was renamed the National Guard in July 1933. A march on Dublin planned for August 1933 was banned, and the National Guard itself was banned in August 1933, though the movement continued under other names.
[hist-ire3-s6.4.3] In September 1933 Cumann na nGaedheal, the National Centre Party and the Blueshirts merged as Fine Gael, with O'Duffy as its first leader. He resigned in 1934, and Cosgrave was leader by 1935.
[hist-ire3-s6.4.4] Vice-Admiral Henry Somerville, a retired naval officer, was killed by the IRA in March 1936. De Valera banned the IRA in June 1936, which removed the Blueshirt and IRA challenge to the state.
[hist-ire3-s6.4.5] O'Duffy later led an Irish Brigade of about 700 men to fight for Franco in Spain in 1936–37.
[hist-ire3-s6.5.b] The new constitution replaced the Free State Constitution of 1922 and gave the state a more Irish and Catholic character. It also avoided the word republic (a state with no monarch as head), which only came into use in 1948–49.
[hist-ire3-s6.5.0] De Valera drafted it himself, with John Hearne (legal adviser in the Department of External Affairs) and Maurice Moynihan (Secretary to the Government). Catholic clerical advice was also taken.
[hist-ire3-s6.5.1] It was approved in a plebiscite (a referendum on the Constitution, a vote of the whole electorate) on 1 July 1937, held on the same day as a general election, by about 56 per cent to 44 per cent. It came into force on 29 December 1937.
[hist-ire3-s6.5.2] Article 4 named the state Éire, or in the English language, Ireland. Article 5 declared that Ireland was a sovereign, independent, democratic state, so it claimed full independent authority but did not call itself a republic.
[hist-ire3-s6.5.3] The head of state was a President elected for seven years, and the head of government was the Taoiseach, the new title replacing President of the Executive Council.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.b] Three articles show the constitution's character most clearly, and each drew criticism. They claimed the whole island, defined the family in a conservative way and recognised the Catholic Church's special position.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.0] Articles 2 and 3 claimed the whole island of Ireland as the national territory, while limiting the exercise of the state's laws to 26 counties until reunification. This was a claim on Northern Ireland without any power there.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.1] Article 41 recognised the family as the foundation of society, spoke of the woman's life within the home, and the Constitution barred divorce.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.2] Article 44 recognised the special position of the Catholic Church as guardian of the faith of the great majority. It also named the Church of Ireland, Presbyterian, Methodist and other churches and the Jewish congregations. The clause was removed in 1972.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.3] Unionists, women's groups and some nationalists criticised the document.
[hist-ire3-s6.6.4] Douglas Hyde was inaugurated as the first President on 25 June 1938.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.b] Three agreements signed in London on 25 April 1938 settled the main disputes between the two governments. They also removed the Treaty's last military restriction, the British hold on the ports.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.0] The defence agreement handed over the Treaty ports at Berehaven, Spike Island (Cobh) and Lough Swilly to Ireland, and the handover took place in July 1938.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.1] The financial agreement settled all annuities and other financial claims for a single payment of £10 million, far less than Britain had claimed. This ended the annuities dispute that had caused the Economic War.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.2] The trade agreement ended the special duties on both sides, which brought the Economic War to a close.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.3] Neville Chamberlain, the British Prime Minister, agreed partly to improve relations as war approached, so Britain preferred a friendlier Ireland to a quarrel over the ports. Winston Churchill opposed giving up the ports.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.4] De Valera raised partition in the talks and linked it to the ports, but Britain would not move on the border.
[hist-ire3-s6.7.5] Controlling its own harbours made it possible for Ireland to remain neutral in 1939.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.b] The inter-party government formally ended the External Relations Act and left the Commonwealth. Britain responded by guaranteeing Northern Ireland's position.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.0] Fianna Fáil lost its majority in the general election of 4 February 1948. John A. Costello of Fine Gael became Taoiseach on 18 February 1948 at the head of an inter-party government (Fine Gael, Labour, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan and others).
[hist-ire3-s6.8.1] Seán MacBride, leader of Clann na Poblachta, was Minister for External Affairs. He wanted to repeal the External Relations Act, which he regarded as an anomaly because it still gave the King a role in Irish diplomacy.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.2] Costello announced the decision in Ottawa in September 1948. The causes of the announcement are disputed.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.3] The Republic of Ireland Act was passed in December 1948 and came into force on Easter Monday, 18 April 1949. It repealed the External Relations Act and ended the state's link with the Commonwealth.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.4] The British Ireland Act 1949 recognised that Ireland was no longer a member of the Commonwealth, but said Irish citizens would not be treated as foreigners.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.5] It also declared that Northern Ireland would remain in the United Kingdom unless the Parliament of Northern Ireland consented to change, which strengthened the unionist position.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.6] The southern parties united in an anti-partition campaign in 1949, but it achieved little.
[hist-ire3-s6.8.7] Independence had largely been achieved by 1938, so 1949 was the formal break with the Crown and the Commonwealth, not the moment Ireland became independent.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.b] De Valera dominated the 1930s and 1940s and shaped the state's constitution, relations with Britain and neutrality. He led Fianna Fáil in government from 1932 to 1948, again in 1951–54 and 1957–59, was President of Ireland from 1959 to 1973, and died in 1975. Judgements about him are divided.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.0] Between 1932 and 1948 he removed the oath, the Governor-General and the Privy Council appeal, passed the 1936 Acts, drafted the 1937 constitution, secured the ports in 1938, banned the IRA in 1936 and kept Ireland neutral in the Second World War.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.1] Events shaped him as well. The Statute of Westminster gave him room to act, the abdication gave him his chance in 1936, the Blueshirts strengthened his hand against opponents, and the approach of war helped persuade Britain to give up the ports.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.2] He was a direct participant: as his own Minister for External Affairs he negotiated personally with Britain, and he drafted the 1937 constitution himself with Hearne and Moynihan.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.3] He did not end partition, and the Economic War cost the farming economy dearly.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.4] The social and economic record was weak, with high emigration and unemployment, a conservative Catholic constitution and wartime censorship.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.5] Contemporaries: supporters, especially small farmers and workers, saw him as the man who finished the work of independence. Opponents in Cumann na nGaedheal and the Blueshirts called him a dictator, or said he damaged the economy for the sake of symbols.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.6] Historians: J.J. Lee (Ireland 1912–1985, 1989) credits him with consolidating democracy and with strong political skills, while criticising the economy. Deirdre McMahon (Republicans and Imperialists, 1984) examined his diplomacy with Britain.
[hist-ire3-s6.9.7] Tim Pat Coogan (De Valera: Long Fellow, Long Shadow, 1993) is critical, while Diarmaid Ferriter (Judging Dev, 2007) is balanced, stressing both his stubbornness and his pragmatism.
[hist-ire3-s6.10.b] Both leaders increased Irish independence, but by different methods and at different costs. Weigh them in pairs: what each achieved, and what each left undone.
[hist-ire3-s6.10.0] Cosgrave's strengths: he secured the Free State's international recognition, used the Commonwealth to widen independence, and avoided economic conflict with Britain.
[hist-ire3-s6.10.1] Cosgrave's limits: he left the oath, the Governor-General and the ports in place, and the Boundary Commission failed.
[hist-ire3-s6.10.2] De Valera's strengths: he removed the symbols of the Treaty, settled the ports and the annuities, and created a constitution that Britain accepted in practice. His ports settlement also made neutrality possible.
[hist-ire3-s6.10.3] De Valera's limits: the Economic War, the failure to end partition, and the fact that his changes relied on the groundwork Cosgrave laid at the Imperial Conferences (Balfour and Westminster).
[hist-ire3-s6.10.4] Contemporary view: Fianna Fáil supporters said de Valera achieved real sovereignty, while Fine Gael said he damaged the economy for the sake of symbols.
[hist-ire3-s6.10.5] Later view: the Republic of 1949 is often judged mainly a formal act, because de Valera had already made independence real. Neither leader ended partition.
