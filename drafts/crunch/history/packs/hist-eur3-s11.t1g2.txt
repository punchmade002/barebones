TARGET hist-eur3-s11: 11. Anti-Semitism and the Holocaust
[hist-eur3-s11.0.b] Hatred of Jews was centuries old in Europe before the Nazis. What Hitler's movement added was a racial doctrine that made Jews the enemy of the German nation, and then turned that doctrine into state policy.
[hist-eur3-s11.0.0] Anti-Semitism, hostility towards or prejudice against Jews, combined religious hostility from Christian teaching, economic resentment and, from the late nineteenth century, racial theories that claimed Jews were a separate and inferior race.
[hist-eur3-s11.0.1] Three examples show how widespread it was before the Nazis: pogroms (violent attacks on Jewish communities) in the Russian Empire from 1881 and the Dreyfus Affair in France, which began in 1894.
[hist-eur3-s11.0.2] The third example is the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, a forgery first published in Russia in 1903, which invented a Jewish plot to rule the world and so gave hatred a false 'proof'.
[hist-eur3-s11.0.3] The Nazis shifted anti-Semitism from religion to race. Under the old religious hatred a Jew could escape by converting, but under racial anti-Semitism ancestry was all that counted, so conversion or assimilation could never make a Jew 'German'.
[hist-eur3-s11.1.b] The Nazis built their politics on the idea that races struggle for survival. This gave Jews a fixed role as the enemy, and it gave the regime a reason for its later policies.
[hist-eur3-s11.1.0] Nazi racial ideology saw history as a struggle between races, with the so-called 'Aryans' at the top. Jews were portrayed as a racial enemy working behind both capitalism and communism.
[hist-eur3-s11.1.1] Hitler's Mein Kampf (first volume 1925) blamed Jews for Germany's defeat in 1918, for communism and for the Versailles settlement, and it set out the aim of removing Jews from German life.
[hist-eur3-s11.1.2] This blame built on the 'stab in the back' myth, the false claim that Germany lost the war because of betrayal at home, and on resentment of the hyperinflation of the early 1920s, so many Germans were ready to listen.
[hist-eur3-s11.1.3] Herrenvolk, the 'master race' of Germanic people, was the key Nazi concept. It required that Jews be defined as racially alien and excluded from the German community, while Slavs were labelled Untermenschen ('subhumans'). Lebensraum (living space) in the east later brought millions more Jews under German rule.
[hist-eur3-s11.1.4] The Nazis came to power when Hitler was appointed Chancellor on 30 January 1933. Their tools were the SS under Heinrich Himmler (Reichsführer-SS), the Gestapo (secret police) and concentration camps, the first of which, Dachau, opened in March 1933.
[hist-eur3-s11.1.5] Anti-Semitism was not confined to Germany: in 1938 Italy introduced racial laws that excluded Jews from schools, the professions and the armed forces and banned mixed marriages.
[hist-eur3-s11.2.b] Nazi ideas reached ordinary Germans through newspapers, films and classrooms. The target of this hatred was a small minority that was mostly well integrated.
[hist-eur3-s11.2.0] Der Stürmer, the anti-Semitic weekly of Julius Streicher (founded 1923), promoted stereotypes of Jews and encouraged hostility towards Jewish neighbours.
[hist-eur3-s11.2.1] Schools taught race 'science', so children learned that Germans and Jews belonged to different races. Two films, The Eternal Jew (Der ewige Jude) and Jud Süß, both of 1940, presented Jews as a threat.
[hist-eur3-s11.2.2] There were about 500,000 Jews in Germany in 1933, under one per cent of the population, which shows how little real basis the 'Jewish threat' had.
[hist-eur3-s11.2.3] Most German Jews were fully integrated into German economic, cultural and civic life, which is why the racial claims had to be imposed by law, and many had served in the First World War.
[hist-eur3-s11.3.b] Once in power, the regime set out to push Jews out of public life, first by pressure and then by law. The Nuremberg Laws gave this exclusion a legal basis.
[hist-eur3-s11.3.0] The boycott of Jewish shops and businesses on 1 April 1933 was announced as a one-day action and lasted one day. Public response was mixed, so the regime moved on to using the law instead.
[hist-eur3-s11.3.1] The Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service (7 April 1933) dismissed Jewish officials. Later measures excluded Jews from the law, medicine, journalism, culture and the universities, and in May 1933 Nazi book burnings destroyed works by Jewish authors.
[hist-eur3-s11.3.2] The Nuremberg Laws of 15 September 1935, announced at the Nuremberg rally of that year, were the Reich Citizenship Law and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honour.
[hist-eur3-s11.3.3] The Reich Citizenship Law removed German citizenship from Jews, who became subjects with no political rights, and the second law banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans.
[hist-eur3-s11.3.4] The laws did not say who counted as a Jew. A supplementary decree of 14 November 1935 defined a Jew by descent, with three or four Jewish grandparents, and people of mixed descent were classed as Mischlinge. This definition was used in later persecution.
[hist-eur3-s11.4.b] As the economic and social exclusion tightened, Jews lost their livelihoods and many tried to leave. Leaving was hard, and this is why so many could not.
[hist-eur3-s11.4.0] 'Aryanisation' meant forcing Jews to sell their businesses and property at low prices, or confiscating them, so that wealth passed to non-Jewish Germans. Jews had to register their property in 1938, which made seizure easier, and the process accelerated that year.
[hist-eur3-s11.4.1] After the Anschluss (union of Austria with Germany) in March 1938, the Jews of Vienna suffered public humiliation and violence.
[hist-eur3-s11.4.2] About 280,000 Jews had left Germany by September 1939, and about 117,000 more left annexed Austria.
[hist-eur3-s11.4.3] Emigration was difficult because emigrants lost their property and faced emigration taxes, and then needed visas and had to meet immigration limits in the countries abroad.
[hist-eur3-s11.5.b] Kristallnacht marked the move from legal discrimination to open, state-encouraged violence. It was triggered by a chain of events in autumn 1938.
[hist-eur3-s11.5.0] In late October 1938 Polish Jews living in Germany were expelled to the Polish border. Herschel Grynszpan, 17, whose family had been expelled, shot the German diplomat Ernst vom Rath in Paris on 7 November 1938.
[hist-eur3-s11.5.1] Vom Rath died on 9 November. That evening Goebbels gave a speech to party leaders in Munich that launched a pogrom, an organised attack on Jews.
[hist-eur3-s11.5.2] Kristallnacht ('Night of Broken Glass'), 9-10 November 1938, took place in Germany and Austria, so it was a violent event on a national scale. Over 250 synagogues were burned or destroyed, about 7,500 Jewish businesses and many Jewish homes were wrecked and about 90 Jews were killed.
[hist-eur3-s11.5.3] About 30,000 Jewish men were sent to Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen, and most were released only if they agreed to emigrate.
[hist-eur3-s11.5.4] Jews were made to pay a collective fine of one billion Reichsmarks, so the victims paid for the damage done to them, and were then excluded from economic life and from German schools.
[hist-eur3-s11.6.b] Violence in Germany and Austria sent thousands of Jews looking for a place to go. Other states mostly refused to take them in, which made emigration a trap for many.
[hist-eur3-s11.6.0] The Évian Conference (July 1938, at Évian-les-Bains, France) was called on the initiative of President Franklin Roosevelt of the United States. 32 countries attended, but almost none agreed to take more refugees, so the conference achieved little.
[hist-eur3-s11.6.1] Only the Dominican Republic offered to take a substantial number of refugees.
[hist-eur3-s11.6.2] Immigration limits and visa rules kept most refugees out, so even Jews with the means to leave often could not find a country willing to admit them.
[hist-eur3-s11.6.3] The Kindertransport (December 1938 to September 1939) took about 10,000 children, mostly Jewish, from Germany, Austria and Czechoslovakia to Britain. Their parents were not admitted, so most children left without them.
[hist-eur3-s11.7.b] The war put millions more Jews under German rule. The regime responded by confining them in ghettos, where conditions killed many even before any deportation.
[hist-eur3-s11.7.0] Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and about 3 million Jews in Poland came under German or Soviet rule. From December 1939 Jews in the General Government (the German-ruled part of occupied Poland) had to wear armbands marked with a Star of David.
[hist-eur3-s11.7.1] Property was seized throughout the occupied territories.
[hist-eur3-s11.7.2] The Madagascar Plan (1940) proposed deporting Jews overseas to the island of Madagascar. It was abandoned when Britain was not defeated.
[hist-eur3-s11.7.3] Ghettos, sealed-off districts of cities, were set up in Polish cities. The Łódź ghetto was sealed in 1940, and the Warsaw ghetto, sealed in November 1940, was the largest, with about 400,000 people at its height.
[hist-eur3-s11.7.4] Jewish Councils (Judenräte, singular Judenrat) were forced to administer the ghettos. Whether their members acted wrongly is debated, but they were coerced and are not counted as collaborators.
[hist-eur3-s11.7.5] Ghetto conditions were marked by overcrowding, starvation rations, typhus (a disease spread by lice) and forced labour, so death rates were high before any deportation began.
[hist-eur3-s11.8.b] When Germany invaded the Soviet Union, killing in the east became organised and open. Jews were shot in large numbers close to their homes.
[hist-eur3-s11.8.0] The Einsatzgruppen were four mobile killing units, A to D, of the SS and police. They followed the army into the Soviet Union after the invasion (Operation Barbarossa) of 22 June 1941.
[hist-eur3-s11.8.1] They shot Jews, Roma and communist officials, helped by German police battalions and local auxiliaries, which meant that killing could be done fast and in the open.
[hist-eur3-s11.8.2] At Babi Yar, a ravine near Kiev, about 33,000 Jews were shot on 29-30 September 1941. It was a mass shooting, not a camp, so do not describe it as a death camp.
[hist-eur3-s11.8.3] In Germany the yellow star became compulsory for Jews from September 1941, and deportations of German Jews to the east began in October 1941.
[hist-eur3-s11.9.b] Mass murder grew in 1941 as the war widened. The Wannsee Conference then coordinated the machinery across the government, rather than starting the killing.
[hist-eur3-s11.9.0] Historians agree that the murder of the Jews grew during 1941, but no written Hitler order survives. Göring gave Reinhard Heydrich a written order on 31 July 1941 to prepare a 'total solution' of the Jewish question.
[hist-eur3-s11.9.1] Himmler, head of the SS, was overall in charge, while Heydrich, head of the Reich Security Main Office, was the organiser. Heydrich was assassinated in Prague in 1942.
[hist-eur3-s11.9.2] The Wannsee Conference of 20 January 1942, held at a villa at Wannsee, Berlin, was chaired by Heydrich. About 15 senior officials attended, and Adolf Eichmann kept the minutes.
[hist-eur3-s11.9.3] The conference coordinated the deportation of all European Jews. It brought the ministries into line with a policy already under way. The phrase 'Final Solution' was in use before the conference, and means the Nazi plan to murder all the Jews of Europe.
[hist-eur3-s11.9.4] The minutes listed about 11 million Jews to be included, even in neutral and unoccupied countries. Britain was included, and Ireland was listed with 4,000.
[hist-eur3-s11.9.5] Historians debate whether the Holocaust was planned from the start or developed step by step: intentionalists stress Hitler's early plan, and functionalists stress the growth of policy under pressure of events.
[hist-eur3-s11.10.b] The killing now moved to camps built for the purpose. Knowing the difference between types of camp, and naming the main death camps, is basic to any answer.
[hist-eur3-s11.10.0] A concentration camp was a prison and labour camp, such as Dachau, Buchenwald and Bergen-Belsen, where many died of disease and starvation. A death (extermination) camp was built for killing. Bergen-Belsen and Dachau were not death camps, because they were built for imprisonment, not for killing.
[hist-eur3-s11.10.1] All six death camps were in occupied Poland. Chełmno used gas vans from December 1941, and the Operation Reinhard camps of Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka opened in 1942. Majdanek, near Lublin, and Auschwitz-Birkenau were both labour and death camps, which is why they are listed in both groups.
[hist-eur3-s11.10.2] Treblinka was a pure killing centre, built only for murder, and estimates of its dead range from about 700,000 to about 900,000. The Reinhard camps killed most arrivals within hours.
[hist-eur3-s11.10.3] Auschwitz, near Oświęcim, had three parts: the main camp (Auschwitz I), Birkenau (Auschwitz II), where the gas chambers were, and Monowitz (Auschwitz III), where prisoners did labour for IG Farben, a chemical company.
[hist-eur3-s11.10.4] At Auschwitz Zyklon B poison gas was used. About 1.1 million people were murdered there, about 1 million of them Jews. On the arrival ramp prisoners were selected for labour or for immediate death.
[hist-eur3-s11.10.5] Staff and gassing techniques from the T4 programme against disabled people were later used in the death camps, so the programme was a rehearsal for mass murder.
[hist-eur3-s11.11.b] Jews were brought to the camps from across Europe by rail. The numbers lost varied sharply from country to country, which depended on who ruled and who helped.
[hist-eur3-s11.11.0] Eichmann's office organised the deportation transports by rail from across Europe, including the round-ups of Jews in France.
[hist-eur3-s11.11.1] Germany occupied Hungary in March 1944, and about 430,000 Hungarian Jews were deported to Auschwitz in about eight weeks between May and July 1944. Most were murdered on arrival.
[hist-eur3-s11.11.2] As Soviet forces advanced in late 1944 and 1945, the SS forced prisoners west into Germany on death marches. Very many died of exhaustion, cold and shooting.
[hist-eur3-s11.11.3] About six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust (the Hebrew word is Shoah, 'catastrophe'), about 3 million of them from Poland.
[hist-eur3-s11.11.4] Losses by country varied: about 90 per cent of Polish Jews and of the Jews of the Baltic states, about 75 per cent of Dutch Jews and about 25 per cent of French Jews.
[hist-eur3-s11.11.5] Anne Frank, who hid in Amsterdam, was deported in 1944 and died in Bergen-Belsen in 1945. Her diary, published after the war, is one individual record of the victims.
[hist-eur3-s11.12.b] The regime's racial ideology did not stop with Jews. It also led to the persecution and murder of other groups seen as inferior or as threats to the 'national community'.
[hist-eur3-s11.12.0] A sterilisation law of July 1933 allowed compulsory sterilisation of people with hereditary conditions, and about 400,000 people were sterilised.
[hist-eur3-s11.12.1] The Aktion T4 programme, begun in 1939, murdered about 70,000 disabled patients in gas chambers by August 1941. It was officially halted that month after public protests, including a sermon by Bishop Galen, though killing of patients continued in secret.
[hist-eur3-s11.12.2] Roma and Sinti, treated as racially inferior because of the same race ideas, were deported, imprisoned and murdered. Estimates of the dead range from about 200,000 to 500,000.
[hist-eur3-s11.12.3] About 3 million of some 5.7 million Soviet prisoners of war died in German captivity, from starvation, disease and execution.
[hist-eur3-s11.12.4] Homosexual men were arrested under Paragraph 175 of the criminal code, Jehovah's Witnesses were persecuted for refusing military service and loyalty oaths, and political opponents were imprisoned.
[hist-eur3-s11.12.5] Slavs in Poland were treated as a labour reserve, and their intelligentsia, the educated leaders of society, was targeted.
[hist-eur3-s11.13.b] Resistance was real but limited, because the Germans controlled weapons and information and the ghettos were sealed. It took armed and unarmed forms.
[hist-eur3-s11.13.0] The Warsaw ghetto uprising ran from 19 April to 16 May 1943. The Jewish Fighting Organisation (ŻOB), led by Mordechai Anielewicz, resisted the attempt to deport the remaining inhabitants, after mass deportations to Treblinka in summer 1942. The ghetto was destroyed, and Anielewicz was killed in May 1943.
[hist-eur3-s11.13.1] Prisoners revolted in the camps: at Treblinka in August 1943, at Sobibór in October 1943, where they killed guards and escaped, and in the Sonderkommando revolt at Auschwitz-Birkenau in October 1944.
[hist-eur3-s11.13.2] Jewish partisans fought in the forests of Eastern Europe. The Bielski brothers' group in Belorussia sheltered about 1,200 Jews.
[hist-eur3-s11.13.3] Unarmed resistance included smuggling food and arms into the ghettos, keeping diaries and maintaining schools, religious and cultural life.
[hist-eur3-s11.13.4] Emanuel Ringelblum's secret archive (Oneg Shabbat) in the Warsaw ghetto recorded life there. It was buried in milk cans and metal boxes and was partly recovered after the war.
[hist-eur3-s11.14.b] Whether Jews lived or died depended greatly on how other Europeans behaved. Some helped the killers, many stood by, some risked their lives, and the Allies debated how far they could act.
[hist-eur3-s11.14.0] Collaborators were governments, police and officials who helped persecute Jews. Vichy France issued its first anti-Jewish statute in October 1940, French police took part in round-ups, Dutch officials helped, and in Lithuania most Jews were murdered in 1941 with local auxiliaries taking part.
[hist-eur3-s11.14.1] Bystanders did nothing, out of indifference or fear. Some neighbours profited from the seizure of Jewish property, and the churches' public protest was limited.
[hist-eur3-s11.14.2] Rescuers hid Jews, forged documents and smuggled people across borders. Yad Vashem in Jerusalem (founded 1953) honours non-Jews who risked their lives to save Jews as the Righteous Among the Nations.
[hist-eur3-s11.14.3] Oskar Schindler, a German industrialist in Kraków, saved about 1,200 Jews by employing them in his factories. Raoul Wallenberg, a Swedish diplomat in Budapest in 1944, issued protective passports to Jews.
[hist-eur3-s11.14.4] In Denmark in October 1943 over 7,000 Jews were ferried to safety in Sweden. This showed that large-scale protection was possible where the population and the local authorities were willing to act.
[hist-eur3-s11.14.5] The Allies knew of the mass murder by 1942, and on 17 December 1942 issued a declaration condemning the extermination of the Jews.
[hist-eur3-s11.14.6] The Allies did not bomb Auschwitz or the railways leading to it, and immigration limits stayed in place, so rescue was left mostly to individuals. Whether more could have been done is debated, for example whether bombing would have saved lives.
[hist-eur3-s11.15.b] Liberation revealed the scale of the crimes to the world, and trials set legal standards. The effects continued after 1945.
[hist-eur3-s11.15.0] Soviet troops liberated Majdanek in July 1944 and Auschwitz on 27 January 1945. American troops liberated Buchenwald and Dachau, and the British liberated Bergen-Belsen on 15 April 1945.
[hist-eur3-s11.15.1] Newsreels and photographs of the camps shocked the world, because they gave proof that could not be denied.
[hist-eur3-s11.15.2] The International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg sat from November 1945 to October 1946 and tried 22 leading Nazis. Its charges included the new charge of crimes against humanity.
[hist-eur3-s11.15.3] Later trials prosecuted doctors, judges, industrialists, SS and camp staff. Eichmann was captured in Argentina in 1960, tried in Jerusalem in 1961 and executed in 1962.
[hist-eur3-s11.15.4] Raphael Lemkin coined the word 'genocide' in 1944, and the UN Genocide Convention was adopted in December 1948. Many Jewish displaced persons, survivors with no home to return to, left Europe for Palestine, and the State of Israel was founded in May 1948.
[hist-eur3-s11.16.b] The effects reached every part of Europe, and historians weigh them as pairs: destruction against rescue, complicity against resistance, and contemporary views against later ones.
[hist-eur3-s11.16.0] Destruction: Jewish communities and their culture were destroyed across Europe, so Europe lost much of its Jewish population. Rescue: the Righteous Among the Nations and the Danish rescue saved many.
[hist-eur3-s11.16.1] Complicity: states, collaborators and bystanders helped or allowed the killing, for example Vichy France and Lithuanian auxiliaries. Resistance: ghetto fighters, camp revolts and partisans fought back, and Ringelblum's archive preserved the record.
[hist-eur3-s11.16.2] Refugee crisis: Évian and immigration limits showed that most states would not solve the crisis. Legacy: Nuremberg, the idea of crimes against humanity and the Genocide Convention made genocide a crime in law.
[hist-eur3-s11.16.3] Contemporary view: in the 1930s and 1940s many contemporaries treated persecution as Germany's internal affair or did not believe the reports. Later view: Allied governments knew of the mass killings by 1942, and historians debate whether more could have been done.
[hist-eur3-s11.16.4] Historians debate intentionalist and functionalist explanations: was the murder planned from the start, or did it develop step by step as the war went on?
[hist-eur3-s11.16.5] They also debate why ordinary men became killers. Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) described a German police battalion's killings, while Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) argued for a deep German 'eliminationist' anti-Semitism, a view widely criticised by historians.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s9: 9. The Second World War: blitzkrieg, wartime alliances, the technology of warfare and Churchill
[hist-eur3-s9.0.b] The Second World War began because Hitler's demands on Poland met a British and French promise to defend it, after Germany and the Soviet Union had agreed to share eastern Europe. It was fought at first with weapons and ideas still shaped by the First World War.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.0] In March 1939 Britain and France guaranteed Poland's independence, which meant that a German attack on Poland would bring them into the war. Hitler's demands on Poland were what tested that promise.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.1] On 23 August 1939 Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact (also called the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact). Its secret protocol divided Poland and eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres, so each side could take its share without fighting the other.
[hist-eur3-s9.0.2] Tanks, military aircraft, submarines and poison gas were all used in 1914-18, and the British first used tanks in battle on the Somme on 15 September 1916. But tanks were slow and unreliable, so the war stayed mostly static trench warfare (armies dug in along fixed lines).
[hist-eur3-s9.1.b] Blitzkrieg is German for 'lightning war'. It names a way of fighting designed to win quickly and so avoid the long trench stalemate of 1914-18.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.0] Its core was the concentrated Panzer division (a German armoured division, with tanks massed together rather than spread out), working with motorised infantry, meaning foot soldiers carried in vehicles so that they could keep up with the tanks.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.1] Close air support came from dive-bombers such as the Junkers Ju 87 Stuka, which attacked enemy positions as the tanks advanced, and radio communication let commanders direct tanks and aircraft together at speed.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.2] The method depended on speed and breakthrough: armour broke through a weak point in the enemy line and then encircled the defenders, which meant they were cut off and forced to surrender instead of fighting a drawn-out war.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.3] 'Blitzkrieg' was a label popularised by the Western press in 1939-40. German commanders did not use it as the name of an official doctrine, so it is best understood as a description of a method.
[hist-eur3-s9.1.4] Poland in 1939 showed the method, although that campaign was largely a conventional advance with tanks in support. France in 1940 is the classic example of blitzkrieg, where it was perfected.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.b] Germany's first campaigns showed how fast armoured forces could win. Britain and France, though at war with Germany, made no major attack in the west for months.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.0] Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.1] The Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on 17 September 1939 under the Nazi-Soviet Pact, and the two powers divided the country between them. Warsaw surrendered at the end of September and the last Polish forces in early October, about five weeks in all.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.2] During the Phoney War, from September 1939 until the German offensive in the west in May 1940, Britain and France made no major attack on the Western Front (the border between France and Germany). It was called phoney because so little fighting took place.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.3] Germany invaded Denmark and Norway on 9 April 1940. Denmark surrendered the same day.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.4] British, French and Polish forces landed in Norway, including at Narvik, but were withdrawn by early June 1940, and Norway capitulated that month.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.5] Victory gave Germany naval bases and secured the route for Swedish iron ore, the raw material for steel, which was shipped through Narvik.
[hist-eur3-s9.2.6] The failure in Norway led to the Norway debate in the House of Commons on 7-8 May 1940, which led to Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain's resignation on 10 May 1940.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.b] In six weeks in the spring of 1940 German armour defeated France. The speed of the defeat is the standard example of blitzkrieg.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.0] The German offensive in the west began on 10 May 1940, with attacks on the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg and the main armoured thrust through the Ardennes (a hilly, wooded region).
[hist-eur3-s9.3.1] This bypassed the Maginot Line, the chain of French fortifications along the German border, so the fortifications were outflanked rather than attacked.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.2] The plan is known as the 'sickle cut' (Sichelschnitt) and is associated with Erich von Manstein, because the armour swung like a sickle blade through the Ardennes to the sea.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.3] German tanks reached the Channel coast near Abbeville on 20 May 1940, cutting off the British and French forces in the north.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.4] From 26 May to 4 June 1940 Operation Dynamo evacuated over 330,000 Allied troops from Dunkirk. Almost all their heavy equipment was left behind, so the army was saved but its tanks and guns were lost.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.5] Italy declared war on Britain and France on 10 June 1940, although it was not ready for war.
[hist-eur3-s9.3.6] The Franco-German armistice (ceasefire agreement) of 22 June 1940 ended the fighting in France. The collapse of the French government and the setting up of the Vichy regime followed, but the military defeat itself took only six weeks.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.b] With France beaten, Britain stood alone. Hitler's plan to invade depended on first winning control of the skies, so the Battle of Britain became the first major campaign fought entirely in the air.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.0] The Battle of Britain was fought from July to October 1940. The Luftwaffe (the German air force) attacked RAF airfields and aircraft factories to gain air superiority, meaning control of the air, which was needed before an invasion called Operation Sea Lion could be risked.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.1] RAF Fighter Command, flying Hawker Hurricane and Supermarine Spitfire fighters and using the Chain Home radar early-warning system, defeated the attempt to win air superiority. Chain Home gave warning of raids so that fighters could be sent up in time.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.2] Operation Sea Lion was postponed in September 1940 (indefinitely, on 17 September) and then abandoned.
[hist-eur3-s9.4.3] Germany then switched to bombing London and other cities in the Blitz, which began on 7 September 1940 and continued until May 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.b] Italy's failures dragged Germany into campaigns it had not planned. This widened the war to the Balkans and North Africa.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.0] Italy invaded Greece in October 1940 and was driven back. British forces defeated the Italians in Libya between December 1940 and February 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.1] Germany had to intervene to rescue its ally: Erwin Rommel and the Afrika Korps (the German force in North Africa) were sent there in February 1941, and Germany invaded Yugoslavia and Greece in April 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.5.2] German intervention in the Balkans and North Africa stretched German resources. Historians debate whether the Balkan campaign delayed the invasion of the Soviet Union in a way that mattered, because the late spring and supply problems also caused delay.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.b] Nazi Germany and communist Russia were bitter ideological enemies, yet from August 1939 until June 1941 they co-operated because each gained from it.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.0] The two powers jointly partitioned Poland in September 1939, so each gained territory at Poland's expense.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.1] German-Soviet trade agreements supplied Germany with Soviet oil, grain and raw materials until 22 June 1941, which meant the Soviet Union was helping to feed and fuel the German war effort.
[hist-eur3-s9.6.2] Operation Barbarossa, the German invasion of the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941, ended the partnership. It had been a convenience for both sides rather than a real friendship.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.b] Hitler's invasion of the Soviet Union opened the largest front of the war. It ended the Nazi-Soviet partnership and committed Germany to a long war in the east.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.0] Operation Barbarossa began on 22 June 1941 along a front of about 3,000 kilometres from the Baltic to the Black Sea.
[hist-eur3-s9.7.1] German forces advanced rapidly in the first months and reached the outskirts of Moscow and Leningrad by late 1941. Leningrad was besieged from September 1941 (until January 1944).
[hist-eur3-s9.7.2] The Germans were halted before Moscow by the winter and by the Soviet counter-offensive that began in December 1941. The hoped-for quick victory, the aim of blitzkrieg, had failed.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.b] By the end of 1941 the wars in Europe and Asia had merged. Japan's attack on the United States brought America in, and Germany and Italy joined the fight against it.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.0] Japan had been at war with China since 1937. It occupied northern French Indochina (France's colony in south-east Asia) in 1940 and the south in July 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.1] The United States responded with an oil embargo in the summer of 1941, which pushed Japan towards the attack of December 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.2] On 7 December 1941 Japan attacked the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, which brought the United States into the war.
[hist-eur3-s9.8.3] Germany and Italy declared war on the United States on 11 December 1941, turning two separate wars into one global conflict.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.b] Japan's early victories were rapid, but they were halted within six months by American carrier power. The United States then advanced island by island towards Japan.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.0] Within months Japan captured Hong Kong (25 December 1941), Malaya, Singapore (which Britain surrendered on 15 February 1942), the Dutch East Indies, the Philippines and Burma, so most of these conquests were complete by mid-1942.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.1] The American victory at the Battle of Midway in June 1942, in which four Japanese aircraft carriers were sunk, ended Japan's advance. The Guadalcanal campaign, from August 1942, began the American counter-offensive.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.2] The United States used an island-hopping strategy, capturing chosen islands as stepping stones towards Japan, backed by submarines and carrier air power. B-29 bombers later attacked Japanese cities.
[hist-eur3-s9.9.3] The Soviet Union declared war on Japan on 8 August 1945, between the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Japan announced its surrender on 15 August, and the formal surrender was signed on 2 September 1945 aboard USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay.
[hist-eur3-s9.10.b] Between late 1942 and mid-1943 the Allies stopped the Axis advance on every major front. After this the Axis powers were mostly on the defensive.
[hist-eur3-s9.10.0] The Second Battle of El Alamein, from 23 October to early November 1942, was won by Montgomery's Eighth Army against Rommel's Axis forces in North Africa. It was the second of two battles there, and the decisive one.
[hist-eur3-s9.10.1] Operation Torch, the Anglo-American landings in Morocco and Algeria, took place on 8 November 1942. The Axis forces in Tunisia surrendered in May 1943, so the Axis was expelled from North Africa.
[hist-eur3-s9.10.2] The Battle of Stalingrad, from August 1942 to 2 February 1943, ended with the surrender of the German Sixth Army under Paulus. It was the turning point of the war in the east.
[hist-eur3-s9.10.3] The Battle of Kursk in July 1943 was the last major German offensive in the east and one of the largest tank battles in history.
[hist-eur3-s9.10.4] The Allies invaded Sicily on 10 July 1943, and Italy's armistice was announced on 8 September 1943. Germany occupied northern and central Italy, so the fighting there continued.
[hist-eur3-s9.10.5] The Battle of the Atlantic, the naval war over Allied supply routes, lasted from 1939 to 1945 and was the longest campaign of the war.
[hist-eur3-s9.10.6] Its turning point came in May 1943, when the U-boats (German submarines) suffered such losses that they were withdrawn from the North Atlantic for a time.
[hist-eur3-s9.11.b] The Normandy landings opened a second front in the west while the Red Army advanced from the east. Germany was squeezed between them until it collapsed in May 1945.
[hist-eur3-s9.11.0] D-Day, 6 June 1944, was Operation Overlord, the Allied landings on five beaches in Normandy. Dwight D. Eisenhower was supreme commander and Montgomery commanded the land forces in the landings.
[hist-eur3-s9.11.1] Landing an army from the sea needed special equipment: landing craft, Mulberry harbours (prefabricated floating harbours towed to Normandy) and PLUTO (Pipe-Line Under The Ocean), a fuel pipeline across the Channel.
[hist-eur3-s9.11.2] Allied forces broke out of the beachheads and liberated Paris on 25 August 1944.
[hist-eur3-s9.11.3] The Soviet Red Army advanced into Poland and the Balkans in 1944, and the Soviet offensive on Berlin began on 16 April 1945.
[hist-eur3-s9.11.4] Hitler killed himself in Berlin on 30 April 1945, and the Berlin garrison surrendered on 2 May.
[hist-eur3-s9.11.5] German forces surrendered in May 1945: the surrender was signed at Reims on 7 May and in Berlin on 8 May, which became VE Day (Victory in Europe Day).
[hist-eur3-s9.12.b] The Allies did not win by one battle or one weapon. Once the Soviet Union and the United States were both fighting Germany, the balance of resources and mistakes decided the outcome.
[hist-eur3-s9.12.0] The United States and the Soviet Union together far out-produced Germany and Japan in tanks, aircraft and ships, and they had far greater manpower, so the Axis could not replace its losses as fast as the Allies.
[hist-eur3-s9.12.1] The Axis made strategic mistakes: Germany fought on too many fronts, and it declared war on the United States in December 1941, adding the strongest industrial power to its enemies.
[hist-eur3-s9.12.2] Allied intelligence and technology, such as radar and Ultra (intelligence from decoded enemy signals), helped the Allies make better use of their strength.
[hist-eur3-s9.13.b] The Axis powers of Germany, Italy and Japan were linked by treaties, but their war aims were not coordinated and they often acted separately.
[hist-eur3-s9.13.0] The Rome-Berlin Axis of 1936 was the forerunner. The Pact of Steel of 22 May 1939 then bound Germany and Italy into a military alliance, although Italy was not ready for war when it entered on 10 June 1940.
[hist-eur3-s9.13.1] The Tripartite Pact of 27 September 1940, signed in Berlin, added Japan to the Axis. It recognised the leadership of Germany and Italy in Europe and of Japan in East Asia.
[hist-eur3-s9.13.2] Hungary, Romania and Slovakia joined in November 1940 and Bulgaria in March 1941. Finland fought alongside Germany against the Soviet Union from June 1941 (the Continuation War) without signing the pact.
[hist-eur3-s9.13.3] The Axis had no joint strategy or command. Italy was a drain on German resources, because its defeats in Greece and North Africa in 1940 and 1941 forced Germany to send help.
[hist-eur3-s9.13.4] Japan and the Soviet Union signed a Neutrality Pact in April 1941, and Japan did not attack the Soviet Union until the Soviet Union attacked it in August 1945. Germany was not consulted over Pearl Harbor, so the two did not coordinate their attacks.
[hist-eur3-s9.14.b] Churchill called the coalition of Britain, the Soviet Union and the United States the 'Grand Alliance'. It grew in stages as each power was drawn into the war.
[hist-eur3-s9.14.0] After June 1940 Britain stood alone, with its Empire and Commonwealth. The Soviet Union joined the coalition after Barbarossa in June 1941, and the United States after Pearl Harbor in December 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.14.1] In the destroyers-for-bases agreement of September 1940, the United States gave Britain 50 old destroyers in return for leases on British bases.
[hist-eur3-s9.14.2] The Lend-Lease Act of March 1941 (signed by Roosevelt on 11 March) allowed the United States to supply Britain with weapons and equipment without immediate payment. It was extended to the Soviet Union later in 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.14.3] Arctic convoys carried supplies to Murmansk and Archangel in the Soviet Union from August 1941.
[hist-eur3-s9.14.4] The Atlantic Charter of 14 August 1941 was agreed by Churchill and Roosevelt at Placentia Bay, Newfoundland, aboard warships. It was a statement of principles: no territorial gains, no territorial changes against the wishes of the peoples concerned, and the right of peoples to choose their government.
[hist-eur3-s9.14.5] The Charter also promised restoration of self-government to those deprived of it (self-determination), freer trade and disarmament of aggressors. The Declaration by United Nations of 1 January 1942 was then signed by 26 nations.
[hist-eur3-s9.14.6] At the Washington ('Arcadia') conference of December 1941 to January 1942 the Allies confirmed 'Germany first', meaning the decision to defeat Germany before Japan, and set up the Combined Chiefs of Staff.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.b] As the Allies moved towards victory, their leaders met to settle strategy and the shape of post-war Europe. The decisions they reached at each meeting were different, so the order matters.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.0] At the Casablanca Conference in January 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill demanded the unconditional surrender of the Axis, meaning no negotiated peace. Stalin did not attend.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.1] The Tehran Conference of 28 November to 1 December 1943 was the first time Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin met together. They agreed the invasion of France (Overlord) for 1944.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.2] At Tehran Stalin promised to enter the war against Japan after Germany's defeat, and Poland's eastern frontier, roughly on the Curzon Line, was discussed.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.3] In October 1944 in Moscow, Churchill and Stalin agreed the percentages agreement on spheres of influence in the Balkans. Churchill proposed, for example, 90 per cent British influence in Greece and 90 per cent Soviet influence in Romania, and Stalin ticked the note.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.4] At the Yalta Conference of 4-11 February 1945, in the Crimea, Germany was divided into four occupation zones, including one for France. The Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.5] Yalta also set Poland's eastern frontier on the Curzon Line, with compensation in the west, and agreed that the Soviet-backed Lublin government should be broadened. The Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan within three months of Germany's defeat.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.6] A conference to found the United Nations was also agreed at Yalta and held at San Francisco in April 1945.
[hist-eur3-s9.15.7] The Potsdam Conference of 17 July to 2 August 1945 brought together Truman (Roosevelt having died on 12 April 1945), Stalin, and Churchill, who was replaced by Attlee during it after the British election result. It ended the wartime alliance.
[hist-eur3-s9.16.b] The Allies were united by the need to beat Germany, but they distrusted each other. Disputes over strategy and Poland foreshadowed the Cold War.
[hist-eur3-s9.16.0] Stalin repeatedly demanded a second front, a major Allied invasion of western Europe that would draw German forces away from the Red Army. The Western Allies delayed it, and the Allied delay was a constant source of Soviet resentment.
[hist-eur3-s9.16.1] The Dieppe raid of August 1942, a mainly Canadian landing, failed with heavy losses. Britain also preferred a Mediterranean strategy, which put off a cross-Channel invasion.
[hist-eur3-s9.16.2] In April 1943 Germany announced the discovery of mass graves of Polish officers killed by the Soviet NKVD (secret police) in 1940, the Katyn revelation. The Soviet Union then broke relations with the Polish government-in-exile in London.
[hist-eur3-s9.16.3] In July 1944 the Soviet Union set up the Polish Committee of National Liberation (the Lublin Committee), a Soviet-sponsored rival to the London Poles.
[hist-eur3-s9.16.4] During the Warsaw Uprising of August to October 1944 the Red Army did not help the Polish fighters, and Stalin at first refused Allied aircraft landing rights.
[hist-eur3-s9.16.5] Mutual suspicion and Soviet control of eastern Europe foreshadowed the tensions of the Cold War.
[hist-eur3-s9.17.b] The 1920s and 1930s produced the ideas and machines that were used in the Second World War. Theorists asked how to avoid another stalemate, and the answer was armour and air power.
[hist-eur3-s9.17.0] British writers J.F.C. Fuller and Basil Liddell Hart argued for armoured warfare. Heinz Guderian, the leading German tank commander and theorist, wrote Achtung - Panzer! (1937), and the first three German Panzer divisions were formed in October 1935. Britain and France instead spread their tanks among infantry units.
[hist-eur3-s9.17.1] The Italian theorist Giulio Douhet argued in The Command of the Air (1921) that bombers would decide wars. Stanley Baldwin, in 1932, said that 'the bomber will always get through', expressing the fear that cities could not be defended.
[hist-eur3-s9.17.2] The Spanish Civil War was a testing ground for aircraft and tactics. The German Condor Legion bombed Guernica on 26 April 1937, which shocked world opinion.
[hist-eur3-s9.17.3] Fighter design improved. The Hawker Hurricane first flew in November 1935 and the Supermarine Spitfire in March 1936, and the Messerschmitt Bf 109 entered service in Germany in 1937.
[hist-eur3-s9.18.b] Several technologies that decided the war were first developed in the 1930s: ways to detect aircraft, read enemy messages, fly without propellers and launch rockets.
[hist-eur3-s9.18.0] In February 1935 Robert Watson-Watt demonstrated that radio waves reflected from an aircraft could detect it. Chain Home stations were built along the east and south coasts from 1936, and a chain was in operation by 1939.
[hist-eur3-s9.18.1] Several countries developed radar in the 1930s, so Britain's advantage was an integrated warning system linking Chain Home to fighter control.
[hist-eur3-s9.18.2] Enigma was the German cipher machine. Polish mathematicians led by Marian Rejewski broke it from 1932, and in July 1939 they passed their methods and replica machines to the British and French.
[hist-eur3-s9.18.3] Frank Whittle applied for his jet engine patent in 1930, and the patent was granted later. The Heinkel He 178, the first jet aircraft to fly, flew on 27 August 1939.
[hist-eur3-s9.18.4] Wernher von Braun worked on army rockets from 1932. The Peenemunde research centre on the Baltic coast opened in 1937 and later developed the V-2 rocket.
[hist-eur3-s9.18.5] ASDIC (sonar), which detects submarines by sound, was developed by Britain from the end of the First World War, and aircraft carriers, ships that launch aircraft, were also developed after 1918.
[hist-eur3-s9.19.b] In 1939-45 the ideas of the 1930s were tested at scale. On land, tanks and mass production gave armies speed and weight, while in the air strategic bombing carried the war to cities.
[hist-eur3-s9.19.0] German Panzer divisions used radio communication and combined armour with motorised infantry. The Soviet T-34 tank, in production from 1940, and the American M4 Sherman, of which about 50,000 were built, show that mass production mattered as much as invention.
[hist-eur3-s9.19.1] Armies answered tanks with the anti-tank gun and, from 1942, the American bazooka, a shoulder-fired anti-tank rocket.
[hist-eur3-s9.19.2] Strategic bombing means attacking an enemy's cities and industry from the air. RAF Bomber Command under Arthur Harris, head of the command from February 1942, practised area bombing at night, aiming at whole city districts. The first thousand-bomber raid was on Cologne on 30-31 May 1942.
[hist-eur3-s9.19.3] The US Army Air Forces flew B-17 Flying Fortress bombers by day, in what was called precision bombing. From early 1944 long-range P-51 Mustang escort fighters protected them over Germany.
[hist-eur3-s9.19.4] Hamburg in July 1943 (Operation Gomorrah) suffered a firestorm, a fire so intense that it drew in air and spread on its own, which killed tens of thousands (estimates are about 35,000-45,000). Dresden, on 13-15 February 1945, lost about 25,000 people.
[hist-eur3-s9.19.5] The morality and effectiveness of strategic bombing are still debated, because it caused fires and many civilian deaths in German cities.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.b] Intelligence and detection did as much as weapons to decide the war. At sea they decided the Battle of the Atlantic, where German submarines tried to cut Britain's supply line.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.0] Radar gave early warning of attacks. The British Chain Home system worked in the Battle of Britain.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.1] The cavity magnetron, developed by John Randall and Harry Boot at Birmingham University in 1940 and shared with the United States in the Tizard Mission that year, allowed small airborne and ship-borne sets.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.2] Small radar sets made night fighters and the hunting of U-boats possible. Bomber Command also used H2S ground-mapping radar from 1943 as a bombing aid.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.3] At Bletchley Park, Buckinghamshire, the home of the Government Code and Cypher School, the British decrypted German Enigma messages. Alan Turing designed the bombe (first in use in 1940), a machine to find Enigma settings.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.4] Ultra was the code name for the intelligence from decrypted high-level enemy signals. It was used in the Battle of the Atlantic and the North African campaign.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.5] Colossus (1944), designed by Tommy Flowers, broke the German Lorenz cipher, not Enigma.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.6] German U-boats hunted in 'wolf packs' under Admiral Karl Donitz and sank large numbers of Allied merchant ships in the Atlantic.
[hist-eur3-s9.20.7] The convoy system (merchant ships sailing together under guard), escort groups, long-range aircraft, radar and Ultra reduced losses from 1943, and in May 1943 the U-boats were withdrawn from the North Atlantic for a time.
[hist-eur3-s9.21.b] In the last year of the war Germany produced rockets and jet aircraft that were far ahead of their time, but too late to change the result. The United States produced the atomic bomb, which ended the war against Japan.
[hist-eur3-s9.21.0] The V-1 flying bomb, a pilotless aircraft that could be shot down, was launched against London from 13 June 1944. The V-2 ballistic rocket, which had no defence against it, was first fired at London on 8 September 1944 and also at Antwerp.
[hist-eur3-s9.21.1] The V-weapons were too late and too few to change the outcome. They were built by forced and concentration-camp labour in the Mittelwerk underground factory.
[hist-eur3-s9.21.2] The Messerschmitt Me 262 jet fighter entered operational service in 1944, and the British Gloster Meteor jet fighter entered service in July 1944. The Me 262 came too late to change the outcome.
[hist-eur3-s9.21.3] The Manhattan Project, the American atomic bomb programme from 1942, was run by General Leslie Groves, with J. Robert Oppenheimer directing the Los Alamos laboratory in New Mexico. The first test, the Trinity test, took place in New Mexico on 16 July 1945.
[hist-eur3-s9.21.4] The United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 ('Little Boy', a uranium bomb) and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945 ('Fat Man', a plutonium bomb). Tens of thousands died immediately and many more later from injuries and radiation.
[hist-eur3-s9.21.5] Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945. The bombs marked the start of the nuclear age.
[hist-eur3-s9.22.b] Churchill's wartime fame followed a long career in which he was both admired for his energy and distrusted for his judgement.
[hist-eur3-s9.22.0] He was born on 30 November 1874 at Blenheim Palace, Oxfordshire, into an aristocratic family, the son of Lord Randolph Churchill. He served as a soldier and war correspondent in the Sudan in 1898 and in the Boer War, where he escaped from captivity in 1899.
[hist-eur3-s9.22.1] He became an MP in 1900 as a Conservative, joined the Liberals in 1904 and rejoined the Conservatives in 1924-25.
[hist-eur3-s9.22.2] As First Lord of the Admiralty (the minister in charge of the Royal Navy) from 1911 to 1915, he resigned in 1915 after the failure of the Dardanelles or Gallipoli campaign, a setback that damaged his reputation for years.
[hist-eur3-s9.22.3] As Chancellor of the Exchequer (finance minister) from 1924 to 1929 he returned Britain to the gold standard in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s9.22.4] He was out of office from 1929 to 1939, the 'wilderness years'. He opposed the Government of India Act of 1935 and warned against Hitler and appeasement, the policy of giving way to Hitler's demands.
[hist-eur3-s9.22.5] He called the Munich agreement of October 1938 'a total and unmitigated defeat'.
[hist-eur3-s9.23.b] Churchill came to power on the day Germany attacked in the west, and in 1940 his leadership turned Britain's isolation into determination to fight on.
[hist-eur3-s9.23.0] He became First Lord of the Admiralty again on 3 September 1939. After the Norway debate he succeeded Neville Chamberlain as Prime Minister on 10 May 1940, the same day Germany attacked in the west.
[hist-eur3-s9.23.1] He led a coalition (a government of several parties) that included Labour's Clement Attlee, Deputy Prime Minister from 1942, and Ernest Bevin, Minister of Labour. Churchill also made himself Minister of Defence, so he directed the war effort himself.
[hist-eur3-s9.23.2] He refused to seek a negotiated peace in 1940.
[hist-eur3-s9.23.3] His speeches on the radio and in Parliament sustained morale after Dunkirk and during the Blitz: 'I have nothing to offer but blood, toil, tears and sweat' (13 May 1940), 'we shall fight on the beaches' (4 June 1940) and 'their finest hour' (18 June 1940).
[hist-eur3-s9.23.4] During the Battle of Britain he said, on 20 August 1940, 'Never in the field of human conflict was so much owed by so many to so few', which made him a symbol of national resistance.
[hist-eur3-s9.24.b] Churchill was a participant who made the key decisions on British strategy and diplomacy. He worked to bring the United States and the Soviet Union into partnership with Britain, though Britain became the junior partner.
[hist-eur3-s9.24.0] He courted Roosevelt through the destroyers-for-bases deal, Lend-Lease, the Atlantic Charter and the policy of Germany first. When Germany invaded the Soviet Union he welcomed it as an ally in a broadcast on 22 June 1941, despite his anti-communism.
[hist-eur3-s9.24.1] He met Roosevelt at Placentia Bay (August 1941), Washington (December 1941 and later), Casablanca (January 1943, where unconditional surrender was announced) and Quebec (August 1943 and September 1944), and both leaders at Tehran and Yalta.
[hist-eur3-s9.24.2] He met Stalin in Moscow in August 1942 and in October 1944, when he proposed the percentages agreement on spheres of influence in the Balkans. This has been seen as wartime realism or as cynicism.
[hist-eur3-s9.24.3] He attended the Potsdam Conference in July 1945 until the election result replaced him with Attlee.
[hist-eur3-s9.24.4] His strategy favoured the Mediterranean and North Africa, supported strategic bombing and was cautious over a cross-Channel invasion.
[hist-eur3-s9.24.5] There were setbacks in 1942: Singapore and, in June, Tobruk, which fell to Rommel. In July 1942 he won a vote of no confidence in the House of Commons by a very large majority.
[hist-eur3-s9.24.6] By 1944 Britain was the junior partner, because the United States and the Soviet Union supplied far more men and resources.
[hist-eur3-s9.25.b] Churchill won the war but lost the election that followed, because voters in 1945 wanted a government they trusted to deliver post-war reform.
[hist-eur3-s9.25.0] In the 1945 general election, with polling on 5 July and results on 26 July, Labour won a landslide and Attlee became Prime Minister, replacing Churchill at Potsdam. Voters trusted Labour more on post-war reform.
[hist-eur3-s9.25.1] He returned as Prime Minister in 1951-55. His 'iron curtain' speech (Fulton, Missouri, March 1946) warned of the division of Europe, and he won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1953. He died in January 1965 and was given a state funeral.
[hist-eur3-s9.26.b] Churchill divided opinion in his own time and still divides historians. The same record looks like triumph or failure depending on what is weighed.
[hist-eur3-s9.26.0] Contemporaries admired him as the voice of national resistance. Before 1940 he had been distrusted for his judgement, over Gallipoli, the return to gold, India and the abdication crisis.
[hist-eur3-s9.26.1] Field Marshal Alan Brooke (Lord Alanbrooke), Chief of the Imperial General Staff from December 1941, wrote diaries, published after the war, that show admiration mixed with exasperation at Churchill's interference. Some generals and Labour also criticised him, and voters rejected him in 1945.
[hist-eur3-s9.26.2] Historians such as Andrew Roberts (Churchill: Walking with Destiny, 2018, largely admiring) praise his resolve in 1940, his strategic leadership and his alliance-building.
[hist-eur3-s9.26.3] John Charmley (Churchill: The End of Glory, 1993) argued that the war ruined Britain financially and made it dependent on the United States, so Churchill's critics say the war cost Britain its empire.
[hist-eur3-s9.26.4] Historians also criticise the bombing of German cities such as Dresden, the diversion of forces to the Mediterranean and Greece in 1941, and his attitude to India and the empire.
[hist-eur3-s9.26.5] They debate his government's response to the Bengal famine of 1943, in which about 2 to 3 million died (estimates vary). How far Churchill's government was responsible is disputed.
[hist-eur3-s9.27.b] Judgements of Churchill and the alliances weigh the British war effort and the coalition's success against its divisions and its costs.
[hist-eur3-s9.27.0] Achievement: Britain's refusal to negotiate a peace in 1940 kept open a base from which the Allies could later invade Europe.
[hist-eur3-s9.27.1] Limit: British strategy relied heavily on American resources after 1941, and Britain lost Hong Kong in December 1941 and Malaya, Singapore and Burma in 1942, although it kept India.
[hist-eur3-s9.27.2] Achievement: the Grand Alliance defeated the Axis powers and produced the United Nations as a post-war organisation.
[hist-eur3-s9.27.3] Limit: the alliance concealed deep divisions over Eastern Europe, and these became the Cold War once Germany was defeated.
[hist-eur3-s9.27.4] Contemporary view: many British people saw Churchill as the embodiment of national resistance, while critics on the left and in his own party questioned his judgement.
[hist-eur3-s9.27.5] Later view: historians still debate how far Churchill's leadership was essential, with some stressing his role in 1940 and others emphasising the decisive contribution of Soviet and American forces.
[hist-eur3-s9.28.b] Technology changed how the war was fought, but historians disagree about how far it decided the result compared with production, manpower and strategy.
[hist-eur3-s9.28.0] Technology brought speed and mobility on land, a new war in the air and at sea, and intelligence from codebreaking, and it made civilians targets through strategic bombing, the V-weapons and the atomic bomb.
[hist-eur3-s9.28.1] Some technology was decisive at key moments: radar in 1940, and Ultra and convoys in the Atlantic.
[hist-eur3-s9.28.2] Some came too late: the V-weapons and the Me 262 could not change the outcome.
[hist-eur3-s9.28.3] Mass production mattered as much as invention, because the American Sherman and the Soviet T-34 were built in huge numbers, and Allied industrial production and manpower outweighed Axis technology.
[hist-eur3-s9.28.4] The morality and effectiveness of strategic bombing and the atomic bomb remain disputed, because they killed large numbers of civilians, and the atomic bombs are linked to the end of the war against Japan.
