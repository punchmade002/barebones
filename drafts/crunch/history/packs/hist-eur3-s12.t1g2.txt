TARGET hist-eur3-s12: 12. Nazi propaganda; case study: the Nuremberg Rallies
[hist-eur3-s12.0.b] Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933, and the regime soon treated control of what Germans read, heard and saw as a main tool of rule. Understanding what the propaganda was for, and which ideas it repeated, explains the choices made in every medium that follows.
[hist-eur3-s12.0.0] By mid-1933 Germany was a one-party state, so the regime could use the whole machinery of government to push its message.
[hist-eur3-s12.0.1] Propaganda is the organised spreading of ideas, images and information to shape opinion and win loyalty to a regime, its leader and its policies.
[hist-eur3-s12.0.2] Its aims were to win and keep consent, build the Hitler cult, create the national community, spread racial ideas and prepare Germans for rearmament and war.
[hist-eur3-s12.0.3] A personality cult presents the leader as an infallible, heroic saviour of the nation through propaganda, ritual and his image in daily life. For Hitler it was known as the Hitler cult or 'Führer myth'.
[hist-eur3-s12.0.4] The same core ideas were repeated: the national community (Volksgemeinschaft, one united German people), the leader principle (all authority flows from Hitler), anti-communism, anti-Semitism, the 'shame' of the Treaty of Versailles, and the glory of the German nation.
[hist-eur3-s12.0.5] Methods were simple themes repeated constantly, because repetition makes slogans stick, emotional appeal rather than argument, the mixing of entertainment with political messages, and the use of every new medium. Posters, parades, slogans and mass rallies bound people to the leader and the party.
[hist-eur3-s12.0.6] Totalitarianism means a state that seeks to control all public and private life through one party, secret police, terror and propaganda. Control of the mass media and culture was one of its main tools.
[hist-eur3-s12.0.7] Propaganda worked alongside terror (the Gestapo, the camps and the courts): persuasion won over the willing, while fear silenced those who could not be persuaded.
[hist-eur3-s12.1.b] The Nazis did not leave the media to chance. They set up one government ministry to direct them and a compulsory membership body to decide who was allowed to work in them.
[hist-eur3-s12.1.0] The Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda was established by decree on 13 March 1933 with Joseph Goebbels as minister (1933-1945). It took charge of the press, radio, film, theatre, music and the arts, so one man gave the orders for all of them.
[hist-eur3-s12.1.1] Gleichschaltung ('coordination') of culture means bringing the press, radio, film and the arts into line with Nazi ideology. Here the term is used only for culture, not for the wider takeover of the state.
[hist-eur3-s12.1.2] The Reich Chamber of Culture was created by a law of September 1933, with Goebbels as president. It had sub-chambers for the press, radio, film, music, theatre, fine arts and literature.
[hist-eur3-s12.1.3] Anyone working in these fields, including writers, painters, musicians and actors, had to be admitted to the chamber. Jews and opponents were excluded, so exclusion meant losing one's livelihood.
[hist-eur3-s12.2.b] Newspapers were the first medium to be brought into line. The regime did this by making editors answerable to the state, telling them daily what to print, and buying out independent owners.
[hist-eur3-s12.2.0] The Editors' Law of October 1933 made editors responsible to the state and barred Jews, so Jewish journalists were excluded from the press.
[hist-eur3-s12.2.1] The Reich Press Chamber and the ministry's daily press conferences told papers what to print and how to present it, so newspapers across the country carried the same line.
[hist-eur3-s12.2.2] Max Amann, head of the party publishing house (the Eher Verlag), bought up or closed independent papers, which put much of the press in party hands.
[hist-eur3-s12.2.3] Three papers are easy to confuse: the Völkischer Beobachter was the party daily, Der Angriff ('The Attack') was Goebbels's Berlin paper founded in 1927, and Der Stürmer was Julius Streicher's anti-Semitic weekly.
[hist-eur3-s12.3.b] Radio could carry Hitler's voice into every home, so the regime took it over early and then made sure ordinary families could afford a set.
[hist-eur3-s12.3.0] Broadcasting was taken under the ministry's control in 1933 when the ministry took over the broadcasting companies, so every programme could be vetted.
[hist-eur3-s12.3.1] The Volksempfänger (People's Receiver) was a cheap radio set launched at the Berlin radio exhibition in August 1933 and priced at 76 Reichsmarks, so that ordinary households could buy one.
[hist-eur3-s12.3.2] Loudspeakers in factories, offices and squares allowed communal listening to major speeches, so people without a set still heard the leaders.
[hist-eur3-s12.3.3] Programmes mixed light music and entertainment with speeches and news. This kept people listening, and the political message reached them along with the music.
[hist-eur3-s12.3.4] From September 1939 listening to foreign stations such as the BBC was a crime with heavy penalties, and some people were executed for spreading what they had heard.
[hist-eur3-s12.4.b] Cinema was the most popular medium, so the regime controlled the film industry closely and used it to carry both entertainment and propaganda.
[hist-eur3-s12.4.0] The Reich Film Chamber (1933) controlled the industry. Scripts were vetted, films the regime judged unsuitable were banned, and the state funded films with propaganda messages.
[hist-eur3-s12.4.1] Most feature films were entertainment, but every programme carried a newsreel. Audiences who came for the entertainment therefore also received the regime's version of events.
[hist-eur3-s12.4.2] From 1940 a single newsreel, the Deutsche Wochenschau, was shown across occupied Europe, so the same view of the war reached audiences in conquered countries.
[hist-eur3-s12.4.3] Hitler Youth Quex (Hitlerjunge Quex, 1933) was an early propaganda film that glorified the movement's young followers.
[hist-eur3-s12.4.4] Jud Süß (1940, directed by Veit Harlan) and The Eternal Jew (Der ewige Jude, 1940) were explicit anti-Semitic propaganda. Jud Süß was shown to audiences in Germany and occupied countries.
[hist-eur3-s12.4.5] Kolberg (1945, also directed by Veit Harlan) was a costly late-war film about resistance to the end, made to stiffen morale when the war was being lost.
[hist-eur3-s12.5.b] Alongside the big media, the regime filled everyday life with signs of loyalty to the leader. This is how the Hitler cult reached people who never went to a rally.
[hist-eur3-s12.5.0] Posters and slogans were widely displayed. 'Ein Volk, ein Reich, ein Führer' (one people, one empire, one leader) was used around the Anschluss of 1938 and packed the regime's message into five words.
[hist-eur3-s12.5.1] The 'Heil Hitler' greeting made an everyday exchange into a small act of loyalty to the leader, so refusing it stood out.
[hist-eur3-s12.5.2] Hitler's portrait hung in public places, his birthday on 20 April was a public celebration, and books of photographs by Heinrich Hoffmann, his personal photographer, showed him as the saviour and father of the nation.
[hist-eur3-s12.5.3] Youth and women's organisations, schools and the Labour Front (the state-run body for workers) were used for indoctrination, so people met the same ideas at school, at work and in their free time.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.b] Controlling culture meant not only promoting approved work but also destroying and condemning anything that did not fit Nazi ideology. Public spectacles made the condemnation part of the propaganda.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.0] On 10 May 1933, organised by the German Student Union, students and party members burned books by Jewish, Marxist, liberal and pacifist authors on the Opernplatz in Berlin and in other university towns. Goebbels spoke in Berlin.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.1] Authors whose books were burned included Karl Marx, Sigmund Freud, Erich Maria Remarque (author of All Quiet on the Western Front) and Heinrich Heine, so the burning targeted ideas the regime feared.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.2] Heine had written in 1821: 'Where they burn books, they will in the end burn people too.' The line is now inscribed at the book-burning memorial in Berlin, and it belongs to Heine, not to Goebbels.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.3] Degenerate art was the Nazi label for modern art such as Expressionism and abstract art, which was condemned and removed from galleries.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.4] The Degenerate Art (Entartete Kunst) exhibition opened in Munich in July 1937, the day after the Great German Art Exhibition of approved art opened in the new House of German Art.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.5] It showed modernist works, including those by Otto Dix, George Grosz, Emil Nolde, Ernst Ludwig Kirchner and Paul Klee, as examples of decadence.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.6] The Degenerate Art exhibition drew about two million visitors, far more than the approved exhibition beside it, so the regime's attempt to teach people to despise modern art was also a very popular show.
[hist-eur3-s12.6.7] In music, jazz was condemned as 'degenerate' and works by Jewish composers were banned.
[hist-eur3-s12.7.b] Hosting the Olympics gave the regime a world audience. It used the Games to present a peaceful, modern and efficient Germany to foreign visitors.
[hist-eur3-s12.7.0] The Winter Games were held at Garmisch-Partenkirchen in February 1936 and the Summer Games in Berlin in August 1936, so say which Games you mean.
[hist-eur3-s12.7.1] The regime wanted visitors to see a peaceful, modern, efficient Germany, and it removed anti-Semitic signs for the games so that foreign guests would not meet open racial hatred.
[hist-eur3-s12.7.2] At the Summer Games in Berlin, Germany won the most medals (about 89, ahead of the USA), which the regime used as proof of national strength. At the Winter Games Norway, not Germany, topped the medal table.
[hist-eur3-s12.7.3] Jesse Owens (USA) won four gold medals. His success was a limit on the racial message, because the regime claimed that some races were superior to others.
[hist-eur3-s12.8.b] In war the aim of propaganda changed from celebrating success to holding the home front together. The message had to shift when the news turned bad.
[hist-eur3-s12.8.0] In the early years of the war, victories were celebrated in the newsreels, which fed the idea that Hitler and the army were unbeatable.
[hist-eur3-s12.8.1] After the German surrender at Stalingrad in early February 1943, the message shifted to endurance and fear of the enemy, because victory could no longer be promised.
[hist-eur3-s12.8.2] On 18 February 1943 Goebbels gave his total war speech at the Berlin Sportpalast, asking the crowd 'Do you want total war?' (Wollt ihr den totalen Krieg?). It was broadcast nationally so that the whole country heard the call.
[hist-eur3-s12.8.3] As defeats mounted, the credibility of propaganda declined, because people could compare the official claims with what they saw and heard around them.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.b] Goebbels was the Gauleiter (party leader) of Berlin and then the head of Nazi propaganda from 1933 to 1945. He directed the machine that controlled what Germans saw and heard.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.0] Born in 1897 in Rheydt, Rhineland, he had a club foot that kept him out of the First World War army. He gained a doctorate in literature (Heidelberg, 1921) and failed as a writer. In the mid-1920s he joined the party on Strasser's 'socialist' northern wing, until Hitler won him over in 1926.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.1] Hitler made him Gauleiter of Berlin in November 1926. There he organised street fights with the communists, mass meetings and his paper Der Angriff (from 1927), and he made Horst Wessel, an SA man killed in 1930, a martyr whose song became the party anthem.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.2] From 1930 he was party propaganda chief (Reich Propaganda Leader). He ran the Reichstag campaigns of 1930 and 1932 and the presidential campaign of 1932, in which Hitler flew across Germany, using posters, films, records, loudspeaker vans and radio.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.3] As Minister of Propaganda from March 1933 he also headed the Reich Chamber of Culture and organised the book burning. His anti-Semitic films and his Munich speech of 9 November 1938, which encouraged the Kristallnacht pogrom, show his role.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.4] In the war he gave the total war speech (1943) and became Plenipotentiary for Total War in July 1944. Hitler's will named him Reich Chancellor, and on 1 May 1945, the day after Hitler's suicide, he and his wife Magda killed their six children and then themselves in Berlin.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.5] He was a participant who directed the propaganda machine rather than a witness to events. He claimed the creation of the Führer myth as his greatest achievement, and his diaries are a major source for historians.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.6] Contemporaries saw a brilliant, cynical speaker and organiser who stayed loyal to Hitler. Rivals in the leadership, Göring, Himmler, Ribbentrop and Rosenberg, disliked and distrusted him, which is why his power over culture was never complete.
[hist-eur3-s12.9.7] Historians judge him the most skilled propagandist of the age, but argue that propaganda worked best when it reinforced existing beliefs (nationalism, anti-communism, the Hitler cult) and less well when it tried to change them. His influence fell in the late 1930s and rose again from 1943.
[hist-eur3-s12.10.b] Born in Berlin in 1902, Riefenstahl trained as a dancer, acted in 'mountain films' and directed and starred in The Blue Light (1932). She admired Hitler and met him in 1932, and her rally and Olympic films gave the regime its most lasting images.
[hist-eur3-s12.10.0] Hitler, not Goebbels, commissioned her to film the rallies, with a large crew; she and Goebbels were on poor terms. Her films were Victory of Faith (1933 rally), Triumph of the Will (released 1935, 1934 rally) and Day of Freedom (1935, the army at the 1935 rally).
[hist-eur3-s12.10.1] Victory of Faith was withdrawn after the murder of Ernst Röhm in 1934. Triumph of the Will opens with Hitler's aeroplane descending through clouds over Nuremberg and uses moving cameras, low angles, no narrator and music by Herbert Windt.
[hist-eur3-s12.10.2] Triumph of the Will sets the leader against massed ranks, which gives its message of unity behind Hitler after the purge and shows Hitler and the party as the sole leadership.
[hist-eur3-s12.10.3] Her documentary Olympia (1938) covers the 1936 Berlin Olympics in two parts, Festival of the Nations and Festival of Beauty. Its new camera techniques (underwater, tracking and slow motion) presented athletic achievement as a national triumph. It premiered on 20 April 1938, Hitler's birthday.
[hist-eur3-s12.10.4] Contemporaries praised her art. Triumph of the Will won the German National Film Prize in 1935 and a gold medal at the Paris World Exhibition in 1937, and foreign audiences admired the films as cinema, while critics abroad recognised their propaganda purpose.
[hist-eur3-s12.10.5] During the war she made Tiefland, for which Sinti and Roma were taken from an internment camp as extras; it was released in 1954. After 1945 she was interned and, after several denazification hearings, was classified as a 'fellow traveller' (Mitläufer), not a major offender.
[hist-eur3-s12.10.6] She always claimed her films were art or documentary, not propaganda, and that she knew nothing of the regime's crimes. She later photographed the Nuba of Sudan and died in 2003, aged 101.
[hist-eur3-s12.10.7] She was a willing participant who gave the regime its most lasting images. Historians and critics judge her films technically brilliant and influential but reject her claim to be non-political, as Susan Sontag did in her 1975 essay 'Fascinating Fascism', and they still debate the line between art and propaganda.
[hist-eur3-s12.11.b] The Nuremberg Rallies were the Nazi party's yearly show of strength. Nuremberg was chosen for the setting because of what the city stood for and because it suited huge gatherings.
[hist-eur3-s12.11.0] The Reichsparteitag (Reich party congress, or rally) was the party's annual gathering. Do not confuse it with the Reichstag, the parliament.
[hist-eur3-s12.11.1] Earlier rallies were held in Munich (1923) and Weimar (1926). The party then met in Nuremberg in August 1927 and 1929, and every September from 1933 to 1938, each rally lasting about a week.
[hist-eur3-s12.11.2] Nuremberg was a medieval free imperial city of the Holy Roman Empire and a symbol of German history and greatness. The Nazis used its old buildings and traditions to link the Third Reich to the First (the Holy Roman Empire).
[hist-eur3-s12.11.3] Practical reasons also counted: it had a central position and good railway links, the local party under Julius Streicher (Gauleiter of Franconia) was strong, and there was open land for vast grounds.
[hist-eur3-s12.12.b] The grounds were built to turn the rallies into a spectacle of order and power, and they were designed around the Dutzendteich lake, away from the old city.
[hist-eur3-s12.12.0] The grounds lay south-east of the city, around the Dutzendteich lake, on open land that could hold vast crowds.
[hist-eur3-s12.12.1] The Luitpold Arena was used for the mass assemblies of the SA, SS and Hitler Youth, and was designed to hold large numbers of marching men. It also contained the Hall of Honour war memorial.
[hist-eur3-s12.12.2] The Zeppelin Field had a grandstand designed by Albert Speer and modelled on the Pergamon Altar. It was used for mass parades and for Hitler's appearance before the party's formations.
[hist-eur3-s12.12.3] The Congress Hall was begun but never completed, and the German Stadium was planned but barely begun, which shows how much grander the grounds were meant to become.
[hist-eur3-s12.12.4] Albert Speer was the young architect who designed the grounds and staging and won Hitler's favour.
[hist-eur3-s12.12.5] Staging used monumental stone architecture, huge swastika banners and eagles, massed standards, torchlight processions, bands and drums. The scale made each individual feel part of something vast.
[hist-eur3-s12.12.6] The 'cathedral of light' was Speer's ring of anti-aircraft searchlights pointed into the night sky around the Zeppelin Field. Sources give about 130 to 150 lights, and it was used in the mid- to late 1930s rallies.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.b] Each year's rally was named for what the regime wanted to celebrate, so the sequence of themes follows the regime's own progress from takeover to expansion.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.0] The 1933 rally in September, the first held after Hitler became Chancellor, was the Rally of Victory, celebrating the seizure of power, and it was filmed as Victory of Faith.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.1] The September 1934 rally was the first after the Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934), when Röhm and the SA leadership were murdered. It was staged to show party, SA, SS, Labour Service and army united behind Hitler, and was filmed as Triumph of the Will.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.2] The 1935 rally was the Rally of Freedom, celebrating the return of conscription (March 1935) and rearmament, which the regime presented as Germany breaking free of Versailles.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.3] During that rally the Reichstag was summoned to Nuremberg and on 15 September 1935 passed the Nuremberg Laws, together with the Reich Flag Law that made the swastika flag the national flag.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.4] The 1936 rally was the Rally of Honour, after the remilitarisation of the Rhineland in March 1936. The Four Year Plan was proclaimed at it, and Göring was put in charge in October 1936.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.5] The 1937 rally was the Rally of Labour and included parades of workers and the Labour Service.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.6] The 1938 rally was the Rally of Greater Germany, celebrating the Anschluss of March 1938. Hitler's closing speech attacked Czechoslovakia over the Sudetenland, only weeks before the Munich Agreement of 29-30 September 1938.
[hist-eur3-s12.13.7] The 1939 Rally of Peace was planned but cancelled because of the invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939 and the outbreak of war.
[hist-eur3-s12.14.b] A fixed programme of set pieces made each rally feel like a ritual of loyalty rather than a party meeting.
[hist-eur3-s12.14.0] Days were set aside for particular groups: the political leaders, the Labour Service, the Hitler Youth, the SA and SS, and the armed forces, who had their own day. Hitler gave an opening proclamation and a closing speech, and there were parades through the old city.
[hist-eur3-s12.14.1] At the Luitpold Arena in 1934 Hitler walked between the SA and SS ranks with Himmler (head of the SS) and Viktor Lutze (SA chief of staff after Röhm) to the war memorial. This staged a reassurance to the SA and an image of the new order after the purge.
[hist-eur3-s12.14.2] In the consecration of the standards, Hitler touched new flags with the Blood Flag (Blutfahne), the banner said to have been carried in the Munich Putsch of November 1923. This linked the new banners to the party's own founding story.
[hist-eur3-s12.14.3] Rituals included oaths of loyalty, and in 1934 the Labour Service marched with spades. Together with the banners they gave the rallies a quasi-religious atmosphere and a sense of sacred purpose.
[hist-eur3-s12.14.4] The night-time ceremonies, such as the political leaders' assembly under the cathedral of light and the torchlight processions, were the most dramatic part of the programme.
[hist-eur3-s12.14.5] Hitler's speeches expressed party doctrine and foreign policy, which is why the rallies were also used to announce policies, as the Four Year Plan was in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s12.15.b] What the rallies meant depended on who was there and who was watching. Participants, foreign diplomats and journalists left very different accounts, and the exam uses them as documents.
[hist-eur3-s12.15.0] Hundreds of thousands of party members, workers, soldiers and youths came by special trains and lived in huge tent camps.
[hist-eur3-s12.15.1] The camps ran under strict rules, such as passes to leave the grounds, fire safety, silence at night and the reporting of sickness, so that large numbers could be controlled.
[hist-eur3-s12.15.2] Days were long, with drilling, marching and waiting. For many it was a powerful experience of comradeship and belonging, while for others it was exhausting.
[hist-eur3-s12.15.3] Participants were organised into units and marched in disciplined formations, which created a visual impression of unity and order.
[hist-eur3-s12.15.4] German participants showed enthusiasm, and foreign visitors and journalists were impressed too, some seeing evidence of the order and vitality of the new Germany.
[hist-eur3-s12.15.5] William Shirer, an American correspondent, saw the 1934 rally and wrote about it in Berlin Diary (1941). Charles Bewley, Irish Minister (envoy) in Berlin from 1933 to 1939, was sympathetic to the regime.
[hist-eur3-s12.15.6] Nevile Henderson, British ambassador to Germany from 1937, called the searchlight display a 'cathedral of ice', a phrase that echoes the 'cathedral of light' but with ice instead of light.
[hist-eur3-s12.16.b] The rallies were staged for several purposes at once, and their reach went far beyond Nuremberg. Their afterlife shows how the city has dealt with that past.
[hist-eur3-s12.16.0] The purpose was to display unity, order and strength, to build the Hitler cult and to bind the individual to the national community, which is why everyone marched, sang and swore oaths together.
[hist-eur3-s12.16.1] Policies were announced at the rallies, including the Nuremberg Laws, the Four Year Plan and foreign policy claims, so racial ideas were presented as part of the national community.
[hist-eur3-s12.16.2] The rallies also aimed to impress and intimidate foreign opinion by showing a disciplined, rearming Germany.
[hist-eur3-s12.16.3] Radio, newsreels, the press and Riefenstahl's films carried the rallies to millions who never attended, and the films reached cinemas in Germany and abroad.
[hist-eur3-s12.16.4] In April 1945 the US Army blew up the swastika on the Zeppelin Field grandstand. After 1945 the grounds were used by the US Army and the city, and parts of the Zeppelin Field survive today.
[hist-eur3-s12.16.5] The Nuremberg war crimes trials (1945-46) were held in the city partly for its symbolism.
[hist-eur3-s12.16.6] The unfinished Congress Hall now holds a Documentation Centre, which opened in 2001.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.b] Judgements of Nazi propaganda weigh its success in creating consent and loyalty against its limits, and ask how far it explains support for the regime.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.0] Achievement: the Hitler cult and genuine popularity in the 1930s, the rallies' spectacle and the films' reach gave the regime a powerful image of unity.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.1] Limit: propaganda failed to win over many workers and churchgoers, and it depended on terror, so the police, courts and camps were needed to suppress opposition.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.2] Limit: people tired of constant political content, which is why so much of the media was entertainment, and after 1943 propaganda focused on defence and national survival, and its credibility fell as the war turned against Germany.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.3] Contemporary view: many Germans responded with enthusiasm, and many foreigners were impressed, seeing awe-inspiring order and unity, while exiled opponents and foreign critics saw the rallies as an exhibition of dictatorship.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.4] Later view: historians see manipulation and a link between the rallies, the racial laws and war, and Riefenstahl's films remain among the most studied examples of film propaganda and have shaped how later film-makers use visual rhetoric.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.5] How far propaganda explains support: Ian Kershaw (The 'Hitler Myth', 1987) stresses that propaganda reinforced existing beliefs, and that economic recovery, foreign policy success, national pride and fear also mattered.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.6] The reports of the exiled Social Democrats (Sopade) show grumbling and limits to belief, which is evidence that many people did not accept everything they were told.
[hist-eur3-s12.17.7] Goebbels and Riefenstahl as contributors: Goebbels was the organiser who controlled what Germans saw and heard, while Riefenstahl was the artist who created the regime's most lasting images.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s2: 2. Weimar Germany and the rise of Hitler, 1920-1933
[hist-eur3-s2.0.b] The Weimar Republic was born out of defeat, and that shaped how many Germans judged it from the start. To follow the rest of the story you need the basic machinery of the new state and the parties that competed inside it.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.0] Germany lost the First World War: the Kaiser (Wilhelm II) abdicated on 9 November 1918 and a republic was proclaimed the same day, and the armistice of 11 November 1918 ended the fighting.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.1] Democracy means government by elected representatives, with free elections and the rule of law. Weimar was Germany's first democracy, and it was fragile because it was created in defeat and many powerful people never accepted it.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.2] The system had an elected President as head of state and a Chancellor as head of government, who needed the support of a majority in the Reichstag (parliament). Because no party won a majority alone, the Chancellor usually led a coalition of several parties.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.3] The main parties, from left to right, were the Communists (KPD), the Social Democrats (SPD), the Catholic Centre Party, the liberal parties (including Stresemann's DVP), the nationalist DNVP and the Nazis (NSDAP). The SPD, Centre and liberals were the parties that supported the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.4] Reparations are payments that a defeated state makes to the victors for war damage; they became a lasting source of bitterness in Germany.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.5] A putsch is an attempt to seize power by force. Several were tried against the republic, from both left and right.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.6] The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 began the worldwide Depression, which later ended Germany's recovery.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.b] The National Assembly drew up a democratic constitution in 1919, but some of its features and some of the people left in charge of the state made it easy to undermine.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.0] The National Assembly met at Weimar, away from the unrest in Berlin, and adopted a constitution on 31 July 1919 creating a federal republic. All men and women over 20 could vote.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.1] The President was elected by the people for seven years and the Chancellor was responsible to the Reichstag.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.2] Proportional representation for the Reichstag (seats shared out in line with each party's share of votes) produced many small parties, so stable majority government was very difficult to form.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.3] Article 48 allowed the President to issue emergency decrees and to rule without the Reichstag. This power was meant for emergencies, but it became central after 1930, when governments no longer had Reichstag majorities.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.4] Many judges, civil servants, army officers and university teachers from the imperial era stayed in post and remained hostile to the republic, so the state's own servants could not be relied on to defend it.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.5] The 'stab in the back' myth claimed that Germany had not really been beaten and that the politicians who made peace in November 1918, labelled the 'November criminals', had betrayed the army. It blamed these politicians, not the army, for defeat.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.b] The republic's first leaders and its peace settlement set the scene for its early troubles. Many Germans blamed the republic for a treaty that it had been forced to sign.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.0] Friedrich Ebert was the first President, from 1919 until his death in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.1] Paul von Hindenburg, a former field marshal, succeeded Ebert in 1925 and was re-elected in 1932. He was President, never Chancellor,.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.2] The Treaty of Versailles of June 1919 returned Alsace-Lorraine to France, took away Germany's colonies, limited the army to 100,000 men with no air force, demilitarised the Rhineland and imposed reparations.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.3] Article 231, the war-guilt clause, placed the blame for the war on Germany, and was felt as a national humiliation.
[hist-eur3-s2.2.4] The London Schedule of 1921 set Germany's reparations at 132 billion gold marks, a sum that many Germans regarded as impossible and humiliating, so parties promising to overturn Versailles gained appeal.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.b] In its first years the republic faced armed challenges from left and right. Its judges and army officers were often sympathetic to the right, so the threats from the right were treated far more lightly.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.0] The Spartacist rising of January 1919 (a communist uprising in Berlin) and the murders of Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg by Freikorps soldiers (volunteer units of ex-soldiers) were put down with the help of Freikorps units.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.1] The Kapp Putsch of March 1920 was led by Wolfgang Kapp with General Walther von Lüttwitz and the Ehrhardt Brigade, a Freikorps unit. They seized Berlin, and the army refused to defend the government, which left Berlin.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.2] The putsch collapsed after four days because the trade unions called a general strike, so the government was saved by workers rather than by its own army.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.3] Political murders by the far right, including the killing of Matthias Erzberger in 1921 and of Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau in June 1922, showed the weakness of the state's protection of its leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.3.4] The judiciary favoured the right: the statistician Emil Gumbel counted 354 political murders by the right and 22 by the left between 1919 and 1922, with far heavier sentences for left-wing killers.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.b] In 1923 a dispute over reparations led to a crisis that destroyed the value of German money. This is hyperinflation, the extreme form of inflation, in which prices rise so fast that money loses nearly all its value.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.0] Germany fell behind on reparations deliveries of timber and coal, so in January 1923 France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr, the main industrial region, to take coal and goods.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.1] The German government under Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno called for passive resistance in the Ruhr (workers refusing to co-operate with the occupiers), which the government funded by printing money.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.2] Printing so much money made it worth less and less, which is the cause of the hyperinflation of 1923. Before the war there were about 4.2 marks to the US dollar; by November 1923 there were about 4.2 trillion.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.3] Prices rose by the hour, so workers were paid daily or twice daily and rushed to spend their wages before they lost value, and barter replaced money in many places.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.4] Middle-class savings, pensions and war loans were largely wiped out, because they were fixed sums in a currency that no longer bought anything. This left many people who had supported the old order resentful of the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.4.5] Debtors, speculators and large industrialists such as Hugo Stinnes, who borrowed to buy assets, gained from the inflation, because they repaid their debts in worthless money, while people on fixed incomes and savers lost most.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.b] Once passive resistance ended and a new currency was issued, the republic recovered. The Dawes Plan then gave Germany a workable way to pay reparations, but it tied recovery to foreign money.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.0] Gustav Stresemann became Chancellor in August 1923 (he held the post only until November) and called off passive resistance in the Ruhr in September.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.1] The Rentenmark, introduced on 15 November 1923, was backed by a mortgage on land and industry, which gave people reason to trust it, and it ended the hyperinflation.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.2] Hjalmar Schacht, as currency commissioner and then Reichsbank president, kept the supply of Rentenmarks tightly limited. It was a temporary currency, replaced by the permanent Reichsmark in 1924.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.3] The Dawes Plan of 1924, drawn up by a committee chaired by the American Charles Dawes, set reparations payments that rose with Germany's capacity to pay and provided a loan of 800 million gold marks. It did not reduce the total.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.4] Under the Dawes Plan the French left the Ruhr by 1925, which ended the occupation.
[hist-eur3-s2.5.5] The German recovery of 1924 to 1929 depended on American money lent under the Dawes Plan, so it collapsed when the loans were withdrawn after the Wall Street Crash began in October 1929.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.b] Hitler built a small Bavarian party into a movement with its own private army, and in 1923 tried to use it to seize power by force.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.0] Hitler joined the small German Workers' Party in Munich in September 1919. It was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) in February 1920 with a 25-point programme, and Hitler became its leader with dictatorial powers in July 1921.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.1] The party's early support came from Bavarian ex-soldiers and anti-communist groups, who were angry at defeat and afraid of revolution.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.2] The Sturmabteilung (SA), a paramilitary force founded in 1921, whose street violence gave the Nazis a reputation for strength.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.3] On 8 to 9 November 1923 Hitler and Erich Ludendorff tried to seize power in Munich, starting in the Bürgerbräukeller beer hall, as a first step to a march on Berlin.
[hist-eur3-s2.6.4] The Munich (Beer Hall) Putsch failed: Bavarian police fired on the marchers, killing sixteen Nazis, and four police officers died.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.b] The failed putsch turned out to help Hitler, because the trial made him famous and convinced him to change tactics. His party stayed small in the prosperous years, until a campaign on reparations gave it respectability.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.0] Hitler was tried for high treason in early 1924 and used the trial as a national platform. The sympathetic court gave him five years, the minimum sentence, and he served only about nine months in Landsberg prison.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.1] He wrote Mein Kampf in prison, setting out his views on race, Lebensraum (living space) and the need to destroy Marxism and the Jews. The first volume was published in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.2] The party had been banned after the putsch. Hitler refounded it in February 1925 and adopted a strategy of winning power legally through elections.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.3] The strategy had little effect while Germany was prosperous: in the 1928 Reichstag election the Nazis won only 12 seats and 2.6 per cent of the vote.
[hist-eur3-s2.7.4] Hitler's prominence in the 1929 campaign against the Young Plan, alongside Alfred Hugenberg's nationalist DNVP and its newspapers (Hugenberg was the owner), gave the Nazis national publicity and respectability on the right.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.b] Gustav Stresemann was Chancellor only from August to November 1923, but as Foreign Minister he won back Germany's place among the powers. Most of his achievements belong to this later role.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.0] As Foreign Minister from 1923 until his death in October 1929, Stresemann pursued the revision of Versailles by negotiation rather than force, a policy of fulfilment.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.1] The Locarno Treaties of October 1925 confirmed Germany's western borders (not its eastern ones), which reassured France, and led to Germany's entry into the League of Nations in 1926.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.2] Stresemann shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1926 with the French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand, in recognition of this improved relationship.
[hist-eur3-s2.8.3] The Young Plan of 1929, chaired by the American Owen D. Young, reduced the total of reparations and spread payments to 1988, so it lowered the annual burden on Germany.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.b] Between 1924 and 1929 Germany enjoyed relative prosperity, and many historians see these as the republic's best years. Look closely, though, and the recovery was less secure than it seemed.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.0] Weimar governments built a welfare state: the eight-hour day (1918) and large state-funded housing programmes in the later 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.1] The 1927 Act created national unemployment insurance, but it was funded for far fewer claimants than the Depression produced, so it broke down after 1929.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.2] The recovery depended on American loans, which meant that Germany had no reserves of its own when the loans were called in.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.3] Agriculture and the middle classes did not share fully in the prosperity, so discontent remained beneath the surface.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.4] Stresemann died in October 1929, and his successors could not secure the political support needed to continue his policies.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.b] The Depression is the slump after 1929, with falling output, bank failures and mass unemployment. In Germany it ended the recovery and brought down the coalition governments.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.0] Unemployment rose from an annual average of about 1.9 million in 1929 to about 6 million registered unemployed by early 1932, because American loans were called in and exports fell. It was above three million by 1930.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.1] The grand coalition of Social Democrats, Centre and liberal parties broke down in March 1930 over unemployment insurance contributions and the budget.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.2] Heinrich Brüning of the Centre Party, the new Chancellor from March 1930, governed through Article 48 emergency decrees, cutting wages, pensions and welfare to balance the budget.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.3] These deflationary policies (cutting spending and prices) raised unemployment and gave the impression that the government was making the crisis worse, so support for the republic weakened.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.4] The banking crisis of 1931 deepened the slump: the Creditanstalt in Vienna collapsed in May and the Danat Bank in Germany in July.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.5] Brüning hoped deflation would prove that Germany could not pay reparations. Hoover's moratorium (a pause in payments) suspended them in 1931, and the Lausanne Conference of 1932, under his successor, effectively ended them.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.6] Middle-class and farming groups, badly hit by falling prices, turned to parties that promised radical solutions.
[hist-eur3-s2.10.7] Brüning, Papen and Schleicher in turn governed through the President, with emergency decrees, rather than through a Reichstag majority. These were the presidential cabinets.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.b] The Nazi party's rapid electoral growth came from its appeal to unemployed people, farmers, small business owners and Protestant voters, especially in rural areas, and from skilful propaganda backed by the SA.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.0] In the September 1930 Reichstag election the NSDAP won 107 seats and 18.3 per cent of the vote, up from 12 seats in 1928, becoming the second largest party after the SPD. The Communists also gained, winning 77 seats.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.1] Nazi propaganda combined attacks on the Versailles settlement, the Weimar parties and communism with promises of jobs, bread and national revival, so it appealed to people who felt let down by the republic.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.2] Goebbels, Gauleiter (regional party leader) of Berlin from 1926, ran propaganda with mass rallies, posters, newspapers such as Der Angriff and film. In 1932 Hitler toured the country by aeroplane, speaking at several rallies a day.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.3] The SA's street violence against communists and Social Democrats gave the party a reputation for strength, and it drew unemployed young men into its ranks, which gave them uniforms, meals and shelter.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.4] Nazi voters were disproportionately Protestant, rural and small-town, middle-class (shopkeepers, farmers, white-collar workers, civil servants and pensioners) and young.
[hist-eur3-s2.11.5] Catholic areas loyal to the Centre Party and industrial workers loyal to the SPD and KPD were less receptive, though the Nazis did win many working-class votes.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.b] Through 1932 the Nazis reached their highest vote in a free election, but they never won a majority, and by the end of the year their support was slipping.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.0] Hitler became a German citizen in February 1932 so that he could stand for President. In the first round in March he won about 30 per cent to Hindenburg's 49.6 per cent.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.1] In the run-off on 10 April Hindenburg won about 53 per cent to Hitler's 37 per cent, so Hindenburg was re-elected.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.2] In the July 1932 election the NSDAP won 230 seats and about 37 per cent of the vote, making it the largest party in the Reichstag.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.3] Hitler refused any post below Chancellor, and Hindenburg refused to appoint him in August 1932.
[hist-eur3-s2.12.4] In the November 1932 election the NSDAP lost about two million votes and 34 seats, falling to 196 seats and about 33 per cent, although it remained the largest party. The party was also short of money.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.b] After Brüning, government depended on the President's favour and on private intrigue, and the SA's violence made politics even less stable.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.0] Brüning banned the SA and SS in April 1932, but Hindenburg dismissed him in May. Franz von Papen, Chancellor from June, lifted the ban, which led to street battles such as the Altona 'Bloody Sunday' of July 1932.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.1] Papen's government removed the Social Democrat government of Prussia in July 1932, in what became known as the Prussian coup, so a major state government lost its elected leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.2] Kurt von Schleicher became Chancellor on 3 December 1932 and tried to split the Nazis by offering the Vice-Chancellorship to Gregor Strasser, and to win trade union support with job-creation schemes.
[hist-eur3-s2.13.3] He failed to build a majority and resigned on 28 January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.14.b] The final step to power came through a deal among conservatives who believed they could use the Nazis' popularity for their own purposes.
[hist-eur3-s2.14.0] Papen met Hitler at the banker Kurt von Schröder's house in Cologne on 4 January 1933, and then persuaded Hindenburg to appoint Hitler Chancellor.
[hist-eur3-s2.14.1] Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933, in a cabinet with only three Nazis (Hitler, Wilhelm Frick and Hermann Göring), because the conservatives believed they could control him.
[hist-eur3-s2.14.2] Papen was Vice-Chancellor and Hugenberg was also in the cabinet, so the conservatives held most posts.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.b] This entry covers Hitler's route to the Chancellorship, from his early life to 30 January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.0] Born in 1889 at Braunau am Inn in Austria, he served in the German army in the First World War and was awarded the Iron Cross.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.1] He joined the German Workers' Party in September 1919 while working as a political informant for the army in Munich, and became its most effective speaker.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.2] As leader of the NSDAP from 1921, he used rallies, a private army (the SA) and personal charisma to make the party the main vehicle of the far right.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.3] He was a participant who learned from failure: after the Munich Putsch he chose the legal road to power through elections, while using the SA's street violence to show strength.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.4] Contemporaries were divided: millions of voters saw him as a national saviour, the left saw him as a tool of big business, and conservatives such as Papen saw a demagogue whom they believed they had hired and could control.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.5] Historians agree that he was appointed legally, but differ on why. Alan Bullock, in Hitler: A Study in Tyranny (1952), stressed his opportunism and will to power.
[hist-eur3-s2.15.6] Henry Ashby Turner, in Hitler's Thirty Days to Power (1996), showed that his appointment depended on intrigues among a few men around Hindenburg in January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.b] Weimar governments faced war debts, housing shortages, inflation and then mass unemployment. Their responses moved from welfare spending to deflation to emergency job creation, with mixed success.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.0] After 1918 the republic recognised trade unions and collective bargaining. The Stinnes-Legien agreement of November 1918 gave the eight-hour day and union recognition in return for employers' co-operation.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.1] The Hauszinssteuer, a tax on house owners who had gained from inflation, funded large municipal housing estates in Berlin and Frankfurt in the later 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.2] Stresemann's government ended hyperinflation in 1923 by ending passive resistance, balancing the budget and issuing the Rentenmark, but the middle classes who lost their savings were never compensated in full.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.3] The 1927 Unemployment Insurance Act gave benefit to workers who paid contributions, but it was designed for about 800,000 claimants and was overwhelmed when unemployment passed three million.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.4] Brüning's emergency decrees of 1930 and 1931 cut civil service pay, unemployment benefit and wages and raised taxes in order to balance the budget, a deflationary policy that deepened the slump.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.5] The Voluntary Labour Service (Freiwilliger Arbeitsdienst) of 1931 gave unemployed young men work on public projects for pocket money, and it was widened under Papen and Schleicher.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.6] Papen's September 1932 programme offered tax certificates to employers who hired extra workers, and Schleicher's emergency job-creation programme of December 1932 tried to win union support, but neither had time to take effect before January 1933.
[hist-eur3-s2.16.7] Social strain was severe by 1932: millions of families lived on reduced benefit or on poor relief, and thousands of young unemployed men joined the SA, which provided uniforms, meals and shelter.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.b] Judgements of the republic divide between blaming its structure and the Treaty of Versailles, and emphasising the impact of the Depression and the choices of its leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.0] Structural view: proportional representation, Article 48 and the lack of a stable parliamentary majority left the republic dependent on emergency government. Against this, the constitution worked in the stable years of 1924 to 1929.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.1] Versailles view: contemporaries on the right blamed the treaty for national humiliation, and this shaped the appeal of parties promising revision. Against this, Stresemann succeeded in revising reparations and winning acceptance for Germany by negotiation.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.2] Achievement: Stresemann's policies produced a recovery between 1924 and 1929, with inflation defeated and Germany admitted to the League of Nations.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.3] Limit: that recovery depended heavily on American loans, so the Wall Street Crash exposed its fragility and left no reserves to cushion the unemployment that followed.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.4] The Depression was the decisive trigger: the Nazis won 12 seats in 1928 but became a mass party by 1930. Others stress long-term causes such as hostile elites, Versailles and the weak constitution.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.5] Later historians such as Henry Ashby Turner stress that Nazi electoral support was falling by late 1932 and that Hitler's appointment was not inevitable but resulted from decisions by a small group of conservative politicians.
[hist-eur3-s2.17.6] Contemporary left-wing and liberal critics blamed the conservative elite for undermining the republic, while the Nazi party presented itself as the only force able to restore order.
