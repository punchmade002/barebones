TARGET hist-eur6-s13: 13. Advances in military, space and information technology; case study: the Moon landing, 1969
[hist-eur6-s13.0.b] After 1945 the United States and the Soviet Union competed in weapons, space and computing, and governments paid for much of the research. Two key ideas, technological development and the military-industrial complex, tie the whole subject together.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.0] Technological development here means the rapid advances in military, space and computing technology after 1945. They were driven largely by Cold War rivalry and by government money, because each superpower feared falling behind the other.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.1] The arms race began after the United States dropped atomic bombs in 1945 and the Soviet Union tested its own atomic bomb in August 1949. Each side then built bigger weapons and better ways of delivering them.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.2] German rocket science mattered too. Wernher von Braun, a German rocket engineer who worked on the V-2 rocket, moved to the United States after 1945 under Operation Paperclip, and he later led the Saturn V work at the Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.3] The military-industrial complex is the alliance of the armed forces, defence contractors (private firms that build weapons and equipment) and politicians. Each gains from high defence spending, so the alliance tends to keep that spending going.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.4] Eisenhower's farewell address on 17 January 1961 warned the nation to guard against the unwarranted influence of the military-industrial complex. Coming from a former general, the warning carried great weight.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.5] In practice the complex funded missiles, aerospace and early computing, and the same research often fed the space and computing programmes. The presidents of the period were Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy, Johnson, Nixon and Reagan.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.b] The first atomic bombs were followed by a far more powerful weapon, the hydrogen (thermonuclear) bomb, which uses the fusion of atoms rather than their splitting. The decision to build it was made quickly and not without argument.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.0] The Soviet atomic test of August 1949 ended the American monopoly on atomic weapons. This is why pressure grew in the United States to develop something even more powerful.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.1] President Truman announced the decision to develop the hydrogen bomb on 31 January 1950. Edward Teller supported the decision.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.2] J. Robert Oppenheimer and the General Advisory Committee of the Atomic Energy Commission opposed it.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.3] Ivy Mike, the first American thermonuclear test, took place at Enewetak (Eniwetok) Atoll in the Pacific in November 1952.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.4] The Soviet Union tested a hydrogen-bomb type device in August 1953. This was less than a year later, so the United States held its lead only briefly.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.b] A bomb is only a threat if it can be delivered. The United States built three ways of delivering nuclear weapons, so that an enemy could not hope to destroy all of them at once.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.0] The B-52 Stratofortress, a long-range jet bomber that entered service in 1955, carried nuclear weapons on strategic patrols during the Cold War. Keeping bombers in the air made them hard to destroy on the ground.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.1] Intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs, rockets that carry a warhead from one continent to another) began with the Atlas, operational from about 1959, and then the Minuteman, deployed from 1962.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.2] An ICBM can reach the Soviet Union from the United States in about thirty minutes. This gave the United States the ability to strike very fast and left the Soviets little time to respond.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.3] USS Nautilus, the first nuclear-powered submarine, entered service in 1954. Nuclear power let a submarine stay under water for long periods.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.4] Polaris missiles, fired from submarines, were first carried on patrol in 1960. They gave the United States a second-strike capability (the power to hit back even after being attacked), because hidden submarines could not easily be destroyed in a surprise attack.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.5] By the late 1960s the United States had about 1,000 Minuteman missiles and about 41 Polaris submarines. From about 1970 MIRVs (several warheads on a single missile, first on Minuteman III) multiplied the number of warheads.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.6] MIRVs made arms control more difficult, because each missile now carried several targets' worth of destruction and it was harder to agree how to count and limit the arsenals.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.b] Each side knew little about the other's real strength, and fear filled the gap. Satellites eventually replaced guesswork with photographs.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.0] The missile gap was the claim, raised in the late 1950s and by Kennedy in the 1960 election campaign, that the Soviet Union was ahead of the United States in missiles. It made voters fear the nation was falling behind.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.1] In fact the United States held a large lead. American satellite photographs in 1961 showed the Soviet Union had far fewer ICBMs than had been claimed, and the missile gap collapsed.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.2] Before satellites the United States relied on U-2 spy aircraft. In May 1960 the U-2 pilot Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union, which showed how risky such flights were.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.3] The Corona programme (run by the CIA and the US Air Force) took its first successful photographs from space in August 1960. Satellites replaced the risky U-2 flights, so no pilot's life or national embarrassment was at stake.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.b] Defence spending did not stay in the army and navy. It paid for industries, shaped whole regions and left behind technologies that civilians later used.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.0] Jet fighters and bombers, nuclear submarines and guided missiles were funded by the Pentagon (the US Defense Department). Defence contracts supported the aerospace industry in California, Texas and Washington State, which is the military-industrial complex at work.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.1] Military research on computers, radar and jet propulsion created technologies that were later adopted in civil aviation and in commercial electronics, so defence money had civilian benefits.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.2] GPS, the satellite navigation system, began as a military programme, with the first satellite launched in 1978. It shows how a military tool can become an everyday civilian one.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.3] Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) in a television address on 23 March 1983. It proposed a space-based defence against missiles, a shield that would destroy them in flight.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.4] Critics nicknamed it 'Star Wars' because it sounded like science fiction. It was never built or deployed, but its research funded lasers and sensors.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.b] On 4 October 1957 the Soviet Union put a small metal sphere into orbit. The United States came to see space as a test of its technological and political strength, and it reacted at once.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.0] Sputnik 1, launched on 4 October 1957, was the first artificial satellite (a human-made object that circles the Earth). Sputnik 2, carrying the dog Laika, followed in November 1957.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.1] America's first reply failed publicly: the Vanguard rocket exploded on its launch pad in December 1957. Explorer 1, launched on 31 January 1958 by a team that included von Braun, put the first American satellite into orbit.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.2] Sputnik caused alarm in the United States about Soviet technology and education.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.3] The National Defense Education Act of 1958 gave funding for science, mathematics and languages, to produce more engineers and scientists.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.4] NASA, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, was created by Act of 1958, signed by Eisenhower in July and operating from 1 October 1958. It directed the civil space programme and took over work from the military and from the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.5] ARPA, the Pentagon's research agency, was also created in 1958 after Sputnik. It later funded the ARPANET, the network that grew into the internet.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.b] Both sides raced to put people into space. The Soviets won the early rounds, and each Soviet success increased American pressure to respond.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.0] Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space, and the first in orbit, on 12 April 1961. This Soviet achievement increased pressure on the United States to respond.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.1] The Mercury programme of 1958 to 1963 sent the first American astronauts into space. It was designed to find out whether people could survive and work there.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.2] Alan Shepard became the first American in space on 5 May 1961. His flight was suborbital (it rose and fell without circling the Earth) and lasted about fifteen minutes.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.3] John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth on 20 February 1962. An orbit means circling the planet, which is much harder than Shepard's up-and-down flight.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.4] The Soviet Union scored further firsts: Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space in June 1963, and Alexei Leonov made the first spacewalk in March 1965.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.b] Kennedy turned the competition into a clear goal with a deadline. The goal then decided how NASA trained, practised and designed its spacecraft.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.0] Kennedy's decision followed Gagarin's flight and the failed Bay of Pigs invasion (April 1961). He believed the Moon landing was a field in which the United States could win.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.1] In a speech to Congress on 25 May 1961 Kennedy committed the United States to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth before the decade was out.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.2] At Rice University on 12 September 1962 he said the nation chose to go to the Moon, not because it was easy but because it was hard.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.3] NASA chose lunar orbit rendezvous in 1962. A separate lunar module descends to the surface while the command module stays in orbit, so only a small craft has to land and take off.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.4] The Gemini programme of 1965 and 1966 practised rendezvous (meeting in space) and docking (joining two craft), as well as long flights. Gemini 8 made the first docking in space in March 1966.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.5] The Soviet Moon rocket, the N1, failed in all four test launches between 1969 and 1972, and the programme was later cancelled. The Soviet crewed lunar effort therefore never reached the Moon.
[hist-eur6-s13.8.b] The Apollo programme was the largest peacetime technological effort the United States had undertaken. Reaching the Moon depended as much on organisation and money as on rockets.
[hist-eur6-s13.8.0] NASA managed Apollo with major contractors: North American Aviation (command and service modules and the second stage), Grumman (lunar module), Boeing (first stage of the Saturn V) and IBM. It employed hundreds of thousands of people across the country.
[hist-eur6-s13.8.1] James Webb, NASA administrator from 1961, managed the political side and the vast contracting system. He had to keep Congress and industry committed over many years.
[hist-eur6-s13.8.2] Each centre had its own job: the Marshall Space Flight Center in Alabama built rockets under von Braun, the Manned Spacecraft Center in Houston, Texas, directed flights, and Kennedy Space Center in Florida launched them.
[hist-eur6-s13.8.3] Universities took part too. The Apollo Guidance Computer was developed at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.
[hist-eur6-s13.8.4] Employment was concentrated in Florida, Texas, Alabama and California, which tied the growth of the Sun Belt (the southern and western states) to federal spending.
[hist-eur6-s13.8.5] Apollo cost about 25 billion dollars in the money of its time. NASA's share of federal spending peaked in the mid-1960s at about 4 to 5 per cent, which shows the scale of the commitment.
[hist-eur6-s13.8.6] Critics attacked the cost. Amitai Etzioni's The Moon-Doggle (1964) called the programme a waste, and some in Congress argued that the money would be better spent on poverty.
[hist-eur6-s13.9.b] A Moon landing needed a giant rocket, a three-part spacecraft and a series of tests, some of them tragic. Each mission tested one more step of the plan.
[hist-eur6-s13.9.0] The Saturn V was a three-stage rocket about 110 metres tall and the most powerful rocket built to that time. It flew thirteen times, and none of its flights lost a crew.
[hist-eur6-s13.9.1] The Apollo spacecraft had three parts: the command module, which carried the crew back to Earth, the service module, which supplied power and propulsion, and the lunar module, which took two astronauts to the surface.
[hist-eur6-s13.9.2] The Apollo Guidance Computer was one of the first computers to use integrated circuits (many tiny electronic parts on one chip). This made the programme a major customer for the new technology.
[hist-eur6-s13.9.3] The Apollo 1 fire on 27 January 1967, in the capsule during a test on the launch pad, killed Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee. It led to a redesign of the spacecraft, so later crews were safer.
[hist-eur6-s13.9.4] Apollo 7 (October 1968) tested the command module in Earth orbit. Apollo 8, 21 to 27 December 1968, carried Frank Borman, Jim Lovell and William Anders as the first humans to orbit the Moon, though not to land.
[hist-eur6-s13.9.5] Apollo 8 returned the Earthrise photograph, which showed the Earth rising over the lunar horizon.
[hist-eur6-s13.9.6] In 1969 Apollo 9 (March) tested the lunar module in Earth orbit, and Apollo 10 (May) flew the lunar module to about 15 kilometres above the surface without landing.
[hist-eur6-s13.10.b] Apollo 11 was launched from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida, landed in the Sea of Tranquillity and returned its crew to the Pacific. The mission can be told in order of dates.
[hist-eur6-s13.10.0] The crew were Neil Armstrong (commander), Edwin 'Buzz' Aldrin (lunar module pilot) and Michael Collins (command module pilot), who stayed in orbit around the Moon in Columbia. Collins did not walk on the Moon.
[hist-eur6-s13.10.1] Apollo 11 was launched on 16 July 1969 on a Saturn V from Kennedy Space Center, and it entered lunar orbit on 19 July.
[hist-eur6-s13.10.2] The lunar module Eagle landed in the Sea of Tranquillity on 20 July 1969. The landing was risky: computer alarms sounded during the descent, and Armstrong took manual control to avoid a field of boulders.
[hist-eur6-s13.10.3] Eagle landed with very little fuel left, so the margin for error had been small.
[hist-eur6-s13.10.4] Armstrong stepped onto the surface late that evening, at 10.56 pm Eastern time. Aldrin joined him, so Armstrong was first and Aldrin second, and the two spent about two and a half hours outside.
[hist-eur6-s13.10.5] The crew collected about 21 kilograms of lunar rock and soil, and left scientific instruments behind, including a seismometer that measured the Moon's seismic activity.
[hist-eur6-s13.10.6] Apollo 11 splashed down in the Pacific Ocean on 24 July 1969, and the USS Hornet picked up the crew. They then spent about three weeks in quarantine in case they carried lunar germs.
[hist-eur6-s13.11.b] The landing was as much a public event as a technical one. What was said and left on the Moon, and who watched, shaped how it was understood.
[hist-eur6-s13.11.0] Armstrong's first words on the Moon are remembered as 'one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind'. He said he intended to say 'a man', but the 'a' is not clearly audible, so it is safest not to claim he definitely said or omitted it.
[hist-eur6-s13.11.1] The crew left a plaque reading 'We came in peace for all mankind' and planted an American flag. They did not claim the Moon for the United States, because the Outer Space Treaty of 1967 forbids national claims to the Moon.
[hist-eur6-s13.11.2] The landing was broadcast live on television around the world. Estimates of the audience run from about 500 to 600 million, so it is safest to say hundreds of millions, which made it one of the most watched events in history.
[hist-eur6-s13.11.3] President Nixon spoke to the astronauts by telephone from the White House. This allowed the President to share in the national triumph.
[hist-eur6-s13.11.4] The Soviet Union's Luna 15, a robot probe, crashed on the Moon on 21 July 1969, while Apollo 11 was on the surface, as it tried to return samples. Soviet media played down the Apollo achievement.
[hist-eur6-s13.12.b] The landing answered Sputnik and Gagarin, but not everyone at home thought it was worth the price. Its meaning differed between the United States, the world and the critics.
[hist-eur6-s13.12.0] At home the landing brought national pride. It fulfilled Kennedy's pledge of 1961 and was a tribute to American technology and organisation.
[hist-eur6-s13.12.1] Abroad it was a Cold War victory for the United States over the Soviet Union. It showed technological leadership, and it became an image of American technology for the rest of the world.
[hist-eur6-s13.12.2] The Soviet reaction was to play down the achievement. The Soviet N1 rocket failed and the programme was cancelled.
[hist-eur6-s13.12.3] The triumph contrasted with the troubled society of 1969, when the nation was divided by Vietnam, urban unrest and protest. Some commentators saw the cost as a diversion from poverty.
[hist-eur6-s13.12.4] Gil Scott-Heron's poem 'Whitey on the Moon' (1970) is a well-known protest at the spending on the Moon while poverty continued.
[hist-eur6-s13.13.b] Once the race was won, the reason for the spending weakened. Public interest and political support fell, and the programme was cut back.
[hist-eur6-s13.13.0] Apollo 12 (November 1969), 14, 15, 16 and 17 landed on the Moon, making five further landings after Apollo 11. The last, Apollo 17 in December 1972, was the last Apollo Moon landing.
[hist-eur6-s13.13.1] Apollo 13 (April 1970) was crippled by an oxygen tank explosion and the crew returned safely without landing. It was praised as a triumph of engineering and teamwork, but it also made the public aware of the risks.
[hist-eur6-s13.13.2] Apollo flights 18 to 20 were cancelled. Public interest and political support declined after 1969, and NASA's budget fell in the early 1970s, so the cuts were a result of the race having been won.
[hist-eur6-s13.13.3] Skylab, the first American space station, was launched in May 1973. The Apollo-Soyuz mission of July 1975 was the first joint US-Soviet crewed flight, a sign of easing rivalry.
[hist-eur6-s13.14.b] After Apollo, NASA turned to a reusable spacecraft. The Shuttle was meant to make space cheap and routine, but its second decade ended in disaster.
[hist-eur6-s13.14.0] The Space Shuttle Columbia made the first Shuttle flight on 12 April 1981. It was intended to make access to space routine and cheaper, because the craft could be used again.
[hist-eur6-s13.14.1] Challenger broke up 73 seconds after launch on 28 January 1986, killing all seven crew members. Among them was the schoolteacher Christa McAuliffe.
[hist-eur6-s13.14.2] The cause was the failure of an O-ring seal in a solid rocket booster in cold weather. The seal let hot gas escape.
[hist-eur6-s13.14.3] The Rogers Commission of 1986 also criticised NASA's decision-making, which shows the disaster was a management failure as well as an engineering one.
[hist-eur6-s13.15.b] Computing grew from room-sized wartime machines into small, cheap devices. Each step made computers smaller and faster, and the steps are best learned as a dated chain.
[hist-eur6-s13.15.0] ENIAC was completed in 1945 at the University of Pennsylvania and unveiled in February 1946. It was one of the first general-purpose electronic computers, built to calculate artillery firing tables for the US Army.
[hist-eur6-s13.15.1] UNIVAC was delivered to the US Census Bureau in 1951 as the first commercial computer in the United States. CBS used one on election night 1952 to predict the presidential election.
[hist-eur6-s13.15.2] The transistor, invented at Bell Laboratories in 1947, replaced the vacuum tube (a bulky glass switch). It made computers smaller, faster and more reliable.
[hist-eur6-s13.15.3] IBM's System/360, launched in 1964, was a family of compatible computers (programs could run on any model). It set a standard for business computers and made IBM the dominant computer firm of the period.
[hist-eur6-s13.15.4] The integrated circuit placed many transistors on a single chip. Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments (1958) and Robert Noyce at Fairchild (1959) developed it independently.
[hist-eur6-s13.15.5] Gordon Moore observed in 1965 that the number of components on a chip doubled roughly every year, later put at about every two years. It became a guide to the industry's growth.
[hist-eur6-s13.15.6] Intel's 4004, released in 1971, was the first commercial microprocessor, putting a complete central processing unit (the part that does the computing) on a single chip.
[hist-eur6-s13.16.b] Computers moved from the Pentagon and universities into homes and offices. Defence money and the space programme were the early customers that made this possible.
[hist-eur6-s13.16.0] ARPANET, funded by ARPA of the Defense Department, was a network of computers. Its first message, sent on 29 October 1969, travelled from the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) to the Stanford Research Institute.
[hist-eur6-s13.16.1] Apple's Apple II (1977) and the IBM Personal Computer (1981) brought computing into homes and offices. The IBM PC used an operating system supplied by Microsoft, which made Microsoft the dominant software firm.
[hist-eur6-s13.16.2] Apple's Macintosh of 1984 popularised the graphical interface, which let users click on pictures instead of typing commands.
[hist-eur6-s13.16.3] Silicon Valley (the Santa Clara Valley in California, south of San Francisco) grew around Stanford University and firms such as Hewlett-Packard, Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel, helped by defence contracts and university research.
[hist-eur6-s13.16.4] Early chips were bought by the US military and by NASA for missiles and the Apollo computer.
[hist-eur6-s13.16.5] The three fields fed one another: defence money and the space race created demand for small, light electronics, and computing in turn helped missiles and spacecraft.
[hist-eur6-s13.17.b] Space and military research produced satellites that transformed communications and weather forecasting. Their civilian uses became a justification for the programmes.
[hist-eur6-s13.17.0] TIROS 1, launched on 1 April 1960, was the first successful weather satellite. It sent back television images of cloud cover.
[hist-eur6-s13.17.1] Telstar, launched on 10 July 1962, was the first active communications satellite. The first public live transatlantic television broadcasts followed on 23 July 1962.
[hist-eur6-s13.17.2] The Communications Satellite Act of 1962 created the Communications Satellite Corporation (COMSAT), a private-public body that set up the Intelsat global satellite system.
[hist-eur6-s13.17.3] Space technology also contributed to the miniaturisation of electronics, computer memory and materials. Historians debate how large these spin-offs were, because some of the advances would have happened anyway.
[hist-eur6-s13.18.b] Examiners reward judgements that weigh the achievements of the programme against its limits, and that compare how contemporaries and later historians saw it.
[hist-eur6-s13.18.0] Achievement: the landing was a triumph of American engineering and organisation, achieved within the decade Kennedy had set. It was also a Cold War victory over the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur6-s13.18.1] Limit: the cost was enormous, about 25 billion dollars. Critics argued the programme was driven by rivalry with the Soviet Union rather than by scientific need.
[hist-eur6-s13.18.2] Achievement: it left lasting technologies, such as satellites and computing, that inspired advances in materials and medicine.
[hist-eur6-s13.18.3] Limit: interest and budgets fell once the race was won, and the Challenger disaster exposed management failures in NASA.
[hist-eur6-s13.18.4] Contemporaries celebrated the landing as a national and global achievement. Critics on the left and in Congress saw the cost as a diversion from poverty and urban problems.
[hist-eur6-s13.18.5] Later historians stress the Cold War motive and regard Apollo as a short burst of spending, shown by the lack of follow-up missions. They also stress its lasting technological legacy in computing and satellites.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s2: 2. US foreign policy, 1945–1972: Berlin, Korea, Cuba and Vietnam
[hist-eur6-s2.0.b] Before 1941 the United States kept out of most quarrels in Europe and Asia. The Second World War ended that, and by 1945 it faced a rival superpower, the Soviet Union, whose system was the opposite of its own.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.0] Pre-war US isolationism (staying out of other countries' conflicts) was ended by Pearl Harbor and the war, so after 1945 the United States followed internationalism: active involvement in world affairs instead of withdrawal.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.1] The war left the United States the richest industrial power and, until the Soviet test in 1949, the only atomic power, which gave it the means to lead a worldwide policy.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.2] The wartime alliance with the Soviet Union broke down because Soviet forces held Eastern Europe at the war's end; the strain showed at the Yalta conference (February 1945) and the Potsdam conference (July to August 1945).
[hist-eur6-s2.0.3] Churchill's "iron curtain" speech at Fulton, Missouri, in March 1946 gave the division of Europe between East and West its best-known image.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.4] The Cold War rested on a clash of systems: communism, with the state controlling the economy under one party, against capitalism, with private ownership and markets.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.5] In China a civil war was being fought between Mao Zedong's Communists and Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists, so Asia was drawn into the same contest as Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.6] Imperialism means domination of one country by another through political, military or economic control. In this topic it is applied to US influence in Cuba and to Soviet control of Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.7] Germany, and Berlin within it, was divided into four occupation zones or sectors (US, British, French and Soviet). Berlin lay deep inside the Soviet zone, so the three Western sectors, which became West Berlin, were cut off from the West by Soviet-held territory.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.b] Containment means checking Soviet and communist expansion by firm resistance. It grew out of a new weapon and one diplomat's analysis of how the Soviet Union behaved.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.0] The atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, on Truman's authority, which left the United States as the only country with atomic weapons.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.1] The Soviet Union tested its own bomb on 29 August 1949, which ended the American monopoly, so the United States could no longer rely on the bomb alone to hold Soviet power back.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.2] George Kennan, a US diplomat in Moscow, sent his Long Telegram in February 1946, arguing that Soviet expansion could be checked by firm, long-term resistance rather than by negotiation.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.3] Kennan's article "The Sources of Soviet Conduct", published in Foreign Affairs in July 1947 under the signature X, made the same case in public and gave containment its name.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.4] Keep four terms apart: containment is the idea (Kennan), the Truman Doctrine is political and military aid to Greece and Turkey, the Marshall Plan is economic aid to Western Europe, and NATO is the alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.b] Between 1947 and 1950 a run of crises turned Kennan's idea into American policy and open commitments.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.0] In February 1947 Britain told the United States it could no longer support Greece and Turkey, so Washington decided to take over that burden.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.1] In the Truman Doctrine speech to Congress on 12 March 1947 Truman asked for about 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey and committed the United States to support free peoples resisting armed minorities or outside pressure.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.2] The Communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 convinced many in Washington and Western Europe that the Soviet threat was immediate, so it made them readier to act.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.3] Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949. Republicans asked "Who lost China?" and used the question to attack Truman's Asia policy, which made it harder for him to look weak abroad.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.4] NSC-68, a paper by the National Security Council (the President's foreign-policy advisory body), called in April 1950 for a large rise in defence spending to meet the Soviet threat. It was approved as policy in autumn 1950, after the Korean War began.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.b] Containment in Western Europe had an economic side. American money would rebuild damaged economies so that poverty and unrest did not push voters towards communism.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.0] George Marshall, the Secretary of State, proposed a European recovery programme in a speech at Harvard University on 5 June 1947.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.1] The European Recovery Program, passed by Congress in April 1948, provided about 13 billion dollars of aid to Western European countries between 1948 and 1952.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.2] The plan had three aims: economic recovery in Western Europe, stopping the spread of communism, and rebuilding European markets for American goods.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.3] Most aid was given as grants of food, fuel and machinery rather than as loans, so recipients received supplies they did not have to repay as ordinary debt.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.4] The Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC) was set up in April 1948 to allocate the aid, and Britain, France and West Germany were the largest recipients.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.5] The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and pressed its satellite states (the Eastern European countries it controlled) to refuse too. Czechoslovakia first accepted, then withdrew in July 1947 under Soviet pressure, so Europe settled more firmly into two blocs.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.b] Aid was matched by a military guarantee. In 1949 the defence of Western Europe and the future of Germany were both settled in ways that made the division of Europe harder to undo.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.0] The North Atlantic Treaty was signed on 4 April 1949 by twelve countries, including the United States, Britain, France, Canada and Italy.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.1] NATO, founded in April 1949 on the Article 5 principle of collective defence, was the first peacetime alliance of its kind that the United States had joined, a clear break from pre-war isolationism and a sign that internationalism now meant permanent commitments.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.2] Article 5 of the treaty stated that an armed attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, which is the basis of collective defence: any attacker would face the whole alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.3] Germany was divided as well: the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) was founded in May 1949 in the Western zones, and the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) followed in October 1949 in the Soviet zone.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.b] Berlin lay inside the Soviet zone, so the Soviet Union could squeeze the Western sectors without invading. The crisis of 1948 to 1949 tested whether the West would stay.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.0] The Soviet Union began blocking road, rail and canal access to West Berlin on 24 June 1948, aiming to force the Western powers out of the city.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.1] The Western powers responded with an airlift that flew food, coal and other supplies into West Berlin. More than two million tons were carried before the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.2] Truman chose the airlift rather than an armed convoy.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.3] The result was a Western success: the Soviet Union lifted the blockade without achieving its aim, and the West held Berlin without firing a shot.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.b] Berlin remained a flashpoint after the airlift. From 1958 the Soviet leader pressed the West to leave, and in 1961 the border between East and West Berlin was sealed.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.0] In November 1958 Khrushchev demanded that the Western powers withdraw from Berlin within six months, and the issue was debated at the Vienna summit with Kennedy in June 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.1] More than two million East Germans had fled to the West between 1949 and 1961, mostly by crossing from East to West Berlin, which drained the East's skilled workforce.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.2] The Berlin Wall was built from 13 August 1961, closing the border between East and West Berlin and stopping the flow of refugees.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.3] In October 1961 American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, the crossing point for foreigners, in a tense standoff that ended without shots.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.4] The Wall was a propaganda defeat for the East, because it had to wall in its own people, but the West accepted it rather than tearing it down, so the division of the city became permanent.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.5] Kennedy visited West Berlin on 26 June 1963 and declared in German "Ich bin ein Berliner" (I am a Berliner), a statement of solidarity that was widely praised in the West.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.b] Korea was split by the superpowers in 1945. In 1950 the split turned into war, and the United States fought under the flag of the United Nations.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.0] Korea was divided at the 38th parallel in 1945. The Republic of Korea in the South under Syngman Rhee and the Democratic People's Republic in the North under Kim Il Sung were both founded in 1948.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.1] US troops left South Korea in 1949, and Secretary of State Dean Acheson's speech of January 1950 placed Korea outside the American defence perimeter in the Pacific. It may have encouraged the North, though historians disagree how far.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.2] North Korean forces invaded the South on 25 June 1950. The UN Security Council condemned the attack and recommended that members help South Korea repel it.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.3] The Council could act because the Soviet delegate was absent (the Soviet Union was boycotting it), so Security Council resolutions could be passed.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.4] Truman sent American forces without asking Congress for a declaration of war and described the operation as a police action, so the war was never formally declared.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.5] Korea applied containment to Asia: Washington saw the invasion as communist expansion that had to be stopped, as the domino theory (if one country falls to communism, its neighbours follow) later argued in Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.b] The war swung up and down the peninsula before settling into a stalemate close to where it began.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.0] General Douglas MacArthur led the UN forces. His landing at Inchon on 15 September 1950 cut North Korean supply lines and led to the recapture of Seoul in late September 1950.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.1] UN forces crossed the 38th parallel in October 1950. Chinese forces entered the war in large numbers from late October and November 1950 and drove them back.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.2] Truman dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 after the general publicly argued for attacking China, because Truman wanted the war kept limited. The decision caused a major domestic row.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.3] An armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953 and the frontier stayed close to the 38th parallel. No peace treaty was ever signed, so the two Koreas remained technically at war.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.4] About 36,000 American servicemen died in the war in all. This figure counts non-battle deaths too, so battle deaths were lower.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.5] The results were a stalemate and a limited war. US troops remained in South Korea after the armistice, and the build-up in defence spending urged by NSC-68 went ahead.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.b] Cuba lay about ninety miles from Florida, and the United States had long treated it as within its sphere of influence. Castro's revolution ended that, and the quarrel pushed Cuba towards the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.0] Under the Platt Amendment of 1901 the United States had the right to intervene in Cuba. It was repealed in 1934 but left a legacy of US economic dominance, an example of the imperialism Cubans resented.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.1] Fidel Castro's revolution took power on 1 January 1959 after Batista fled, and the new government nationalised (took into state ownership) US-owned sugar, oil and other property in 1960.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.2] Eisenhower retaliated by cutting the Cuban sugar quota in July 1960, imposing a partial trade embargo in October 1960 and breaking off diplomatic relations in January 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.3] Cut off from its main market, Cuba turned to the Soviet Union for trade and oil, which brought Cold War rivalry to the Caribbean.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.4] CIA-trained Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs on 17 April 1961 and were defeated within three days, a humiliation for Kennedy.
[hist-eur6-s2.9.5] Operation Mongoose, a covert campaign against Castro's government, was authorised in November 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.b] In 1962 the Cold War came closest to nuclear war. It runs from the shipment of the missiles to Kennedy's decision on how to respond.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.0] Soviet medium-range missiles were shipped to Cuba from September 1962. Photographs taken by a US U-2 spy plane on 14 October 1962 revealed the launch sites, and Kennedy was shown them on 16 October.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.1] Khrushchev's motives were to defend Cuba from another invasion, to answer US Jupiter missiles in Turkey and Italy, and to offset Soviet weakness in long-range missiles.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.2] Kennedy and his advisers, the Executive Committee (ExComm), rejected an air strike or invasion and chose a naval measure instead.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.3] On 22 October 1962 Kennedy announced on television a naval quarantine of Cuba, meaning US ships would stop and search vessels heading there.
[hist-eur6-s2.10.4] Kennedy said "quarantine" and not "blockade" because a blockade is an act of war in law, so the word kept the move short of war.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.b] Within a week the crisis reached its most dangerous days and was settled, but only part of the settlement was made public.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.0] On 24 October Soviet ships turned back from the quarantine line, so a clash at sea was avoided.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.1] On 27 October ("Black Saturday") a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.2] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed on Moscow radio to remove the missiles in return for a public US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.3] Kennedy also secretly agreed to withdraw US Jupiter missiles from Turkey. The withdrawal came in 1963, and the deal was kept private for years.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.4] A hotline between Washington and Moscow was installed in 1963, so leaders could talk directly in a future crisis instead of by slow diplomatic messages.
[hist-eur6-s2.11.5] Both sides could claim success. Kennedy got the missiles out and a public victory, while Khrushchev gained a public US pledge not to invade Cuba and the secret removal of the Jupiters.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.b] US involvement in Vietnam grew out of the containment of communism in Asia. Johnson's escalation of 1964 to 1968 is a separate case study. There is more on this in the case study on Lyndon Johnson and Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.0] Vietnam was part of French Indochina, and the Viet Minh under Ho Chi Minh fought the French, who were defeated at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.1] From 1950 Truman gave aid to the French war, and by 1954 the United States was paying about three quarters of its cost, which shows how early the commitment began.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.2] In April 1954 Eisenhower described the domino theory: if Vietnam fell to communism, the rest of Southeast Asia would follow, like a row of falling dominoes.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.3] The Geneva Accords of July 1954 divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with elections planned for 1956 that were never held.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.4] The United States backed Ngo Dinh Diem in South Vietnam and joined the Southeast Asia Treaty Organisation (SEATO, an anti-communist alliance for the region) in 1954.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.5] Under Eisenhower the United States trained the South Vietnamese army with a military advisory group, and the first American advisers killed in the conflict died in 1959.
[hist-eur6-s2.12.6] The National Liberation Front, known to the Americans as the Viet Cong, was formed in December 1960 to fight the government in the South.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.b] Kennedy deepened the commitment that Eisenhower had begun, and the weakness of the South Vietnamese government became plain.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.0] Kennedy increased US military advisers in South Vietnam to about 16,000 by late 1963, whereas Eisenhower had sent advisers but not combat troops.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.1] Kennedy backed the Strategic Hamlet programme (1962), which moved peasants into fortified villages. It was widely resented.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.2] In 1963 Diem's persecution of Buddhists provoked protests, including the self-immolation (burning himself to death) of the monk Thich Quang Duc in Saigon in June 1963.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.3] Diem was overthrown and killed in a coup on 2 November 1963, which US officials had tacitly encouraged. After it the South suffered a series of unstable governments.
[hist-eur6-s2.13.4] Diem's fall came three weeks before Kennedy's assassination, so it is easy to mix the two events up.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.b] Nixon wanted to end US ground fighting without abandoning South Vietnam. His method, Vietnamisation, withdrew American troops but widened the war into neighbouring countries.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.0] The Nixon Doctrine, announced at Guam in July 1969, stated that Asian nations should take the main responsibility for their own defence, so the United States would reduce its direct role.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.1] Vietnamisation handed the ground war to the South Vietnamese army while US troops were withdrawn.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.2] US troops numbered about 540,000 at the 1969 peak and had fallen to under 30,000 by late 1972.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.3] Secret bombing of Cambodia, which communist forces used as a base, began in March 1969.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.4] In April 1970 Nixon sent US and South Vietnamese troops into Cambodia to attack communist bases, which sparked protests at home, including Kent State.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.5] In 1971 South Vietnamese forces invaded Laos with US air support and were driven back, which showed the limits of Vietnamisation.
[hist-eur6-s2.14.6] In 1972 Nixon answered North Vietnam's Easter Offensive by mining Haiphong harbour and launching the Linebacker bombing raids, then ordered the Christmas bombing of Hanoi in December 1972.

[hist-eur6-s2.15.b] Presidents also had to decide how to use nuclear weapons to support containment, and Eisenhower and Kennedy chose very different strategies.
[hist-eur6-s2.15.0] Eisenhower's Secretary of State John Foster Dulles relied on massive retaliation (1954): the threat of nuclear attack to deter communist aggression, which was cheaper than large conventional forces.
[hist-eur6-s2.15.1] Eisenhower is said to have signalled through intermediaries in 1953 that he might use nuclear weapons if the Korean talks failed, though historians dispute how far this forced the armistice.
[hist-eur6-s2.15.2] Kennedy replaced massive retaliation with flexible response, building up conventional and special forces (such as the Green Berets) so the United States could fight limited wars instead of choosing between doing nothing and nuclear war.
[hist-eur6-s2.15.3] After the missile crisis Kennedy and Khrushchev signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty (August 1963), banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.b] Harry Truman became President on Roosevelt's death in April 1945 and faced the start of the Cold War. His domestic record is a separate topic.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.0] At the Potsdam conference of July and August 1945 Truman met Stalin for the first time, and he authorised the atomic bombings shortly afterwards.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.1] His main achievements were the Truman Doctrine speech to Congress (12 March 1947), the Marshall Plan, NATO and the Berlin airlift, which together contained Soviet power in Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.2] He recognised the new state of Israel on 14 May 1948, within minutes of its declaration of independence, against the advice of parts of his State Department.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.3] He decided in June 1950 to send American forces to defend South Korea without a congressional vote, a decision that set the pattern for later presidential war-making, and he dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 to keep the war limited.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.4] He took the decisions himself: he chose the airlift rather than an armed convoy to Berlin and accepted a limited war in Korea rather than MacArthur's wider war with China. A sign on his desk read "The buck stops here".
[hist-eur6-s2.16.5] Contemporaries were divided: Republicans attacked him over China and spy cases, and by 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.6] Revisionist historians from the 1960s, such as William Appleman Williams, argued that US economic expansion and the atomic bomb helped cause the Cold War. Post-revisionists such as John Lewis Gaddis stressed that Stalin bore great responsibility and that containment worked in Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.16.7] His reputation has risen since he left office.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.b] Each president from Truman to Nixon had successes and failures, and judging them means weighing the two against each other.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.0] Truman was strongest in Europe, where the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO and the airlift contained Soviet power, but weaker in Asia, where the Communists took power in China, prompting the "Who lost China?" attack by Republicans, and Korea ended in stalemate.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.1] Eisenhower relied on massive retaliation, which was cheaper than large conventional forces, but he also began the commitment in Vietnam with aid to France, support for Diem and US advisers.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.2] Kennedy suffered the Bay of Pigs failure and then handled the missile crisis with ExComm and the quarantine, yet he raised the number of advisers in Vietnam to about 16,000.
[hist-eur6-s2.17.3] Nixon cut US troops in Vietnam from about 540,000 at the 1969 peak to under 30,000 by late 1972, but he also widened the war into Cambodia and Laos.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.b] Judging US policy means setting its successes against its failures, episode by episode, and then deciding how much containment achieved overall.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.0] Success in Western Europe: the Marshall Plan, NATO and the Berlin airlift contained Soviet power in Europe and helped to create a prosperous Western alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.1] Failure in Asia: the Korean War ended in a stalemate after about three years, with heavy American losses and no victory.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.2] Korea can also be judged a limited success: South Korea survived, and Truman kept the war from spreading to China or becoming nuclear.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.3] In Cuba the Bay of Pigs was a clear failure, but the missile crisis avoided nuclear war and removed the missiles, although the Jupiters in Turkey were withdrawn as a secret concession alongside the public no-invasion pledge.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.4] Vietnam showed the limits of containment: a commitment that began with aid and advisers had escalated to about 500,000 troops by 1968.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.5] Supporters argue that US commitments in Asia deterred further Soviet and Chinese expansion, while critics say they drew the United States into costly wars in places of little strategic importance.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.6] Contemporaries and later historians differ over whether the Cold War could have been managed by diplomacy earlier, and the debate shapes judgements of Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy and Nixon.
[hist-eur6-s2.18.7] Korea and Vietnam both came from containment and the domino theory, but the methods differed: a UN force with a declared limit in Korea, and a gradual commitment in Vietnam.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s5: 5. The decline of Cold War certainties, 1973–1989: withdrawal from Vietnam, détente, SALT and Star Wars
[hist-eur6-s5.0.b] By 1973 American foreign policy rested on ideas set in the late 1940s, and Vietnam had begun to shake them. Three ideas help the later blocks make sense: containment, internationalism and technological development.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.0] Containment was the policy, in place since 1947, of stopping the spread of communism by resisting Soviet expansion. The arms race and mutual deterrence (each side stayed safe because the other feared nuclear retaliation) grew out of it.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.1] Internationalism means a continuing US commitment to active engagement in world affairs. After Vietnam Americans debated how far that commitment should go, which is why this period matters for the whole story of US foreign policy.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.2] Technological development means weapons and space systems that shaped the arms race, such as MIRVs (missiles carrying several warheads, each aimed at a different target), missile defence and verification satellites, which let each side check the other's arsenal.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.3] Vietnam is the background to the period. Johnson's escalation, the Tet Offensive of 1968, the anti-war movement and Nixon's Vietnamisation (handing the fighting to South Vietnamese forces) had already weakened support for the war.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.4] Nixon resigned over Watergate on 9 August 1974, so three presidents followed in this period: Gerald Ford, Jimmy Carter (1977 to 1981) and Ronald Reagan (1981 to 1989).
[hist-eur6-s5.1.b] The United States left Vietnam in 1973 through a negotiated settlement, and Congress then closed off the option of fighting again. This weakened the consensus behind containment.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.0] The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973 by the United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Provisional Revolutionary Government (the Viet Cong). They provided for a ceasefire, US withdrawal and the release of prisoners of war.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.1] Kissinger and Le Duc Tho negotiated the Accords and were awarded the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize, though Le Duc Tho declined it. North Vietnamese troops were allowed to remain in the South, which left South Vietnam exposed once the Americans had gone.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.2] The last US combat troops left by 29 March 1973, and American prisoners of war were released in the same weeks.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.3] Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto on 7 November 1973 (House 284 to 135, Senate 75 to 18). It requires the President to consult and report to Congress and to withdraw forces within 60 days, with a 30-day extension for withdrawal, unless Congress authorises them.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.4] The War Powers Resolution was meant to stop another undeclared war like Vietnam, because it put limits on a President sending troops abroad without Congress. Do not confuse it with the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of 1964, which had given Johnson wide powers.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.5] The Case-Church amendment barred funds for US combat in Indochina from 15 August 1973. So when the North later broke the Accords, the United States could not intervene, and South Vietnam relied on its own forces and on aid from Congress.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.b] Two years after American troops left, South Vietnam collapsed. The defeat left Americans cautious about overseas commitments and changed the debate about internationalism.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.0] The North Vietnamese offensive of 1975 overran South Vietnam. In April 1975 Congress refused President Ford's request for emergency aid for South Vietnam, which showed how far the American consensus behind the war had collapsed.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.1] Ford, not Nixon, was president when Saigon fell. Speaking at Tulane University, New Orleans, on 23 April 1975, he said that the war was finished as far as America was concerned.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.2] Saigon fell on 30 April 1975. Operation Frequent Wind evacuated Americans and many South Vietnamese by helicopter on 29 and 30 April, so images of the evacuation became the symbol of the defeat.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.3] Phnom Penh, the capital of Cambodia, fell to the Khmer Rouge on 17 April 1975, and the communist Pathet Lao took power in Laos in December 1975. All three Indochinese states therefore had communist governments by the end of 1975.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.4] About 58,000 Americans died in the war. The defeat produced the Vietnam syndrome, a reluctance to commit US forces abroad, which made later presidents cautious about overseas military commitments.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.b] Détente means the easing of tensions through negotiation, based on a balance of power rather than on trust. Nixon and Kissinger used it to manage rivalry with both communist giants while the US was weakened by Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.0] Henry Kissinger was national security adviser from 1969 and Secretary of State as well from 1973. He aimed for a stable balance of power in which no side could easily gain an advantage.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.1] His policy of linkage tied arms control and trade to Soviet behaviour in other regions. In other words, the US would offer the Soviet Union agreements it wanted only if Moscow behaved well elsewhere.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.2] Nixon visited China from 21 to 28 February 1972 and met Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai. The Shanghai Communiqué set out a process to normalise relations, which also put pressure on the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.3] Formal US recognition of the People's Republic of China came on 1 January 1979 under Carter, building on Nixon's opening.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.4] Nixon was in Moscow from 22 to 30 May 1972, meeting Leonid Brezhnev, the Soviet leader. The first major arms control agreements, SALT I, were signed there on 26 May 1972, and trade agreements were discussed.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.b] The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT) aimed to limit the superpowers' nuclear arsenals. SALT I was the first agreement and it slowed the arms race without ending it.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.0] SALT I was signed in Moscow on 26 May 1972 by Nixon and Brezhnev, with Kissinger as the key American negotiator. It had two parts: the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty and an Interim Agreement.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.1] The ABM Treaty limited missile defence systems. Because each side stayed open to attack, neither could hope to launch a first strike and be safe from retaliation, so deterrence was preserved. The Senate approved it by 88 votes to 2 in August 1972.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.2] The Interim Agreement froze the numbers of land-based ICBMs (intercontinental ballistic missiles) and submarine-launched missile launchers for five years.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.3] It did not limit the number of warheads each missile carried, so MIRVs (multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles) let each side add warheads without breaking the limits.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.4] Both sides therefore kept building up their arsenals, so SALT I limited growth without reducing the number of warheads.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.b] Détente brought real agreements, but it also drew attacks from critics and was strained by events in the Middle East and Africa.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.0] The Helsinki Final Act was signed on 1 August 1975 by 35 states, including the United States and the Soviet Union. It recognised European borders as they stood and set out human rights principles, which the Soviet Union later failed to honour.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.1] Ford and Brezhnev agreed a framework for a second SALT agreement at Vladivostok in November 1974, which kept arms control going after Nixon's fall.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.2] In October 1973 the Yom Kippur War in the Middle East led Nixon to put US forces on alert (DEFCON 3) after Soviet threats of intervention, showing that détente did not remove the risk of confrontation.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.3] The Jackson-Vanik Amendment of 1974 linked US-Soviet trade to freedom of emigration, notably for Soviet Jews. It angered Moscow.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.4] Critics of détente, including conservative Democrats such as Senator Henry Jackson and others on the right, argued that the Soviet Union was gaining military strength while the United States made concessions.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.5] Soviet and Cuban backing for the MPLA in Angola in 1975 and 1976 convinced critics that the Soviet Union was exploiting détente to gain influence in the developing world while talking of peace.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.b] Carter came to office stressing moral principles in foreign policy, but events in Iran and Afghanistan pushed him into a harder line.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.0] Carter stressed human rights from 1977. He criticised Soviet treatment of dissidents (people who openly oppose their government) such as the physicist Andrei Sakharov, which annoyed Moscow.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.1] He gave full diplomatic recognition to the People's Republic of China on 1 January 1979, building on Nixon's opening.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.2] The Shah of Iran, a US ally who had been deposed, was admitted to the United States in October 1979. In response the US embassy in Tehran was seized on 4 November 1979.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.3] The hostages were held for 444 days and were released on 20 January 1981, the day Reagan took office.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.4] Operation Eagle Claw, a rescue attempt in April 1980, failed: helicopters broke down, and at a desert staging site a helicopter collided with a transport aircraft, killing eight US servicemen. The failure contributed to Carter's defeat in the 1980 election.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.b] SALT II was meant to build on SALT I, but within months of its signing the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan ended détente.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.0] SALT II was signed in Vienna on 18 June 1979 by Carter and Brezhnev. It set an equal ceiling of 2,400 strategic delivery vehicles for each side, to fall to 2,250, and limited multiple-warhead missiles.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.1] Critics, led by Senator Henry Jackson and the Committee on the Present Danger, said SALT II let the Soviet Union keep a lead in heavy land-based missiles, so ratification was in doubt.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.2] Soviet forces entered Afghanistan on 24 and 25 December 1979. Carter condemned the invasion as an unacceptable threat to regional stability, and it ended the era of détente.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.3] SALT II was never ratified. In January 1980 Carter asked the Senate to postpone consideration, after the invasion of Afghanistan.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.4] Carter announced a grain embargo against the Soviet Union in January 1980, which cut off supplies the Soviet Union wanted from the US.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.5] In his State of the Union address in January 1980 he set out the Carter Doctrine: the United States would resist any outside attempt to control the Persian Gulf region.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.6] Carter led a boycott of the Moscow Olympics: the US Olympic Committee voted in April 1980 not to send a team, and dozens of other countries also stayed away. Some national teams, including Britain's, attended despite government pressure.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.7] The Soviet Union and the United States both said they would observe SALT II's limits informally after 1980, but Reagan's government ceased to observe the limits in 1986.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.b] Reagan rejected the logic of détente and deterrence, arguing that the United States should deal with Moscow from strength. This opened what is often called a new Cold War.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.0] Reagan described the Soviet Union as an evil empire in his "evil empire" speech, given to the National Association of Evangelicals on 8 March 1983, a phrase that showed how far he had moved from Nixon's approach.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.1] Defence spending rose sharply from 1981 to 1985. Programmes included the B-1 bomber, the MX missile and Pershing II missiles in Western Europe.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.2] American Pershing II and cruise missiles began arriving in Western Europe from late 1983, which led to protests, including at Greenham Common in Britain.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.b] Reagan wanted a defence against nuclear missiles instead of relying on the threat of retaliation. His proposal was a big step in technological development, and it also changed the arms talks that followed.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.0] Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) in a television address on 23 March 1983, proposing space-based and other systems to destroy incoming missiles.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.1] Critics gave it the nickname Star Wars, after the film, partly because many scientists doubted it could work and partly because of its cost.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.2] It was never deployed, but it alarmed Soviet leaders, who feared an American technological lead they could not match.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.3] SDI also differed from the ABM Treaty of 1972, which had limited defences, and critics said SDI undermined that treaty.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.b] A new generation of Soviet leadership made talks possible, and Reagan and Gorbachev then agreed the first treaty to abolish a whole class of nuclear weapons.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.0] Brezhnev died in November 1982, and the leadership changed rapidly: Yuri Andropov (to February 1984), Konstantin Chernenko (to March 1985), then Mikhail Gorbachev, who became General Secretary in March 1985.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.1] Gorbachev's policies of glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring of the economy) loosened Soviet control over Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.2] Reagan and Gorbachev met at Geneva in November 1985 and at Reykjavik in October 1986. At Reykjavik they came close to agreeing deep cuts in nuclear weapons.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.3] The Reykjavik talks broke down over SDI: Gorbachev wanted it confined to the laboratory and Reagan refused.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.4] The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was signed in Washington on 8 December 1987. It eliminated all US and Soviet land-based missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres, with on-site inspection.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.5] It was the first treaty to abolish a whole class of nuclear weapons, unlike SALT, which only limited growth. The Senate ratified it in May 1988, and Reagan visited Moscow for a fourth summit in May and June 1988.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.6] Reagan spoke at the Berlin Wall (the Brandenburg Gate) on 12 June 1987 and said: "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall."
[hist-eur6-s5.11.b] In 1989 the division of Europe that defined the Cold War broke up, largely because the Soviet Union stopped using force to hold it together.
[hist-eur6-s5.11.0] Soviet troops finished leaving Afghanistan in February 1989, ending the costly war that had begun in December 1979.
[hist-eur6-s5.11.1] The Soviet Union did not intervene when communist governments in Eastern Europe fell during 1989, which was a break with earlier practice, so the changes could go ahead.
[hist-eur6-s5.11.2] The Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November 1989, and the collapse of communist governments across Eastern Europe ended the division of the continent.
[hist-eur6-s5.11.3] Reagan's successor, George H. W. Bush, met Gorbachev off Malta on 2 and 3 December 1989, and the two leaders described a new era in relations.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.b] Examiners reward judgements that weigh US policy against Soviet internal failure and the choices of Gorbachev, and that also weigh the successes and failures of détente.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.0] Achievements of détente and arms control: SALT I, Helsinki, the opening to China and later the INF Treaty. Limits: arsenals kept growing, SALT II was never ratified, and détente had collapsed by 1979.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.1] Supporters of Reagan argue that his build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative forced the Soviet Union into an arms race it could not afford, and that negotiating from strength created the conditions for Gorbachev's reforms.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.2] Critics of Reagan argue that the Soviet economy was already stagnating before 1980 and that Gorbachev's own reforms were the decisive cause of the change.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.3] Reagan's turn to negotiation owed much to Gorbachev, so critics say the talks were not simply a reward for American strength.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.4] Continuity: détente and arms control created the framework and the habit of verification used in 1987, so the INF Treaty built on the 1970s.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.5] The costs of the Afghan war and falling oil revenues in the mid-1980s added to the Soviet Union's economic difficulties.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.6] Vietnam reduced US willingness to intervene abroad, which affected internationalism. Yet US power and ideas outlasted the Soviet system.
