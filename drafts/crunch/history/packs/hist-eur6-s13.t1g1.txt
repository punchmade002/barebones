TARGET hist-eur6-s13: 13. Advances in military, space and information technology; case study: the Moon landing, 1969
[hist-eur6-s13.0.b] Nuclear weapons and the means of delivering them drove military technology after 1945, and the same research often fed the space and computing programmes.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.0] The United States tested the first thermonuclear device, Ivy Mike, at Eniwetok Atoll in the Pacific on 1 November 1952, and the Soviet Union tested a thermonuclear device in August 1953.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.1] Intercontinental ballistic missiles, first the Atlas in 1959 and then the Minuteman from 1962, gave the United States the ability to strike the Soviet Union within about thirty minutes.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.2] Submarine-launched Polaris missiles, first deployed in 1960, gave the United States a second strike capability that could not easily be destroyed in a surprise attack.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.3] The B-52 Stratofortress, a long-range jet bomber that entered service in 1955, carried nuclear weapons on strategic patrols during the Cold War.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.4] Reconnaissance satellites, first successfully used in the Corona programme from 1960, allowed the United States to monitor Soviet missile sites without flying over Soviet territory.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.5] Military research on computers, radar and jet propulsion created technologies that were later adopted in civil aviation and in commercial electronics.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.6] The decision to build the hydrogen bomb in 1950 was opposed by scientists such as J. Robert Oppenheimer and supported by Edward Teller, and Truman approved it in January 1950 after the Soviet atomic test.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.7] Warning of a missile gap in 1960, Kennedy's campaign argued that the Soviet Union was ahead in missiles; in fact the United States held a large lead, which later became clear from satellite pictures.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.8] By the late 1960s the United States had about 1,000 Minuteman missiles, 41 Polaris submarines, and from 1970 multiple warheads (MIRVs) on single missiles, which made arms control more difficult.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.9] Jet fighters and bombers, nuclear submarines (USS Nautilus, 1954) and guided missiles were funded by the Pentagon, and defence contracts supported the aerospace industry in California, Texas and Washington State.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.10] Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative of 1983 sought a space-based missile shield, and it was not built, but its research funded lasers and sensors.
[hist-eur6-s13.0.11] The satellite navigation system GPS began as a military programme with the first satellites launched in 1978.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.b] The Soviet launch of Sputnik in 1957 began a competition in space that the United States came to see as a test of its technological and political strength.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.0] The Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, on 4 October 1957, and the American satellite Explorer 1 followed on 31 January 1958.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.1] The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) was created in 1958 to direct the civil space programme, taking over work from the military and from the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.2] The Mercury programme of 1958 to 1963 sent the first American astronauts into space: Alan Shepard became the first American in space on a short suborbital flight on 5 May 1961, and John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth on 20 February 1962.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.3] Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on 12 April 1961, a Soviet achievement that increased pressure on the United States to respond.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.4] In a speech to Congress on 25 May 1961 President Kennedy committed the United States to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely before the end of the decade.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.5] The Gemini programme of 1965 and 1966 tested the techniques of rendezvous and docking needed for a Moon landing, and it prepared the way for the Apollo missions.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.6] Sputnik caused alarm in the United States about Soviet technology and education, and it led to the National Defense Education Act of 1958 and to the creation of NASA, which Eisenhower signed in July 1958.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.7] The Soviet Union scored further firsts: the first woman in space (Valentina Tereshkova, 1963) and the first spacewalk by Alexei Leonov in March 1965.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.8] Kennedy's Moon decision followed Gagarin and the Bay of Pigs, and he believed the Moon landing was a field in which the United States could win; at Rice University on 12 September 1962 he said the nation chose to go to the Moon, not because it was easy but because it was hard.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.9] NASA chose lunar orbit rendezvous in 1962 as the method for the Moon landing, with a separate lunar module descending from the orbiting command module.
[hist-eur6-s13.1.10] The Soviet Union's Moon rocket, the N1, failed in every test, and the Soviet crewed lunar programme was cancelled.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.b] The Apollo programme was the largest peacetime technological effort the United States had undertaken, and its cost and organisation shaped the Moon landing.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.0] The Apollo programme was managed by NASA with major contractors such as North American Aviation, Grumman, Boeing and IBM, and it employed hundreds of thousands of people across the country.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.1] The Saturn V rocket, the most powerful rocket built until then, was used for the Moon missions and was developed under the direction of Wernher von Braun at the Marshall Space Flight Center.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.2] The Apollo programme cost about 25 billion dollars in the money of its time, and NASA's budget peaked in 1965 and 1966 at about 4 to 5 per cent of federal spending.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.3] The Apollo spacecraft had three parts: the command module, which carried the crew back to Earth, the service module, and the lunar module, which took two astronauts to the surface.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.4] The Apollo 1 fire on 27 January 1967, during a test on the launch pad, killed the astronauts Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee and led to a redesign of the spacecraft.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.5] Apollo 8 in December 1968 was the first crewed mission to orbit the Moon, with Frank Borman, Jim Lovell and William Anders, and it returned images of the Earth rising over the lunar horizon.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.6] The Apollo 13 mission of April 1970 was crippled by an oxygen tank explosion, and the crew returned safely, an achievement that was widely praised as a triumph of engineering and teamwork.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.7] NASA administrator James Webb, appointed by Kennedy in 1961, managed the political side and the vast contracting system, and Mission Control in Houston, Texas, directed flights from the Manned Spacecraft Center, opened in 1963.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.8] The Saturn V stood about 110 metres tall, and its first stage burned fuel at a rate of about 13 tonnes a second; it flew thirteen times without a failure that lost a crew.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.9] The Apollo Guidance Computer, developed at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, was one of the first to use integrated circuits, which made the programme a major customer for the new technology.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.10] Apollo 7 (October 1968) tested the command module in Earth orbit, and Apollo 9 and Apollo 10 in 1969 tested the lunar module, the last mission flying to within about 15 kilometres of the Moon's surface.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.11] Employment in the programme was concentrated in Florida, Texas, Alabama and California, which tied the Sun Belt's growth to federal spending.
[hist-eur6-s13.2.12] Amitai Etzioni's The Moon-Doggle (1964) criticised the programme as a waste, and some in Congress argued that the money would be better spent on poverty.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.b] Apollo 11 was launched from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida, landed in the Sea of Tranquillity, and returned its crew to the Pacific.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.0] The crew were Neil Armstrong, commander, Edwin Buzz Aldrin, lunar module pilot, and Michael Collins, command module pilot, who remained in orbit around the Moon.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.1] Apollo 11 was launched on 16 July 1969 from Kennedy Space Center, Florida, and entered lunar orbit on 19 July 1969.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.2] The lunar module Eagle landed in the Sea of Tranquillity on 20 July 1969, and Armstrong stepped onto the surface several hours later.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.3] Armstrong's first words on the Moon were one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind, and Aldrin joined him on the surface for about two and a half hours of activity.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.4] The crew collected about 21 kilograms of lunar rock and soil, placed a plaque and a flag, and set up experiments that measured the Moon's seismic activity.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.5] Apollo 11 splashed down in the Pacific Ocean on 24 July 1969, and the crew were recovered by the aircraft carrier USS Hornet.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.6] The landing was broadcast live on television to an audience estimated at about 600 million people across the world, making it one of the most watched events in history.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.7] The landing was risky: during the descent computer alarms sounded, and Armstrong took manual control to avoid a boulder field, landing Eagle with very little fuel left.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.8] Armstrong's words as he stepped on to the surface at 10.56 pm Eastern time on 20 July were meant to be one small step for a man, and he said afterwards that he had intended the word a; Aldrin joined him, and the two spent about two and a half hours outside.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.9] The crew left a plaque reading We came in peace for all mankind, and planted an American flag; they did not claim the Moon for the United States, as the 1967 Outer Space Treaty banned national claims.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.10] Nixon spoke to the astronauts by telephone from the White House, and the crew spent 21 days in quarantine after their return in case of lunar germs.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.11] The Soviet Union's Luna 15 probe crashed on the Moon on 21 July 1969 while trying to return samples, and Soviet media played down the Apollo achievement.
[hist-eur6-s13.3.12] The mission came in the same year as the rise of the anti-war movement, and some commentators contrasted the technical triumph with the troubles at home.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.b] The Moon landing ended the race to the Moon, but later missions and the space shuttle showed the limits of US ambitions in space.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.0] The Apollo missions after 11 landed on the Moon in five further expeditions, the last being Apollo 17 in December 1972.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.1] Public interest and political support for the Moon missions declined after 1969, and NASA's budget fell substantially in the early 1970s.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.2] Skylab, the first American space station, was launched in May 1973, and the Apollo-Soyuz mission of July 1975 was the first joint US-Soviet space flight.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.3] The Space Shuttle made its first flight on 12 April 1981, and it was intended to make space access routine and cheaper.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.4] The Space Shuttle Challenger broke up 73 seconds after launch on 28 January 1986, killing all seven crew members, and the failure of the seals in the solid rocket boosters was identified as the cause.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.5] Apollo 12 (November 1969) landed on the Moon after Apollo 11, and Apollo 13's near-disaster in April 1970 made the public aware of the risks.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.6] Apollo 17 in December 1972 was the last crewed lunar mission, and no human has been beyond low Earth orbit since; budget cuts cancelled Apollo 18 to 20.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.7] The Moon landing was a national and global event, a Cold War victory for the United States over the Soviet Union, and it was an image of American technology for the rest of the world.
[hist-eur6-s13.4.8] The Challenger disaster killed the teacher Christa McAuliffe, and the Rogers Commission report of 1986 blamed the O-ring seals and failures in NASA's decision-making.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.b] Computing developed from wartime machines into commercial and military systems, and the microprocessor made the personal computer possible.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.0] ENIAC, completed in 1945 and publicly unveiled in February 1946 at the University of Pennsylvania, was one of the first general-purpose electronic computers, built to calculate artillery firing tables.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.1] The transistor, invented at Bell Laboratories in 1947, replaced the vacuum tube and made computers smaller, faster and more reliable.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.2] IBM's System/360 family of computers, launched in 1964, set a standard for compatible business computers and made IBM the dominant computer firm of the period.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.3] The integrated circuit was developed independently by Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments in 1958 and Robert Noyce at Fairchild in 1959, placing many transistors on a single chip.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.4] Gordon Moore's 1965 observation that the number of components on a chip doubled roughly every year, later revised to about two years, became a guide to the industry's growth.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.5] Intel's 4004 microprocessor, released in 1971, put a complete central processing unit on a single chip.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.6] The ARPANET, a Defense Department network, sent its first message between UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute on 29 October 1969.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.7] Apple's Apple II (1977) and IBM's personal computer (1981) brought computing into homes and offices, and the Santa Clara Valley became known as Silicon Valley, the centre of the industry.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.8] UNIVAC, the first commercial computer in the United States, was delivered in 1951, and it was used by CBS to predict the 1952 presidential election.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.9] Defence money paid for much early computing: the SAGE air-defence system and the Pentagon's research agency ARPA, created in 1958 after Sputnik, funded the ARPANET.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.10] Silicon Valley grew around Stanford University and firms such as Hewlett-Packard, Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel, which were helped by defence contracts and by university research.
[hist-eur6-s13.5.11] Apple's Macintosh of 1984 popularised the graphical interface, and Microsoft supplied the operating system for the IBM PC, which made it the dominant software firm.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.b] Space and military research produced satellites that transformed communications and weather forecasting, and their civilian uses became a justification for the programmes.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.0] TIROS 1, launched on 1 April 1960, was the first successful weather satellite, and it sent back television images of cloud cover.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.1] Telstar, launched on 10 July 1962, was the first active communications satellite to relay live television and telephone signals across the Atlantic.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.2] The Communications Satellite Act of 1962 created the Communications Satellite Corporation, a private-public body that set up the Intelsat global satellite system.
[hist-eur6-s13.6.3] Space technology also contributed to the miniaturisation of electronics, computer memory and materials, although the scale of these spin-offs has been debated by historians.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.b] Examiners reward judgements that weigh the scientific and prestige gains of the programme against its costs and its link to the Cold War.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.0] Supporters argue that the Moon landing was a triumph of American engineering and organisation, and that it inspired advances in computing, materials and medicine.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.1] Critics argue that the programme was driven by Cold War competition rather than scientific need, and that the cost of Apollo could have funded other priorities.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.2] Contemporaries celebrated the landing as a national and global achievement, while some on the left saw the cost as a diversion from poverty and urban problems.
[hist-eur6-s13.7.3] Later historians have stressed that the space race helped to shape the technologies of computing and satellites, but that the lack of follow-up missions suggests the programme was a short burst of Cold War spending.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s2: 2. US foreign policy, 1945–1972: Berlin, Korea, Cuba and Vietnam
[hist-eur6-s2.0.b] After 1945 the United States abandoned its pre-war isolation and committed itself to resisting Soviet expansion, a policy known as containment.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.0] The United States emerged from the Second World War as the richest industrial power and, from 1945, the only country with atomic weapons.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.1] The atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, on Truman's authority, and the Soviet Union tested its own bomb on 29 August 1949.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.2] George Kennan, a diplomat in Moscow, sent his Long Telegram in February 1946 arguing that Soviet expansion could be checked by firm, long-term resistance rather than by negotiation.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.3] Kennan's article The Sources of Soviet Conduct, published in Foreign Affairs in July 1947 under the signature X, gave containment its name.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.4] The Truman Doctrine of 12 March 1947 asked Congress for about 400 million dollars in aid to Greece and Turkey, and it committed the United States to support free peoples resisting armed minorities or outside pressure.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.5] The Communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 convinced many in Washington and Western Europe that the Soviet threat was immediate.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.6] Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949, and Republicans later used the question "Who lost China?" to attack Truman's Asia policy.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.7] In April 1950 the National Security Council paper NSC-68 called for a large rise in defence spending to meet the Soviet threat, and it was adopted after the Korean War began.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.b] Economic aid and a military alliance were the two pillars of containment in Western Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.0] George Marshall, the Secretary of State, proposed a European recovery programme in a speech at Harvard University on 5 June 1947.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.1] The European Recovery Program, passed by Congress in April 1948, provided about 13 billion dollars of aid to Western European countries between 1948 and 1952.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.2] The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and pressured its satellite states in Eastern Europe to refuse it too, so the division of Europe became more fixed.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.3] Most aid was given as grants of food, fuel and machinery rather than loans, and the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC) was set up in April 1948 to allocate it; Britain, France and West Germany were the largest recipients.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.4] The North Atlantic Treaty was signed on 4 April 1949 by twelve countries, including the United States, Britain, France, Canada and Italy.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.5] Article 5 of the NATO treaty stated that an armed attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, the basis of collective defence.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.6] Germany was divided: the Federal Republic of Germany was founded in May 1949 in the Western zones, and the German Democratic Republic followed in October 1949 in the Soviet zone.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.b] Berlin, divided into four sectors, was the most dangerous point of Cold War contact in Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.0] The Soviet Union began blocking road, rail and canal access to West Berlin on 24 June 1948, aiming to force the Western powers out of the city.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.1] The Western powers responded with an airlift that flew food, coal and other supplies into West Berlin; more than two million tons were carried before the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.2] In November 1958 Khrushchev demanded that the Western powers withdraw from Berlin within six months, and the issue was debated at the Vienna summit with Kennedy in June 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.3] The Berlin Wall was built from 13 August 1961, closing the border between East and West Berlin and stopping the flow of East German refugees.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.4] In October 1961 American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, the crossing point for foreigners, in a tense standoff that ended without shots.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.5] Well over two million East Germans had fled to the West between 1949 and 1961, most of them by crossing from East to West Berlin, which drained the East's skilled workforce.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.6] Kennedy visited West Berlin on 26 June 1963 and declared in German "Ich bin ein Berliner" (I am a Berliner), a statement of solidarity that was widely praised in the West.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.b] The Korean War was the first major armed conflict of the Cold War, fought under a United Nations flag.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.0] Korea had been divided at the 38th parallel in 1945; the Republic of Korea in the South under Syngman Rhee and the Democratic People's Republic in the North under Kim Il Sung were both founded in 1948.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.1] US troops had left South Korea in 1949, and Secretary of State Dean Acheson's speech of January 1950 left Korea outside the American defence perimeter in the Pacific, which communist leaders may have read as a green light.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.2] North Korean forces invaded the South on 25 June 1950, and the UN Security Council, with the Soviet delegate absent, condemned the attack and then recommended that members help South Korea repel it.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.3] Truman sent American forces without asking Congress for a declaration of war, and he described the operation as a police action.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.4] General Douglas MacArthur led the UN forces; his landing at Inchon on 15 September 1950 cut North Korean supply lines and led to the recapture of Seoul on 28 September.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.5] UN forces advanced north of the 38th parallel in October 1950, and Chinese forces entered the war in large numbers from late October and November 1950, driving them back.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.6] Truman dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 after the general publicly argued for attacking China, a decision that caused a major domestic row.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.7] An armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953; the frontier stayed close to the 38th parallel, and no peace treaty was ever signed.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.8] About 36,000 American servicemen died in the war, and US troops remained in South Korea after the armistice.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.b] Cuba lay ninety miles from Florida, and the United States had long treated the island as within its sphere of influence.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.0] Under the Platt Amendment of 1901 the United States had the right to intervene in Cuba, a clause repealed in 1934 but leaving a legacy of US economic dominance.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.1] Fidel Castro's revolution took power on 1 January 1959 after Batista fled, and the new government nationalised US-owned sugar, oil and other property in 1960.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.2] Eisenhower cut the Cuban sugar quota in July 1960, imposed a partial trade embargo in October 1960 and broke off diplomatic relations in January 1961; Cuba turned to the Soviet Union for trade and oil.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.3] CIA-trained Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs on 17 April 1961; they were defeated within three days, a humiliation for Kennedy.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.4] Operation Mongoose, a covert campaign against Castro's government, was authorised in November 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.5] Soviet medium-range missiles were shipped to Cuba from September 1962, and photographs taken by a US U-2 spy plane on 14 October 1962 revealed the launch sites; Kennedy was shown them on 16 October.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.6] Khrushchev's motives for the missiles included defending Cuba from another invasion, answering US Jupiter missiles in Turkey and Italy, and offsetting Soviet weakness in long-range missiles.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.7] Kennedy and his advisers (the Executive Committee, ExComm) rejected an air strike or invasion, and on 22 October he announced on television a naval quarantine (blockade) of Cuba.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.8] On 24 October Soviet ships turned back from the quarantine line; on 27 October ("Black Saturday") a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.9] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed on Moscow radio to remove the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.10] Kennedy also secretly agreed to withdraw US Jupiter missiles from Turkey within months, a deal kept private for years; a hotline between Washington and Moscow was installed in 1963.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.b] US involvement in Vietnam grew out of the containment of communism in Asia. Johnson's escalation of 1964 to 1968 is covered in the case study on Johnson and Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.0] Vietnam was part of French Indochina; the Viet Minh under Ho Chi Minh fought the French, who were defeated at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.1] From 1950 Truman gave aid to the French war, and by 1954 the United States was paying about three quarters of its cost.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.2] In April 1954 Eisenhower described the domino theory: if Vietnam fell to communism, the rest of Southeast Asia would follow, like a row of falling dominoes.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.3] The Geneva Accords of July 1954 divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with elections planned for 1956 that were never held.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.4] The United States backed Ngo Dinh Diem in South Vietnam and joined the Southeast Asia Treaty Organisation (SEATO) in 1954.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.5] Under Eisenhower the United States trained the South Vietnamese army with a military advisory group, and the first American advisers killed in the conflict died in 1959.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.6] The National Liberation Front, known to the Americans as the Viet Cong, was formed in December 1960 to fight the government in the South.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.7] Kennedy backed the Strategic Hamlet programme (1962), which moved peasants into fortified villages and was deeply resented.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.8] In 1963 Diem's persecution of Buddhists provoked protests, including the self-immolation of the monk Thich Quang Duc in Saigon in June 1963.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.9] Diem was overthrown and killed in a coup that US officials had tacitly encouraged, on 2 November 1963, after which the South suffered a series of unstable governments.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.10] Kennedy increased US military advisers in South Vietnam to about 16,000 by late 1963.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.11] The Nixon Doctrine, announced at Guam in July 1969, stated that Asian nations should take the main responsibility for their own defence, and US troop numbers began to fall.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.12] Nixon's Vietnamisation handed the ground war to the South Vietnamese army while US troops were withdrawn, falling from about 540,000 in early 1969 to under 30,000 by late 1972.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.13] In April 1970 Nixon sent US and South Vietnamese troops into Cambodia to attack communist bases, which sparked protests at home, including Kent State; secret bombing of Cambodia had begun in March 1969.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.14] In 1971 South Vietnamese forces invaded Laos with US air support and were driven back; in 1972 Nixon answered North Vietnam's Easter Offensive by mining Haiphong harbour and the Linebacker bombing raids, and ordered the Christmas bombing of Hanoi in December 1972.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.15] The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973, and the last US combat troops left Vietnam by late March 1973; the fall of Saigon in 1975 is outside this subsection.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.0] Eisenhower's Secretary of State John Foster Dulles relied on massive retaliation (1954): the threat of nuclear attack to deter communist aggression, which was cheaper than large conventional forces.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.1] Eisenhower is said to have signalled through intermediaries in 1953 that he might use nuclear weapons if the Korean talks failed, though historians dispute how far this forced the armistice.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.2] Kennedy replaced massive retaliation with flexible response, building up conventional and special forces (such as the Green Berets) so the United States could fight limited wars.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.3] After the missile crisis Kennedy and Khrushchev signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty (August 1963), banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.b] Truman's domestic record is covered in the notes on US politics. This block covers his foreign policy and how he is judged.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.0] At the Potsdam conference of July and August 1945 Truman met Stalin for the first time, and he authorised the atomic bombings shortly afterwards.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.1] The Truman Doctrine speech to Congress (12 March 1947) set out the principle that the United States would support free peoples against communist pressure.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.2] He recognised the new state of Israel on 14 May 1948, within minutes of its declaration of independence, against the advice of parts of his State Department.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.3] He decided in June 1950 to send American forces to defend South Korea without a congressional vote, a decision that set the pattern for later presidential war-making.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.4] He was a direct participant in every decision: he chose the airlift rather than an armed convoy to Berlin (1948) and accepted a limited war in Korea rather than MacArthur's wider war with China.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.5] A sign on his desk read "The buck stops here", summing up his view that the President must take final responsibility.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.6] Contemporaries were divided: Republicans attacked him over China and the spy scandals of the period, and by 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.7] Revisionist historians from the 1960s, such as William Appleman Williams, argued that US economic expansion and the atomic bomb helped cause the Cold War; post-revisionists such as John Lewis Gaddis stressed Stalin's own responsibility and argued that containment worked in Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.0] Success in Western Europe: the Marshall Plan, NATO and the Berlin airlift contained Soviet power in Europe and helped to create a prosperous Western alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.1] Failure in Asia: the Korean War ended in a stalemate after about three years, with heavy American losses and no victory.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.2] Korea can also be judged a limited success: South Korea survived, and Truman kept the war from spreading to China or becoming nuclear.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.3] The Cuban missile crisis avoided nuclear war, but the Bay of Pigs was a clear failure and the Soviet missiles were removed only in exchange for a secret US concession.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.4] Vietnam showed the limits of containment: a commitment that began with advisers had escalated to over half a million troops by 1968.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.5] Supporters argue that US commitments in Asia deterred further Soviet and Chinese expansion, while critics say they drew the United States into costly wars in places of little strategic importance.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.6] Contemporaries and later historians differ over whether the Cold War could have been managed by diplomacy earlier, and the debate shapes judgements of Truman, Kennedy and Johnson.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s5: 5. The decline of Cold War certainties, 1973–1989: withdrawal from Vietnam, détente, SALT and Star Wars
[hist-eur6-s5.0.b] The United States withdrew from the Vietnam War through a negotiated settlement, a result that weakened the consensus in favour of containment.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.0] The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973 by the United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Viet Cong, and they provided for the withdrawal of American forces.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.1] The last US combat troops left Vietnam by late March 1973, and the release of American prisoners of war was completed in the same period.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.2] Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto on 7 November 1973, requiring the President to report to Congress and to withdraw forces within sixty days unless Congress approved their continued use.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.3] From August 1973 the Case-Church amendment barred further US combat in Indochina, so when the North broke the Accords the United States could not intervene, and South Vietnam depended on its own forces and on aid from Congress.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.4] The North Vietnamese offensive of 1975 overran South Vietnam, and the capture of Saigon on 30 April 1975 ended the war.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.5] In April 1975 Congress refused Ford's request for emergency military aid for Saigon, a sign of how far the American consensus behind the war had collapsed.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.6] Ford told a university audience on 23 April 1975 that the war was finished as far as America was concerned, and the last Americans and many South Vietnamese were airlifted from Saigon by helicopter (Operation Frequent Wind) on 29 and 30 April.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.7] Cambodia fell to the Khmer Rouge on 17 April 1975, and Laos came under communist control later that year, so all three Indochinese states had communist governments by the end of 1975.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.8] About 58,000 Americans had died in the war, and the defeat produced a lasting caution about overseas military commitments, sometimes called the Vietnam syndrome.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.b] Détente was a policy of easing tensions with the Soviet Union and China through negotiation, based on a balance of power rather than on trust.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.0] Henry Kissinger, as national security adviser and later Secretary of State, designed a policy of linkage that tied arms control to Soviet behaviour in other regions.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.1] Nixon visited China from 21 to 28 February 1972, meeting Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai, and the Shanghai Communiqué set out a process to normalise relations.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.2] Nixon visited Moscow in May 1972, when the first major arms control agreements were signed and trade agreements were discussed.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.3] The Helsinki Final Act of August 1975, signed by thirty-five states including the United States and the Soviet Union, set out principles on borders and human rights, which the Soviet Union later failed to honour.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.4] Critics of détente, including conservative Democrats such as Senator Henry Jackson, argued that the Soviet Union was gaining military strength while the United States made concessions.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.5] The Jackson-Vanik Amendment of 1974 linked trade with the Soviet Union to freedom of emigration for Soviet Jews, and the Soviet Union rejected the link.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.6] Détente faced strain in October 1973 when the Yom Kippur War in the Middle East led Nixon to put US forces on a nuclear alert (DEFCON 3) after Soviet threats of intervention.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.7] Ford and Brezhnev reached a framework at Vladivostok in November 1974 for a second SALT agreement.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.8] Soviet and Cuban support for the MPLA in Angola in 1975 and 1976 convinced critics that the Soviet Union was exploiting détente.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.b] The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks aimed to limit the size of the nuclear arsenals of the superpowers, and produced two agreements of very different outcomes.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.0] SALT I was signed in Moscow on 26 May 1972 and contained an Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty limiting missile defence systems, and an Interim Agreement limiting the number of strategic launchers.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.1] The Interim Agreement froze the numbers of land-based ICBMs and submarine-launched missiles for five years, but it did not limit the number of warheads each missile carried.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.2] SALT II was signed in Vienna on 18 June 1979 by Carter and Brezhnev, setting an equal ceiling of 2,400 strategic delivery vehicles for each side, to fall to 2,250, and limiting the number of multiple-warhead missiles.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.3] The Senate did not ratify SALT II, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 led Carter to withdraw it from Senate consideration in January 1980.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.4] Both sides continued to build up their arsenals under SALT I and after SALT II collapsed, so that the agreements limited growth without reducing the number of warheads.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.5] SALT I was negotiated by Nixon and Brezhnev with Kissinger as the key American negotiator, and the Senate approved the ABM Treaty by 88 votes to 2 in August 1972.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.6] Critics of SALT II, led by Senator Henry Jackson, said it let the Soviet Union keep a lead in heavy land-based missiles, and the Committee on the Present Danger campaigned against it.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.7] The Soviet Union and the United States both said they would observe SALT II's limits informally after 1980, and Reagan's government dropped that restraint in 1986.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.b] Carter combined a human rights emphasis with a sharp hardening of policy after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.0] Carter made human rights a central theme of US foreign policy from 1977, and he criticised Soviet treatment of dissidents such as Andrei Sakharov.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.1] The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan on 24 to 25 December 1979 was condemned by Carter as an unacceptable threat to regional stability, and it ended the era of détente.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.2] Carter announced a grain embargo against the Soviet Union in January 1980, proclaimed in his State of the Union speech that the United States would resist any attempt to gain control of the Persian Gulf (the Carter Doctrine), and led a boycott of the Moscow Olympics: the US Olympic Committee voted to stay away in April 1980, and dozens of other countries did too, though Britain, France and Ireland sent teams.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.3] Carter's Iran policy, including the admission of the deposed Shah to the United States in October 1979, led to the seizure of the US embassy in Tehran on 4 November 1979.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.4] The failed rescue attempt of April 1980 (Operation Eagle Claw) was abandoned after helicopters broke down, and a helicopter collided with a transport aircraft at a desert staging point, killing eight servicemen; it contributed to Carter's defeat in the 1980 election.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.5] Carter also gave full diplomatic recognition to the People's Republic of China from 1 January 1979, building on Nixon's opening.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.b] Reagan rejected the logic of mutual deterrence and pursued a large increase in military spending, combined with a missile defence programme.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.0] Reagan described the Soviet Union as an evil empire in a speech to the National Association of Evangelicals on 8 March 1983.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.1] He announced the Strategic Defense Initiative on 23 March 1983 in a television address, proposing space-based systems that could destroy incoming missiles.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.2] The programme, nicknamed Star Wars by critics, drew scientists' doubts about feasibility and cost and was never deployed, but it alarmed Soviet leaders who feared an American technological lead they could not match.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.3] Reagan's military budget rose sharply between 1981 and 1985, and the B-1 bomber, MX missile and Pershing II missiles in Western Europe were among the projects funded.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.4] The Soviet Union's leadership changed rapidly from Brezhnev's death in November 1982 to Andropov, Chernenko and then Mikhail Gorbachev, who came to power in March 1985.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.b] The meetings between Reagan and Gorbachev produced the first treaty to abolish a whole category of nuclear weapons, and the end of the Cold War followed.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.0] Reagan and Gorbachev met at Geneva in November 1985, and at Reykjavik in October 1986, where they came close to agreeing deep cuts in nuclear weapons but the talks broke down when Gorbachev insisted that SDI be confined to the laboratory and Reagan refused.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.1] The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was signed in Washington on 8 December 1987 and eliminated all US and Soviet land-based missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.2] Reagan spoke at the Berlin Wall on 12 June 1987, declaring Mr Gorbachev, tear down this wall.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.3] Gorbachev's policies of glasnost and perestroika reduced Soviet control over Eastern Europe, and the Soviet Union did not intervene when communist governments fell in 1989.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.4] The Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November 1989, and the collapse of communist regimes across Eastern Europe during 1989 ended the division that had defined the Cold War.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.5] The Senate ratified the INF Treaty in May 1988, and Reagan visited Moscow for a fourth summit in May and June 1988; a Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan was completed in February 1989.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.6] Reagan's successor George H. W. Bush met Gorbachev off Malta in December 1989, and the two leaders declared that the Cold War was ending.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.b] Cold War competition extended into Central America, where revolutions and civil wars drew in US military and financial support.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.0] The Sandinista National Liberation Front overthrew the Somoza dictatorship in Nicaragua in July 1979, and it established a government with close links to Cuba and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.1] Carter's administration gave aid to the Sandinista government in its early years, but Reagan opposed it as a Soviet and Cuban foothold in the Americas and backed the Contra rebels against it.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.2] The Contra war in Nicaragua was funded in part by secret arrangements that Congress had sought to prevent through the Boland Amendments of 1982 to 1984.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.3] In El Salvador the government fought left-wing guerrillas from 1980, and the United States gave military aid to the government while criticising its record on human rights.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.4] Carter's administration suspended aid to the Sandinistas in January 1981, and Reagan authorised covert support for the Contras in late 1981.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.5] In El Salvador, the murder of Archbishop Oscar Romero in March 1980 and of four American churchwomen in December 1980 drew attention to death squads linked to the security forces, yet Reagan continued military aid.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.b] Popular fear of nuclear war grew in the early 1980s, and the nuclear freeze movement pressed for limits on arms that Reagan's build-up had not satisfied.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.0] The Nuclear Freeze campaign called for a bilateral freeze on the testing, production and deployment of nuclear weapons, and it gathered wide public support in 1982 and 1983.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.1] A very large demonstration in New York's Central Park in June 1982 was among the largest peace rallies in the country's history.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.2] The House of Representatives passed a non-binding freeze resolution in 1983, which showed how far the nuclear issue had moved into mainstream politics.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.3] The television film The Day After, about a nuclear attack on Kansas, was seen by about 100 million Americans on 20 November 1983, and in January 1984 Reagan said that a nuclear war could not be won and must never be fought.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.4] Massive protests in Western Europe, including at Greenham Common in Britain, opposed the deployment of American Pershing II and cruise missiles from late 1983.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.b] Examiners reward judgements that weigh US policy against Soviet internal failure and the choices of Gorbachev.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.0] Supporters of Reagan argue that his build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative forced the Soviet Union into an arms race it could not afford, and that his firm stance created the conditions for Gorbachev's reforms.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.1] Critics of Reagan argue that the Soviet economy was already failing before 1980 and that Gorbachev's reforms were the decisive cause of the change.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.2] Détente and arms control under Nixon, Ford and Carter laid the foundations for the later treaties, and the INF Treaty built on the principle of verifiable limits established in the 1970s.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.3] The failures of the period included the collapse of détente after 1979, the Iran hostage crisis and the costs of the Afghan war, which weakened US confidence before Reagan's arrival.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.4] The Soviet Union faced economic stagnation, the costs of the Afghan war and falling oil revenues in the mid-1980s, and Reagan's own adviser Jack Matlock argued that Gorbachev's reforms drove the change, rather than American pressure.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.5] Reagan's turn to negotiation after 1984, once he had rebuilt American strength, is credited by supporters as proof that strength led to a settlement.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s6: 6. The US economy, 1945–1968: sources of the boom and the multinational corporation
[hist-eur6-s6.0.b] The Second World War left the United States with the strongest industrial economy in the world, and wartime production and savings provided the platform for the postwar boom.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.0] US factories produced vast quantities of aircraft, ships, tanks and munitions between 1941 and 1945, and the economy expanded rapidly, with unemployment falling to very low levels.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.1] The United States produced roughly half of the world's manufactured goods in 1945, while its European and Japanese rivals had been devastated by the war.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.2] Wartime wage and price controls, and the savings that Americans built up during the war, left households with large sums to spend once rationing and controls ended.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.3] Wartime shortages of consumer goods gave way to a pent-up demand for cars, houses and household appliances after 1945.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.4] The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, known as the GI Bill, paid tuition and living costs for veterans and offered government-guaranteed loans for homes and businesses.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.5] The GI Bill's home loans helped to create a mass market for housing, which fed demand for consumer goods and for the construction industry.
[hist-eur6-s6.0.6] The wartime government became the largest customer in the economy, and it built factories that private firms bought cheaply after 1945, in industries such as aluminium, synthetic rubber and aircraft.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.b] The United States designed the postwar international monetary order, which made the dollar the central currency and financed recovery abroad.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.0] The Bretton Woods conference of July 1944 in New Hampshire created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank to support exchange rates and reconstruction.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.1] Under the Bretton Woods system the US dollar was convertible to gold at 35 dollars an ounce, and other currencies were pegged to the dollar.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.2] The Marshall Plan of 1948 to 1952 gave about 13 billion dollars of US aid to Western Europe, much of it spent on American goods, which boosted US exports.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.3] The dollar's role as the reserve currency, and the shortage of dollars in war-damaged Europe (the dollar gap), meant that Marshall aid and American loans were spent on US goods, and American companies could invest abroad cheaply.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.4] The Korean War and NSC-68 raised the federal defence budget from about 14 billion dollars in 1950 to about 50 billion dollars by 1953, which fed the economy's growth.
[hist-eur6-s6.1.5] The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) of 1947 committed the United States and other states to reduce trade barriers, which suited the world's biggest exporter.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.b] Government spending on infrastructure, defence and science drove growth in several key industries during the 1950s and 1960s.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.0] The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 funded an Interstate Highway System of about 41,000 miles, which opened up suburban development and boosted car production and steel.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.1] Federal defence spending remained high through the 1950s and 1960s and supplied a large market for aircraft, electronics and missile manufacturers.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.2] The National Science Foundation was created in 1950 and federal research funding grew steadily, with universities acting as partners to government research.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.3] The space programme, created after the launch of Sputnik in 1957 and the founding of NASA in 1958, employed thousands of engineers and contractors in the 1960s.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.4] Federal support for education was expanded through the National Defense Education Act of 1958, which funded science and language teaching after Sputnik.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.5] The Interstate programme was paid for by a Highway Trust Fund financed by a petrol tax, and it was justified partly on defence grounds, since Eisenhower had seen the German autobahns.
[hist-eur6-s6.2.6] Defence research funded the development of jet aircraft, radar, nuclear power for submarines and later computers, which spread into civilian industry.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.b] Defence spending created a network of contractors, military officers and politicians with shared interests, a development that Eisenhower warned of in 1961.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.0] Eisenhower used the phrase military-industrial complex in his farewell address of 17 January 1961, warning of the acquisition of unwarranted influence by a combination of the armed forces and the defence industry.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.1] Major defence contractors such as Lockheed, Boeing, General Dynamics and Douglas received large government contracts, and many companies depended on them for much of their revenue.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.2] The warning came from a former general whose own administration had built up the nuclear arsenal and relied on it, a point critics raised about the speech.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.3] Defence spending and the Cold War gave high-technology industries, including electronics and computing, a stable source of contracts that encouraged the growth of Silicon Valley.
[hist-eur6-s6.3.4] C. Wright Mills's The Power Elite (1956) had argued that a small group of corporate, military and political leaders made the major decisions, an idea that Eisenhower's speech echoed in warning form.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.b] The Organization Man is not a single person. It is the type of corporate employee that William H. Whyte described in his 1956 book, The Organization Man.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.0] William H. Whyte, an editor at Fortune magazine, published The Organization Man in 1956 as a study of white-collar workers in large corporations and suburban communities.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.1] Whyte argued that the corporate employee had accepted a social ethic that valued belonging, teamwork and conformity over individualism and personal conscience.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.2] Whyte described a social ethic with three beliefs: that the group is the source of creativity, that belonging is a person's greatest need, and that science can be applied to achieve belonging.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.3] He studied the suburb of Park Forest, Illinois, where young corporate families moved often and valued being sociable, and he criticised the personality tests that companies used in hiring.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.4] The same worry about conformity appeared in David Riesman's The Lonely Crowd (1950), which described the other-directed person, and in C. Wright Mills's White Collar (1951), which described anxious salaried workers.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.5] The Organization Man was a salaried manager or professional, often moving between cities and suburbs as the company required, whose career depended on loyalty to the corporation and on fitting in with its expectations.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.6] Sloan Wilson's novel The Man in the Gray Flannel Suit (1955) portrayed a similar figure, a corporate executive torn between career success and family life.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.7] Contemporaries praised the corporation for stability and rising living standards, but many critics saw the Organization Man as a threat to individual creativity and independence.
[hist-eur6-s6.4.8] Historians have used the figure to explain the conformity of the 1950s, although later scholars have argued that the book overstated the conformity of American corporate life.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.b] From the 1950s American companies expanded overseas, creating multinational corporations that linked the US economy to the rest of the world.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.0] General Motors, IBM, Coca-Cola and the International Telephone and Telegraph Corporation (ITT) were among the American multinationals that established large overseas operations by the 1960s.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.1] IBM's World Trade Corporation, created in 1949, managed the company's overseas operations, and by the 1960s a large share of IBM's revenue came from outside the United States.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.2] Coca-Cola's bottling networks spread across Europe, Latin America and Asia from the 1950s, using local franchises to supply the local market.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.3] US multinationals invested heavily in Western Europe after the Treaty of Rome of 1957 created the European Economic Community, which offered a large single market.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.4] The expansion of the multinationals was supported by US foreign policy, which encouraged open trade and investment as part of containment.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.5] Critics in Europe, such as the French writer Jean-Jacques Servan-Schreiber in The American Challenge (1967), argued that US companies were gaining control of key European industries.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.6] General Motors owned Opel in Germany and Vauxhall in Britain, and Ford had large European subsidiaries, so American carmakers built and sold cars across Europe.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.7] ITT under Harold Geneen became a conglomerate in the 1960s, buying firms in many industries and countries, and it later became notorious for its involvement in Chile.
[hist-eur6-s6.5.8] The Fortune 500 list of the largest American industrial companies was first published in 1955, and it showed how far a few big firms dominated the economy.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.b] Trade unions were strong in the core industries of the boom, and their agreements with large firms helped to share its gains.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.0] Union membership reached a peak of roughly a third of non-farm workers in the mid-1950s, concentrated in steel, car manufacturing, mining and transport.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.1] The United Auto Workers and General Motors signed a five-year contract in 1950, often called the Treaty of Detroit, which tied wage rises to productivity and provided pensions and health insurance.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.2] Such agreements gave many manual workers rising real wages and benefits, and they became a model for other unions in the core industries.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.3] The Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 restricted union tactics such as secondary boycotts, and it required union leaders to sign affidavits disavowing communism until 1959.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.4] The merger of the American Federation of Labor and the Congress of Industrial Organizations in 1955 created a single labour federation led by George Meany.
[hist-eur6-s6.6.5] Unions were weakest in the South and among service and farm workers, who were often excluded from the postwar bargain.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.b] Government economists believed that spending and tax policy could sustain growth, and the 1964 tax cut was a major test of that belief.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.0] John Maynard Keynes's ideas, that governments could manage demand through spending and taxation, were accepted by the economic advisers of Kennedy and Johnson.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.1] Walter Heller, chairman of the Council of Economic Advisers under Kennedy, argued for a tax cut to stimulate demand and growth.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.2] The Revenue Act of 1964 cut income tax rates across the board and reduced the top rate from 91 per cent to 70 per cent.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.3] The tax cut was followed by strong economic growth through the mid-1960s, and it is often cited as a successful example of demand management before the costs of the Vietnam War appeared.
[hist-eur6-s6.7.4] Critics such as Milton Friedman, a monetarist, argued that governments could not fine-tune the economy, a view that gained ground in the 1970s when demand management failed to cope with stagflation.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.b] Examiners reward evaluation that weighs the scale of the boom against its dependence on US dominance and on defence spending.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.0] Achievements: a sustained rise in living standards, widespread home ownership and a large middle class were features of the period from 1945 to 1968.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.1] Limits: the boom depended partly on the temporary dominance of the US economy after 1945, which was bound to decline as Europe and Japan recovered.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.2] Critics argue that the boom was financed by defence spending and by the large public investment that followed the war, rather than by the free market.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.3] Some groups did not share fully in the boom, and black Americans and many southern workers faced discrimination in jobs, housing and access to the GI Bill benefits.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.4] John Kenneth Galbraith's The Affluent Society (1958) argued that the boom created private wealth but left public services such as schools and parks neglected.
[hist-eur6-s6.8.5] Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) showed that tens of millions still lived in poverty despite the boom.
