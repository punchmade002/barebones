TARGET hist-eur1-s3: 3. The Germanic lands from Confederation to Empire, 1850–1871
[hist-eur1-s3.0.b] To follow how the German lands reached empire in 1871, you need the starting point: a loose league of states, two rival great powers and a lesson learned from failure in 1848–50. Every later block builds on this.
[hist-eur1-s3.0.0] The German Confederation, set up in 1815 at the Congress of Vienna, was a loose league of sovereign states with a Diet (an assembly of state delegates) at Frankfurt, presided over by Austria. Austria and Prussia were the two great powers in it, so their rivalry decided its future.
[hist-eur1-s3.0.1] In 1849 Frederick William IV of Prussia refused the imperial crown offered by the Frankfurt Parliament (the elected assembly of 1848–49 that tried to unite Germany). Nationalists drew the lesson that unity would need Prussian power, not parliamentary votes.
[hist-eur1-s3.0.2] The point to carry forward is that votes had failed and Prussian power was the alternative.
[hist-eur1-s3.0.3] At the Punctation of Olmütz (November 1850) Prussia abandoned its Erfurt Union plan (a Prussian-led union of German states) under Austrian and Russian pressure. This humiliated Prussia; the Confederation and Diet were restored in 1851, and Bismarck became Prussia's delegate at the Diet that year.
[hist-eur1-s3.0.4] The choice facing Germans was Kleindeutsch (a smaller Germany without Austria, under Prussia) or Grossdeutsch (a larger Germany including Austria). The events of 1866 settled the question in favour of Kleindeutsch.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.b] After the failure of 1848, the balance between Prussia and Austria within the German Confederation turned on economic power and on the ability of each state to manage its own affairs. Prussia grew stronger and Austria weaker, which is why Prussia could later challenge it.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.0] The Zollverein (the German customs union, in force from 1834 under Prussian leadership) included almost all German states except Austria by the 1850s; Hanover joined in 1854. It gave Prussia control of a large common market.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.1] Tariff revenue was shared among Zollverein members, so the members were tied to Prussia economically.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.2] The Ruhr (Rhineland-Westphalia) and Silesia, both Prussian, provided coal and iron. Rapid railway building from the 1840s linked the Prussian industrial regions to Berlin and to the Rhineland, so goods and, later, troops could move quickly.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.3] Alfred Krupp's cast-steel works at Essen in the Ruhr, expanded rapidly from the 1850s, produced railway materials and steel guns. It symbolised Prussia's industrial power because it supplied both the railways and the army.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.4] Prussia's 1862 commercial treaty with France, made for the Zollverein, lowered tariffs and set a low-tariff course. The other Zollverein states had accepted it by 1865, a course that Austria's protectionist system (high tariffs to shield its own industry) could not match.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.5] Austria's finances were weak and its government remained centralised and conservative, with the Habsburg monarchy heavily in debt. This left it less able to modernise or to pay for a large army.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.6] Austria's defeat in 1859 by France and Piedmont, at the battles of Magenta and Solferino, cost it Lombardy and exposed its military weakness.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.7] Austria had alienated Russia by its hostile neutrality towards Russia in the Crimean War (1853–56), so it lacked a friend when Prussia challenged it.
[hist-eur1-s3.1.8] Austria's attempt to reform the Confederation, the Frankfurt Princes' Congress (Fürstentag) of August 1863 called by Emperor Franz Joseph, collapsed because William I stayed away on Bismarck's advice. Without Prussia the plan could not work.
[hist-eur1-s3.2.b] Prussia's military strength was not automatic: it came from an army reform that provoked a fierce clash with the liberals in parliament. That clash is the setting in which Bismarck came to power.
[hist-eur1-s3.2.0] William, the brother of Frederick William IV (who was incapacitated from 1857), became regent in 1858 and King William I in January 1861.
[hist-eur1-s3.2.1] Roon's army reform kept three-year service, enlarged the regular army and reduced the role of the Landwehr (the citizen reserve militia). Liberals in the Landtag (the Prussian parliament) opposed it.
[hist-eur1-s3.2.2] Albrecht von Roon, War Minister from 1859, organised the army, while Helmuth von Moltke, chief of the general staff from 1857, handled planning and command. Do not swap their roles.
[hist-eur1-s3.2.3] The German Progressive Party (Fortschrittspartei) was founded in 1861. The liberal majority in the Prussian lower house refused to vote the army budget in 1862, so king and parliament were deadlocked.
[hist-eur1-s3.2.4] The constitutional crisis deepened until William I considered abdicating and drew up an abdication document in September 1862.
[hist-eur1-s3.2.5] In September 1862 William I appointed Otto von Bismarck Minister-President and Foreign Minister of Prussia to break the deadlock.
[hist-eur1-s3.3.b] Bismarck had to govern without the parliament's consent to the budget. He justified this with a legal argument and set out his view of how Germany would be united.
[hist-eur1-s3.3.0] On 30 September 1862, in a speech to the Budget Committee of the Landtag, Bismarck said the great questions of the day would be decided not by speeches and majority decisions, the mistake of 1848 and 1849, but by "blood and iron".
[hist-eur1-s3.3.1] By this he meant that force, not parliamentary debate, would settle German unity.
[hist-eur1-s3.3.2] In the same speech he argued that Germany looked not to Prussia's liberalism but to its power. This told the liberals that he would use the army, not their ideas, to unite Germany.
[hist-eur1-s3.3.3] Bismarck used the gap theory (Lückentheorie): the constitution did not say what happened if king and parliament disagreed, so the king's government could carry on. On this reasoning his government declared it was entitled to go on collecting taxes.
[hist-eur1-s3.3.4] Taxes were collected and the army reform was carried out without an approved budget from 1862 to 1866. The conflict lasted until the Indemnity Bill of 1866.
[hist-eur1-s3.4.b] The first of Bismarck's three wars showed his method: act with Austria against a weaker opponent, then use the settlement to prepare the quarrel with Austria itself.
[hist-eur1-s3.4.0] Schleswig and Holstein (with Lauenburg) were ruled by the Danish king. Holstein was German-speaking and in the Confederation, while Schleswig was mixed Danish and German and outside it, and Germans wanted links with Germany.
[hist-eur1-s3.4.1] Christian IX became King of Denmark in November 1863 and signed the November Constitution (1863), binding Schleswig to Denmark. This broke Danish undertakings of 1851–52 that were confirmed by the London Protocol of 1852, which also settled the Danish succession, and it provoked the German states.
[hist-eur1-s3.4.2] Bismarck chose to act with Austria rather than alone so that the issue stayed a matter for the two great powers, not for the Diet or the liberals. It also kept a later quarrel with Austria available.
[hist-eur1-s3.4.3] Prussia and Austria invaded Denmark jointly in February 1864. Prussia stormed Düppel (Dybbøl) in April 1864 and captured the island of Als in June 1864.
[hist-eur1-s3.4.4] By the Treaty of Vienna (October 1864) Denmark ceded Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg to Prussia and Austria jointly. Their future was left to be settled between the two victors.
[hist-eur1-s3.4.5] By the Convention of Gastein (August 1865) Austria administered Holstein and Prussia Schleswig, and Prussia bought Austria's rights in Lauenburg. This divided administration only, so it was a ready-made source of dispute and set up the conflict of 1866.
[hist-eur1-s3.5.b] Bismarck did not fight Austria until he had made sure it had no friends. Three separate diplomatic steps prepared the ground for war in 1866.
[hist-eur1-s3.5.0] The Alvensleben Convention (February 1863) promised Prussian help to Russia against the Polish rising of 1863. Russian goodwill followed, which mattered because Austria had already lost Russian friendship in the Crimean War.
[hist-eur1-s3.5.1] Bismarck met Napoleon III at Biarritz in October 1865, and Napoleon III remained neutral in the coming war. This freed Prussia from the risk of French intervention.
[hist-eur1-s3.5.2] In April 1866 Prussia made an alliance with Italy that promised Venetia to Italy if Prussia won. Italy would attack Austria on a second front, so Austria faced war on two fronts.
[hist-eur1-s3.5.3] In June 1866 Prussia declared the Gastein arrangements at an end and occupied Holstein. The Diet voted to mobilise against Prussia.
[hist-eur1-s3.5.4] Most German states sided with Austria, including Bavaria, Saxony, Hanover, Württemberg and Hesse-Kassel. Prussia went to war against much of the Confederation, but its military and diplomatic advantages were greater.
[hist-eur1-s3.6.b] The war was over in about seven weeks. Prussia's military superiority, not just its diplomacy, decided it, and Bismarck then chose a moderate peace to leave Austria a possible future friend.
[hist-eur1-s3.6.0] Prussia won because of the Dreyse breech-loading needle-gun, which could be fired lying down and was faster than Austrian muzzle-loaders, and because of Moltke's planning. Moltke used railways to move armies quickly into Bohemia.
[hist-eur1-s3.6.1] The battle of Königgrätz (also called Sadowa) in Bohemia, fought on 3 July 1866, was the decisive Prussian victory over the Austrian army under Benedek. The war lasted about seven weeks, which is why it is called the Seven Weeks' War.
[hist-eur1-s3.6.2] Italy was beaten by Austria at Custoza (on land) and Lissa (at sea) in 1866 but still gained Venetia, thanks to Prussia's victory.
[hist-eur1-s3.6.3] After Königgrätz, Bismarck persuaded William I and the generals to halt at the Nikolsburg preliminaries (July 1866), with no march on Vienna and no Austrian territory taken. His aim was to keep Austria as a future friend.
[hist-eur1-s3.6.4] The Treaty of Prague (August 1866) dissolved the German Confederation and Austria accepted exclusion from German affairs. Venetia was ceded, via France, to Italy, and the Habsburg lands lay outside any German state.
[hist-eur1-s3.6.5] Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, Frankfurt and Schleswig-Holstein in 1866.
[hist-eur1-s3.6.6] In August 1866 Bavaria, Württemberg and Baden signed secret offensive-defensive alliances with Prussia (each promising to fight alongside it if attacked), which tied the southern states to Prussia.
[hist-eur1-s3.7.b] Victory in 1866 changed how Prussian liberals saw Bismarck. The question of the years without a budget had to be settled, and it divided them.
[hist-eur1-s3.7.0] The Indemnity Bill, passed by the Prussian Landtag in September 1866, asked the Landtag to approve retrospectively the years of government without an approved budget. It gave Bismarck's rule a legal cover after the event.
[hist-eur1-s3.7.1] The Bill split the liberals into those who gave way and those who stayed in opposition.
[hist-eur1-s3.7.2] The National Liberal Party was founded in 1867 by liberals who backed Bismarck's national policy, so many liberals accepted his leadership in return for national unification. The remaining Progressives stayed in opposition.
[hist-eur1-s3.8.b] Prussia now had a framework to organise the north and to draw in the south. The Confederation was a step towards the Empire, built on Prussian dominance.
[hist-eur1-s3.8.0] The North German Confederation was formed in 1867 by Prussia and the states north of the River Main, under Prussian leadership.
[hist-eur1-s3.8.1] Its constitution, in force from 1 July 1867, made the King of Prussia president of the Confederation, with control of foreign policy and the armed forces. Bismarck was Federal Chancellor, so Prussia's king and minister ran the new union.
[hist-eur1-s3.8.2] The Reichstag (the Confederation's parliament) was elected by universal manhood suffrage (every adult man could vote). The Bundesrat was made up of delegates of the state governments, and Prussia dominated it.
[hist-eur1-s3.8.3] A Customs Parliament (Zollparlament) first met in 1868 with deputies from the southern states as well as the north. Southern voters returned many anti-Prussian (Catholic and particularist, meaning favouring each state's separate independence) deputies, so the south was not yet won.
[hist-eur1-s3.9.b] France resented Prussia's rise, and Bismarck used a dispute over the Spanish throne to turn that resentment into a war in which France appeared the aggressor.
[hist-eur1-s3.9.0] French resentment of Prussia's victory in 1866 was summed up as revenge for Sadowa. In the Luxembourg crisis of 1867 Napoleon III tried to buy Luxembourg from the King of the Netherlands; Prussian opposition stopped it, and the London Conference of 1867 made Luxembourg neutral.
[hist-eur1-s3.9.1] In 1870 Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, from a Catholic branch of the Prussian royal family, accepted the Spanish crown, vacant since the 1868 revolution. This alarmed France, which feared encirclement by Hohenzollern rulers on two sides.
[hist-eur1-s3.9.2] The withdrawal of Leopold's candidature was announced on 12 July 1870 after French pressure. France then demanded a guarantee that it would never be renewed.
[hist-eur1-s3.9.3] On 13 July 1870 at Bad Ems the French ambassador Benedetti asked William I for that guarantee. William refused politely and had a report of the meeting telegraphed to Bismarck.
[hist-eur1-s3.9.4] Bismarck shortened the report and released it to the press that evening. In its shortened form the Ems telegram made the exchange read as an insult to France.
[hist-eur1-s3.9.5] France declared war on 19 July 1870 and appeared the aggressor. Bavaria, Württemberg and Baden joined Prussia under their treaties, while Austria, Italy and Britain stayed neutral and Russia was friendly to Prussia.
[hist-eur1-s3.9.6] In July 1870 Bismarck published in The Times Benedetti's earlier draft treaty on French designs on Belgium, which turned British opinion against France. France was left with no ally.
[hist-eur1-s3.10.b] Prussia's armies, with the southern states' troops beside them, won quickly. The war ended French resistance and removed Napoleon III.
[hist-eur1-s3.10.0] Wörth and Spicheren (6 August 1870) were the first big French defeats. At Gravelotte–St Privat (18 August 1870) Prussia shut Bazaine's army in Metz.
[hist-eur1-s3.10.1] Napoleon III had led the army to the front. At the battle of Sedan on 1 September 1870 the French were defeated, and Napoleon III and his army surrendered on 2 September 1870.
[hist-eur1-s3.10.2] The Second Empire fell on 4 September 1870 when the Third Republic was proclaimed in Paris. Gambetta and the Government of National Defence carried on the war, and Gambetta left Paris by balloon to organise resistance in the provinces.
[hist-eur1-s3.10.3] Bazaine surrendered Metz in October 1870. The siege of Paris lasted from September 1870 to January 1871, and the city capitulated in January 1871 and an armistice was signed on 28 January 1871.
[hist-eur1-s3.10.4] Prussia won because of railways, superior artillery and Moltke's planning, together with the southern states' troops and France's isolation.
[hist-eur1-s3.11.b] Victory over France let Bismarck complete unification. He brought the southern states in by treaty and staged the proclamation in the palace of the defeated enemy.
[hist-eur1-s3.11.0] In November 1870 treaties brought Baden, Hesse-Darmstadt (south of the Main), Württemberg and Bavaria into the union. Bavaria kept special rights: its own army in peacetime, post and railways.
[hist-eur1-s3.11.1] In late 1870 King Ludwig II of Bavaria sent the Kaiserbrief, a letter drafted by Bismarck, asking William I to accept the title of emperor. This made the offer come from a southern prince, not from Prussia.
[hist-eur1-s3.11.2] The German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles, with William I as German Emperor (Deutscher Kaiser). The date was the anniversary of the crowning of the first King in Prussia in 1701.
[hist-eur1-s3.11.3] William was proclaimed at Versailles, in France, not in Berlin, and the title was German Emperor, not Emperor of Germany.
[hist-eur1-s3.11.4] The Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871) ended the war. It transferred Alsace (except Belfort) and north-eastern Lorraine, including Metz, to Germany and required France to pay an indemnity of five billion francs, with German occupation until it was paid.
[hist-eur1-s3.12.b] The Empire's constitution gave Germany a parliament but kept real power with the emperor and Prussia.
[hist-eur1-s3.12.0] The imperial constitution of April 1871 was based on the 1867 constitution. It created a federal state of 25 member states plus the Reichsland of Alsace-Lorraine.
[hist-eur1-s3.12.1] The King of Prussia was always German Emperor, so Prussian royal power and imperial power were joined in one person. The emperor controlled the army and foreign policy.
[hist-eur1-s3.12.2] The Reichstag was elected by manhood suffrage, but the Chancellor was appointed by and answerable to the emperor alone, not to the Reichstag. This meant the elected house could not remove the head of government.
[hist-eur1-s3.12.3] The Bundesrat, made up of the states' governments, gave Prussia 17 of 58 votes, and 14 votes could block a constitutional change. Prussia alone could therefore veto any amendment.
[hist-eur1-s3.12.4] Prussia, with about two-thirds of the Empire's territory and about three-fifths of its population, kept its own three-class franchise, a voting system weighted towards the wealthy. This kept the dominant state conservative.
[hist-eur1-s3.13.b] Bismarck was a Prussian conservative and Junker landowner who used war and diplomacy to create the German Empire. This part covers his role in unification.
[hist-eur1-s3.13.0] Bismarck was born on 1 April 1815 at Schönhausen, in the Prussian province of Saxony, into a Junker (Prussian landowning noble) family. He sat in the United Diet of 1847 as an ultra-conservative and in the Prussian lower house from 1849.
[hist-eur1-s3.13.1] He was Prussian delegate at the restored German Diet in Frankfurt from 1851 to 1859, which taught him to regard Austria as Prussia's rival. He was then ambassador at St Petersburg (1859–62) and at Paris (1862) before becoming Minister-President in September 1862.
[hist-eur1-s3.13.2] As Minister-President he gave the "blood and iron" speech and used the gap theory to collect taxes and carry through the army reform without an approved budget. These steps gave him the means to build a strong army.
[hist-eur1-s3.13.3] He was a participant who directed each stage of unification: the Danish War (1864), the Gastein settlement (1865), the alliance with Italy (1866), the war with Austria and the Treaty of Prague (1866), the North German Confederation (1867) and the Ems telegram (1870).
[hist-eur1-s3.13.4] He became Chancellor of the North German Confederation in 1867 and Imperial Chancellor in 1871, holding office until 1890, and he was made a prince (Fürst) in 1871.
[hist-eur1-s3.13.5] Contemporaries were divided: conservatives admired his success, while many liberals saw an authoritarian who had broken the constitution. Most liberals were won over by 1866–71, as the National Liberal Party shows.
[hist-eur1-s3.13.6] Historians disagree. The Prussian school (for example Heinrich von Sybel and Heinrich von Treitschke) saw a master plan, and A. J. P. Taylor, in Bismarck: The Man and the Statesman (1955), portrayed an opportunist who improvised.
[hist-eur1-s3.13.7] Lothar Gall's biography Bismarck: The White Revolutionary (German 1980) argued that he used revolutionary means, nationalism, manhood suffrage and war, to preserve the conservative order. The phrase had been used of Bismarck before Gall.
[hist-eur1-s3.14.b] Bismarck's aim was a Germany united under Prussian leadership. His methods were flexible, and historians argue about whether he followed a plan or seized chances.
[hist-eur1-s3.14.0] Realpolitik, a term coined by Ludwig von Rochau in Principles of Realpolitik (1853), meant pursuing state interests through power and diplomacy rather than through principles or ideology. It was Rochau who coined it, not Bismarck.
[hist-eur1-s3.14.1] Bismarck used liberal demands for national unity to win support for his policies, while keeping the army and the monarchy under royal control. In other words, he used liberal nationalism for Prussian ends.
[hist-eur1-s3.14.2] He isolated each enemy before war: Russian goodwill after the Alvensleben Convention of 1863, Napoleon III's neutrality after Biarritz in 1865 and the alliance with Italy in 1866 against Austria, and France's lack of an ally in 1870.
[hist-eur1-s3.14.3] He fought short wars with limited aims and avoided humiliating Austria in 1866, which made it easier for Austria to become a partner later.
[hist-eur1-s3.14.4] He exploited chances as they arose: the Danish succession crisis, the Gastein arrangement, the Hohenzollern candidature and the Ems telegram, which created a public crisis that drew the southern states into the war.
[hist-eur1-s3.14.5] The debate is whether he had a long-term plan to unite Germany under Prussia or exploited opportunities with a general aim of Prussian power. The Prussian school saw a plan, while Taylor saw an improviser.
[hist-eur1-s3.14.6] The usual balanced verdict is a clear aim with flexible methods.
[hist-eur1-s3.15.b] Italy and Germany were unified in the same years, so examiners ask you to compare them. Both had a strong kingdom leading the way, but their paths and results differed.
[hist-eur1-s3.15.0] Italy was unified between 1859 and 1871, with the Kingdom of Italy proclaimed in March 1861. Venetia was added in 1866 and Rome in September 1870, after the French garrison withdrew for the war with Prussia, and Rome became the capital from 1871.
[hist-eur1-s3.15.1] Both unifications were led by a strong kingdom, Piedmont or Prussia, which combined war with diplomacy and treated Austria as the common enemy.
[hist-eur1-s3.15.2] Italy won Venetia from Prussia's victory in 1866 even though Austria beat it at Custoza and Lissa. Rome was blocked until the French garrison left in 1870.
[hist-eur1-s3.15.3] Piedmont needed French armies against Austria in 1859, whereas Prussia needed only foreign neutrality, so Prussia's military strength was far greater.
[hist-eur1-s3.15.4] Italy's unity came partly from below, through Garibaldi's volunteers and plebiscites (votes by the people), while Germany's came from above, through the Prussian army and the princes.
[hist-eur1-s3.15.5] Germany emerged far stronger economically and militarily than Italy.
[hist-eur1-s3.15.6] Italy left Italian-speaking Trentino and Trieste in Austria, so unity was unfinished. Germany placed Danes in north Schleswig, Poles in Prussia's eastern provinces and Alsatians inside the Empire, and left out the Austrian Germans.
[hist-eur1-s3.16.b] The Empire was a real achievement but a limited one, and contemporaries and later historians judged it differently.
[hist-eur1-s3.16.0] Achievement: unity, the aim of the liberals of 1848, was achieved by 1871 through Bismarck's wars and diplomacy.
[hist-eur1-s3.16.1] Limit: unity came from above, by Prussian arms and the princes, not by a parliament, so it was not the liberal unity of 1848.
[hist-eur1-s3.16.2] Limit: it was Kleindeutsch, leaving Austria, with its millions of Germans, outside the Empire. The Empire contained national minorities, Poles, Danes and, after 1871, Alsatians.
[hist-eur1-s3.16.3] Limit: the Empire was a Prussian-dominated semi-autocracy (a system in which the ruler keeps most real power). The Reichstag, elected by manhood suffrage, had no control over the Chancellor and less power than the liberals had hoped.
[hist-eur1-s3.16.4] Contemporary views: liberal supporters of unification saw the Empire as a national triumph (nationalism, the belief that a people sharing a language and culture should form one state, had been satisfied).
[hist-eur1-s3.16.5] Later views: critics argued that the Empire was a Prussian-led autocracy built on militarism.

CANDIDATES hist-eur1-s4: 4. Italy moves towards unity, 1815–1871
[hist-eur1-s4.0.b] Italian unity was driven by two ideas and fought over by three programmes. Knowing these terms makes the whole story of 1815-1871 easier to follow.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.0] Nationalism is the belief that people who share a language, culture and history should form one state. In Italy it meant expelling Austria and uniting the separate states into one country.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.1] Liberalism means constitutional government, an elected parliament and civil liberties. In Italy it was represented by Piedmont's constitution, the Statuto, and by Cavour, so liberal ideas and nationalist ideas often worked together.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.2] The Risorgimento (Italian for 'resurgence') is the name for the movement for Italian unity and independence, 1815-1870.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.3] Nationalists disagreed about what kind of Italy they wanted. Mazzini wanted a unitary republic, so one state with no kings and the people as sovereign.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.4] The neo-Guelphs wanted a federation of Italian states under the Pope. Their name recalls the medieval Guelphs, the papal party.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.5] The moderates, and later Cavour, wanted a constitutional monarchy led by Piedmont. This is the programme that finally won, which is why the split between these three matters for exam answers on success and failure.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.b] Napoleon had redrawn the map of Italy, but the Congress of Vienna (the 1815 peace settlement) restored the old rulers and made Austria the dominant power. Italy was a patchwork of states, most under Austrian influence, with the Pope ruling the centre.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.0] Napoleon had created a Kingdom of Italy in the north (1805) with himself as king, and his brother Joseph and then Murat ruled Naples. He brought the Code Napoleon, a single centralised administration and the idea of a united Italy, which restored rulers could not entirely undo.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.1] The Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, ruled by the House of Savoy, was the only major state ruled by a native Italian dynasty. It was enlarged in 1815 by Genoa, the former republic, which made it the largest independent state in the north-west.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.2] Austria directly ruled Lombardy and Venetia in the north. Tuscany and Modena had Habsburg rulers, and Parma went to Marie-Louise, Napoleon's Habsburg widow, until 1847, so family ties tied the duchies to Vienna.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.3] The Papal States covered central Italy from Rome to Bologna. The Pope was an absolute monarch (a ruler with unlimited power) as well as head of the Church, with the clergy running the administration, so Italian unity threatened his state.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.4] The Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, ruled by the Bourbons from Naples, covered the south and Sicily. Its kings were Ferdinand I (to 1825), Ferdinand II (1830-59) and Francis II (1859-61).
[hist-eur1-s4.1.5] Austria also had military means. Its fortresses of the Quadrilateral (Mantua, Peschiera, Verona and Legnago) guarded the north, and Field Marshal Radetzky commanded its army in Italy, which Austria used to put down revolts.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.6] The Austrian Empire was multinational, so Italian nationalism threatened it directly. Metternich, its chief minister, said 'Italy is a geographical expression' (1847), meaning he saw a map region and not a nation.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.7] Italy was also divided in society. The economy was mainly agricultural, communications were poor, people felt loyal to their region and spoke dialects, illiteracy was widespread and the south was poor.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.b] Opposition to the restored rulers began in secret societies. Their risings in 1820-21 and 1831 all failed, and the reasons for those failures shaped what came next.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.0] The Carbonari were a secret society that spread liberal and nationalist ideas among army officers, students and professional men in the 1820s. Being secret kept them safe from the police but also kept them small.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.1] The Carbonari took part in the rising in Naples (July 1820) and the rising in Piedmont (March 1821). Both were crushed by Austrian troops in 1821, after the Congress of Laibach, and the leaders were imprisoned or exiled.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.2] In February 1831 risings broke out in Modena, Parma and the Papal Legations (the northern provinces of the Papal States). They were crushed with Austrian help, and Ciro Menotti, leader of the Modena rising, was executed in 1831.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.3] Louis-Napoleon, later Napoleon III, took part in the 1831 rising in the Papal States as a young man. This helps explain why he later posed as a friend of Italian nationalism.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.4] The early risings failed for several reasons: they were run by small secret groups, had no mass support, had local rather than national aims, had no foreign help and faced Austrian military power.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.5] The lesson Mazzini drew was that Italy needed a national movement with popular support, and this is why he founded Young Italy.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.b] Mazzini was the most influential theorist and organiser of Italian nationalism before 1848. His ideal of a republican, unified Italy shaped the movement even though his methods failed and he was left disappointed in 1861.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.0] Mazzini was born in Genoa on 22 June 1805, took a law degree (1827) and joined the Carbonari in 1827. He was arrested in November 1830, imprisoned at Savona and went into exile early in 1831.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.1] In 1831 he founded Young Italy at Marseille. It was a secret society for young men under 40 who swore an oath to work for an independent, united republic, and it spread through branches across Italy.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.2] His ideas were a unitary republic with the people as sovereign, and the motto 'God and the People' (Dio e Popolo). He wanted unity through education and popular insurrection (rising), not through kings or foreign help.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.3] His book The Duties of Man (published in full 1860) put duty to the nation above individual rights, so citizens were asked to serve Italy before themselves.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.4] His risings all failed for lack of popular support: the Piedmont army plot (1833, about a dozen executed), the invasion of Savoy (February 1834), the Bandiera brothers in Calabria (1844), Milan (February 1853) and Pisacane's expedition at Sapri (1857, Pisacane killed).
[hist-eur1-s4.3.5] He founded Young Europe at Bern (April 1834) to link revolutionary movements. He lived in exile in Switzerland, France and, from January 1837, London, which stayed his main base for the rest of his life, where he ran his network of exiles.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.6] In 1848 he returned to Italy. In 1849 he was one of the three triumvirs (joint rulers) of the Roman Republic, with Garibaldi commanding its defence. After 1849 his reputation suffered as risings failed and moderates turned to Piedmont.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.7] His supporters, among them Francesco Crispi and Rosolino Pilo, helped prepare the Sicilian rising of April 1860 and pressed Garibaldi to lead an expedition.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.b] Mazzini's later life shows the gap between the idea he gave Italy and the state that was built. Contemporaries and historians judge him differently from how he judged himself.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.0] Mazzini was a participant, not a witness: he planned risings and sent agents into Italy, and his writings inspired followers, including Garibaldi.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.1] Contemporaries were divided. Radicals and republicans admired him, Cavour and the monarchists distrusted him, Austria and the Pope saw a dangerous conspirator, and to many moderates his risings wasted lives.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.2] He rejected the monarchical Italy of 1861 as a betrayal, because he had wanted a republic built by the people.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.3] In August 1870 he was arrested on arrival at Palermo while trying to start a republican rising in Sicily, and was imprisoned at Gaeta. He was released in the amnesty after Rome was taken (October 1870).
[hist-eur1-s4.4.4] He died at Pisa on 10 March 1872.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.5] Historians now see him as the prophet and ideologue (thinker who supplies the ideas) of the Risorgimento, who kept the idea of unity alive and inspired a generation. His methods failed and his republic was not achieved.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.b] Not every nationalist wanted revolution or a republic. In the 1840s two writers offered peaceful alternatives, and a new Pope seemed to prove that reform could work.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.0] Vincenzo Gioberti, in On the Moral and Civil Primacy of the Italians (1843), proposed a federation of Italian states under the Pope. His followers were called neo-Guelphs.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.1] Cesare Balbo, in The Hopes of Italy (1844), argued for reform and independence led by Piedmont's monarchy, not revolution. Massimo d'Azeglio, a Piedmontese moderate, was Prime Minister 1849-52.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.2] Pius IX was elected Pope in June 1846. An amnesty for political prisoners (1846), a civic guard and some press freedom raised hopes of a liberal Pope who might lead Italy.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.3] This explains the early 1848 mood: many moderates expected reform from rulers and the Pope. When Pius IX turned against the national cause the neo-Guelph programme collapsed.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.b] In 1848 revolution spread across Italy and, for the first time, a state went to war with Austria. Austria won in 1848-49, but the failure showed which road unity would have to take.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.0] The causes were economic distress, hopes raised by Pius IX and news of the February revolution in Paris. The first rising was at Palermo on 12 January 1848, and rulers granted constitutions: Naples, Tuscany, Piedmont (the Statuto, 4 March 1848) and the Papal States.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.1] The Five Days of Milan (18-22 March 1848) forced Radetzky to withdraw to the Quadrilateral fortresses. Venice rose under Daniele Manin, who proclaimed the Republic of San Marco on 22 March 1848.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.2] Charles Albert of Piedmont declared war on Austria on 23 March 1848, the First War of Independence, with the slogan 'Italia farà da sé' ('Italy will do it by herself'). He was defeated at Custoza on 24-25 July 1848, and an armistice followed in August.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.3] Pius IX refused war against Catholic Austria in his Allocution of 29 April 1848. After the murder of his minister Pellegrino Rossi (15 November 1848) he fled to Gaeta, in the Kingdom of Naples.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.4] The Roman Republic was proclaimed on 9 February 1849. Mazzini, Aurelio Saffi and Carlo Armellini governed as triumvirs from March, and Garibaldi commanded the defence.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.5] Charles Albert was defeated at Novara on 23 March 1849 and abdicated that night; he died in exile in Portugal in 1849. His son Victor Emmanuel II made peace with Austria (Treaty of Milan, August 1849).
[hist-eur1-s4.6.6] French troops under General Oudinot, sent by Louis-Napoleon, then President of France, took Rome at the start of July 1849 after a siege, restored the Pope and kept a garrison there. Garibaldi retreated with about 4,000 men, and his wife Anita died on the retreat (August 1849).
[hist-eur1-s4.6.7] Venice held out until August 1849 (22-24 August), then surrendered after siege, cholera and bombardment.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.b] The failure of 1848-49 matters because it fixed the lessons that later nationalists acted on. It pushed moderates away from Mazzini's way and towards Piedmont.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.0] Austria's army was the main reason for defeat, because Radetzky's forces came back from the Quadrilateral and won at Custoza and Novara.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.1] Nationalists were divided between republicans and monarchists, and between different states, which means there was no single leader or plan.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.2] The Pope's withdrawal from the war (April 1848) broke the neo-Guelph hope because it showed a Pope would not fight Catholic Austria, and no foreign ally helped Italy.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.3] The lesson was that Italy could not do it alone, as Charles Albert had claimed, so foreign help was needed.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.4] Piedmont kept its constitution and its flag after 1849, so it became the hope of moderates and the leader of the national cause.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.b] After 1849 Piedmont was the one Italian state that kept its constitution, and under Cavour it became the most advanced state in Italy. Cavour then used the Crimean War to turn Piedmont into a player in European diplomacy.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.0] The Statuto Albertino (1848) was kept after 1849, which is why liberals trusted Piedmont. It gave a king, a nominated senate and an elected chamber with a narrow property franchise, and Piedmont kept the Italian tricolour. It was the only constitutional state in Italy after 1849, a refuge for exiles.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.1] The Siccardi Laws (1850), named after the justice minister Giuseppe Siccardi, abolished separate church courts and the right of asylum in churches. This caused conflict with the Papacy.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.2] Count Camillo Benso di Cavour, born in Turin in 1810, was a landowner and journalist who founded the newspaper Il Risorgimento (1847). He was minister of agriculture and commerce (1850), then of finance (1851), and admired Britain's economy and parliament.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.3] He became Prime Minister on 4 November 1852 through the connubio ('marriage'), an alliance with Urbano Rattazzi's centre-left.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.4] He modernised Piedmont with free trade treaties with Britain, France and other states, railway building, banking and credit, agricultural improvement and a reformed army. Its railway network became the largest of any Italian state by 1859, making Piedmont the most advanced Italian state.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.5] Piedmont joined the Crimean War (Britain and France against Russia, 1853-56) in January 1855. About 15,000 troops under Alfonso La Marmora fought at the Chernaya (August 1855), which won Piedmont a seat at the Congress of Paris (February-April 1856).
[hist-eur1-s4.8.6] At Paris Cavour raised the Italian question and the problem of Austria's presence in Italy before the Great Powers. Austria's unfriendly neutrality in the war had cost it Russia's friendship, so Austria was more isolated.
[hist-eur1-s4.9.b] Cavour knew Piedmont could not beat Austria alone, as 1848-49 had shown. He turned to Napoleon III, who wanted to undo the 1815 settlement.
[hist-eur1-s4.9.0] The Italian National Society (1857) was founded by Daniele Manin, Giorgio Pallavicino and Giuseppe La Farina. Garibaldi joined as vice-president. It backed unity under Victor Emmanuel and Piedmont instead of Mazzini's republic, and former republicans such as Manin and Garibaldi supported it.
[hist-eur1-s4.9.1] Napoleon III, Emperor of the French from 1852, wanted to undo the 1815 settlement and posed as a friend of nationalities. He also needed Catholic support at home, so he protected the Pope.
[hist-eur1-s4.9.2] Felice Orsini's bombs in Paris (14 January 1858) aimed at Napoleon III. Orsini was executed after writing to the Emperor urging him to free Italy, which pushed Napoleon III towards action.
[hist-eur1-s4.9.3] At Plombieres (20-21 July 1858) Cavour and Napoleon III secretly agreed that France would fight Austria if Austria appeared the aggressor. Piedmont would get a kingdom of Upper Italy, and France would get Savoy and, less firmly, Nice.
[hist-eur1-s4.9.4] Princess Clotilde, Victor Emmanuel's daughter, was to marry Prince Napoleon (Jerome), Napoleon III's cousin. The marriage, which sealed the alliance, took place in January 1859, and a formal Franco-Piedmontese treaty that month included Savoy and Nice.
[hist-eur1-s4.9.5] The price was the cession of Nice and Savoy to France, which angered nationalists, above all Garibaldi, because he was born in Nice.
[hist-eur1-s4.10.b] Cavour's plan worked at first: France fought beside Piedmont and Austria lost Lombardy. Napoleon III then stopped the war short of the goal, and central Italy took matters into its own hands.
[hist-eur1-s4.10.0] Piedmont mobilised, and Austria issued an ultimatum on 23 April 1859 and began war on 29 April, so Austria appeared the aggressor as the Plombieres deal required. France joined Piedmont, and Garibaldi led volunteers called the Cacciatori delle Alpi ('Alpine Hunters').
[hist-eur1-s4.10.1] The allies won at Magenta (4 June 1859) and at Solferino (with San Martino, 24 June 1859). Napoleon III and Victor Emmanuel II entered Milan after Magenta.
[hist-eur1-s4.10.2] Napoleon III stopped the war because of heavy losses, Prussian mobilisation on the Rhine, Catholic anger in France and fear of a too-strong Piedmont.
[hist-eur1-s4.10.3] He agreed the Villafranca armistice with Franz Joseph on 11 July 1859 without Cavour, who resigned. Lombardy went to Piedmont but Venetia stayed Austrian.
[hist-eur1-s4.10.4] The Treaty of Zurich (10 November 1859) confirmed this: Lombardy was ceded through France to Piedmont.
[hist-eur1-s4.10.5] In central Italy the rulers of Tuscany, Modena and Parma fled and the Romagna rose against the Pope. Cavour returned as Prime Minister in January 1860, and plebiscites on 11-12 March 1860 in Tuscany and Emilia (Modena, Parma, the Romagna) voted to join Piedmont.
[hist-eur1-s4.10.6] The Treaty of Turin (24 March 1860) ceded Nice and Savoy to France in return for Napoleon III's acceptance, and plebiscites in Nice and Savoy followed in April 1860.
[hist-eur1-s4.11.b] In 1860 an adventure that Cavour neither stopped nor openly backed turned the unification of the north into the unification of most of Italy. Garibaldi supplied the military daring that diplomacy could not.
[hist-eur1-s4.11.0] Garibaldi was born in Nice on 4 July 1807 and was a Mazzinian, condemned to death in his absence after the 1834 plot. He spent about 1836-48 in South America, in Brazil and Uruguay, where his Italian Legion wore red shirts, and he defended the Roman Republic in 1849.
[hist-eur1-s4.11.1] After a rising in Sicily the Thousand (about 1,000 men) sailed from Quarto near Genoa on the night of 5-6 May 1860. Cavour neither stopped them nor openly backed them.
[hist-eur1-s4.11.2] They landed at Marsala on 11 May 1860. Garibaldi proclaimed himself dictator in Victor Emmanuel's name at Salemi, won at Calatafimi (15 May) and took Palermo at the end of May, with fighting from 27 May. He then won at Milazzo in July.
[hist-eur1-s4.11.3] Garibaldi crossed to Calabria in August, and entered Naples on 7 September 1860 as Francis II withdrew to Gaeta. The battle of the Volturno followed on 1-2 October 1860.
[hist-eur1-s4.11.4] Cavour feared a march on Rome and French or Austrian intervention, so he sent the Piedmontese army through the Papal Marches and Umbria, avoiding Rome itself, and it defeated the papal army at Castelfidardo (18 September 1860).
[hist-eur1-s4.11.5] At Teano (26 October 1860) Garibaldi handed his conquests to Victor Emmanuel II and went to his island home of Caprera. Plebiscites approved annexation in Naples and Sicily (21 October) and the Marches and Umbria (November).
[hist-eur1-s4.11.6] The first Italian parliament in Turin proclaimed the Kingdom of Italy on 17 March 1861, with Victor Emmanuel II as king (to 1878) and Turin as capital. Cavour died on 6 June 1861, aged 50, and his successors struggled to resolve the southern question.
[hist-eur1-s4.11.7] Garibaldi's slogan was 'Rome or death' (Roma o morte). Italian troops stopped him at Aspromonte on 29 August 1862, where he was wounded. French and papal troops defeated him at Mentana on 3 November 1867.
[hist-eur1-s4.12.b] Two areas remained outside the new kingdom: Venetia under Austria and Rome under the Pope. Venetia came in 1866 because Prussia defeated Austria, and Rome in 1870 after the French garrison left and Italian troops entered the city.
[hist-eur1-s4.12.0] The September Convention (15 September 1864) had France promise to withdraw from Rome, and Italy moved its capital to Florence in 1865.
[hist-eur1-s4.12.1] Italy allied with Prussia on 8 April 1866. Italy lost on land at Custoza (24 June 1866) and at sea at Lissa (20 July 1866), but Prussia beat Austria at Koniggratz (Sadowa) on 3 July 1866 and this forced Austria to give up Venetia.
[hist-eur1-s4.12.2] Venetia was handed through Napoleon III to Italy by the Treaty of Vienna (3 October 1866), and a plebiscite in October 1866 approved it.
[hist-eur1-s4.12.3] French troops left Rome in August 1870 on the outbreak of the Franco-Prussian War, so the Pope lost his protector.
[hist-eur1-s4.12.4] After Sedan (1-2 September 1870), the capture of Rome followed: Italian troops entered through a breach at the Porta Pia on 20 September 1870, and a plebiscite on 2 October 1870 approved annexation.
[hist-eur1-s4.12.5] Rome became the capital in 1871 (July), the third capital after Turin and then Florence. The Law of Guarantees (13 May 1871) offered the Pope privileges, but Pius IX rejected it and called himself a prisoner of the Vatican.
[hist-eur1-s4.12.6] Pius IX had declared papal infallibility at the Vatican Council in July 1870, so his hostility to the new state was strong, and this kept many Catholics apart from it.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.b] A good answer pairs each obstacle with the way it was removed. Early attempts met all the obstacles with none of the answers, while after 1852 the answers arrived together.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.0] Obstacle: Austria's military power and its hold on Lombardy-Venetia and the Quadrilateral until 1866. The war of 1859 with France removed Austria from Lombardy only, and the Prussian victory in 1866 that gave Italy Venetia ended the obstacle.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.1] Obstacle: the Papacy and its French protectors until 1870. It was overcome when the French garrison left Rome in August 1870 because of the Franco-Prussian War.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.2] Obstacle: regionalism, local loyalties, no common spoken language, illiteracy and peasant indifference. It was overcome in part by plebiscites and by the enthusiasm of 1860, though the problem remained after 1861.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.3] Obstacle: divisions among nationalists (republicans against monarchists, unitary against federal) and reliance on small conspiracies. It was overcome when the National Society brought many former republicans behind Piedmont, ending the 1848 divisions.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.4] Success factor: Piedmont was a strong leading state, constitutional, modernised and with a regular army, so there was now a real centre for the movement.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.5] Success factor: Cavour's diplomacy (the Crimean War, Paris 1856, Plombieres) isolated Austria and won French help. Foreign help also came from France in 1859, Prussia in 1866 and 1870, Britain's goodwill and Austria's isolation after the Crimean War.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.6] Success factor: Garibaldi's volunteers and popular enthusiasm in 1860 moved faster than a regular army could, for example through Sicily and Naples in a few months.
[hist-eur1-s4.13.7] The roles compared: Mazzini gave the idea and inspiration, Cavour the diplomacy and the Piedmontese state, Garibaldi the military daring and Victor Emmanuel II the monarchy all could accept. A textbook summary of this is the soul, the brain and the sword.
[hist-eur1-s4.14.b] The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in 1861, but it started with serious weaknesses. Ordinary Level questions often ask about these problems and not only about how unification happened.
[hist-eur1-s4.14.0] The franchise was narrow: only about 2 per cent of the population could vote in 1861, so the state rested on a tiny political class.
[hist-eur1-s4.14.1] 'Piedmontisation' meant that Piedmont's constitution, laws, officials and taxes were extended to the whole kingdom. It was a centralised state, not a federation.
[hist-eur1-s4.14.2] The south gained little for peasants. The army fought brigandage in the south for several years after 1861 (roughly 1861-65), and the Pica Law (1863) allowed military courts. This is the southern question.
[hist-eur1-s4.14.3] About three-quarters of Italians were illiterate at the 1861 census. Estimates of how many spoke standard Italian range from about 2.5 to about 10 per cent.
[hist-eur1-s4.14.4] The Pope's hostility kept many Catholics apart from the new state, as Pius IX rejected the Law of Guarantees.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.b] Examiners reward paired judgements: what was achieved against what was left unfinished, and how far each leader's vision won. Name a historian to add weight.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.0] Achievement: by 1871 the peninsula was united under one constitutional monarchy with Rome as its capital, and Austria had been expelled.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.1] Limit: unity came largely through French and Prussian victories and Piedmontese expansion, not a popular national rising, and the state was narrow, centralised and poorly integrated.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.2] Achievement for Mazzini: the idea of a united Italy, which he had kept alive, won. Limit: his republican, popular Italy did not, and he was left disappointed.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.3] Limit: the new kingdom stayed mostly agricultural and the south poor. What was extended was Piedmont's constitution, laws and administration, not a modern economy.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.4] Denis Mack Smith (Cavour and Garibaldi 1860, 1954) saw Cavour and Garibaldi as rivals as well as partners, and thought Cavour's aims in 1860 were limited.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.5] Antonio Gramsci (Prison Notebooks, written 1929-35) called the Risorgimento a 'passive revolution' led by moderates that left the peasants out.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.6] Lucy Riall (Garibaldi: Invention of a Hero, 2007) argued that the Garibaldi legend was deliberately created through print and images. Older nationalist and liberal histories saw a heroic, almost inevitable rise of the nation.
[hist-eur1-s4.15.7] Compared with Germany, Italy was also led by one strong kingdom using war and diplomacy, but it relied more on foreign help, ended weaker and less united, and was a constitutional monarchy rather than a Prussian-dominated empire.
