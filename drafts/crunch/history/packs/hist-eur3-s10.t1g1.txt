[hist-eur3-s3.1.8] Dachau, opened near Munich in March 1933, was the first concentration camp, and others such as Sachsenhausen (1936) and Buchenwald (1937) followed.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.9] Political opponents, trade unionists, Social Democrats and members of religious groups were the first people held in the camps.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.10] Courts were restricted by the Special Courts and the people's court, which handed down political verdicts with little legal protection for defendants.
[hist-eur3-s3.1.11] The Gestapo and the SS were given powers to detain people without trial, which the regime described as protective custody.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.b] Nazi economic policy reduced unemployment through public works and rearmament, and prepared the economy for war.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.0] Hjalmar Schacht, appointed president of the Reichsbank in 1933 and Economics Minister in 1934, financed rearmament through Mefo bills, which concealed military spending.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.1] Public works, including autobahns (the first section opened in 1935), housing and military construction, reduced unemployment, which fell sharply between 1933 and 1936.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.2] The Reich Labour Service became compulsory for young men in June 1935, and conscription was reintroduced in March 1935.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.3] The New Plan of 1934 controlled foreign trade through bilateral agreements and exchange controls, which gave priority to imports needed for rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.2.4] The Four Year Plan of September 1936, under Hermann Göring, aimed to make Germany ready for war within four years and to achieve economic self-sufficiency (autarky).
[hist-eur3-s3.2.5] Schacht resigned as Economics Minister in 1937 after disagreements with Göring over the pace and cost of rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.b] Hitler's first foreign moves rejected the Versailles restrictions and gained international acceptance of rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.0] On 14 October 1933 Germany left the League of Nations and the Geneva Disarmament Conference, citing unequal treatment.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.1] The Saar territory, governed by the League since 1920, returned to Germany after a plebiscite in January 1935 in which over 90 per cent voted for reunion.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.2] On 16 March 1935 Hitler announced the reintroduction of conscription and an air force, in breach of the Treaty of Versailles.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.3] The Stresa Front of Britain, France and Italy condemned the move in April 1935, but took no effective action against Germany.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.4] The Anglo-German Naval Agreement of June 1935 allowed Germany a navy about 35 per cent the size of the British navy, and it was accepted by Britain without consulting France.
[hist-eur3-s3.3.5] The agreement gave Hitler the diplomatic recognition that he had sought, and it encouraged him to test the western powers further.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.b] Between 1936 and 1939 Hitler took territory by threat and by diplomacy, and each success made the western powers more reluctant to resist him.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.0] On 7 March 1936 German troops reoccupied the demilitarised Rhineland, and the Locarno Treaties were breached without military resistance from Britain or France.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.1] The Anschluss, the annexation of Austria, took place on 12 March 1938 after German pressure forced Austrian Chancellor Kurt Schuschnigg to cancel a plebiscite on independence and resign, and German troops entered Austria.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.2] Hitler's demands for the German-speaking Sudetenland in Czechoslovakia led to the Munich Agreement of 29 to 30 September 1938, which Britain and France signed with Germany and Italy.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.3] Czechoslovakia was not consulted at Munich, and Britain's Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain described the agreement as securing "peace for our time".
[hist-eur3-s3.4.4] German troops entered Prague on 15 March 1939, and Bohemia and Moravia became a German protectorate while Slovakia became a nominally independent client state.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.5] Britain gave a guarantee to Poland on 31 March 1939, followed by France, but Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939, with a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe between them.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.6] Konrad Henlein's Sudeten German Party, funded from Berlin, demanded autonomy for the three million Sudeten Germans, giving Hitler a pretext to threaten Czechoslovakia in 1938.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.7] Chamberlain flew to meet Hitler at Berchtesgaden on 15 September and again at Bad Godesberg on 22 September, where Hitler raised his demands; the Munich conference followed on 29 September.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.8] The Munich Agreement required Czechoslovakia to hand over the Sudetenland to Germany between 1 and 10 October 1938; the Soviet Union, which had a treaty with Czechoslovakia, was not invited.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.9] Hitler's occupation of Prague in March 1939 broke the Munich promises and ended the belief that his claims were limited to Germans, which led Britain to abandon appeasement and guarantee Poland.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.10] The Nazi-Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939 removed the threat of a war on two fronts; Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and Britain and France declared war on 3 September.
[hist-eur3-s3.4.11] Earlier steps included the German-Polish non-aggression pact of January 1934, a failed Austrian Nazi coup in July 1934 in which Chancellor Dollfuss was murdered, and the German Condor Legion's intervention for Franco in the Spanish Civil War from 1936.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.b] Hitler's foreign policy was driven by racial ideology and the goal of Lebensraum in the east, which the intentionalist school of historians sees as a plan for war from the start.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.0] In Mein Kampf Hitler argued that Germany needed Lebensraum (living space), to be won in Eastern Europe and settled by a racially pure population, with the Slavs to be subjugated.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.1] The idea of a herrenvolk, a master race of Germanic people, underpinned both domestic policy and plans for the conquest and resettlement of Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.2] At a meeting of 5 November 1937, later recorded in the Hossbach Memorandum, Hitler told his military and foreign policy chiefs that Germany's space problem could be solved only by force, probably around 1943 to 1945.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.3] Appeasement was British and French policy to satisfy Germany's grievances through concessions, based on the belief that Versailles had been unfair and that war must be avoided.
[hist-eur3-s3.5.4] Historians disagree about whether Hitler planned a general war from 1933 (the intentionalist view) or responded to opportunities and his own followers' initiatives (the functionalist view).
[hist-eur3-s3.5.5] A.J.P. Taylor's 1961 book argued that Hitler's aims were opportunist and similar to those of earlier German leaders, a view that was widely challenged by later historians.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.b] Hitler's rule from 1933 to 1939 combined a consolidated dictatorship at home with a foreign policy of rearmament, expansion and war.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.0] As Chancellor and then Führer, he set the direction of policy, while rival agencies competed for influence, a structure historians call polycracy.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.1] He was a participant in the foreign policy decisions of 1933 to 1939, making the key choices about withdrawal from the League, rearmament, the Anschluss and Czechoslovakia.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.2] Public opinion in Germany responded enthusiastically to his foreign successes, especially the Saar, the Rhineland and the Anschluss, which made his popularity high by 1938.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.3] Foreign contemporaries were divided: some British politicians saw him as a revisionist who could be satisfied, while others, such as Winston Churchill, warned from the 1930s that he was a threat.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.4] Later historians discuss how far he controlled events and how far his policies were shaped by pressure from the military, the party and the economy.
[hist-eur3-s3.6.5] His foreign policy and the deaths of Jews, opponents and others in the camps are judged together by historians as the central crimes of his regime, a matter taken up in the notes on the Holocaust.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.b] The regime aimed to unite Germans of 'pure' race in a national community under party control, and it reshaped work, education and family life to do so.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.0] The German Labour Front under Robert Ley replaced the free unions; its Strength Through Joy (Kraft durch Freude) section organised cheap holidays, cruises and theatre trips, and its Beauty of Labour section improved factory conditions.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.1] The Volkswagen savings scheme, in which workers paid weekly instalments towards a Strength Through Joy car, never delivered cars to the savers, because the factory was turned over to military production.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.2] The Hitler Youth, led by Baldur von Schirach, became the only legal youth organisation under the Hitler Youth Law of December 1936, with boys in the Jungvolk and Hitler Youth and girls in the League of German Girls (BDM).
[hist-eur3-s3.7.3] Schools taught racial science and nationalist history, textbooks were rewritten, and teachers were pressed to join the National Socialist Teachers' League.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.4] Nazi ideology assigned women the roles of wife and mother (often summed up as Kinder, Küche, Kirche); marriage loans from 1933 were reduced by a quarter for each child born, and the Mother's Cross was awarded from 1938 to women with large families.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.5] Women were pushed out of the civil service and professions such as law and medicine in 1933, although rearmament and labour shortages brought many back into work by the late 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.6] Opponents, 'asocials' and people judged racially or biologically inferior were excluded from the community: Jews by the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, and disabled people by forced sterilisation under the law of July 1933.
[hist-eur3-s3.7.7] Unemployment, officially about six million in early 1933, fell below one million by 1937 and gave way to labour shortages, although the figures excluded Jews and many women, and wages were held down.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.b] The war gave the regime radical new powers; the course of the fighting is covered in the notes on the Second World War.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.0] Albert Speer, Hitler's architect, became Minister for Armaments in February 1942 and raised output of weapons by rationalising production, even as Allied bombing increased.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.1] After the defeat at Stalingrad, Goebbels called for total war in his Sportpalast speech in Berlin on 18 February 1943, leading to the closure of non-essential businesses and the conscription of women up to the age of 45 into war work.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.2] Millions of forced labourers from occupied Europe, including prisoners of war and concentration camp inmates, worked in German factories and farms.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.3] On 20 July 1944 Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg planted a bomb at Hitler's headquarters at Rastenburg (the Wolf's Lair), but Hitler survived and the plot, which involved army officers and conservative civilians, was crushed; thousands were arrested and several hundred executed.
[hist-eur3-s3.8.4] Hitler remained in command to the end, committing suicide in Berlin on 30 April 1945.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.b] Judgements of Hitler's foreign policy divide between seeing him as the driving force of a deliberate war and seeing the war as the product of wider forces and of appeasement.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.0] Achievement: by 1939 Hitler had reversed the main Versailles restrictions, rebuilt the army and expanded German territory without a major war.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.1] Limit: the expansion depended on the restraint of the western powers, which many German generals feared would fight, and the economy was strained by rearmament.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.2] Intentionalist view: Hitler's Mein Kampf aims, the Hossbach Memorandum and the invasion of Poland show a deliberate plan for war.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.3] Functionalist view: the policies of the Nazi state, the competition between agencies and the choices of Britain and France shaped how and when the war began.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.4] Contemporary view: appeasement was supported by many British people who wanted to avoid another war, and Munich was welcomed at first as a rescue of peace.
[hist-eur3-s3.9.5] Later view: Munich is generally judged a failure, because it removed Czechoslovakia's defences and left Hitler convinced that the western powers would not fight.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s6: 6. France: the Third Republic, 1920-1940, and the Vichy state
[hist-eur3-s6.0.b] France emerged from the First World War victorious but exhausted, and its governments were dominated by parties that demanded firm treatment of Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.0] The Third Republic, founded in 1870, was governed by an elected Chamber of Deputies and a President with limited powers.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.1] The November 1919 election returned the Bloc National, a coalition of conservative, centre and Catholic parties, which governed until 1924.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.2] France had lost about 1.4 million dead in the war, and many villages and farms in the north-east were devastated by fighting.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.3] Reconstruction of the devastated areas was financed by German reparations, which the government expected Germany to pay in full.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.4] Raymond Poincaré, Prime Minister in 1922 to 1924, pursued a hard line towards Germany and ordered the occupation of the Ruhr in January 1923.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.5] The Ruhr occupation damaged French relations with Britain and the United States, which opposed it, and it ended in 1925 without securing full reparations.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.6] The Cartel des Gauches, a coalition of Radicals and Socialists, won the May 1924 election and Édouard Herriot formed the first government of the Cartel.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.7] The Cartel fell in April 1925 after a run on the franc and a financial crisis, and governments changed frequently in the following years.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.8] Poincaré returned as Prime Minister in July 1926 with a national unity government and stabilised the franc in practice by the end of 1926 through tax increases and cuts in spending, and the Poincaré franc was fixed in law in 1928 at about a fifth of its pre-war value.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.9] The Radical Party, a centre-left party with strong rural support, was the largest force in most governments of the 1920s and early 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.10] The Chamber of Deputies held frequent votes of no confidence, and the government's ministers were often replaced after only a few months in office.
[hist-eur3-s6.0.11] The constitution gave the President a largely ceremonial role, so policy was decided by the Prime Minister and the parliamentary majority.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.b] France's economy was less affected by the Depression at first than Britain or Germany, but the slump arrived later and was prolonged by deflationary policy.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.0] The French economy was built on small family farms and small businesses, which cushioned the country against the immediate effects of the Wall Street Crash.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.1] Industrial production fell sharply from 1931, and unemployment rose more slowly than in Germany or Britain but remained a serious problem.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.2] Governments kept the franc at its gold value until 1936, so deflation was used to defend the currency and lower prices, which hurt industry and farming.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.3] The franc was devalued by the Popular Front government in October 1936, after which exports recovered slightly.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.4] Heavy spending on the Maginot Line, a chain of fortifications along the German border built between 1930 and 1938, was one of the largest state investments of the period.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.5] Alexandre Stavisky, a financier with political protection, was found shot dead in January 1934 after the collapse of his fraudulent bond sales; the police called it suicide but the left and right both suspected that he had been silenced.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.6] The scandal forced the resignation of the Radical Prime Minister Camille Chautemps in January 1934, and right-wing leagues used it to attack the republic as corrupt.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.7] On 6 February 1934 the leagues, including the Croix-de-Feu, Action française and the Solidarité française, marched on the Chamber of Deputies, and police opened fire; about fifteen people were killed and over a thousand injured.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.8] The Daladier government resigned after the riots, and a national government under Gaston Doumergue was formed to restore order.
[hist-eur3-s6.1.9] The left responded with a general strike and mass demonstration on 12 February 1934, and in July 1935 Socialists, Communists and Radicals united in the Popular Front against the threat of fascism, after the Comintern had reversed the Communists' hostility to other left parties.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.b] The Popular Front won the 1936 election and introduced major social reforms, but its economic policies failed and it split over Spain.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.0] In the elections of April and May 1936 the Popular Front coalition won a majority, and Léon Blum, a Socialist and the first Jew to hold the post, became Prime Minister in June 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.1] The Matignon Agreements of June 1936, negotiated between employers and unions under Blum's chairmanship, gave wage rises of about 7 to 15 per cent, union recognition, collective bargaining and elected workers' delegates, following a wave of sit-down strikes; laws that month brought in the 40-hour week and two weeks of paid holiday.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.2] Around two million workers took part in strikes and factory occupations in May and June 1936, and the agreement was designed to end them.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.3] The Popular Front's programme of nationalising armaments and the railways, and of spending on public works, was limited by financial pressure and capital flight.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.4] Blum's government adopted a policy of non-intervention in the Spanish Civil War in August 1936, which was opposed by many on the left but supported by Britain, and it dissolved the far-right leagues in June 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.5] Blum paused the reforms in February 1937 and resigned in June 1937 when the Senate refused him emergency financial powers; after a brief second ministry in March and April 1938, Édouard Daladier became Prime Minister and the coalition ended, and in November 1938 his decrees undid the 40-hour week.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.6] The Croix-de-Feu, led by Colonel François de la Rocque, was a veterans' league that attracted hundreds of thousands of members, and it became the Parti Social Français after it was dissolved in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.7] Action française, led by Charles Maurras, was a monarchist and anti-Semitic movement which opposed the republic from before 1914.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.8] Historians disagree on how far the leagues threatened democracy: René Rémond saw them as part of France's traditional right, while Zeev Sternhell argued that fascist ideas had deep roots in France.
[hist-eur3-s6.2.9] Economic hardship and fear of war also split French opinion into left and right camps, and Communists and Socialists gained from the unity forged after February 1934.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.b] France declared war on Germany in September 1939, but the defeat of its army in June 1940 ended the Third Republic and brought the Vichy state into being.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.0] Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939 after the invasion of Poland, and the French army, commanded by General Maurice Gamelin, remained largely inactive during the Phoney War.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.1] The German invasion of France and the Low Countries began on 10 May 1940, and German armoured divisions broke through the Ardennes to reach the Channel coast by late May.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.2] The British Expeditionary Force and many French troops were evacuated from Dunkirk between 26 May and 4 June 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.3] Paris, declared an open city, was occupied on 14 June 1940; the government left for Bordeaux, Paul Reynaud resigned on 16 June, and Pétain became head of government and asked Germany for an armistice, before the government settled in the spa town of Vichy in July.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.4] On 22 June 1940 an armistice was signed at Compiègne in the same railway carriage used for the 1918 armistice, dividing France into an occupied zone and an unoccupied zone.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.5] On 10 July 1940 the National Assembly, the Chamber and Senate meeting together at Vichy, voted by 569 to 80, with about 20 abstentions, to give full powers to Marshal Philippe Pétain to draw up a new constitution, which ended the Third Republic.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.6] The Maginot Line, named for the War Minister André Maginot, guarded the Franco-German border with deep forts, but it did not extend along the Belgian frontier or through the Ardennes.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.7] French generals including Gamelin and Pétain believed that the Ardennes forest was difficult terrain for tanks, and the main French and British forces moved into Belgium in May 1940 on the Dyle plan.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.8] France had as many tanks as Germany, but it spread them among infantry units instead of concentrating them in armoured divisions as Germany did.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.9] German armoured units crossed the Meuse at Sedan on 13 and 14 May 1940 and reached the Channel coast by 20 May, cutting off the northern armies.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.10] Contemporary explanations blamed political decadence under the Third Republic, but historians such as Ernest May stress poor intelligence, slow command and German skill rather than moral failure.
[hist-eur3-s6.3.11] France lost about 90,000 killed in six weeks and well over a million men were taken prisoner.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.b] Pétain's regime rejected republican and secular values in favour of authoritarian conservatism, which it called the National Revolution.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.0] The regime renamed the state the État français and replaced the republican motto of Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité with Travail, Famille, Patrie (Work, Family, Fatherland).
[hist-eur3-s6.4.1] Pétain's National Revolution blamed the Third Republic and the left for the defeat, and promoted traditional values, the Catholic Church and rural life.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.2] Pierre Laval, dismissed in December 1940, returned as head of government in April 1942 and pursued a policy of close collaboration with Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.3] Pétain met Hitler at Montoire in October 1940 and announced that he had entered a path of collaboration with Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.4] The Vichy regime was an authoritarian state that relied on a veterans' organisation, the Legion of French Combatants, rather than a mass party, and it ended elections, banned strikes and dissolved the trade unions in favour of a Labour Charter in 1941.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.5] Vichy's own press and radio supported the National Revolution, and its youth organisations aimed to instil discipline and loyalty to Pétain.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.6] Under the armistice Germany occupied the north and the Atlantic coast, while the south stayed 'free' under Vichy until German troops occupied it in November 1942, after the Allied landings in North Africa.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.7] France had to pay the costs of the German occupation, set at 400 million francs a day, and its food and industrial output were directed to Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.8] The British attack on the French fleet at Mers-el-Kébir on 3 July 1940 turned many Vichy supporters against Britain.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.9] Food rationing, shortages and a black market affected daily life, and about 1.5 million French prisoners of war remained in Germany for years.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.10] Vichy's sense of 'neutral' independence ended in 1942, as Laval's return led to closer co-operation, and the Germans occupied the whole country.
[hist-eur3-s6.4.11] Collaboration took forms from economic cooperation and propaganda to active fascist parties such as the Parti Populaire Français of Jacques Doriot and the Rassemblement National Populaire of Marcel Déat.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.b] The Vichy government took active steps against Jews, political opponents and resisters, and created a paramilitary force that fought against the Resistance.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.0] Vichy passed the first anti-Jewish statute in October 1940, without German demand, excluding Jews from public office and many professions, and in 1942 it agreed to hand over foreign Jews in the unoccupied zone for deportation; about 76,000 Jews were deported from France and about 2,500 survived.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.1] On 16 and 17 July 1942 French police arrested about 13,000 Jews in Paris, including about 4,000 children, and many were held at the Vélodrome d'Hiver cycling stadium before being sent via the Drancy camp to Auschwitz.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.2] The Milice, a paramilitary force founded in January 1943, hunted members of the Resistance and carried out reprisals against civilians.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.3] The Service du Travail Obligatoire (compulsory labour service), introduced in February 1943 after a voluntary scheme had failed, sent young French men to work in Germany, and many went into hiding in the maquis to avoid it.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.4] Vichy's police and administration carried out many of the arrests and deportations, and collaboration extended to the economy, with supplies sent to Germany.
[hist-eur3-s6.5.5] Historians since Robert Paxton's Vichy France of 1972 have argued that Vichy's collaboration was active and not merely a shield against harsher German policy.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.b] Resistance to Vichy and the German occupation grew from a small group in London to an organised movement inside France by 1943.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.0] Charles de Gaulle, a brigadier-general who had been Under-Secretary for War in Reynaud's government in June 1940, broadcast an appeal from London on 18 June 1940 calling on the French to continue the fight.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.1] De Gaulle formed the Free French movement in London and was recognised by Britain, though his relations with Britain and the United States were often strained.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.2] Jean Moulin united the main resistance groups in 1943 and presided over the first meeting of the National Council of the Resistance in May 1943.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.3] Resistance activity included the production of underground newspapers, sabotage of railways, intelligence gathering and armed attacks on German troops by the maquis, groups of guerrillas in rural areas.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.4] The Resistance was divided among Gaullists, Communists and other political groups, and the Communist-led Francs-Tireurs et Partisans, formed in 1942, became one of the largest armed groups.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.5] Resistance groups carried out executions of collaborators and informers after the liberation, and these reprisals were a source of political tension.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.6] Early groups such as Combat, Libération and Franc-Tireur ran underground newspapers and helped Allied airmen to escape, and Communists joined the armed struggle after Germany invaded the USSR in June 1941.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.7] The maquis grew as young men avoided compulsory labour service in 1943 and 1944, and Resistance groups helped the Allied invasion by cutting railways.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.8] The Germans took hostages and shot them in reprisal for attacks, and on 10 June 1944 SS troops massacred more than 600 civilians at Oradour-sur-Glane.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.9] The village of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, where Protestant villagers led by Pastor André Trocmé sheltered thousands of Jewish refugees, became a symbol of civilian rescue.
[hist-eur3-s6.6.10] After the war the myth of a nation united in Resistance, promoted by de Gaulle, was challenged from the 1970s by historians and by the film The Sorrow and the Pity (1969-71), and in 1995 President Chirac acknowledged the state's responsibility for the Vél' d'Hiv round-up.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.b] Allied landings in Normandy and southern France in 1944 led to the liberation of France and the collapse of Vichy.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.0] The Allied invasion of Normandy began on 6 June 1944, and the Allies landed in southern France on 15 August 1944.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.1] Paris was liberated on 25 August 1944 after an uprising by the Resistance and the arrival of General Leclerc's Free French armoured division, and on 26 August de Gaulle led a procession down the Champs-Élysées as head of the Provisional Government.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.2] Pétain was taken to Germany in 1944 and was later tried in France, where he was sentenced to death in 1945; the sentence was commuted to life imprisonment by de Gaulle.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.3] Laval was tried and shot in October 1945 after being convicted of treason.
[hist-eur3-s6.7.4] An ordinance of April 1944 gave women the vote, first used in 1945, and the Fourth Republic was established after a new constitution was approved in 1946.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.b] Judgements of the Third Republic weigh its stability and democratic survival against its inability to deal with the Depression or to resist invasion.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.0] Achievement: the Third Republic survived the Depression and the leagues' challenge in 1934 without a coup, and its institutions held until 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.1] Limit: frequent government changes and the failure to modernise the army left France unprepared for the German attack of 1940.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.2] Achievement: the Popular Front brought paid holidays and shorter working hours to French workers in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.3] Limit: the Popular Front's economic policies failed to reduce unemployment, and it could not agree on how to respond to the Spanish Civil War.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.4] Contemporary view: many French conservatives in 1940 saw the armistice and Pétain's rule as a necessary response to military defeat and the failures of the republic.
[hist-eur3-s6.8.5] Later view: historians stress that Vichy was not forced into collaboration, and that its anti-Jewish policies went beyond German demands in some areas.
