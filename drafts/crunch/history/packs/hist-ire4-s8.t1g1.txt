[hist-ire4-s2.0.b] After the Famine emigration did not stop; it became a settled part of Irish rural life, with a steady outflow in good years and bad.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.0] About 4 million people left Ireland in the six decades after the Famine (estimates vary), and the population of the whole island fell from about 6.5 million in 1851 to about 4.4 million in 1911.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.1] The United States took about four out of five emigrants; Britain, Canada, Australia and New Zealand took most of the rest.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.2] The number of Irish-born people in the United States peaked at about 1.87 million in the census of 1890.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.3] Emigrants were mostly young and single, aged roughly 15 to 30, and Ireland was unusual in Europe in sending about as many women as men; in some decades women outnumbered men.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.4] Emigration was heaviest from the west and south-west, and the poorest counties of the west sent the highest proportion of their people.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.5] After the Famine farms were no longer subdivided: one son inherited the holding (impartible inheritance) and one daughter received a dowry, so the other children had to emigrate or stay unmarried.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.6] Late marriage, the shift from tillage to cattle grazing, and the lack of industry outside the north-east pushed young people out.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.7] Agricultural depression and evictions around 1879 to 1882 caused a fresh surge, especially from the west; the Land War was partly a response to the insecurity that fed it.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.b] The crossing and the farewell both changed in this period.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.0] The American wake was a farewell gathering with music, dancing, food and drink as well as tears, held on the night before an emigrant left, because most emigrants never came back.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.1] Steamships replaced sail from the 1860s, cutting the Atlantic crossing from about six weeks to about two weeks by the 1870s and making the voyage far safer.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.2] Liners called at Queenstown (Cobh) and at Moville near Derry to pick up emigrants travelling steerage.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.3] In New York emigrants landed at Castle Garden from 1855 and at Ellis Island from 1892; the first person processed at Ellis Island, on 1 January 1892, was Annie Moore from Cork.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.4] Colonial governments in Australia and New Zealand paid or subsidised passages to attract settlers, so Irish emigration there was largely assisted, while emigration to America was paid for privately, often with money sent from relatives.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.0] In the USA the Irish settled overwhelmingly in cities of the north-east and Midwest: New York, Boston, Philadelphia, Chicago and the mill and mining towns of New England and Pennsylvania.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.1] Irish men mostly began as unskilled labourers on docks, railways, canals and building sites and in mines and factories, and many moved up into skilled trades, police forces and fire departments.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.2] Irish women dominated domestic service in cities such as New York and Boston; the job gave board, lodging and wages that could be saved and sent home.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.3] Irish Americans moved into city politics through the Democratic Party, and by 1900 the second generation was entering teaching, clerical work and the professions.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.4] In Britain the Irish settled in Liverpool, Glasgow, London, Manchester and the industrial towns of Lancashire and west-central Scotland, working on the docks, in building, in mills and in mines.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.5] Irish nurses, teachers and domestic servants were prominent among women emigrants to Britain, and Irish harvest labourers continued to cross seasonally.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.6] In Australia Irish-born people and their descendants made up roughly a quarter of the European population, settling as farmers, labourers and gold-miners in New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.7] Emigrants also went to Canada, where many Protestants settled in Ontario, and to New Zealand, and some went to Argentina.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.0] Emigrants' remittances: money and pre-paid tickets sent home by emigrants, amounting to well over a million pounds a year in the later 19th century.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.1] Much of the money came from servant girls and labourers, who sent home a large share of their wages.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.2] Remittances paid for the passages of younger brothers and sisters, so one emigrant could bring out a whole family over a few years.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.3] Remittances also paid rent, shop debts and dowries and helped families keep their holdings, which made emigration a support for the rural economy as well as a drain on it.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.4] The American letter, often containing dollars, was a regular event in western households, and it kept relatives informed of jobs and wages abroad.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.5] Chain-emigration meant that new arrivals stayed with kin, found work through them, and settled in the same parishes, so emigrant communities reproduced the networks of home.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.6] Letters collected by historians such as David Fitzpatrick (Oceans of Consolation, 1994, on the Irish in Australia) show how strongly emigrants kept family ties.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.0] The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 limited immigrants of each nationality to 3 per cent of that nationality's foreign-born population in the 1910 US census.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.1] The Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed Act) set quotas at 2 per cent of each foreign-born group in the 1890 census, a base chosen to favour north-western Europeans over southern and eastern Europeans.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.2] The Irish Free State received a quota of 28,567 a year under the 1924 Act, cut to 17,853 from 1929 when the national-origins formula took effect; Northern Ireland came under the British quota.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.3] The quotas redirected rather than ended emigration: emigration to the USA fell sharply from the 1920s, and Britain became the main destination.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.4] The Great Depression after 1929 cut American demand for labour and reduced Irish emigration to the USA still further, and some emigrants returned.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.5] Britain was easy to enter, jobs were close and the fare was low, and the Irish moved to Britain in large numbers in the 1930s.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.6] The Free State governments did not try to restrict emigration, which they treated as an economic problem they could not quickly solve.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.0] During the Emergency (1939 to 1945) Irish people needed travel permits and identity documents to go to Britain, and both governments controlled who could travel.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.1] Britain recruited Irish workers for war factories, building and farm work, and well over 100,000 people from the Irish state went to work in Britain during the war; tens of thousands more joined the British forces.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.2] The Ireland Act of 1949 confirmed that Irish citizens in the United Kingdom were not treated as aliens and could live, work and vote there freely, and the Common Travel Area allowed free movement between the two islands.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.3] Irish people were also exempted from the controls of the Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.4] Post-war reconstruction created huge demand for labour in Britain: Irish men worked as navvies on roads, building sites and power stations, while Irish women trained as nurses in the new National Health Service (1948) or worked in factories and offices.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.5] The Irish in Britain clustered in London (Kilburn, Camden Town, Cricklewood), Birmingham, Coventry, Luton, Manchester and Liverpool.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.6] The US Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 abolished the national-origins quotas from 1968 and favoured family ties and skills, which made it harder for Irish people without American relatives to enter.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.0] The government set up the Commission on Emigration and Other Population Problems in 1948, and it reported in 1954.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.1] The Commission found that emigration was caused mainly by economic factors such as low incomes, unemployment and small farms, but also by the attractions of better living standards and a livelier social life abroad.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.2] It accepted that emigration could not be ended quickly and urged economic development and improved rural conditions rather than any ban on leaving.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.3] Emigration peaked in the 1950s: about 400,000 people left the state between 1951 and 1961 (net), most of them for Britain.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.4] The 1961 census recorded the lowest population in the history of the state, about 2.8 million.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.5] The Vanishing Irish (1953), a collection of essays edited by the American priest John A. O'Brien, warned that the Irish nation was dying out through emigration and late marriage.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.6] Economic Development (1958), drawn up by T.K. Whitaker, and the First Programme for Economic Expansion, pursued by Seán Lemass as Taoiseach from 1959, brought growth that cut emigration sharply in the 1960s.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.0] Many young emigrants arrived in Britain with no training and lived in lodging houses or navvy camps where hard drinking and long hours were common.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.1] Navvy work was often arranged through subcontractors who paid cash in hand, leaving workers without insurance or job security.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.2] Notices reading No Irish are widely remembered from lodging-house windows in the 1950s and 1960s, though historians disagree about how common they were.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.3] Catholic chaplaincies, the Legion of Mary and Irish centres in London and other cities helped new arrivals find lodgings and work.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.4] Irish dance halls such as the Galtymore in Cricklewood and the Gresham on the Holloway Road became centres of emigrant social life.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.5] Assimilation in the USA was helped by the English language, the Democratic Party, the Catholic parish and its schools, and the size of the Irish community; by 1914 the second generation increasingly thought of itself as American.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.6] In Britain the Irish were also English-speaking but remained a visible Catholic working-class minority, and Catholic schools and parishes helped to keep a distinct identity; Irish-born people could return home easily, so many saw their stay as temporary.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.7] Enda Delaney (The Irish in Post-War Britain, 2007) argued that Irish emigrants to Britain kept strong ties with home while integrating into British working life.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.8] Irish-speaking emigrants from the Gaeltacht tended to lose the language within a generation abroad.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.b] Domhnall (Dónall) Mac Amhlaigh (1926–1989) was an Irish-language writer whose diary of navvy life is the best-known first-hand account of 1950s emigration to Britain.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.0] Born in Galway in 1926 and raised in Co. Kilkenny, he was a native of the west who wrote in Irish.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.1] Facing poor prospects at home he went to England in 1951 and worked as a labourer on building sites and roads, based in Northampton.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.2] His diary of 1951 to 1957, Dialann Deoraí (An Exile's Diary), was published in 1960; Valentin Iremonger translated it as An Irish Navvy: The Diary of an Exile (1964).
[hist-ire4-s2.8.3] It describes the work, the lodgings, the pubs and dance halls, homesickness, the company of other Irish navvies and the mixture of welcome and prejudice the Irish met in England.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.4] He was a participant, not an observer: he lived the life he described and stayed in England for the rest of his life, dying in Northampton in 1989.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.5] He also wrote novels and short stories in Irish, including Schnitzer Ó Sé (1974), and journalism.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.6] Contemporaries in Ireland valued the diary as a frank picture of a life that official Ireland preferred not to discuss.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.7] Historians now treat it as a key source on the Irish in post-war Britain, showing a generation that worked, socialised and kept its identity in an Irish world inside English cities; as a single author's account it cannot stand for every emigrant's experience.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.0] Safety valve versus drain: emigration relieved rural poverty and unemployment and brought in remittances, but it took the young and kept the population falling until 1961.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.1] Opportunity versus exile: many emigrants gained better wages and lives, while others, as Mac Amhlaigh shows, felt themselves exiles doing the hardest work.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.2] US restriction redirected rather than stopped emigration: Britain's open door absorbed the flow after the 1930s.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.3] Contemporary view: churchmen and politicians spoke of the vanishing Irish and blamed modern attractions as well as poverty; the Commission of 1954 placed the cause in economic weakness.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.4] Later view: Kerby Miller stressed exile and banishment, while historians such as Enda Delaney see emigration to Britain as a choice by individuals within family strategies, not simply as forced exile.
