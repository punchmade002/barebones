CANDIDATES hist-ire4-s3: 3. The Irish in the USA and Britain: anti-Irish sentiment and political participation
[hist-ire4-s3.0.b] Famine-era emigrants were mostly poor, Catholic and unskilled, and they settled in the cities of the north-east USA and industrial Britain, where host communities saw them at close quarters. Hostility rested on religion, poverty and competition for work, and it was reinforced by stereotypes that were widely printed. This is discrimination: unfair treatment of a group in jobs, housing, politics or public life.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.0] Religion: most Irish emigrants were Catholic, and Protestant Americans and Britons often saw Catholicism as foreign and a threat to their institutions, because they believed the Pope was a rival authority to the state.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.1] Poverty and disease: the Irish who arrived in the Famine years were poor and crowded into slums, so outbreaks of cholera and typhus (diseases that spread easily in overcrowded, dirty housing) were blamed on them.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.2] Crime and drink: Irish migrants were blamed for crime, drunkenness and disorder in the cities where they lived, and they were over-represented in arrest figures, which seemed to confirm the prejudice.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.3] Competition for work: Irish labourers and servants competed with native-born and other workers, and their willingness to accept low wages created resentment among those who feared being undercut.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.4] Racial stereotypes: the Irish were portrayed as a separate and inferior race, and in cartoons they were often drawn with apelike features, which made hostility seem natural rather than prejudiced.
[hist-ire4-s3.0.5] Political fear: Protestants feared that Catholic voters would take orders from their priests, and that Irish nationalists would drag the host country into Irish quarrels.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.b] American nativism, the movement of native-born Protestants against immigrants and Catholic influence, was strongest in the 1840s and 1850s and went on in cartoons and job notices afterwards. In the USA it grew into a political party.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.0] The Philadelphia riots of 1844 (May and July) were anti-Catholic disturbances in which nativist mobs attacked Irish Catholic homes and churches, after a dispute over which Bible, the Protestant King James Bible or the Catholic one, should be used in public schools.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.1] Nativism became a political force, for example: the Know-Nothings, formally the American Party, won major state elections in 1854, including Massachusetts.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.2] The Know-Nothings took their name from the secrecy of their members, who answered questions about the movement with 'I know nothing'.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.3] They wanted longer residence before immigrants could become citizens and vote (a 21-year wait was a common demand) and wanted to keep Catholics and the foreign-born out of public office. The movement split over slavery in the mid-1850s and faded before the Civil War (1861).
[hist-ire4-s3.1.4] 'No Irish Need Apply' (NINA) was said to appear on job advertisements and signs in the 19th century. Richard Jensen argued in 2002 that written signs were rare and the memory exaggerated, while Rebecca Fried in 2015 found real newspaper advertisements, so historians disagree on how common they were.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.5] Thomas Nast, in cartoons in Harper's Weekly in the 1870s, drew Irish Catholics, and Irish politicians especially, as ape-like and corrupt, which spread the stereotype to a mass audience.
[hist-ire4-s3.1.6] Irish Americans responded by building their own parishes, schools, newspapers and societies, so they no longer depended on a hostile society, and by using the vote.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.b] In Britain anti-Irish feeling was mixed with sectarian rivalry (hostility between Protestants and Catholics), and it flared at moments of Fenian threat. The Fenians were an Irish republican movement active in Ireland, Britain and America in the 1860s.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.0] Punch magazine caricatured the Irish in the 1840s and 1860s, presenting them as simian (ape-like), violent and backward, so a respectable magazine helped to spread the stereotype.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.1] The Stockport riots of June 1852 saw Irish Catholic homes and churches attacked in Stockport, Cheshire, after a Catholic procession in the town.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.2] The Murphy riots of 1867 followed the anti-Catholic lectures of William Murphy and brought attacks on Irish districts in English towns, chiefly Birmingham and also Wolverhampton.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.3] In Liverpool and Glasgow, Protestant Orange lodges (societies of Protestants loyal to the Crown) and Catholic Irish communities clashed, so sectarian rivalry shaped local politics and street life.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.4] Three Fenians, the Manchester Martyrs, were executed on 23 November 1867. Three weeks later, on 13 December 1867, a Fenian bomb at Clerkenwell prison in London killed about a dozen people, and fears grew that all Irish people in Britain were a security threat.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.5] Fenian scares led to heavy policing of Irish districts, and Irish nationalists claimed that the state treated the whole community as suspect.
[hist-ire4-s3.2.6] Writers blamed the Irish for typhus, low wages and crime in industrial towns, and the Victorian press often presented them as a separate race.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.b] Irish Americans turned their numbers into political power through the Democratic Party and the city machines. To vote a migrant needed citizenship (naturalisation), and city government ran through patronage, meaning jobs and favours given in return for support, between two parties, Democrats and Republicans.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.0] Most Irish Americans voted Democratic, because the party defended immigrants against nativist attacks and offered them jobs and help.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.1] Ward politics: local party organisers in each ward (a district of a city) gave immigrants jobs, housing and legal help in return for votes, so the party became part of daily life.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.2] Tammany Hall, the Democratic organisation in New York City, controlled city politics through patronage, jobs and contracts. John Kelly led it from 1872, the first Irish boss, after the Tweed era.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.3] William R. Grace, born in Ireland, was elected mayor of New York in 1880, the first Irish-born Catholic mayor, and took office in 1881. Hugh O'Brien became mayor of Boston in 1885.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.4] John F. Fitzgerald ('Honey Fitz', mayor 1906 to 1908 and 1910 to 1914) and James Michael Curley (mayor four times, first term from 1914) dominated Boston politics in the early 20th century.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.5] Al Smith, a Catholic partly of Irish descent, was governor of New York in the 1920s and the Democratic presidential candidate in 1928, when he was defeated by Herbert Hoover.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.6] Irish men were prominent in urban police forces and fire companies, and these jobs were a route into local politics because city jobs were handed out through the party.
[hist-ire4-s3.3.7] Irish-American political success was built on group loyalty, so it gave immigrant communities a voice in city government.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.b] Richard Croker turned Tammany Hall into the most powerful political machine in New York City, and his career shows both the opportunity and the corruption of machine politics.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.0] Richard Croker was born in Co. Cork in 1843 and was taken to New York as a child, where he grew up in a poor Irish district.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.1] He rose, for example, through the Fourth Avenue gang, the fire companies and the local Democratic clubs, and held minor city offices such as alderman and coroner.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.2] He led Tammany Hall from the mid-1880s until 1902, using patronage, jobs in city departments and contracts to win support among immigrants and the poor.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.3] He controlled Democratic nominations and so could place allies in office, including Robert Van Wyck, mayor of New York from 1898 to 1901.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.4] The Lexow Committee (1894, a state inquiry into police corruption) and the Mazet Committee (1899, an inquiry into Tammany government) exposed the system. Tammany lost the mayoral election of 1901, and Croker left the leadership in 1902.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.5] He retired to Ireland, where he lived at Glencairn near Dublin and bred racehorses; his horse Orby won the Epsom Derby in 1907.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.6] Contemporary reformers and much of the respectable press condemned Croker's corruption, while many Tammany voters regarded him as a friend of the poor.
[hist-ire4-s3.4.7] Historians now present him as the clearest example of machine politics, in which immigrant political success came with corruption, though they also see his career as a route of upward mobility for an immigrant.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.b] Joseph P. Kennedy showed how an Irish American could rise to the top of business and then government, and his career was marked by controversy.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.0] Joseph P. Kennedy was born in Boston in 1888, the son of Patrick J. Kennedy, a Boston politician and businessman, and he married Rose Fitzgerald, daughter of Mayor John F. Fitzgerald.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.1] He built a fortune, for example, in banking, the stock market and the film industry before entering government.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.2] He was the first chairman of the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) from 1934, a body set up to regulate the stock market after the crash of 1929, and he chaired the US Maritime Commission in 1937.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.3] President Franklin D. Roosevelt appointed him ambassador to Britain, 1938 to 1940, widely described as the first Irish Catholic in the post.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.4] As ambassador he supported Neville Chamberlain's policy of appeasement (giving way to Germany to avoid war) and doubted that Britain could win a war with Germany. He resigned in late 1940 after an interview that was read as defeatist about Britain's chances.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.5] Contemporary Irish-American opinion was divided because he was both a success story and a conservative: some were proud of his success, while others distrusted his conservative views and his closeness to the British government.
[hist-ire4-s3.5.6] Historians judge him a self-made businessman and an appeaser whose pessimism ended his public career, and a father whose ambitions shaped his sons, including John F. Kennedy.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.b] Paul O'Dwyer combined a career as a New York lawyer with campaigns for civil rights and for Irish causes.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.0] Paul O'Dwyer was born in Bohola, Co. Mayo, in 1907 and emigrated to New York in 1925, becoming a lawyer there in the 1930s. He was the brother of William O'Dwyer, mayor of New York from 1946 to 1950.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.1] For example, he defended trade unionists and took on civil rights cases and causes for working people and immigrants, and he supported the civil rights movement.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.2] He was a Democratic politician who was the party's candidate for the US Senate in 1968, opposed to the Vietnam War, and lost to Jacob Javits. He was president of the New York City Council from 1974 to 1977, in the mid-1970s.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.3] He was active in Irish causes, and in later decades he supported civil rights for Catholics in Northern Ireland and criticised British policy there.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.4] Contemporaries saw him as a liberal and a critic of the establishment, and because of this some thought his views on Northern Ireland too sympathetic to the republican cause.
[hist-ire4-s3.6.5] Historians place him in the tradition of liberal Irish-American politics, in which civil rights and Irish nationalism were linked.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.b] Irish people in Britain were British subjects after the Act of Union (1801), so they needed no naturalisation and their political voice took a different form from American machines. It worked through Irish nationalist organisation and then through Labour.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.0] The Irish National League of Great Britain, founded in 1883, organised Irish voters behind the Irish Parliamentary Party, led by Charles Stewart Parnell.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.1] In the general election of November and December 1885 Parnell told Irish voters in Britain to vote against the Liberals, which in effect meant voting Conservative.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.2] T. P. O'Connor was elected MP for Liverpool Scotland in 1885 and held the seat until 1929, as a nationalist and a journalist. He is usually described as the only Irish nationalist elected for a British seat.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.3] O'Connor founded newspapers in London, such as The Star (1888), and used the press to support Home Rule (self-government for Ireland) and Irish causes.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.4] After Gladstone, the Liberal prime minister, introduced the first Home Rule Bill in 1886 (it was defeated), Irish voters in Liverpool and Glasgow largely supported the Liberal Party as the party of Home Rule.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.5] From the early 20th century Irish voters in Britain increasingly backed the Labour Party, which also drew support from the working class.
[hist-ire4-s3.7.6] Irish-born men could vote and stand for Parliament as British subjects, though the franchise was limited by property qualifications until the reforms of 1867 and 1884. This made participation easier than in the USA, where naturalisation was needed.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.b] The Molly Maguires were a secret society among Irish miners in the Pennsylvania anthracite (hard coal) coalfields, whose conflict with mine owners ended in executions.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.0] The society operated in the anthracite coalfields of eastern Pennsylvania, especially Schuylkill County, in the 1860s and 1870s. It drew on the tradition of the Ribbonmen, an Irish agrarian secret society.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.1] Irish miners worked in dangerous conditions for low pay, which fed their grievances, and many members belonged to the Ancient Order of Hibernians, an Irish Catholic fraternal organisation.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.2] Violence against mine bosses and police was blamed on the Mollies, so they were treated as a criminal conspiracy, who were accused of murders and arson.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.3] Franklin B. Gowen, head of the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad and its coal company, hired the Pinkerton detective agency, and the detective James McParland infiltrated the society under the name James McKenna.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.4] The Long Strike (December 1874 to June 1875) failed and broke the miners' union, the Workingmen's Benevolent Association. The trials of 1876 to 1878 followed, with convictions depending heavily on the evidence of informers and of McParland.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.5] Twenty men were hanged between 1877 and 1879. Ten were hanged on a single day, 21 June 1877, known as the Day of the Rope, at Pottsville and Mauch Chunk, and the leader Jack Kehoe was hanged in December 1878.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.6] Labour historians see the Mollies as victims of an employer-backed frame-up, while others see a real secret society using violence. Kevin Kenny (Making Sense of the Molly Maguires, 1998) argued they were a genuine society rooted in Irish agrarian violence, but that the trials were unfair.
[hist-ire4-s3.8.7] Jack Kehoe was pardoned after his death, by the governor of Pennsylvania in 1979.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.b] Judgements on Irish political life in America and Britain turn on whether the Irish gained real influence despite discrimination, or only built machines and patronage. Assimilation, adopting the language, customs and habits of the new country, is the thread: political participation was one route to it.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.0] Achievement: Irish politicians built lasting power in American cities and in some British seats, so they gave immigrants a route into public life.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.1] Limit: machine politics relied on patronage and corruption, and reform movements challenged it from the 1880s onwards.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.2] Success against discrimination: by the early 20th century Irish Americans were prominent in the police and city government, and Al Smith and Joseph Kennedy had reached national prominence, a sign that discrimination was being overcome.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.3] Contemporary view: nativists and reformers condemned Irish politicians as corrupt, while Irish communities defended them as champions of immigrants.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.4] Later view: historians disagree on whether Irish political success advanced assimilation or delayed it, because machine politics kept communities separate.
[hist-ire4-s3.9.5] Britain versus the USA: the Irish in Britain had votes sooner, but their political voice was channelled into the Irish Parliamentary Party and Labour rather than into city machines.

CANDIDATES hist-ire4-s2: 2. Emigration, 1855–1914 and 1920–1966; restriction and regulation after 1920
[hist-ire4-s2.0.b] The Famine (1845–1852) broke the old pattern of Irish life, and after it emigration did not stop. It became a settled part of Irish rural life, with a steady outflow in good years and bad.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.0] About 4 million people left Ireland in the six decades after the Famine (estimates vary), so the population of the whole island fell from about 6.5 million in 1851 to about 4.4 million in 1911.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.1] The United States took about four out of five emigrants; Britain, Canada, Australia and New Zealand took most of the rest.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.2] The number of Irish-born people in the United States peaked at about 1.87 million in the census of 1890, the highest total ever recorded there.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.3] Emigrants were mostly young and single, aged roughly 15 to 30, so the country lost people in the years when they would have worked and raised families.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.4] Ireland was unusual in Europe in sending about as many women as men; in some decades women outnumbered men.
[hist-ire4-s2.0.5] Emigration was heaviest from the west and south-west (Connacht and Munster), and the poorest counties of the west sent the highest proportion of their people.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.b] Emigrants were pushed out by how rural Ireland worked after the Famine. Several changes together left young people with few chances at home.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.0] After the Famine farms were no longer subdivided. Under impartible inheritance (the whole holding passes to one heir) one son inherited the farm and one daughter received a dowry (a sum of money paid to a husband's family on marriage), so the other children had to emigrate or stay unmarried.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.1] Late marriage was another cause, and it left many young adults without prospects at home.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.2] The shift from tillage (growing crops, which needs many workers) to cattle grazing (which needs few) reduced the work available on the land.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.3] There was little industry outside the north-east (Belfast and the Lagan valley), so young people from the rest of the country had no factory jobs to turn to.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.4] Agricultural depression from 1879 and evictions around 1880 to 1882 (families put out of their holdings for not paying rent) caused a fresh surge, especially from the west.
[hist-ire4-s2.1.5] The Land War, which began in 1879, was partly a response to the insecurity that fed this emigration.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.b] Leaving became a normal stage of life, and both the crossing and the farewell changed in this period.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.0] The American wake was a farewell gathering with music, dancing, food and drink as well as tears, held on the night before an emigrant left. It was treated like a wake (a gathering for the dead) because most emigrants never came back.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.1] Steamships replaced sail from the 1860s, cutting the Atlantic crossing from about six weeks to about two weeks (or less) by the 1870s, which made the voyage far safer.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.2] Liners called at Queenstown (Cobh) and at Moville near Derry to pick up emigrants travelling steerage (the cheapest, most crowded accommodation).
[hist-ire4-s2.2.3] In New York emigrants landed at Castle Garden from 1855 to 1890 and at Ellis Island from 1892. Traditionally the first person processed at Ellis Island, on 1 January 1892, was Annie Moore from Cork, though recent research has questioned details of her identity.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.4] Colonial governments in Australia and New Zealand paid or subsidised passages to attract settlers, so Irish emigration there was largely assisted. Passages to America were mostly paid privately, often with money sent by relatives.
[hist-ire4-s2.2.5] Cheap passages and pre-paid tickets (tickets bought abroad and sent home) were already used before 1855, so the system the later emigrants used was well established.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.b] Where emigrants settled and what work they did depended on their destination, their sex and the skills they brought. Most started at the bottom and many worked their way up.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.0] In the USA the Irish settled overwhelmingly in cities such as New York, Boston, Philadelphia and Chicago, and in the mill and mining towns of New England and Pennsylvania, where unskilled work was plentiful.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.1] Irish men mostly began as unskilled labourers on docks, railways, canals and building sites and in mines and factories. Many later moved up into skilled trades, police forces and fire departments.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.2] Irish women dominated domestic service in American cities, because the job gave board, lodging and wages that could be saved and sent home.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.3] Irish Americans moved into city politics through the Democratic Party, and the second generation (the American-born children of emigrants) began to enter teaching, clerical work and the professions.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.4] In Britain the Irish settled in Liverpool, Glasgow, London, Manchester and the industrial towns of Lancashire and west-central Scotland, working on the docks, in building, in mills and in mines.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.5] Irish nurses, teachers and domestic servants were prominent among women emigrants to Britain, and Irish harvest labourers continued to cross seasonally, returning home when the work ended.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.6] In Australia Irish-born people and their descendants made up roughly a quarter of the European population (estimates vary), settling as farmers, labourers and gold-miners in New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland.
[hist-ire4-s2.3.7] Many Protestants who went to Canada settled in Ontario, and some Irish emigrants went to New Zealand and Argentina.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.b] Emigrants did not cut their ties with home. Money and letters flowed back, and the same flow brought more people out.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.0] Emigrants' remittances were money and pre-paid tickets sent home by emigrants, and in total they were large.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.1] Much of the money came from servant girls and labourers, who sent home a large share of their wages.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.2] Remittances paid for the passages of younger brothers and sisters, so one emigrant could bring out a whole family over a few years.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.3] Remittances also paid rent, shop debts and dowries and helped families keep their holdings, which made emigration a support for the rural economy as well as a drain on it.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.4] The American letter, often containing dollars, was a regular event in western households, and it kept relatives informed of jobs and wages abroad.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.5] Chain-emigration (emigration in stages along family and parish networks) meant that new arrivals stayed with kin, found work through them and settled in the same parishes, so emigrant communities reproduced the networks of home.
[hist-ire4-s2.4.6] Letters collected by historians such as David Fitzpatrick (Oceans of Consolation, 1994, on emigrant letters from Australia) show how strongly emigrants kept family ties.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.b] After 1920 the United States began to limit immigration by law. Ireland was partitioned and the Free State was set up in 1922; its economy was weak and mostly agricultural, so the quotas changed where emigrants went rather than whether they left.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.0] The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 limited immigrants of each nationality to 3 per cent of that nationality's foreign-born population in the 1910 US census.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.1] The Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed Act) set quotas at 2 per cent of each foreign-born group in the 1890 census, a base chosen to favour north-western Europeans over southern and eastern Europeans.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.2] The Irish Free State received a quota of 28,567 a year under the 1924 Act, cut to 17,853 from 1929 when the national-origins formula took effect. Northern Ireland was counted under the British quota.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.3] The quotas redirected rather than ended emigration: emigration to the USA fell sharply from the 1920s, and Britain became the main destination.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.4] Britain was easy to enter, jobs were close, the journey was short and the fare was low, and many Irish people already had family and friends there. This is why the Irish moved to Britain in large numbers in the 1930s.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.5] The Great Depression after 1929 cut American demand for labour and reduced Irish emigration to the USA still further, and some emigrants returned.
[hist-ire4-s2.5.6] The Free State governments did not try to restrict emigration, which they treated as an economic problem they could not quickly solve.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.b] The Second World War and the years after it brought new rules about who could travel, and made Britain even more open to the Irish while the USA grew harder to enter.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.0] During the Emergency (the Irish name for the Second World War, 1939 to 1945) Irish people needed travel permits and identity documents to go to Britain, and both governments controlled who could travel.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.1] Britain recruited Irish workers for war factories, building and farm work, so over 100,000 people from the Irish state worked in Britain during the war (estimates vary); tens of thousands more joined the British forces.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.2] The Ireland Act of 1949 confirmed that Irish citizens in the United Kingdom were not treated as aliens and could live, work and vote there.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.3] The Common Travel Area allowed free movement between the two islands, which kept the journey simple for Irish workers.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.4] Irish people were also exempted from the controls of the Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962, so they stayed free to enter when other newcomers were restricted.
[hist-ire4-s2.6.5] The US Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart-Celler Act) abolished the national-origins quotas from 1968 and gave priority to family ties and skills, which made it harder for Irish people without American relatives to enter.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.b] After the war Britain was rebuilding and needed workers, and the Irish were the nearest and easiest supply.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.0] Post-war reconstruction created huge demand for labour in Britain, so Irish men worked as navvies (labourers who built roads, canals and railways) on roads, building sites and power stations.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.1] Irish women trained as nurses in the new National Health Service, founded in 1948, or worked in factories and offices.
[hist-ire4-s2.7.2] The Irish in Britain clustered in London (Kilburn, Camden Town, Cricklewood), Birmingham, Coventry, Luton, Manchester and Liverpool.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.b] In the 1950s emigration reached a level that alarmed the state. The government investigated its causes, and a change of economic policy later eased the problem.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.0] The government set up the Commission on Emigration and Other Population Problems in 1948, and it reported in 1954.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.1] The Commission found that emigration was caused mainly by economic factors such as low incomes, unemployment and small farms, but also by the attractions of better living standards and social life abroad.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.2] It accepted that emigration could not be ended quickly and urged economic development and better rural conditions rather than any ban on leaving.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.3] Emigration peaked in the 1950s: about 400,000 people left the state between 1951 and 1961 (net, meaning departures minus returns), most of them for Britain.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.4] The 1961 census recorded about 2.8 million people, the lowest population in the history of the state.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.5] The Vanishing Irish (1953), a collection of essays edited by the American priest John A. O'Brien, warned that the Irish nation was dying out through emigration and late marriage.
[hist-ire4-s2.8.6] Economic Development (1958), drawn up by T.K. Whitaker, and the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958), pursued by Seán Lemass as Taoiseach from 1959, brought growth, so emigration fell in the 1960s, though it did not end.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.b] Life for young emigrants in Britain was hard at first. Churches and community groups helped them cope.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.0] Many young emigrants arrived in Britain with no training and lived in lodging houses or navvy camps, where hard drinking and long hours were common.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.1] Navvy work was often arranged through subcontractors who paid cash in hand, leaving workers without insurance or job security.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.2] Notices reading 'No Irish' are widely remembered from lodging-house windows in the 1950s and 1960s, though historians disagree about how common they were.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.3] Catholic chaplaincies, the Legion of Mary and Irish centres in London and other cities helped new arrivals find lodgings and work.
[hist-ire4-s2.9.4] Irish dance halls such as the Galtymore in Cricklewood and the Gresham on the Holloway Road became centres of emigrant social life, where people met others from home.
[hist-ire4-s2.10.b] Assimilation means adopting the language, customs and identity of the new country. The Irish assimilated quickly in the USA and more partially in Britain.
[hist-ire4-s2.10.0] Assimilation in the USA was helped by the English language, the Democratic Party, the Catholic parish and its schools, and the size of the Irish community; by 1914 the second generation increasingly thought of itself as American.
[hist-ire4-s2.10.1] In Britain the Irish were also English-speaking but remained a visible Catholic working-class minority, and Catholic schools and parishes helped to keep a distinct identity.
[hist-ire4-s2.10.2] Irish-born people could return home easily, so many saw their stay in Britain as temporary, which slowed assimilation.
[hist-ire4-s2.10.3] Enda Delaney (The Irish in Post-War Britain, 2007) argued that Irish emigrants to Britain kept strong ties with home while integrating into British working life.
[hist-ire4-s2.10.4] Irish-speaking emigrants from the Gaeltacht (the Irish-speaking districts) tended to lose the language within a generation abroad.
[hist-ire4-s2.11.b] Domhnall (Dónall) Mac Amhlaigh (1926–1989) was an Irish-language writer whose diary of navvy life is the best-known first-hand account of 1950s emigration to Britain.
[hist-ire4-s2.11.0] Born in Galway in 1926 and raised in Co. Kilkenny, he wrote in Irish.
[hist-ire4-s2.11.1] Facing poor prospects at home he went to England in 1951 and worked as a labourer (a navvy) on building sites and roads, based in Northampton.
[hist-ire4-s2.11.2] His diary of 1951 to 1957, Dialann Deoraí (An Exile's Diary), was published in 1960; Valentin Iremonger translated it as An Irish Navvy: The Diary of an Exile (1964).
[hist-ire4-s2.11.3] It describes the work, the lodgings, the pubs and dance halls, homesickness, the company of other Irish navvies and the mixture of welcome and prejudice the Irish met in England.
[hist-ire4-s2.11.4] He was a participant, not an observer, because he lived the life he described and wrote it down as it happened.
[hist-ire4-s2.11.5] He also wrote novels and short stories in Irish, including Schnitzer Ó Sé (1974), and journalism; he died in 1989.
[hist-ire4-s2.11.6] Contemporaries in Ireland valued the diary as a frank picture of a life that official Ireland preferred not to discuss.
[hist-ire4-s2.11.7] Historians now treat it as a key source on the Irish in post-war Britain. As one author's account (a single man, an Irish speaker, a navvy, in the 1950s) it cannot stand for every emigrant's experience.
[hist-ire4-s2.12.b] Historians weigh emigration as both a loss and a relief, and as both a choice and a necessity. These paired judgements help to frame an answer.
[hist-ire4-s2.12.0] Safety valve versus drain: emigration relieved rural poverty and unemployment and brought in remittances, but it took the young and kept the population falling until 1961.
[hist-ire4-s2.12.1] Opportunity versus exile: many emigrants gained better wages and lives, while others, as Mac Amhlaigh shows, felt themselves exiles doing the hardest work.
[hist-ire4-s2.12.2] US restriction redirected rather than stopped emigration, because Britain's open door absorbed the flow after the 1930s.
[hist-ire4-s2.12.3] Assimilation: it was fast in the USA, helped by language, the Democratic Party and the parish, but only partial in Britain, where the Irish stayed a visible Catholic working-class minority with easy return.
[hist-ire4-s2.12.4] Contemporary view: churchmen and politicians spoke of the vanishing Irish and blamed modern attractions as well as poverty, while the Commission of 1954 placed the cause in economic weakness.
[hist-ire4-s2.12.5] Later view: Kerby Miller (Emigrants and Exiles, 1985) stressed exile and banishment, while historians such as Enda Delaney see emigration to Britain as a choice by individuals within family strategies, not simply as forced exile.
